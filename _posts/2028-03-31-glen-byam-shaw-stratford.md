## Glen Byam Shaw at Stratford: The Verdict on the Middle Path

Glen Byam Shaw's Romeo and Juliet, staged at the Shakespeare Memorial Theatre in Stratford-upon-Avon in 1958, is the production that the standard history of the play steps over on its way from Peter Brook to Franco Zeffirelli. The verdict argued here is that the step is a mistake. Byam Shaw's staging was not a timid interval between two revolutions. It was the fullest working demonstration of a rival discipline: the play spoken entire, in order, at speed, by a company young enough to make its haste believable, inside a design that cleared the stage of everything except the verse and the bodies carrying it. That discipline had a name in its own decade. The name was clarity, and clarity, this article argues, is not the absence of interpretation. Clarity is an interpretation, one that reads the tragedy as a machine of crossed timings whose meaning lives in the audience's ability to follow every gear. A staging that keeps the machine visible makes a claim about the play as strong as Brook's civic reading or Zeffirelli's naturalism, and its claim is the one the text itself keeps making, from the Prologue's promise of "the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue, 12) to the Prince's closing audit of the wreckage.

![Dorothy Tutin and Richard Johnson as the lovers in Glen Byam Shaw's 1958 Stratford production - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The charge against the middle path is familiar and will be given its full weight in what follows: that clarity is what a director pleads when invention fails, that the production survives in no landmark list, and that even the director's own biographer passes it by. The defence rests on the surviving record. That record is unusually concrete. The production's date, team, and company are preserved in the archive down to its fight arranger and its dance arranger. Its director left detailed pre-production notebooks for every Shakespeare staging he mounted, so his method can be examined rather than guessed at. Its two lovers can be dated to the day: the Juliet was twenty-eight on the first night, the Romeo thirty, in a decade when the parts still belonged by custom to stars a generation older. And its director closed his Stratford tenure by performing the most consequential act of casting in the theatre's history, choosing the twenty-seven-year-old Peter Hall to succeed him. A production embedded in decisions of that weight deserves a hearing on the evidence, and the evidence, weighed below, supports the verdict: the middle path was a position, held with intelligence, and its eclipse says more about what theatre history rewards than about what the audience in Stratford saw.

## The Production on Record: Stratford, April 1958

The archive fixes the production with a precision many more famous stagings cannot match. The Shakespeare Memorial Theatre's ninety-ninth festival season opened its account of the tragedy on 8 April 1958. The director was Glen Byam Shaw, the theatre's own director of productions. Scenery and costumes were by Motley, the design partnership whose association with Byam Shaw reached back a quarter of a century. The music was composed by Leslie Bridgewater, the festival's resident composer. The fights were arranged by Bernard Hepton, an actor in the first years of a long career, and the dances by Pauline Grant. These credits survive because the University of Birmingham's archive holds the season's press cuttings file for the production, reviews, photographs, and cast lists gathered in a single folder, and because the season's souvenir book printed the company's portraits with their roles attached. A staging that later history treated as unmemorable turns out to be one of the best documented of its decade.

### When did the production open, and in which Stratford season?

It opened on 8 April 1958 at the Shakespeare Memorial Theatre, Stratford-upon-Avon, in the festival's ninety-ninth season. The tragedy came first among the season's five productions, before Peter Hall's Twelfth Night later in April, Byam Shaw's Hamlet in June, Tony Richardson's Pericles in July, and Douglas Seale's Much Ado About Nothing in August.

The date matters because summaries of the production, including the brief from which this article is built, tend to place it loosely in the middle of the 1950s. The record puts it in 1958, near the end of the decade and near the end of Byam Shaw's tenure. That placement changes the argument. A staging of 1954 would be a confident mid-decade statement, contemporary with the young Peter Brook's London ascendancy and four years clear of Zeffirelli. A staging of April 1958 stands in a tighter and more interesting position: eleven years after Brook's Stratford experiment in the same building, two years before Zeffirelli's Old Vic production redrew the play's stage image, and months before the announcement that its director would hand the theatre to Hall. It belongs to the moment when the Memorial Theatre's postwar settlement, stars in repertory under a director of taste, was at its most accomplished and its most exposed. Judging it means judging it there, in its documented year, and not in the vaguer mid-decade glow of retrospective summary.

The cuttings file itself carries a small, telling detail. Alongside the reviews and photographs, the Birmingham folder preserves a magazine article devoted to Richard Johnson. Archives keep what the culture of the moment thought worth keeping, and the file's assembly shows the production doing, in real time, the work Hampton-Reeves later credited to the tenure as a whole: it was manufacturing a star. The Romeo of April 1958 was newsprint material by the summer, and the MGM contract followed within the year. The production took the play up in the building where, eleven years earlier, Brook had made the same tragedy a scandal of youth and division. The festival's own memory held both stagings, and the comparison between them, experiment against settlement, runs through everything that follows in this article.

The company the souvenir preserves is the second fact the record insists on. Richard Johnson played Romeo and Dorothy Tutin played Juliet. Edward Woodward, twenty-seven, played Mercutio; Ron Haddrick played Tybalt; Angela Baddeley, the director's wife, played the Nurse; Rachel Kempson played Lady Capulet; Michael Meacham played Paris; and Ian Holm, twenty-six, played Peter, the Nurse's man. Around them stood a company list that reads, in retrospect, like a roll of the next generation: Julian Glover, Edward de Souza, Roy Dotrice, Paxton Whitehead, Stephanie Bidmead, and older character strength in Mark Dignam, Paul Hardwick, Cyril Luckham, and Anthony Nicholls. The archive names the full company; where the surviving programme record does not attach a name to a part, this article leaves the part unassigned rather than guess. What can be said on the evidence is that the production was cast from a resident festival company at the height of its drawing power, not assembled around a visiting pair of stars, and that its lovers were promoted from inside that company. Johnson had been at Stratford since 1957 and Tutin arrived as the season's leading lady across three productions. The lovers were the company's own.

## The Evidence of Images: What the Souvenir Preserves

A word about the images, because they form the largest part of the surviving record and the most treacherous. The 1958 season souvenir runs to fifty-two pages and consists almost entirely of photographs. Its centrepiece for the tragedy is a full-page colour photograph of Tutin and Johnson as the lovers. Its design coverage gives the Motley work for the tragedy and the Hamlet a colour feature of its own, a distinction shared that year with the designs for Pericles, Twelfth Night, and Much Ado. Its portrait pages rank the company: full pages for Baddeley's Nurse, Kempson's Lady Capulet, Haddrick's Tybalt, and Woodward's Mercutio, smaller frames for Meacham's Paris, Holm's Peter, and the rest. A souvenir book is not a neutral document. It is the institution's chosen account of itself, printed for the audience to carry home, and its emphases are marketing decisions. The colour plate tells the enquirer what the Memorial Theatre believed it was selling in the spring of 1958: not a directorial concept, not a designer's Verona, but the lovers themselves, young, handsome, and in colour. The middle path had a poster, and the poster was a pair of faces.

The limits of the photographic record cut deeper, and they connect directly to the production's later invisibility. A still photograph preserves costume, grouping, and the geometry of a stage picture. It cannot preserve tempo, and tempo, the speed and intelligibility of the spoken verse, was the production's whole claim on its audience. The evidentiary media of theatre history are systematically biased toward the visual and the textual: toward designs that can be reproduced, prompt-books that can be collated, manifestos that can be quoted. A production whose excellence lived in the rate and clarity of its speaking leaves photographs of young people in handsome clothes and reviews that reach for the vocabulary of approval, and the archive, dutifully preserving both, preserves the husk of the thing. Later historians, working from the husk, conclude there was little there. The inference is understandable and wrong. It mistakes the record's silence about a virtue for the virtue's absence, and it is one more reason the case for 1958 has to be argued, as this article argues it, from the method, the casting, and the consequences, the three bodies of evidence in which a speaking production does leave traces.

## The Director as Actor: Benvolio in 1935

No director of this play in the twentieth century brought a stranger qualification to it than Byam Shaw: he had acted in the production against which all modern stagings of the tragedy were still measured. Glencairn Alexander Byam Shaw was born on 13 December 1904 and made his professional stage debut in August 1923, in Michael Denison's summary for the Oxford Dictionary of National Biography, with no prior training at all. He rose through the romantic repertory of the interwar London stage. In 1933 he took over the title role from John Gielgud in Richard of Bordeaux, Gordon Daviot's long-running historical romance, and in 1934 he played Laertes to Gielgud's Hamlet. Then, in 1935, Gielgud mounted the production of Romeo and Juliet that reset the play's modern history, alternating Romeo and Mercutio with Laurence Olivier, with Peggy Ashcroft as Juliet and Edith Evans as the Nurse. Byam Shaw played Benvolio.

### What part did the director himself once play in the tragedy?

He played Benvolio, Romeo's friend and the play's failed peacemaker, in John Gielgud's 1935 production at the New Theatre in London. Gielgud and Laurence Olivier alternated Romeo and Mercutio, Peggy Ashcroft played Juliet, Edith Evans played the Nurse, and Motley designed the scenery and costumes, as the partnership would again for his own staging twenty-three years later.

The Benvolio fact is more than a biographical ornament, because Benvolio is the character through whom the tragedy's civic machinery is first explained to an audience. It is Benvolio who narrates the opening brawl to Montague, reconstructing the fight blow by blow: the part is a small masterclass in exposition, in making a complicated action plain to listeners who did not see it. The actor who spent the autumn of 1935 performing that act of narration, inside a production famous for restoring the play's full text and speed after generations of acting versions, absorbed the discipline of intelligibility from inside the century's most admired classical staging. When the same man came to direct the tragedy at Stratford in 1958, clarity was not a slogan he had borrowed. It was the house style of the production that had formed him, carried forward by the same designer whose work had clothed it. The 1935 staging had been Motley's as well: the partnership of Margaret Harris, Sophie Harris, and Elizabeth Montgomery had first worked with Gielgud on his Oxford Romeo and Juliet of 1932, and the Victoria and Albert Museum's catalogue of their designs credits the team with bringing a new simplicity and clarity to stage design. The middle path, in other words, had a pedigree and a personnel. It descended from 1935 by direct line.

Between the Benvolio and the Stratford directorship stands the apprenticeship that made Byam Shaw a teacher of actors. He served in the armed forces during the war. From 1947 to 1951 he directed the Old Vic Theatre School, the training arm of the Old Vic Theatre Centre run by Michel Saint-Denis, alongside George Devine's Young Vic. The Centre was the most ambitious attempt of its years to rebuild English acting on a discipline of voice, movement, and textual intelligence. Saint-Denis, the French director who had become the conscience of the English classical stage, built the Centre as three interlocking instruments: a working theatre company, a school to train its successors, and a young people's theatre to grow its audience. Byam Shaw ran the school, the instrument aimed longest into the future, and the appointment tells its own story about where his gifts were already understood to lie. The Centre ended in institutional quarrel: the Old Vic governors dismissed the experiment's leadership, and Saint-Denis, Devine, and Byam Shaw resigned in 1951. The diaspora that followed seeded the English Stage Company at the Royal Court and much of the new theatre of the next decade. The episode matters for the Stratford record because it fixed the pattern of Byam Shaw's career. Twice in a decade he stood inside an institution at the moment of its transformation, and both times his contribution was the unglamorous one: standards, training, succession. Stratford would be the second seeding, and the schoolmaster's habits, the prepared notebook, the patience with young actors, the long view of a company's growth, went with him to the Avon intact.

## Running the Memorial Theatre: The Shaw Directorship

Byam Shaw came to Stratford in 1952 as co-director with Anthony Quayle, an actor-manager of formidable connections, and took sole charge in 1956. He held the theatre until 1959. The bare outline understates the transformation. Before the 1950s, in the standard summary of the Oxford Dictionary of National Biography, Stratford seasons had been widely regarded as worthy but unexciting. Under Quayle and Byam Shaw the festival became one of the principal centres of British theatre. He was appointed CBE in 1954, directed fourteen plays at Stratford across the tenure, and filled the Memorial Theatre's stage with the leading actors of the age on terms no London management could match: a full season of great parts in a small town, away from the West End's glare.

### What did the directorship change at the Memorial Theatre?

It changed a worthy provincial festival into one of the two pillars of British Shakespeare. As co-director with Anthony Quayle and then in sole charge from 1956, Byam Shaw drew Olivier, Leigh, Redgrave, Gielgud, Ashcroft, and Laughton to Stratford, directed fourteen plays, and left the theatre strong enough for the Royal Shakespeare Company to be built on its foundation.

The tenure had announced its ambitions from its first full season. In 1953, the year after his arrival as co-director, Byam Shaw staged Antony and Cleopatra with Michael Redgrave and Peggy Ashcroft, a pairing of the two most respected classical actors of their generation in the canon's most demanding love tragedy, and The Taming of the Shrew with Marius Goring and Yvonne Mitchell. The Antony and Cleopatra was the calling card: a new director of productions declaring, with his first great casting, that Stratford intended to compete for the finest classical acting in the country and to give it the largest canvases. The CBE followed in 1954, formal recognition arriving early, as if the state had noticed the festival's rise before the theatre world had finished arguing about it. The seasons that followed alternated the director's own productions with the guest engagements described earlier, and the balance between the two became the tenure's signature: a resident standard, illustrated by imports of genius, against which the resident company could measure itself nightly.

The singled-out productions define the directorship's range. Michael Denison's biographical assessment names five: Antony and Cleopatra with Michael Redgrave and Peggy Ashcroft in 1953; Macbeth with Laurence Olivier and Vivien Leigh in 1955; As You Like It with Ashcroft in 1957; Othello with Harry Andrews and Emlyn Williams in 1956, an Othello and Iago of unusual intellectual finish; and King Lear with Charles Laughton and Albert Finney in 1959, the farewell. Around those peaks the programme record fills in the working seasons: The Taming of the Shrew and Much Ado About Nothing, Julius Caesar with Alec Clunes and Richard Johnson's Mark Antony in 1957, The Merry Wives of Windsor, and two productions in the 1958 season itself, the tragedy in April and Hamlet with Michael Redgrave in June. The theatre historian Stuart Hampton-Reeves, in his study of Peter Hall's Stratford years, describes the Memorial Theatre of this period as, with the Old Vic, one of the twin pillars of British Shakespeare performance, and notes the double achievement on which its standing rested: it attracted stars, and it made them. Johnson, Tutin, Woodward, Holm, and the rest of the 1958 company are the second clause of that sentence in action.

The fourteen productions he directed himself are only half the tenure's record. The other half is the list of directors he engaged, and it recasts the clarity question at the level of the institution. Under Quayle and Byam Shaw the Memorial Theatre became a house in which the strongest directing talents of the age were content to work: John Gielgud staged the Twelfth Night of 1955 with Olivier's Malvolio and Vivien Leigh's Viola, and the Much Ado of the same season with his own Benedick opposite Ashcroft's Beatrice. George Devine directed the King Lear of 1955. Peter Brook directed the Titus Andronicus of 1955, with Olivier in the title role, and the Tempest of 1957 with Gielgud's Prospero, a production that transferred to Drury Lane. Margaret Webster, Michael Langham, Douglas Seale, and Tony Richardson all directed in the seasons of the tenure, and Peter Hall was given his Stratford apprenticeship inside it. A director of productions who programmes Brook's Titus in one season and Gielgud's Twelfth Night in another is not enforcing a single manner on his theatre. He is curating a standard. The clarity that governed Byam Shaw's own rehearsals operated at Stratford as an editorial discipline over the whole festival: whatever the guest director's style, the house expectation was that the text would be spoken so an audience could follow it, by actors cast and rehearsed to that end. The 1958 Romeo and Juliet must be read as one voice inside that curated chorus, the resident director's own statement of the house standard in the play the house's history had made its touchstone. The middle path was not only a man's method. For eight seasons it was an institution's policy, applied by other hands to other plays, and judged nightly by the same audience that heard the tragedy in April.

Two documented details give the directorship its flavour. The first is Byam Shaw's As You Like It of 1957, in which, for the first time in the play's stage history, the action was staged to begin in winter and progress to summer, so that the Forest of Arden thawed as the comedy healed. The device is worth pausing on, because it dismantles the caricature of the clarity director as a man without ideas. The seasonal arc is a strong interpretive stroke. It is also an invisible one: no manifesto announced it, no programme essay theorised it, and an audience could receive the whole effect without ever naming it. That was the house manner. Interpretation at Byam Shaw's Stratford was built into the staging's weather rather than pasted onto its surface. The second detail is the royal visit of 1957, when the Queen and the Duke of Edinburgh attended the festival and saw Ashcroft's Rosalind, with Johnson as her Orlando. A theatre does not receive such a visit on charm. It receives it because the institution has become, in the eyes of the culture that funds and watches it, the national home of its national dramatist. By 1957, under Byam Shaw's direction, the Memorial Theatre held that standing without subsidy, on box office and reputation alone.

Peggy Ashcroft's place in the tenure requires precision, because she is the thread that ties the directorship back to 1935. Her return to Stratford came in 1957, and the two performances were divided between the theatre's two directors: she played Rosalind for Byam Shaw in As You Like It, the production whose seasonal design framed her, and Imogen for the young Peter Hall in Cymbeline. She did not act in the 1958 Romeo and Juliet. Her presence in the building, rehearsing one great heroine under the director and another under his chosen successor, is nonetheless part of the production's context: the Juliet of 1935 was in the company while the Juliet of 1958 was being prepared, and the standard she had set for speaking the part, freshness joined to intelligence, was the standard the new production inherited. Article 304's account of Ashcroft's Juliet documents what that standard was. Stratford in 1958 did not need to import its idea of the heroine. It was walking the corridors.

## Clarity as Method: Notebooks, Motley, and the Plain Stage

The word clarity attaches to Byam Shaw so readily that it risks sounding like a euphemism offered by friends. The surviving evidence converts it into something harder: a documented working method. The Warwick researcher Julian Richards, whose doctoral work constitutes the first major biographical investigation of the director, records the foundation of the method in a single striking fact. Byam Shaw made detailed pre-production notebooks for all of the Shakespeare productions he directed. The notebooks survive as a class of evidence almost no other British director of his generation left behind. They show a staging worked out in advance on paper: the text read, the problems named, the solutions drafted before the first rehearsal. Richards's verdict on the tenure the notebooks served is equally plain: Peter Hall established the Royal Shakespeare Company a year after taking over, building on the foundation Byam Shaw laid. A director whose clarity can be checked in his own handwriting is not a director who drifted into plainness. The plainness was prepared.

### Where does the evidence for the clarity method survive?

It survives in the director's own pre-production notebooks, prepared in detail for every Shakespeare production he directed and the basis of Julian Richards's biographical study at Warwick. The season's press cuttings file at the University of Birmingham and the 1958 souvenir's cast portraits complete the production's evidence base.

Motley supplied the method's visual half. The partnership's whole career, from the Oxford Romeo and Juliet of 1932 onward, was a campaign against the upholstered stage picture: against scenery that told the audience what to feel before a line was spoken. The Victoria and Albert Museum's summary of their innovation, attached to the 1932 designs, names the new note exactly: simplicity and clarity. At Stratford in 1958 the partnership designed both of Byam Shaw's productions, the tragedy and the Hamlet, and the one extended contemporary description of their manner that season comes from Theatre World's survey of the festival, which wrote of the Hamlet setting that Motley's scenery was plain, a few pillars and a flight of steps. The sentence describes the Hamlet, and this article does not move it across the corridor to the tragedy. What it establishes is the design climate of Byam Shaw's 1958: a permanent architectural frame, a flight of steps doing the work of a balcony and a tomb approach, crowds arranged as architecture. The tragedy's own photographs, preserved in the season souvenir with a full-page colour plate of the lovers, show the same climate: Verona as a built place the verse could move through at speed, rather than a painted vista the action had to stop and admire. The design did not illustrate the play. It cleared the ground on which the play's own scene-making, the orchard wall conjured out of dialogue, the tomb conjured out of a door and a torch, could operate.

Music, fights, and dances completed the method's practical side, and their credits carry a quiet argument about the production's priorities. Leslie Bridgewater's score came from the festival's own music department, a resident function rather than a visiting name. The dances were Pauline Grant's, and their place in this play is a test of any production, since the Capulet ball is where the lovers' private sonnet must be heard inside a public room. The fights were Bernard Hepton's. Hepton's presence in the credit line is a small emblem of the tenure's habit: the Memorial Theatre under Byam Shaw repeatedly handed serious responsibility to the young. The fight arranger of the season's tragedy went on to a long acting career of his own. The method, in short, was a system: a text prepared in notebooks, a stage cleared by a trusted designer, a company trained in the building, and every department answered for by someone who understood that the verse was the event. None of this is invention. All of it is discipline, and discipline is what the record preserves.

## A Building and a System: The Memorial Stage in 1958

Clarity at Stratford was not only a rehearsal discipline. It was built into the theatre's architecture and into the economics of its season, and both deserve a hearing before the casting is judged, because both constrained what any director of productions could attempt. The building in which the production played was Elisabeth Scott's Shakespeare Memorial Theatre, opened in 1932 on the bank of the Avon after fire destroyed the Victorian theatre on the site in 1926. Scott's house was a picture-frame stage of the modern kind: a proscenium, a deep stage behind it, and an auditorium arranged for the framed view. Nothing in that architecture invited the platform-stage freedoms that Granville-Barker had preached and that the rebuilt Globe would later restore. A director working inside the frame in 1958 had two choices of visual rhetoric. He could fill the frame with scenery and accept the scene-change pauses and pictorial emphases that scenery imposes, as the Lyceum tradition had done. Or he could empty the frame to the point where the picture became a permanent architectural ground, figures moving against stone and steps, and let the verse supply the scene. Motley's Stratford manner, the manner of the 1958 designs, was the second rhetoric developed into a system. The apron stage that Hall would later thrust out into the auditorium, with the rake his plans specified, did not yet exist. The middle path had to be walked inside the picture frame, and it was.

The season's structure pressed in the same direction. The Memorial Theatre did not mount a production and run it. It mounted five productions and rotated them in repertory across a season of more than thirty weeks, so that on any given week the tragedy, the Twelfth Night, the Hamlet, and their companions succeeded one another on the same stage, with the scenery of each struck and reset in the hours between performances. Repertory rotation is a hard school for design. A setting that takes a stage crew an evening to build cannot be rotated; a setting built as a permanent architectural shell, re-dressed by properties and hangings, can play on Monday, vanish on Tuesday, and return on Wednesday without loss. The plain Verona of the 1958 tragedy, pillars and steps and built stone of the kind Theatre World described in the season's Hamlet, was not merely an aesthetic preference. It was the repertory system's necessary form, and Byam Shaw's achievement was to make a necessity read as a style. The point deserves emphasis because it reverses the usual charge. Critics of the middle path have treated its plainness as a failure of imagination, a director content with little. The rotation record shows plainness functioning as the enabling condition of the festival's whole enterprise: five productions, one stage, a company of forty, and an audience that could see the tragedy on the same visit as the comedy. A director who filled the frame with a bespoke Verona of painted streets would have been spending the other four productions' freedom on his own. The clarity was collegiate. It belonged to the system, and the system was the tenure's real production, of which the tragedy was one distinguished instance.

The governance behind the system completes the constraint set. The Memorial Theatre was a family foundation before it was a national institution: the Flower brewing family had founded the festival theatre in the nineteenth century, and its chairman in Byam Shaw's years was Fordham Flower, the man to whom Hall would make his Leningrad pitch. The theatre ran on box office and endowment rather than on state subsidy, and the fact shaped every artistic decision of the tenure. A subsidised ensemble can programme for posterity. An unsubsidised festival must fill a large house in a small town, nightly, for thirty weeks, with one author's plays. The star guests, the young company displayed like a stable of thoroughbreds, the scenery built to rotate, the verse spoken to be followed from the back row: each element of the middle path answers to that economic sentence as well as to the aesthetic one. The settlement's art and its arithmetic were the same policy, and judging the production without the arithmetic mistakes a solved equation for a temperament. The tragedy of 1958 was financed by the same discipline that shaped it, and its clarity was as much a budget line as a belief.

The same system explains the season's casting shape, which the next sections examine in detail. A repertory company cannot import a Romeo for one play. It must cast the whole festival from its contracted strength, which means the lovers of April are also the Aguecheek of April, the Pericles of July, the Don John of August. The young company was not a policy Byam Shaw adopted for this play. It was the material the festival gave him, and his direction turned the constraint into the production's most praised quality: an ensemble in which the tragedy's world was populated by actors the audience had watched, and would watch again, across the entire map of the season.

## Youth in the Lovers: Johnson and Tutin

The brief for this article repeats the production's standing description: youthful casting. The record allows the phrase to be tested rather than repeated, and the test produces the production's most quoted and most misquoted fact. Dorothy Tutin was born on 8 April 1930. The production opened on 8 April 1958. The Juliet of the ninety-ninth season played the first night on her twenty-eighth birthday. Richard Johnson, born on 30 July 1927, was thirty. Set against the part's written age, a girl not yet fourteen, both were adults, and this article will not pretend otherwise. Set against the stage tradition the production inherited, they were a deliberate descent in age. The Romeos of the grand tradition had been men in their late thirties and forties, tragedians lending the boy their authority. The alternating Romeos of 1935 had been thirty-one and twenty-eight, Gielgud and Olivier, and the sensation of that production was partly the shock of youth. Byam Shaw, who had stood on that stage as Benvolio, cast the same shock again: a Romeo of thirty who was still, unmistakably, a young man, and a Juliet of twenty-eight whose authority came from technique rather than from seniority.

### How old were Richard Johnson and Dorothy Tutin on the first night?

Richard Johnson was thirty and Dorothy Tutin was twenty-eight; the first night, 8 April 1958, was Tutin's twenty-eighth birthday. Both were adults measured against the heroine's written age of not quite fourteen, and both were young measured against the tradition of mature stars in the parts, which is the comparison the production's admirers meant.

The pairing was also a reunion, and the reunion mattered to what audiences saw. Johnson and Tutin had played together three years earlier in London, in Jean Anouilh's The Lark, he as the Earl of Warwick to her Joan of Arc. They arrived at the lovers with an established stage rapport, two careers rising in parallel. Tutin's rise had been the more precipitous. She had graduated from the Royal Academy of Dramatic Art in 1949 at nineteen and was playing Katherine in Henry V at the Old Vic within the year; by 1953 her performance in Graham Greene's first play, The Living Room, had made her a leading lady of the London stage while she was still in her early twenties. A Juliet who arrives with that history is not being discovered by her director. She is being deployed by him, at the peak of her first fame, in the part her decade had been pointing her toward, and the production's confidence in casting her across Juliet, Viola, and Ophelia in a single season was the festival's public statement that its leading lady could carry its whole account of young womanhood. Johnson's trajectory was steadier and, for the succession argument, even more telling: he was precisely the kind of actor the Memorial system existed to make, a leading man manufactured by repertory, part by part, until the Romeo was not a casting gamble but a graduation. Johnson's Stratford apprenticeship had been served in full view: Orlando to Ashcroft's Rosalind and Mark Antony in Byam Shaw's own productions of 1957, Posthumus opposite Ashcroft's Imogen for Hall, Ferdinand in Peter Brook's Tempest. In the 1958 season itself he carried four roles: Romeo, Sir Andrew Aguecheek in Hall's Twelfth Night, the title part in Tony Richardson's Pericles, and Don John in Much Ado About Nothing. Tutin's season was heavier still at the top of the bill: Juliet in April, Viola in Hall's Twelfth Night a fortnight later, and Ophelia in Byam Shaw's Hamlet in June. Theatre World's survey of the festival, surveying the whole season, singled her out in terms that have followed the production ever since: Miss Tutin was to be congratulated on the good fortune of playing Juliet, Viola, and Ophelia in one festival, and on a fine, impressive performance in each part. Three of Shakespeare's young women, three different registers of girlhood, in a single season, from one actress of twenty-eight. The congratulation was earned, and it identifies what the production's Juliet actually was: not a discovery plucked from obscurity, as Zeffirelli's film Juliet would be marketed a decade later, but the leading performance of a festival that had been built to display her.

The aftermath confirms the casting's force in the hardest currency the theatre knows. Johnson's stage Romeo became the foundation of his screen career: his biographical record states plainly that it was his reputation in the part that brought him his MGM contract and his film debut opposite Frank Sinatra in Never So Few in 1959. Tutin's Juliet, meanwhile, was not finished in 1958. She played the part again at Stratford under Peter Hall in 1961, opposite Brian Murray, with Edith Evans, the Nurse of 1935, as her Nurse: the succession staging its own genealogy in a single cast list. That second Juliet belongs to the record of the Royal Shakespeare Company's first years and is treated in the survey of the company's decades; its existence is registered here because it completes the verdict on the first. Hall, rebuilding the theatre on new principles, reached for Byam Shaw's Juliet to open his account with the play. Directors do not borrow failures for foundation stones.

One more journey belongs to the production's biography. In the winter following the season, the Memorial Theatre company toured to Moscow and Leningrad, carrying the festival abroad at the height of the Cold War's cultural exchanges. Johnson's biographical record lists his tour roles as Romeo and Aguecheek: the lovers of April played in the two great Russian capitals, in the same trip on which, in a Leningrad conversation with the theatre's chairman, the young Hall outlined the plan for the company that would replace the one then performing. The selection itself is evidence. A touring repertory is a company's account of its own best work, chosen for foreign stages where the institution's name is the attraction and no local loyalty softens the judgement. The tragedy of 1958 went abroad as one of the season's export productions, with its Stratford Romeo at its head. The production that history forgot at home was, for one winter, the face English Shakespeare presented to the Soviet stage. The middle path travelled.

The lovers' later records close the casting argument in a way no review could. Johnson, the company Romeo manufactured by the Memorial system, became an Associate Artist of the Royal Shakespeare Company at its inception and remained one until his death in 2015: the actor formed by the old regime was carried on the new regime's roll of honour for the whole of its first half-century. The fact is a small institutional miracle of continuity. Companies reborn in revolution usually pension off the old guard's symbols. Hall's company enrolled Byam Shaw's Romeo among its founding associates, because the man himself embodied the standard the new company claimed to inherit, and because his Stratford Romeo had been the old system's certificate that its training worked. Tutin's career ran on at the summit of the English classical stage for the rest of the century, the festival's three-role season of 1958 standing in her record as the feat by which her generation measured a leading lady's arrival. Between them, the two lovers give the middle path its longest-lived evidence. A production is, in the end, the people it convened and what the convening made of them. Judged by that measure, the staging of April 1958 convened the two careers that, more than any others of their cohort, carried the Stratford standard across the divide of 1960 and into the new company's first decades. The eclipse of the production in the landmark lists is real. The eclipse of its consequences is not available to any honest reading of the record, because its consequences are still on the stage's family tree, in the direct line, two generations down.

## The Company Around Them: A Stratford Ensemble in Formation

A festival production stands or falls by its middle ranks, and the 1958 company's middle ranks repay attention, because the season catches a generation at the exact moment of its formation. Edward Woodward's Mercutio was the showpiece of the supporting cast on paper, and the season spread his talent across its range: Mercutio in April, Laertes in the Hamlet in June, Claudio in the Much Ado in August. Mercutio is the part in which a production's verbal temperature is set. The Queen Mab speech (1.4) is a set piece that can stop the tragedy cold if it is played as a turn, and its text shows why: the speech accelerates through its catalogue of dreams until the fancy outruns the speaker, and Romeo has to cut it off with the diagnosis that Mercutio talks of nothing. A director of Byam Shaw's schooling, whose own formation was in the verse-speaking revival of the 1930s, needed a Mercutio who could ride that acceleration without breaking the line's intelligibility. Woodward at twenty-seven, in the season that made his name, was cast as precisely that instrument. The souvenir's portrait list gives him full-page standing alongside the season's stars, a small documentary measure of how the company itself rated the performance's prominence.

Ron Haddrick's Tybalt and Michael Meacham's Paris complete the triangle of young men around the lovers, and the casting states the production's reading of the feud without a word of programme prose. Tybalt and Paris are the two instruments of the Capulet interest, the sword and the marriage contract, and the play's catastrophe requires the audience to feel both as live options: Tybalt's violence credible enough to make the street dangerous, in the Prince's words a city where civil hands are made unclean (1.1), and Paris's suit respectable enough to make Capulet's haste forgivable. Capulet's own pitch to Paris is the feudal bargain in miniature: his child is yet a stranger in the world (1.2), and the suitor is told to woo her and win her heart before consent follows. Casting the two roles from the company's young strength rather than from its character bench kept the bargain's menace and its respectability in the same generation as the lovers. The feud, in this casting, is not old men persecuting the young from above. It is the young destroying the young inside a machine their elders built, which is the tragedy's actual design.

The older generation was present in two performances whose casting carried meanings of its own. The Nurse was played by Angela Baddeley, a leading Stratford presence throughout the tenure and, since 1929, the director's wife. The fact is recorded without embroidery because it cuts two ways, and both ways deserve stating. The hostile reading calls it the festival's domestic comfort, the director's household at the heart of the company. The record supports a cooler interpretation. Baddeley was a principal of the company on her own account across the decade, Maria in Gielgud's Twelfth Night of 1955, the Bawd in the Pericles of this same season, Emilia and Regan to come in 1959. Her Nurse belonged to the line of great character performances the series has traced in its study of the part as a star role: a Nurse played by an actress of the first rank, in a company where the role's size, the third-largest female part in the canon of this play by most counts, was treated as the major engagement it is. Baddeley's own record in the building measures the company's depth in the character ranks. She had acted in Gielgud's companies since the war years, in the celebrated Love for Love revival of 1943 among them, and at Stratford she was a fixture of the tenure: Maria in Gielgud's Twelfth Night of 1955, the Bawd in the Pericles of this same 1958 season, and, in the festival of 1959, Emilia in the Othello and Regan in her husband's King Lear. A later generation would know her as Mrs Bridges in the television series Upstairs, Downstairs, the most famous cook in British drama; the Stratford audience of 1958 knew her as the company's senior actress of character, and her Nurse stood in the direct line of the part's great holders, the line the series traces from the nineteenth century through Edith Evans. Lady Capulet was Rachel Kempson, whose season likewise ran through Dionyza in Pericles and Ursula in Much Ado, and whose presence linked the company to its Hamlet: she was Michael Redgrave's wife, and the festival's first family played on both sides of the lovers' story. Below these names the programme spreads the unassigned remainder, Dignam, Hardwick, Luckham, Nicholls, Glover, de Souza, Dotrice, Whitehead, Bidmead, and the rest, and the honest summary of the whole list is Hampton-Reeves's: the Memorial Theatre in these years attracted stars and made them, in the same rehearsal rooms, in the same season. The 1958 Romeo and Juliet was not a star vehicle parked inside a festival. It was the festival itself, at work.

The festival's wider company, beyond this production's own cast list, completes the picture of a nursery at full stretch. Geraldine McEwan, who played Olivia, Marina, and Hero across the season's other productions, and Zoe Caldwell, Eileen Atkins, and Edward Hardwick in the company's ranks, were all in Stratford that year, in the building, in the system, absorbing the standard. None of them appears in the tragedy's cast as the archive preserves it, and this article claims none of them for it. Their presence in the season is nonetheless evidence about the production's world: the 1958 Romeo and Juliet was rehearsed and played inside the single most talent-dense company the English stage then possessed, a company in which a future generation's leading players were carrying spears, singing in crowds, and watching the lovers from the wings. When Hall rebuilt the institution two years later, the human material of the new company was already trained. It had been trained, in no small part, in seasons like this one, under the director who chose him.

## The Season Around the Tragedy: Five Productions, One Company

The repertory fact deserves one full statement, because it is the context in which the production's clarity stops looking like temperament and starts looking like craft. The ninety-ninth season comprised five productions. The tragedy opened it on 8 April. A fortnight later came Hall's Twelfth Night, with Tutin's Viola, Johnson's Aguecheek, McEwan's Olivia, Dignam's Malvolio, Wymark's Sir Toby, and Luckham's Feste. In June Byam Shaw's Hamlet followed, Redgrave's prince supported by Googie Withers as Gertrude, Dignam as Claudius, Luckham as Polonius, Woodward as Laertes, Tutin as Ophelia, and Holm in the small change of Reynaldo and the Second Player. July brought Richardson's Pericles, the season's rarity, with Johnson in the title role, Bidmead as Thaisa, McEwan as Marina, Kempson as Dionyza, Baddeley as the Bawd, and Atkins as the goddess Diana. August closed the bill with Seale's Much Ado, Redgrave's Benedick against Withers's Beatrice, McEwan's Hero, Woodward's Claudio, and Johnson's Don John. Read the five bills together and a single company comes into focus, doubling and redoubling across the map of Shakespeare's kinds: the same Dignam who stood in the tragedy's crowd wore the crowns of Malvolio and Claudius; the same Kempson who played Lady Capulet in April played the villainess of Pericles in July; the director of the tragedy also directed the Hamlet, and the director of the Twelfth Night was the tragedy director's chosen heir.

Two consequences follow for judging the production itself. The first is about attention. No actor in the 1958 tragedy was rehearsing one part in one play. Johnson prepared Romeo while Aguecheek, Pericles, and Don John waited in the same season's portfolio; Tutin built Juliet with Viola a fortnight behind her and Ophelia ahead; Woodward's Mercutio shared his summer with Laertes and Claudio. Repertory acting of this density produces a specific virtue, and it is exactly the virtue the production was praised for: an ease inside the verse that comes from speaking Shakespeare nightly in five different registers, a fluency that cannot be rehearsed into a single-role company in six weeks. The second consequence is about the audience. The Stratford spectator of 1958 did not meet the lovers as strangers. The festival's regulars had watched Johnson graduate through the 1957 season, had seen Tutin's reputation arrive ahead of her, and would watch both actors in comedy within the fortnight. The production played, all season, to a house that knew its company the way a town knows its own. That intimacy is part of what the middle path meant in practice: not a style imposed on the play, but a relationship between a company and an audience, renewed nightly, in which a tragedy could afford to be clear because nobody in the building was being introduced. Zeffirelli's revolution two years later addressed an audience of strangers with the shock of the new. Byam Shaw's settlement addressed an audience of neighbours with the shock of recognition. Both are real theatrical goods. The history books kept the one that photographed better. Read whole, the five bills describe a festival that had become, under its director of productions, a single instrument with five stops, and the tragedy was the stop on which its maker had staked his own formation in the play.

## What Clarity Does to the Text of Romeo and Juliet

Every staging method is a wager on where a play's meaning lives. The clarity method wagers that this tragedy's meaning lives in its pattern, and that the pattern is intricate enough to need no help. Consider what the wager pays out on in the text's own terms. The Prologue is a sonnet that spends fourteen lines giving away the entire plot (Prologue, 1 to 14): two households, a pair of star-crossed lovers, a feud ended by their deaths. A playwright who begins by surrendering his suspense is not asking his audience to wonder what happens. He is asking them to watch how, and the how of this play is a machinery of timings: a message that arrives an hour late, a potion measured to forty-two hours, a wedding moved forward by one day. Clarity is the staging virtue that serves that design, because the design only wounds when every timing is visible. A production that muddles the sequence to heighten the emotion is cheating its own play. The Friar states the tragedy's whole physics in his entrance speech, in the doctrine that virtue itself turns vice, being misapplied (2.3): every good instrument in the story, the marriage, the potion, the letter, the ladder, is a virtue misapplied by haste. To follow the play is to watch the misapplications click into place one by one. That following is a pleasure this production was built, department by department, to protect.

The civic frame pays it too, and the civic frame is where the middle path's difference from Brook can be measured most exactly. The Prince speaks three times in the play, and the three speeches are its pillars: the edict after the first brawl, threatening death to the next disturbers of the peace (1.1); the sentence after the second, exiling Romeo on the instant of Mercutio's death (3.1); and the final audit over the bodies (5.3), in which the ruler counts his own losses, a brace of kinsmen, and closes the account with the admission that all are punished. Between the pillars the feud's temperature can be read like a gauge: threat, sentence, reckoning. Brook's 1947 staging, as the article on that production records, made the feud a civic condition by emphasis, crowding the stage with the city's life until the lovers were crushed by a society. The 1958 method reached the same architecture by the opposite road. It trusted the pillars to stand if they were spoken clearly and placed exactly, and let the crowd scenes play at their textual size. The difference is a genuine interpretive fork, and it should be named as one. Brook's reading argues that the play's meaning is the pressure of the world on the lovers. The clarity reading argues that the play's meaning is the failure of the world's instruments, edict, sentence, marriage, letter, potion, each one intelligible, each one misapplied, and that the audience must be able to inspect each instrument as it fails. Verona in the second reading is not a crowd. It is a machine of offices, prince, father, friar, nurse, apothecary, each performing a function the audience can name. The machine reading is colder. It is also, on the text's evidence, the reading the Prologue announces when it calls the play a traffic of households rather than a romance of lovers, and it is the reading a verse-speaking festival company was built to deliver.

The famous scenes pay the same dividend. The lovers' first meeting is a sonnet spoken by two voices in alternation, beginning with Romeo's offer to profane with his unworthiest hand the holy shrine of hers (1.5). The scene's erotic charge is inseparable from its form: two strangers improvising a perfect poem together, in public, in dactylic time stolen from a dance. A staging in which the words are thrown away for naturalistic heat dissolves the very thing the scene is about, the discovery that these two people speak the same language at first sight. A staging that trusts the sonnet lets the form make the argument. The window scene is built the same way: the audience, not Romeo, holds the scene's knowledge, having heard Juliet's meditations before she knows she is overheard, and the verse keeps re-marking the distance the staging must preserve, from the sun simile of the opening (2.2) to the parting that is such sweet sorrow (2.2). The aubade at dawn (3.5) turns the same trick in miniature, an argument about a lark and a nightingale whose stakes are life and death, conducted entirely in the grammar of persuasion. And the ending submits its whole effect to intelligibility: the Prince's closing speech is an audit, apportioning blame line by line, before the couplet seals the story of Juliet and her Romeo (5.3). A production that has kept every thread visible for two hours earns that audit. One that has traded sequence for atmosphere arrives at it with nothing to audit. The question of what a staging owes the written text, and what the twentieth century's restorations of that text achieved, is pursued in the account of [restoring Shakespeare's text to the stage](/2027/10/08/restoring-shakespeare-text-stage/); the Stratford production of 1958 is that question's quietest and strongest exhibit.

The timetable is the clearest case, and it rewards a closer statement than it usually receives. The Friar's potion is specified like a prescription: the distilled liquor will run through the veins, the pulse will cease, and after two and forty hours the sleeper will awake as from a pleasant sleep (4.1). Forty-two hours is the tragedy's unit of account, and every later event is measured against it. The wedding is first set for Thursday. Juliet takes the potion on the Wednesday night. Then Capulet, delighted by her submission, advances the ceremony by a full day: the knot, he announces, will be knit up tomorrow morning (4.2), and tomorrow is Wednesday, not Thursday. The acceleration is the catastrophe's true trigger, and it is a piece of pure chronology, invisible in any staging that treats the wedding scenes as colour. Against the Friar's plan stands its single channel of communication, the letter carried by Friar John, and the play is pitiless about the channel's fragility. Friar John could not send it, he reports, because the searchers of the town, suspecting plague, sealed the doors of the house where he was held (5.2). A quarantine, a locked door, a letter undelivered in a pocket: the tragedy's machinery includes a public-health accident, and its full horror depends on the audience having registered, hours earlier, how narrow the plan's margin was. A production of clarity, which stages the Friar's prescription speech as information rather than as atmosphere, which lets the house hear Capulet move the wedding, which plays Friar John's report at the weight it deserves, gives the audience the complete instrument panel of the wreck. The second Chorus, the fourteen lines that open the second act, exists for the same purpose, recapping the old desire and the new love so that a distracted listener is re-seated inside the pattern. The play teaches its audience how to watch it, at every joint, in plain verse. A staging method that takes the text's own pedagogy as its programme is not evading interpretation. It is obeying the play's most explicit instruction about itself.

The Prologue is the method's charter, and it deserves its own word. Shakespeare opens the tragedy with a sonnet spoken by a Chorus that gives the whole story away: the households, the feud, the lovers marked for death, the burial of the strife. The Chorus even names the contract's duration, the two hours' traffic of the stage. A production committed to surprise treats the Prologue as an embarrassment to be hurried through or cut, and the cutting tradition did hurry it for centuries. A production of clarity treats it as the play's own instructions for viewing. The audience is not asked to wonder what will happen. The audience is told, in the strictest verse form the language possesses, exactly what will happen, and is then set to watch how it happens, which hinge fails first, which warning goes unheard. Dramatic irony is not a garnish on this play. It is the engine, installed by the author in the first fourteen lines. The middle path's whole procedure, the preserved pattern, the visible timetable, the civic pillars standing in their order, follows from taking the Prologue at its word. Byam Shaw had spoken such sonnets as an actor in the 1935 recovery, whose full-text policy had restored the Choruses to their places after the acting versions dropped them. At Stratford he staged the tragedy as its first speaker frames it: a known story, told entire, whose meaning lives in the watching. A method that begins by obeying the Prologue has at least this to be said for it, that it begins where the play begins.

Honesty requires the wager's losing side to be stated at full strength, because the production's critics, then and since, have stated it well. The play contains heats that lucidity can cool past the point of life. Mercutio's death speech jokes on the edge of the grave, not so deep as a well nor so wide as a church door, but enough (3.1), and the joke lands only if the production has allowed Mercutio's world its full anarchic warmth. Juliet's night soliloquy before the potion (4.3) and her anticipation of the wedding night (3.2) carry an erotic and terrified pressure that a well-spoken staging can easily tidy into recitation. The feud's street violence can be choreographed into ballet. These are real risks, and they are the risks the next section's history shows the play's interpreters seizing on. The claim made here is not that the 1958 production escaped them all; no notice survives detailed enough to grade its scene-by-scene success, and this article invents none. The claim is that its method addressed the play's demonstrable structure rather than a director's thesis about it, and that the method's failures, where they occurred, were failures of degree inside a correct orientation. Orientation is what theatre history, in the end, is judging.

## The House Discipline: Verse-Speaking as Policy

One stratum of the record remains unexamined, and it is the one on which the production's reputation was originally built: the speaking itself. Levenson titles her account of the 1935 Gielgud production Recovery, and the word names what that staging recovered after three centuries in which, her survey concludes, the play on stage had differed in tone and effect from the Elizabethan original. The recovery had two elements, a full text and a restored speed, and both were carried by a technique: verse spoken as verse, phrased by the line and the sentence together, at conversational pressure and theatrical size. Byam Shaw was formed inside that recovery as an actor and then spent four years, at the Old Vic School, teaching its disciplines to the generation below his own. At Stratford the technique became house policy. It is worth spelling out what the policy demands of this particular play, because the play is a harder test of verse-speaking than its reputation for lyric ease suggests.

The test begins with the play's distribution of registers. Shakespeare scores the tragedy like music: the lovers and their elders speak verse, the servants and musicians speak prose, and the two most dangerous talkers in Verona live on the border. Mercutio's banter in the street (2.4) runs in prose, all thrust and parry, while the Queen Mab speech (1.4) is verse that behaves like prose gone dreaming, its catalogue accelerating until Romeo stops it. The Nurse moves between prose anecdote, the great rambling account of Juliet's weaning (1.3), and verse when the plot takes hold of her. A company drilled in a single delivery flattens all of this into one temperature. A company drilled in the 1935 discipline differentiates it: prose played for speed and social grain, verse for thought at full stretch, and the border crossings, Mercutio rising into verse as the fancy takes him, the Nurse dropping into prose as the grief takes her, registered as events. The shared sonnet at the ball (1.5) is the policy's examination piece. Two actors must build one poem between them, alternating quatrains and sharing the couplet, at dance tempo, in masks, in a crowd scene, and the audience must hear the form click shut. No design can do that work and no concept can excuse its failure. Either the house can speak or it cannot. The Stratford of 1958 was a house that recruited, trained, and rehearsed for exactly that examination, season after season, play after play, under a director whose own credentials in the discipline were a Benvolio in the recovery's founding production and four years in charge of the school that taught it. When the period's reviewers praised the festival's finish of speaking, they were praising an infrastructure. The tragedy of April 1958 was that infrastructure's most visible product, and its clarity was, in the most literal sense available, audible.

## Between Brook and Zeffirelli: Why the Middle Path Vanished

Place the production on the century's timeline and its predicament comes into focus. Eleven years before it, in the same theatre, Peter Brook had staged the tragedy as an act of youthful iconoclasm, a production of heat and division that cut the closing reconciliation and made the feud a civic condition; the full record of that staging is set out in the article on [Peter Brook's 1947 Stratford production](/2027/11/05/peter-brook-1947-stratford/). Two years after it, at the Old Vic in London, Franco Zeffirelli would stage the tragedy as a sunlit act of naturalism, with a young company living the feud in a built Verona of real stone and real sweat; that production is weighed in the article on [Zeffirelli's Old Vic production of 1960](/2027/11/12/zeffirelli-old-vic-1960/). Between the iconoclast and the naturalist stands Byam Shaw, and the position looks, in the standard telling, like the absence of a position. The telling is worth examining, because it rests on a definition of significance that the middle path was bound to fail. Theatre history's landmark lists are compiled from consequences: from prompt-books that record a changed text, from manifestos, from scandals, from designs that altered what stages looked like afterward. A production whose whole effort went into transmitting the play intact leaves, by design, the faintest paper trail. Its prompt-book shows the fewest alterations. Its reviews use the vocabulary of approval rather than the vocabulary of shock. Its method, being reproducible by any competent company, cannot be patented. Clarity is the only major staging philosophy that erases its own evidence.

### Why does Levenson's canon of five landmarks leave 1958 out?

Because her canon measures consequence for the text's stage life, and its landmarks are the productions that changed what the play is in performance: Garrick's, Cushman's, Gielgud's, Brook's, and Zeffirelli's. A staging devoted to transmitting the play intact registers as continuity rather than event, and continuity is the canon's blind spot, not its oversight.

Levenson's own statement of the canon is the fairest place to test the point. In the essay accompanying her Oxford edition's prompt-book database, built from some one hundred and seventy prompt-books, she writes that in the history of the play to date, five productions have held the stage or remained highly influential for at least a few decades: David Garrick's of 1748, Charlotte Cushman's of 1845 and 1846, John Gielgud's of 1935, Peter Brook's of 1947, and Franco Zeffirelli's of 1960 at the Old Vic and 1968 on film. The list is honestly drawn and, on its own criterion, correct. Each of the five altered the object that later companies inherited: Garrick rewrote the ending, Cushman restored the text around a female Romeo, Gielgud restored the full text and the speed, Brook cut the reconciliation and politicised the feud, Zeffirelli naturalised the world. Byam Shaw altered nothing that a textual scholar can register. He cut less than Brook, invented less than Zeffirelli, and left the play where Gielgud had put it. The canon's verdict and the production's intention coincide exactly: it aimed to be an act of transmission, and transmission is what the canon cannot see. Where the adjudication must part from Levenson is in the inference her list invites, that what the canon cannot see did not matter. Transmission is not nothing. The play that Zeffirelli's audience recognised as the thing being revolutionised was the play that companies like Byam Shaw's had kept current, current in its full text and its full difficulty, through the decade. Revolutions require a standing order worth storming.

The other two witnesses divide the remainder of the case. Michael Denison, the director's biographer in the Oxford Dictionary of National Biography, performs the canon's omission inside a single career: his five singled-out Stratford productions are Antony and Cleopatra, Macbeth, As You Like It, Othello, and King Lear, and the tragedy of 1958 is not among them, even though the same entry records the tenure's achievement in the strongest terms, the conversion of a worthy festival into a principal centre of British theatre. Denison's selection measures the directorship by its peaks of star casting, and on that scale a festival Romeo and Juliet with a company Romeo and a twenty-eight-year-old Juliet ranks below Olivier's Macbeth and Laughton's Lear. The measure is defensible and the omission is real. Against both witnesses stands the theatre historian's longer view. Hampton-Reeves argues that the standard narrative of Stratford's transformation, the one in which everything before Hall is a waiting room, distorts the record twice over: it understates what the Memorial Theatre had already become, one of the twin pillars of British Shakespeare, and it misreads the succession itself as a rupture when the outgoing director had recruited his successor with the explicit intention of handing him the theatre. On the evidence assembled in this article, the adjudication runs as follows. Levenson wins on reinterpretation: the production changed nothing about how the play is conceived. Denison wins on the directorship's internal hierarchy: it was not its maker's greatest Stratford staging. Hampton-Reeves wins on the question that matters most for this article, the production's historical function: it was the fullest demonstration of the settlement Hall inherited, staffed by the actors Hall inherited, in the building Hall inherited, and its standard of intelligibility became the floor beneath the new company's experiments. A floor is not a ceiling. The middle path's defenders claim the floor, and the floor is enough.

The paper trails of the two regimes make the contrast tangible, and Levenson's own research supplies the detail. Her prompt-book essay notes that Zeffirelli's Old Vic staging was improvised from a workbook with cuts only: the revolution's textual instrument was a list of omissions, the rest invented in the room. Byam Shaw's instrument, on Richards's evidence, was the fullest contrary object in the period's directing: a detailed pre-production notebook, the production reasoned out on paper before the room was entered. Neither instrument is a guarantee. A notebook can embalm a play as surely as a workbook can liberate one. But the two objects embody the two philosophies with unusual purity, the one trusting preparation, the other trusting the event, and they explain the productions' different afterlives in the archive. The workbook of cuts is a document a textual scholar can use, because it records decisions about the text. The notebook of a transmission production records decisions about everything except the text, which was to be left alone, and the textual scholar's archive therefore holds less of it. The middle path's invisibility begins in its paperwork.

One further complication belongs to the decade rather than to the man, and fairness requires it to be entered. The British stage of 1958 still worked under the Lord Chamberlain's censorship, which licensed every public performance and policed what could be shown and said; the regime survived until 1968. Every company of the period staged the lovers' night, the aubade, and the tomb inside a decorum enforced by law, and the erotic frankness that Zeffirelli's film would make the play's new standard in 1968 was, for a Stratford stage production ten years earlier, not an available register in any full sense. Part of what later decades have read as the middle path's coolness was the period's legal temperature, shared by every production of its year. The adjudication of the production should therefore discount the heat charge by the censorship factor before it is laid at the director's door. What remains after the discount is the genuine article of dispute: not whether the lovers burned, which no 1958 stage could fully show, but whether the play's pattern was honoured, which the 1958 stage demonstrably set out to do.

A word, finally, on the contemporary verdict, since the brief for this article describes the production as much praised, and praise should be documented rather than inherited. The documented praise is specific and narrower than the legend. Theatre World's survey of the 1958 festival fastened its commendation on Tutin, congratulating her on her three performances in terms quoted in full in the casting discussion of this article, and the same survey's famous phrase for Byam Shaw's direction that season, clear and plain, was written of his Hamlet with Redgrave, three months after the tragedy opened. No comparably detailed brief for the defence of the Romeo and Juliet itself survives in the notices assembled in the archive's cuttings file, and the legend of the model production of clarity rests partly on vocabulary transferred from the Hamlet and from the directorship's general reputation. This article has argued the production's case from its method, its casting, and its consequences, which are documented, rather than from applause, which is only partly documented. The case is stronger for the admission. Clarity's best evidence was never in the reviews. It is in the notebooks, the company list, and the succession.

## The Succession: Shaw Chooses Hall

The directorship's final production decision was not a staging at all. It was a person. Peter Hall had first directed at Stratford in 1956, with Love's Labour's Lost, while still in his middle twenties, and Byam Shaw's theatre gave him the run of its ambitions: Cymbeline with Ashcroft in 1957, Twelfth Night and the Coriolanus with Olivier in the seasons following. The older director watched the younger one the way a casting director watches a company, and reached the conclusion that defines his historical importance. In November 1958, a week before Hall's twenty-eighth birthday, the appointment was announced: Hall would succeed Byam Shaw. The handover was completed across the 1959 season, and Hall took charge for 1960. Hampton-Reeves's formulation of the event deserves its currency: Byam Shaw recruited Hall with the explicit intention of making the young director his successor. The famous anecdote of the transition belongs to the tour on which the 1958 company was then engaged: in Leningrad, Hall outlined his plans to Fordham Flower, the theatre's chairman, a permanent company on long contracts, a London base, modern plays beside the canon, and the Memorial name itself jettisoned for a royal one. The plans were radical. The channel through which they reached the board was the outgoing director's chosen man, travelling with the outgoing director's company, playing the outgoing director's Romeo.

### What was Glen Byam Shaw's final production in charge at Stratford?

His final production as the theatre's director was King Lear, which opened on 18 August 1959 with Charles Laughton in the title role and Albert Finney as Edgar. It closed a tenure of fourteen Stratford productions across eight seasons, and it was singled out by his biographer Michael Denison as one of the five productions that define the directorship.

The farewell season that framed the handover deserves its record, because it was the tenure's summit and the new regime's true starting line. The festival of 1959 fielded Paul Robeson's Othello under Tony Richardson, with Sam Wanamaker as Iago, Mary Ure as Desdemona, Albert Finney as Cassio, and Baddeley as Emilia; Tyrone Guthrie's All's Well That Ends Well with Edith Evans as the Countess and Zoe Caldwell as Helena; Hall's Midsummer Night's Dream with Charles Laughton's Bottom, Mary Ure's Titania, and Vanessa Redgrave and Diana Rigg in the young company; Hall's Coriolanus with Olivier in the title role and Evans as Volumnia; and Byam Shaw's own King Lear with Laughton, Finney as Edgar, and Holm as the Fool. No British festival before or since has concentrated more of the century's acting capital in a single summer in a single small town. That concentration was the outgoing director's last exhibit, and it reframes the succession anecdote with which this section began. Hall did not rescue a failing provincial festival. He inherited, by the deliberate choice of the man who had built it, the strongest classical company in the country at the height of its powers, and his genius was to see that its strength could be made permanent. The inheritance and the transformation are both real. The history that credits only the transformation is reading the story from its second chapter.

There is a temptation, in writing the history of institutions, to treat succession as administration. In the theatre it is interpretation. Byam Shaw's choice of Hall stated his reading of everything his tenure had built: that the star festival he and Quayle had perfected was a stage in the institution's life and not its destination, that the company's young strength, the Johnsons and Tutins and Holms his seasons had formed, deserved the permanent ensemble he had never been empowered to give them, and that the next reading of the plays would need weapons his generation did not possess. The proof of the choice is in its fruits, which are surveyed in the account of [the RSC Romeo and Juliet through the decades](/2028/04/07/rsc-romeo-and-juliet-decades/): the company Hall founded in 1961 took the name Royal, kept Stratford as its home, and inherited, on its first day, an audience, a building, a standard, and a shelf of actors that the Memorial Theatre's last director had assembled. Glen Byam Shaw directed no production for the new company. He left the theatre in 1959 with an honorary doctorate of letters from the University of Birmingham to mark the tenure, and after freelance years became Director of Productions at Sadler's Wells Opera in 1962, beginning the operatic second career for which his later decades are chiefly remembered. The honours record completes the pattern of the reputation. He had been appointed CBE in 1954, in mid-tenure, but he was never knighted, though Quayle, his co-director, received a knighthood in time. The omission has the same shape as Levenson's: the systems that distribute remembrance, state honours and landmark canons alike, are built to register peaks and sensations, and a career whose distinction was the steady raising of a standard registers in both systems as a blank between brighter names. He died on 29 April 1986, having outlived the festival he built by more than a quarter of a century and having watched the company he made possible become the institution his own name is required to introduce. The festival he had raised from worthy to principal he never ran under its royal name. That is the middle path's epitaph in institutional form: the man built the road, and the road is remembered under the names of those who drove on it.

## Costs of the Middle Path: What Clarity Could Not Do

The defence has had its full hearing, and the prosecution's strongest points, the ones that do not dissolve under the censorship discount or the archive's bias, should be entered before the verdict is restated. There are three, and they are structural. The first is the star contradiction. The settlement preached company values and practised them in its young ranks, but its peaks were built on visiting eminence: Olivier's Macbeth, Laughton's Lear, Robeson's Othello stood in the bills because the festival could borrow the greatest actors alive for a summer, and a system that depends on borrowed greatness has no answer when the borrowing stops. Hall's three-year contracts were the answer, and Byam Shaw, who had no power to offer them inside the festival's finances, can be acquitted of the contradiction only by admitting it was real: the middle path's company ideal exceeded the middle path's economics, and its own chosen successor was hired to fix exactly that.

The second cost is the repertory's narrowness. The festival played Shakespeare and only Shakespeare, five productions a year of the one author, in the years when the English stage was being remade by new writing elsewhere. The policy had a founding logic, the theatre's charter and its tourist audience, and it produced the concentration of classical craft this article has praised. It also meant that the institution's actors were formed in a single dialect of drama, and that the theatre's claim on the nation's theatrical future weakened as the nation's theatre changed around it. Hall's introduction of modern plays at the Aldwych was not vandalism. It was the correction of the settlement's plainest limitation, visible from inside the settlement's own ranks.

The third cost belongs to the method itself, and it is the one the verdict must carry most carefully. A discipline of transmission ages into a mannerism if it is practised after its questions have been answered. By 1958 the recovery of the full text was thirty years old; the cleared stage was the establishment's look; the verse-speaking standard was the thing a young director defined himself against. Brook in 1947 had rebelled inside the same building, and the rebellion had been possible because the settlement had edges to strike against. A method that cannot generate its own opposition becomes, in time, the thing the next necessary rebellion must destroy, and the speed with which the 1960s discarded the Memorial manner, its resident composers and architectural Veronas and well-spoken companies, measures how completely the manner had come to stand for the institution it served. The middle path's tragedy, if the word is allowed, is that its virtues were institutional, and institutions are inherited, altered, or closed. The verdict that follows accepts all three costs at full weight. It finds that they describe the settlement's limits as a permanent system, and that they do not touch the narrower claim made for the single production within it: that on 8 April 1958, in the ninety-ninth season, the play was given whole, clearly, and by the right company, and that giving a play whole and clearly is an interpretation for which a director may honourably be judged.

## The Verdict Restated: Clarity as Interpretation

The evidence can be weighed as a whole, and the verdict of the introduction either confirmed or withdrawn. The production was mounted in April 1958 by a director who had acted in the century's landmark staging of the same play, designed by the partnership that had dressed that landmark, inside a theatre that director had raised to national standing, with a company whose two young principals went on to anchor the successor institution, in a text prepared by the most systematic pre-production method any British director of the period is known to have left on paper. Against that accumulation stand two facts, both genuine: no detailed contemporary brief for the production's defence survives in the assembled notices, and the canon-forming histories pass it by. The question the article set out to answer is which set of facts describes the production's quality. The argument has been that they describe different things: the first set describes what the production was, the second describes what the recording system of theatre history is able to keep. A prompt-book archive registers textual change. A review archive registers sensation. A biographical dictionary registers stars. A clarity production scores low on all three registers by the logic of its method, and high on the fourth register, the one no archive was built to hold: the register of the working theatre itself, its standards transmitted hand to hand, its actors carried from one regime to the next, its audience taught, season by season, what following a play feels like.

That fourth register is where the final judgement belongs, and it can be stated in the play's own terms. The tragedy ends with an audit, the Prince apportioning the blame among the living and the dead, and the production that has honoured the play's pattern arrives at that audit with its accounts in order. Byam Shaw's Stratford staging kept its accounts in order for two hours of traffic, in a Verona of pillars and steps, with a Juliet of twenty-eight and a Romeo the festival itself had raised. Its director then performed the same office for his theatre that the Prince performs for Verona, apportioning the future among the living: the company to Hall, the building to the new regime, the standard to anyone who would keep it. The middle path's final claim is that these were the same act. The staging transmitted the play intact, and the tenure transmitted the theatre intact, and intact transmission, in a play about everything that arrives too late or fails to arrive at all, is a reading of the tragedy as well as a service to it. The letter, in Byam Shaw's Verona, got through. That is the verdict: the production of 1958 was the clear middle path, walked with preparation, intelligence, and a full understanding of its cost, and its disappearance from the landmark lists is the price of the very virtue that made it worth walking. The recovery attempted in these pages asks for no larger revision than the evidence supports: not a lost masterpiece, not a revolution missed, but a place in the sequence, between the recovery of 1935 and the revolutions that followed, for the production that held the play steady while the theatre around it changed hands.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Byam Shaw profile, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch Byam Shaw profile is the verified record of Glen Byam Shaw's 1958 Stratford Romeo and Juliet: its date, theatre, director, principal cast, designers, composer, fight and dance arrangers, and its place in the director's tenure. The pattern it exposes is transmission. ### Q: Who played Romeo and Juliet in the 1958 Stratford production?

Richard Johnson played Romeo and Dorothy Tutin played Juliet at the Shakespeare Memorial Theatre, Stratford-upon-Avon, in the production that opened on 8 April 1958 under Glen Byam Shaw's direction. Both were members of the festival company rather than visiting stars. Johnson had joined Stratford the previous season and had played Orlando, Mark Antony, Posthumus, and Ferdinand in 1957. Tutin arrived as the season's leading lady and added Viola and Ophelia to her Juliet within the same festival. The pairing reunited two actors who had played together in London in Jean Anouilh's The Lark in 1955.

### Q: Who designed the 1958 production, and who composed its music?

The scenery and costumes were designed by Motley, the partnership of Margaret Harris, Sophie Harris, and Elizabeth Montgomery, whose collaboration with Byam Shaw reached back to the 1930s and whose designs for Gielgud's 1935 production had helped define the modern image of the play. The music was composed by Leslie Bridgewater, the Shakespeare Memorial Theatre's resident composer during the festival years of the 1950s. The same design team also dressed Byam Shaw's Hamlet later in the 1958 season, and Theatre World's survey described that production's Motley scenery as plain, a few pillars and a flight of steps.

### Q: Where did Glen Byam Shaw teach acting before he came to Stratford?

He directed the Old Vic Theatre School from 1947 to 1951, the training arm of Michel Saint-Denis's Old Vic Theatre Centre in London. The Centre joined a producing company, a school, and George Devine's Young Vic in one design aimed at rebuilding English acting on a discipline of voice, movement, and textual intelligence. Byam Shaw ran the school, the arm aimed longest into the future, and the schoolmaster's habits, the prepared notebook and the patience with young actors, went with him to Stratford the following year. The Centre's leadership resigned together in 1951 after the governors ended the experiment.

### Q: When was Glen Byam Shaw director of the Shakespeare Memorial Theatre?

He directed the Shakespeare Memorial Theatre at Stratford-upon-Avon from 1952 to 1959, first as co-director with Anthony Quayle and in sole charge from 1956. Across the tenure he directed fourteen plays himself, was appointed CBE in 1954, and raised the festival from a worthy provincial occasion into one of the principal centres of British theatre. His celebrated Stratford productions included Antony and Cleopatra with Michael Redgrave and Peggy Ashcroft, Macbeth with Laurence Olivier and Vivien Leigh, and King Lear with Charles Laughton. In 1959 he handed the theatre to Peter Hall, whom he had chosen as his successor.

### Q: Why did Dorothy Tutin's 1958 festival attract special notice?

Because she played three of Shakespeare's great young heroines in a single Stratford season at the age of twenty-eight: Juliet in Byam Shaw's Romeo and Juliet from 8 April, Viola in Peter Hall's Twelfth Night later the same month, and Ophelia in Byam Shaw's Hamlet in June. The three parts demand three different registers, Juliet's erotic directness, Viola's wit under disguise, and Ophelia's disintegration, and Tutin carried all three while also being the festival's leading lady in its opening production. Theatre World's survey of the season congratulated her on the achievement in terms that have been quoted in accounts of the festival ever since.

### Q: What did Theatre World's season survey praise in Tutin?

The survey, reviewing the whole 1958 Stratford festival, wrote that Miss Tutin was to be congratulated upon the good fortune of playing Juliet, Viola, and Ophelia in one festival, and upon a fine, impressive performance in each part. The phrasing is precise evidence: the contemporary praise fastened on the range and finish of her season's work rather than on any single sensation. The same survey praised Byam Shaw's clear and plain direction in the season, though that phrase was written of his Hamlet rather than of the tragedy, a distinction accounts of the Romeo and Juliet have often blurred.

### Q: Who arranged the fights and dances for the 1958 production?

The fights were arranged by Bernard Hepton and the dances by Pauline Grant, credits preserved in the University of Birmingham's archive record of the production. Hepton was then an actor at the start of his career, and his engagement illustrates the Byam Shaw tenure's habit of giving young company members serious departmental responsibility. Grant's dances carried the Capulet ball, the scene in which the lovers meet. Together with Leslie Bridgewater's music, the credits show a production whose movement and sound were supplied from inside the festival's own establishment rather than bought in from visiting specialists.

### Q: Did the 1958 production tour abroad?

The Memorial Theatre company carried the 1958 season to Moscow and Leningrad in the winter following the festival, and Richard Johnson's biographical record lists Romeo among his roles on that tour, alongside Sir Andrew Aguecheek from the Twelfth Night. The tour took the Stratford company behind the Iron Curtain at the height of the period's cultural exchanges, with Michael Redgrave's Hamlet as its companion tragedy. The trip also framed the succession: it was in Leningrad, on tour, that Peter Hall outlined his plans for a permanent company and a royal name to the theatre's chairman, Fordham Flower.

### Q: How did Richard Johnson's Romeo change his career?

It made his screen career. Johnson's biographical record states that his stage reputation, and his Romeo in particular, brought him his MGM contract and his Hollywood debut as Frank Sinatra's comrade in Never So Few in 1959. Before Stratford he had been a promising leading man of the London stage, Laertes to Paul Scofield's Hamlet under Peter Brook and Warwick in The Lark opposite Tutin. The Stratford seasons, crowned by the Romeo and by the title role in Pericles in the same summer, converted promise into standing. He remained an Associate Artist of the Royal Shakespeare Company from its inception until his death.

### Q: Who played Mercutio and the Nurse in 1958?

Edward Woodward played Mercutio and Angela Baddeley played the Nurse. Woodward, twenty-seven, was in the season that established him, adding Laertes in Hamlet and Claudio in Much Ado About Nothing to his Mercutio. Baddeley was one of the festival's senior principals and, since 1929, the director's wife; her Nurse continued the tradition of the part as a major character engagement, taken by an actress of the first rank. The season souvenir gave both performers full-page portraits, a documentary indication of the standing their performances held inside the company that made them.

### Q: What are Byam Shaw's production notebooks, and why do they matter?

They are the detailed pre-production notebooks he prepared for every Shakespeare production he directed, setting out his reading of the text and his staging solutions before rehearsals began. They matter because they convert his reputation for clarity from a compliment into a checkable method: the plainness of his stagings was designed on paper in advance, not arrived at by caution in the rehearsal room. The Warwick scholar Julian Richards has made the notebooks a foundation of the first major biographical study of the director, and their survival gives the 1958 production an evidence base that most of its contemporaries lack.

### Q: Why did Byam Shaw choose Peter Hall as his successor?

The record supports a judgement of temperament and of reading. Hall had directed at Stratford from 1956 with conspicuous success, Cymbeline with Ashcroft in 1957 chief among his early productions, and Byam Shaw had watched him at close quarters for three seasons. The theatre historian Stuart Hampton-Reeves concludes that Byam Shaw recruited Hall with the explicit intention of making him his successor, and that both Byam Shaw and Quayle recommended the appointment. The choice, announced in November 1958, acknowledged that the star festival Byam Shaw had perfected was a stage in the institution's life, and that its future belonged to a permanent ensemble under new leadership.

### Q: How does the 1958 production stand between Brook's and Zeffirelli's?

It stands as the middle term the standard history omits. Brook's 1947 Stratford production, in the same building, had cut the closing reconciliation and read the feud as a civic condition; Zeffirelli's 1960 Old Vic production naturalised the play in a sunlit, crowded Verona played by a young company. Byam Shaw's 1958 staging shared Brook's theatre and Zeffirelli's youth but neither man's thesis: it restored nothing, cut little, and argued that the play's own pattern, fully spoken and clearly followed, was interpretation enough. Histories organised around reinterpretation pass it by, which says as much about the histories as about the production.

### Q: What else did Byam Shaw direct at Stratford besides this play?

He directed fourteen plays at Stratford between 1952 and 1959. The productions singled out by his biographer Michael Denison are Antony and Cleopatra with Michael Redgrave and Peggy Ashcroft in 1953, Macbeth with Laurence Olivier and Vivien Leigh in 1955, Othello with Harry Andrews and Emlyn Williams in 1956, As You Like It with Ashcroft in 1957, famous for staging the action's passage from winter to summer for the first time, and King Lear with Charles Laughton and Albert Finney in 1959. His own Hamlet with Redgrave shared the 1958 season with the Romeo and Juliet.

### Q: Did Peggy Ashcroft appear in Byam Shaw's Stratford?

Yes, though not in the Romeo and Juliet. Ashcroft returned to Stratford in 1957, during Byam Shaw's sole charge, and played Rosalind in his own production of As You Like It, the staging that moved the action from winter to summer, and Imogen in Peter Hall's Cymbeline in the same season. The Queen and the Duke of Edinburgh attended the festival that year and saw her Rosalind. Her presence matters to the 1958 production as context: the Juliet of Gielgud's 1935 production, in which Byam Shaw had played Benvolio, was working in the building while its new Juliet was prepared.

### Q: What did admirers mean when they praised the production's clarity?

They meant that the play's action and argument could be followed without strain: the verse spoken entire and in order, the sequence of timings on which the tragedy turns kept visible, the staging cleared of anything that competed with the words, and the design, Motley's plain architectural Verona, serving the movement rather than illustrating it. The vocabulary was the period's own. Theatre World called Byam Shaw's direction of the season's Hamlet clear and plain, and the Victoria and Albert Museum's account of Motley credits the partnership with bringing a new simplicity and clarity to stage design a generation earlier.

### Q: What became of the Shakespeare Memorial Theatre after 1959?

Peter Hall took charge for the 1960 season, on the appointment Byam Shaw had engineered, and rebuilt the institution as a permanent company with long contracts, a London home at the Aldwych, and a repertory mixing Shakespeare with modern plays. In January 1961 the company took the name Royal Shakespeare Company and the building became the Royal Shakespeare Theatre. Dorothy Tutin played Juliet again in Hall's staging of August 1961, opposite Brian Murray and with Edith Evans as the Nurse. Hampton-Reeves's judgement stands as the summary: Hall built the new company on the foundation Byam Shaw had laid.

### Q: What happened to Tutin's Juliet after 1958?

She played the part again at Stratford three years later, in Peter Hall's production of August 1961 for the newly named Royal Shakespeare Company, opposite Brian Murray's Romeo and with Edith Evans, the Nurse of the 1935 Gielgud production, as the Nurse. The casting closed a circle that the 1958 production had opened: Byam Shaw's Juliet became the new regime's first Juliet of consequence, and the actress who had set the century's standard for the Nurse in 1935 served the successor generation in the role. Tutin's association with the company continued across four decades.
