The anti defection law india tenth schedule debate begins with a plain fact of constitutional design: a legislator elected on one party's ticket and symbol may be unseated for leaving that party or defying its whip, and the provision that does this work, the Tenth Schedule inserted by the 52nd Amendment on 1 March 1985, has never settled the question it was built to answer, namely how much of a representative's vote belongs to the party that carried the representative into the House and how much belongs to the representative's own judgment. This article defends one namable claim. The Tenth Schedule has architectural-substantive tension between party-discipline and individual-conscience, and that tension operates through the Schedule's own features rather than through accidents of politics: the whip-violation ground in Paragraph 2, the voluntary-relinquishment ground beside it, the merger-only door left open after 2003, and the Speaker acting as tribunal under Paragraph 6. Read the text closely, follow the holdings in Kihoto Hollohan v Zachillhu of 18 February 1992 and Subhash Desai v Principal Secretary, Governor of Maharashtra of 11 May 2023, and the tension is visible in the architecture itself.

## The Era Before the Law: Defection as a System, 1967 to 1985

The Tenth Schedule was written against a documented record, not against a fear. Between the first general election of 1951 to 1952 and the fourth general election of 1967, the Committee on Defections chaired by Union Home Minister Y. B. Chavan counted roughly 542 instances of legislators crossing from one party to another, and it found that close to four fifths of them, 438 crossings, fell inside a single twelve-month span from March 1967 to February 1968. Those numbers matter because they describe a structural condition. The 1967 verdict had broken the Congress majority in several states and produced assemblies in which a handful of members held the balance. A government in such a House lived or fell by arithmetic that a small group could rewrite in an evening, and the Chavan Committee, reporting in 1969, recorded the reward structure that powered the traffic: of 210 defecting legislators studied across seven states, 116 received ministerial office in the governments their crossing helped to form. The Committee treated that finding as the centre of the problem. Floor crossing, on its account, was not primarily an expression of conscience or ideology. It was a market in which office was the price and the electorate's verdict was the commodity traded.

Haryana supplied the era with its enduring shorthand. Gaya Lal, elected to the Haryana Legislative Assembly from Hasanpur in 1967, crossed from the Congress to the United Front, returned to the Congress, and crossed again inside a single day, three switches of allegiance compressed into hours; at a Chandigarh press conference the Congress leader Rao Birender Singh presented him with the words that Gaya Lal had become Aaya Lal, and the phrase Aaya Ram Gaya Ram entered the language as the name of the practice itself. The anecdote survived because it was representative. B. Venkatesh Kumar, writing in the Economic and Political Weekly in 2003, estimated that almost half of the roughly 4,000 legislators elected between 1967 and 1971 defected at some point in their terms, and roughly 142 crossings touched Parliament itself in that span. Ministries in Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Punjab and Madhya Pradesh rose and collapsed on the movement of small blocs, President's rule followed in state after state, and the brief for this article records the aggregate consequence as approximately fifty Vidhan Sabha government-instability events tied to defection between 1967 and 1985. The figure is an estimate, and it is used here as one, but the direction of the record is not in dispute among the Chavan Committee, the Election Commission's 1968 to 1969 mid-term election report, or the parliamentary debates of the period: defection had become a routine instrument of government formation and government destruction.

### Why did defections topple so many governments before 1985?

Between 1967 and 1971 the state assemblies elected in the fourth general election sat with thin margins, and a legislator crossing the floor could erase a majority in a single afternoon. The Chavan Committee counted 438 crossings in twelve months from March 1967, and ministries in Haryana, Uttar Pradesh and Bihar fell because arithmetic, not argument, had moved.

The political class attempted a legislative answer long before 1985. The Lok Sabha adopted a resolution in December 1967 that produced the Chavan Committee itself, and the Committee's 1969 report recommended legislation against defection driven by the lure of office. The Constitution (Thirty-second Amendment) Bill of 1973, introduced under Home Minister Uma Shankar Dikshit in Indira Gandhi's government, carried the proposal into Parliament and was referred to a Joint Committee in December 1973, where it stayed until the Fifth Lok Sabha dissolved in January 1977 and the bill lapsed. The Janata government of Morarji Desai tried again with the Constitution (Forty-eighth Amendment) Bill of 1978, moved by Law Minister Shanti Bhushan, and that attempt also failed, in a Parliament whose own majority was being consumed by the practice the bill targeted; the Desai government itself fell in 1979 amid defections from the Janata ranks. Two failed bills across two opposed governments establish the point that the demand for an anti-defection law was neither partisan nor novel by 1985. What changed in 1984 was arithmetic at the centre: Rajiv Gandhi's Congress won 404 Lok Sabha seats in the December 1984 election held after Indira Gandhi's assassination, a majority large enough to carry a constitutional amendment through both Houses and to absorb the internal dissent that such a law, binding every party's legislators, was bound to provoke.

### How did the Chavan Committee frame the defection problem?

The Committee on Defections, chaired by Home Minister Y. B. Chavan and reporting in 1969, framed floor crossing as a betrayal of the electorate rather than a private choice. Its record showed 116 of 210 defectors in seven states receiving ministerial office, and it treated the lure of office, not ideology, as the engine of the practice.

That framing shaped everything that followed, and it deserves scrutiny because the Tenth Schedule inherited both its strength and its blind spot. The Chavan diagnosis located the wrong in the defector's motive: a member elected on a party's programme who crossed for office deceived the voters who had chosen that programme. The diagnosis said little about the opposite case, the member whose party leadership abandoned the programme on which the election had been fought, or the member instructed to vote for a measure the member's constituents opposed. Conscience, in the Committee's account, was the excuse defectors offered rather than a democratic value in its own right. When the 52nd Amendment finally translated the diagnosis into constitutional text in 1985, it therefore built machinery aimed at movement itself: the Schedule penalised leaving, splitting and disobeying, and it made no attempt to distinguish the purchased defection from the principled one. The distinction between those two acts is the substance of the tension this article traces. A law that cannot tell them apart in its text will have the argument about them in its courts and its Speakers' chambers instead, and that is precisely where the argument has been held, from Kihoto Hollohan in 1992 to Subhash Desai in 2023.

## The 52nd Amendment of 1985: What Parliament Built and Debated

The Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act received presidential assent on 15 February 1985 and entered into force on 1 March 1985. Its architecture reached beyond the new Schedule. It inserted Article 102(2) for members of Parliament and Article 191(2) for members of state legislatures, each providing that a member shall be disqualified for being a member of the House if the member is disqualified under the Tenth Schedule, and it placed the Schedule itself at the end of the constitutional text as a self-contained code of grounds, exceptions and adjudication. The amendment was the first occasion on which the expression political party acquired a defined place in the Constitution, a point worth pausing on: the founding text of 1950 had regulated elections, houses and members while leaving parties almost entirely outside its frame, and the settlement described in [the constitutional rights framework](/2018/12/06/ambedkar-drafting-committee-constitution/) had treated the legislator as the Constitution's unit of account. The 52nd Amendment reversed the presumption for one purpose. For the purpose of keeping a seat, the member's party became a constitutional fact about the member, recorded at election and enforceable against the member's later conduct.

The debates on the bill, preserved in the Parliament Library and identified in the brief for this article as the under-cited primary source of the whole subject, show the tension named at the moment of creation rather than discovered later. Members supporting the bill returned repeatedly to the Chavan record: the 438 crossings of 1967 to 1968, the ministries bought with portfolios, the assemblies dissolved into President's rule. Members opposing or qualifying the bill returned to a different record, the long parliamentary tradition in which a legislator's vote against the party line could be an act of representation rather than betrayal, and they pressed the government on the whip clause in particular. A direction, under the proposed Paragraph 2, would bind a member on every vote in the House, not only on votes deciding a government's survival, and several members warned that the clause converted the legislator into a delegate of the party secretariat. The government's answer, given the size of its majority, prevailed in the division lobbies, and the bill passed the Lok Sabha on 30 January 1985 and the Rajya Sabha on 31 January 1985 with the whip clause intact. The debates therefore leave a precise historical finding for the architectural reading: the breadth of Paragraph 2 was not an oversight. It was chosen, against stated objection, by a Parliament that ranked the stability of governments above the voting freedom of members and said so on the record.

The amendment also made a second choice whose consequences took longer to surface. The question of whether a member had incurred disqualification was not given to the courts, not given to the Election Commission, and not given to the President or Governor acting on advice. It was given to the Speaker or Chairman of the House concerned, an office that in Indian practice is held by a member of the ruling party or coalition and, unlike the Westminster convention, is not vacated by its holder's resignation from that party. Paragraph 7 then barred the jurisdiction of every court in respect of any matter connected with disqualification under the Schedule. The combination was deliberate: an insider adjudicator and an ouster clause together were meant to keep defection disputes inside the legislature, swiftly decided and politically final. Kihoto Hollohan would dismantle the ouster clause in 1992 while leaving the insider adjudicator in place, and the space between those two holdings is where the Schedule's adjudicatory stress has accumulated ever since.

## The Text of the Schedule, Paragraph by Paragraph

The Tenth Schedule runs to eight paragraphs, and its meaning sits in the interaction among them. Paragraph 1 supplies definitions, among them legislature party, meaning the group consisting of all members of a House belonging to a political party, and original political party, meaning the party to which a member belongs for the purposes of the Schedule. The distinction between those two defined terms sounds technical and turns out to be load-bearing: a member's original political party is a fact about the member, while the legislature party is a body that can act, resolve and merge, and the Maharashtra litigation of 2022 and 2023 turned in part on which of the two entities appoints a whip and speaks for the party inside the House. Paragraph 8 empowers the Speaker or Chairman to make rules for the House in relation to the conduct of business under the Schedule, subject to approval by the House, and deems proceedings under Paragraph 6 to be proceedings within the legislature for the purposes of Articles 122 and 212, the provisions that shield legislative proceedings from judicial inquiry into irregularity. Those framing paragraphs surround the operative code, which sits in Paragraphs 2 to 7.

Paragraph 2 states the grounds. Under Paragraph 2(1)(a), a member of a House belonging to a political party shall be disqualified if the member has voluntarily given up membership of that party. Under Paragraph 2(1)(b), the member shall be disqualified if the member votes or abstains from voting in the House contrary to any direction issued by the political party to which the member belongs, or by any person or authority authorised by the party in that behalf, without obtaining prior permission, and the voting or abstention has not been condoned by the party within fifteen days. Three features of that text repay attention. The direction is not confined to confidence votes or money bills; it covers voting generally. The escape routes are internal to the party, prior permission or condonation within fifteen days, both of which the party leadership controls. And the ground is triggered by abstention as fully as by a contrary vote, so a member cannot withhold assent silently and keep the seat. Paragraph 2(2) treats the elected independent member who joins a party after election as disqualified, and Paragraph 2(3) gives a nominated member six months from taking the seat to join a party, disqualifying the nominated member who joins one after that window. The Schedule, in short, assigns every member a party position at the threshold and then polices movement away from it.

Paragraph 3, as enacted in 1985, protected a split: a member was not disqualified where the member's original political party split and the member belonged to a faction arising from the split that consisted of not less than one third of the members of the legislature party. Paragraph 4 protects a merger: a member is not disqualified where the member's original political party merges with another political party and the member claims the protection, with Paragraph 4(2) deeming a merger to have taken place if, and only if, not less than two thirds of the members of the legislature party concerned have agreed to the merger. The asymmetry between the two thresholds is the architectural point. A split at one third let a faction walk out of a party while keeping seats; a merger at two thirds required the faction to carry the legislature party with it into another existing party. Between 1985 and 2003 both doors stood open, and the one-third door was used so often, in state after state, that the split became the standard form of the defection the Schedule existed to prevent. Paragraph 5 exempts a member elected Speaker, Deputy Speaker, Chairman or Deputy Chairman who gives up party membership by reason of that election, and protects the officeholder who rejoins the party after ceasing to hold the office. Paragraph 6(1) provides that if any question arises as to whether a member has become subject to disqualification, the question shall be referred to the Speaker or Chairman whose decision shall be final. Paragraph 7, as enacted, barred every court from jurisdiction over any matter connected with disqualification, notwithstanding anything in the Constitution.

### Which officeholders does Paragraph 5 protect, and why?

Paragraph 5 protects a member elected Speaker or Deputy Speaker of a House, or Chairman or Deputy Chairman of a state legislative council, who gives up party membership on election to the chair. The protection exists because the presiding office is meant to stand apart from party combat, and the Schedule declines to punish a member for performing that separation.

The text, read whole, is neither a crude anti-party-hopping statute nor a considered charter of legislative dissent. It is a machine with a clear centre of gravity. Its grounds punish exit and disobedience, its exceptions ration collective movement, its adjudicator sits inside the institution whose composition the dispute will alter, and its original design excluded the courts entirely. Every later development, the judicial review restored by Kihoto Hollohan, the deletion of the split door by the 91st Amendment, the merger claims in Goa and Telangana in 2019, the Speaker's chamber in Maharashtra in 2022 and 2023, is a working out of pressures that the 1985 text built into its own paragraphs. That is the first finding of the architectural reading, and it is a finding about the text before it is a finding about politics.

## Kihoto Hollohan v Zachillhu: The 1992 Settlement Stated by Holding

The constitutional challenge arrived from the states where defections had been thickest, including Nagaland, whose legislator Zachillhu gave the case its name, and the Supreme Court's Constitution Bench pronounced its operative conclusions on 12 November 1991 and delivered its reasoned judgments on 18 February 1992 in Kihoto Hollohan v Zachillhu, reported at 1992 Supp (2) SCC 651. The bench consisted of Chief Justice M. H. Kania's successor court led in the opinions by Justices M. N. Venkatachaliah, K. Jayachandra Reddy and S. C. Agrawal for the majority, with Chief Justice Lalit Mohan Sharma and Justice J. S. Verma in the minority. Both opinions agreed on the threshold question, and the agreement is easy to miss in summaries: Paragraph 7 did effect a change in Articles 136, 226 and 227 of the Constitution by excluding the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and the High Courts, and it therefore attracted the proviso to Article 368(2), which requires ratification by the legislatures of not less than half the states for amendments touching those provisions. No such ratification had been obtained before the bill was presented for presidential assent. The bench divided on what followed from that shared premise.

The majority held that Paragraph 7 alone was unconstitutional and that it was severable from the rest of the Schedule. Strike the ouster clause, and the remainder of the Tenth Schedule stood valid. On that foundation the majority built the doctrinal structure that has governed defection law since 1992. The Speaker or Chairman deciding a disqualification question under Paragraph 6 acts as a tribunal, not as an ordinary presiding officer performing a procedural act, and the decision is accordingly amenable to judicial review under Articles 136, 226 and 227. The review is not an appeal: the majority confined it to the grounds on which tribunal decisions are ordinarily reviewed, including jurisdictional error, mala fides, perversity, violation of the constitutional mandate and failure to observe the rules of natural justice, and it held that judicial review is not available at a stage prior to the Speaker's decision, save in exceptional situations such as a threatened immediate disqualification operating in a manner the Court described through the quia timet exception. The majority also rejected the challenge that Paragraph 2(1)(b) violated the freedom of speech of legislators under Articles 105 and 194. The right to vote in the House, the Court reasoned, is subject to the constitutional provisions that structure membership itself, and a provision that attaches loss of membership to defection does not trench upon the member's speech; it defines the conditions on which the member holds the seat from which the speech is made. The Tenth Schedule, on the majority's reading, did not violate the basic structure of the Constitution, because stability of government and the integrity of the electoral mandate are themselves democratic values the Constitution protects.

The minority held otherwise, and its holding must be stated at full strength because it identified, in 1992, the two pressure points that the following three decades exposed. Justices Sharma and Verma held that Paragraph 7 was not severable: the exclusion of judicial review was woven into the design of Paragraph 6, since the framers of the amendment had made the Speaker's decision final precisely because no court could examine it, and the failure of ratification therefore invalidated the entire Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act, rendering the presidential assent non est and the Tenth Schedule an abortive amendment. The minority went further on the institutional question. The Speaker, in their view, is not an independent adjudicatory authority for disqualification disputes, because a Speaker in the Indian system retains party membership and depends on the House majority for continuance in office, and vesting the decision in such an office is inconsistent with a basic feature of democracy. Where the majority saw a tribunal whose errors courts could correct after the event, the minority saw an interested judge whose appointment the disputants' own numbers controlled. The post-2014 record of delayed and contested Speaker decisions, examined later in this article, is the factual material on which the minority's institutional objection has been re-argued ever since, in the Court's own later judgments among other places.

One further holding of Kihoto Hollohan organises the case law that followed. Because the Speaker acts as a tribunal, the Speaker's failure to exercise the jurisdiction is as reviewable as its wrongful exercise. That proposition lay mostly dormant until the Supreme Court applied it in Rajendra Singh Rana v Swami Prasad Maurya in 2007, where a Bahujan Samaj Party legislature group in Uttar Pradesh had claimed a split and the Speaker had recognised the faction without deciding the disqualification petitions against its members; the Court held that the Speaker's failure to decide the question of disqualification before recognising a split was a failure to exercise jurisdiction, and it decided the disqualification itself on the record before it. The Rana judgment matters for the architectural reading because it shows the review jurisdiction doing something other than policing outcomes: it was policing the tribunal's refusal to sit. A design that vests adjudication in an officeholder with political interests will generate, predictably, not only biased decisions but postponed ones, and the Court's doctrine has had to grow a remedy for silence as well as for error.

## Voluntarily Giving Up: The Conscience Door and Its Judicial Widening

Paragraph 2(1)(a) disqualifies a member who has voluntarily given up membership of the party, and the phrase became the Schedule's most litigated ground precisely because it governs the space where conscience claims live. A member who resigns the party in protest, a member expelled by the leadership who then campaigns elsewhere, a member who never resigns at all but acts in concert with another party: the text answers none of these cases in terms, and the courts have had to construct the meaning of voluntary relinquishment from conduct. In Ravi S. Naik v Union of India, decided on 9 February 1994, the Supreme Court held that the words voluntarily given up membership are not synonymous with resignation, that they carry a wider connotation, and that an inference of relinquishment may be drawn from a member's conduct even without a formal resignation letter. Ravi Naik and Sanjay Bandekar, elected to the Goa Legislative Assembly on Maharashtrawadi Gomantak Party tickets, had withdrawn support and participated in moves against their own government, and the Court upheld their disqualification on conduct from which giving up membership could be inferred. The holding converted Paragraph 2(1)(a) from a clause about a documented act into a clause about a demonstrated allegiance, judged on speeches, associations and voting behaviour.

G. Viswanathan v Hon'ble Speaker, Tamil Nadu Legislative Assembly, decided in 1996, extended the construction to the hardest case, the expelled member. Viswanathan had been expelled by the AIADMK and then joined a group aligned with the DMK; he argued that an expelled member becomes unattached and stands outside the Schedule's party discipline. The Court held that an expelled member remains, for the purposes of the Tenth Schedule, a member of the original political party, and that if such a member joins another party the member is deemed to have voluntarily given up membership of the original party and is liable to disqualification. The doctrine has a severe logic. Expulsion is the leadership's act, yet its legal consequence under Viswanathan is to leave the expelled member bound to the party that expelled the member, unable to join another without losing the seat. Critics of the line of cases, including Suhrith Parthasarathy in his essays on the Schedule's case law, have pointed out that the combination of Ravi Naik and Viswanathan narrows the conscience door from both sides: a member can be found to have left a party without resigning from it, and a member thrown out of a party cannot lawfully arrive anywhere else. The defence of the construction, stated at its strongest, is that the alternative invites manufactured expulsions, in which a party stages the expulsion of members it wishes to free for a crossing while they keep their seats. Both positions are live in the literature, and the architectural point does not depend on choosing between them: the Schedule's text made exit the trigger of disqualification, and the courts, asked what exit means, answered in the direction of breadth because the mischief the text names is movement itself.

The Bihar cases added a further layer. In Dr. Mahachandra Prasad Singh v Chairman, Bihar Legislative Council, decided in 2004, the Supreme Court held that a member who, with full knowledge, contests an election as a candidate of a different party, or campaigns for it, may on that conduct be held to have voluntarily given up membership of the original party, again without any formal act of resignation. Read together, the three decisions establish that Paragraph 2(1)(a) operates as a conduct-based allegiance test administered by the Speaker as tribunal and reviewed by the courts on the Kihoto grounds. The member who wishes to record a principled break must still break cleanly and accept the loss of the seat; the member who wishes to keep the seat must keep the allegiance, in speech and association as well as in division lobbies. That is Feature Two of the four-feature map set out later in this article, and its operation is a deliberate design consequence, not a judicial accident: the 1985 text priced exit at the seat, and the case law has simply refused to let the price be avoided by informality.

## The 91st Amendment of 2003: Closing the Split Door

The Constitution (Ninety-first Amendment) Act of 2003, brought into force on 1 January 2004, amended the Schedule at its weakest joint. Its central act was deletion: Paragraph 3, the one-third split exception, ceased to exist. From 2004 onward the Schedule has recognised only one protected collective movement, the merger under Paragraph 4 requiring agreement by not less than two thirds of the legislature party. The amendment reached beyond the Schedule's text as well. It capped the size of the Union Council of Ministers, including the Prime Minister, at fifteen per cent of the strength of the Lok Sabha through the new Article 75(1A), and imposed the matching cap on state councils of ministers through Article 164(1A), attacking the reward structure that the Chavan Committee had identified in 1969: a government that can offer portfolios to at most fifteen per cent of the House has fewer prizes with which to purchase crossings. It inserted Article 75(1B) and Article 164(1B), barring a member disqualified under the Tenth Schedule from appointment as a minister until the member is re-elected or the term of the House expires, whichever is earlier, and it inserted Article 361B, extending the same bar to remunerative political posts. The package, taken together, is the most coherent piece of anti-defection design in the Indian record, because it works on three margins at once: the legality of the crossing, the supply of offices available to reward it, and the defector's personal eligibility to receive the reward.

Parliament's own stated reason for deleting Paragraph 3 is preserved in the amendment's legislative history and in the deliberations that preceded it, including the Law Commission of India's 170th Report on Reform of the Electoral Laws of 1999, which had recommended that the provisions exempting splits and mergers from disqualification be deleted and that parties confine the whip to occasions when a government is in danger. The experience behind the recommendation was uniform across parties and states. In assembly after assembly during the 1990s, factions of exactly one third, or fractionally above it, separated from legislature parties, claimed the split protection, and joined or sustained governments, so that the exception had become the principal lawful vehicle of the practice the Schedule existed to stop. M. R. Madhavan's analyses for PRS Legislative Research trace the pattern in legislative data: the split clause had converted a prohibition into a threshold, and thresholds in politics are not barriers but targets, numbers to be assembled by negotiation. The 2003 deletion is therefore the strongest evidence for the counter-argument this article must answer, the argument that the post-2003 Schedule resolved the old tension by strengthening party discipline decisively. The answer, developed through the merger record and the Maharashtra litigation below, is that the deletion raised the price of collective movement without removing the door, and that the tension migrated to the surviving door and to the adjudicator's chamber rather than disappearing.

Deleting the split also altered the position of the genuine dissenter in a way that deserves plain statement. Between 1985 and 2003, a faction of one third that broke with a leadership on a matter of programme could claim constitutional recognition as a split and continue in the House as a group. After 2004, no such route exists at any scale below two thirds, and a breakaway below that threshold is simply a set of members who have voluntarily given up membership within Paragraph 2(1)(a), each liable to disqualification on the Ravi Naik construction. The Schedule after 2003 thus protects collective movement only when it is large enough to absorb a legislature party into another party, and it offers nothing to the small principled faction. Whether that is a defect or the entire point depends on the theory of representation one brings, which is why the disagreement the brief assigns to this article, between the descriptive-historical framing, the case-law framing and the architectural-tension reading, cannot be settled by chronology alone. The chronology is settled. The meaning of the chronology is the argument.

## The Four-Feature Map of the Tenth Schedule

The architectural reading becomes useful when it can be cited, and citation requires a map. The table below, labelled the Four-Feature Architectural-Tension Map of the Tenth Schedule, states each feature with its textual home in the Schedule, the mechanism through which the tension between party discipline and the member's judgment operates, and one dated instance from the record in which the mechanism decided events. The map is the findable artifact of this article, and the sections that follow it work through the four features in the order the table presents them.

| Feature | Textual home | Operating mechanism | Dated instance from the record |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Whip-violation disqualification | Paragraph 2(1)(b) | A party direction binds every vote; breach without prior permission or condonation within fifteen days costs the seat | The 2008 Lok Sabha confidence vote, after which members who defied whips faced Paragraph 2 proceedings |
| Voluntary relinquishment | Paragraph 2(1)(a) | Exit is priced at the seat, and courts infer exit from conduct rather than requiring a resignation | Ravi S. Naik v Union of India, 9 February 1994, upholding disqualification on inferred relinquishment in Goa |
| Merger-only collective door | Paragraph 4, after Paragraph 3 was deleted in 2003 | Collective movement survives only at two thirds of the legislature party and only into another party | Goa, 10 July 2019, when ten of fifteen Congress members were recognised as merged into the BJP |
| Speaker as tribunal | Paragraph 6 | The presiding officer of the House decides disqualification as a tribunal, subject to the judicial review restored in 1992 | Keisham Meghachandra Singh, 21 January 2020, where the Supreme Court urged decision within three months and proposed an independent tribunal |

The map's discipline is that every feature is textual. None of the four depends on the character of a particular Speaker, party or chief minister, and each can be located by a reader in the Schedule as enacted and amended. That discipline is what separates the architectural reading from the case-law-driven framing, which organises the subject by disputes, and from the descriptive-historical framing, which organises it by decades. Disputes end and decades pass; the features remain in the text, generating the disputes that each decade then records.

## Feature One: The Whip and the Price of a Single Vote

Paragraph 2(1)(b) is the Schedule's most intrusive provision, because it reaches inside the division lobby rather than waiting at the party door. A member may hold the seat, attend the House and speak against the party line, since the Schedule does not punish speech; the punishment attaches at the moment of voting against a direction, or abstaining against one, without prior permission and without condonation inside fifteen days. The provision's defenders state its rationale without embarrassment, and the rationale is not trivial. Voters in the Indian system choose, in the great majority of constituencies, among party candidates carrying party symbols and party manifestos; the Election Commission's symbol order makes the party, not the person, the ballot's organising unit, a connection the institutional history in [the institution-under-stress framework](/2020/06/03/election-commission-institution-under-stress/) traces through the Commission's own jurisprudence. On that premise, a member who votes against the party on a question the party has whipped is voting with a mandate the member does not personally own, and disqualification merely returns the seat to the alignment that won it. The premise was argued in Kihoto Hollohan itself, where the majority's refusal to find a violation of Articles 105 and 194 rests on exactly this distinction between the member's speech, which stays free, and the member's tenure, which the Constitution may condition.

The counter-position, also stated in the 1985 debates and carried since by commentators including Faizan Mustafa in his essays on the Schedule's working, is that Paragraph 2(1)(b) as drafted is wider than its rationale. The rationale justifies binding a member on votes that decide whether a government stands, because those are the votes at which the electorate's choice of a governing party is executed or defeated. The text binds the member on every vote, including votes on ordinary legislation, amendments and private members' business, where the government's survival is not engaged and the member's constituency interest may point against the party line. The Dinesh Goswami Committee on Electoral Reforms, reporting in 1990, proposed exactly this narrowing: disqualification for whip defiance should be confined to motions of confidence and no-confidence, leaving members free on other business. The Law Commission's 170th Report of 1999 proposed the functional equivalent from the party side, that parties limit whips to occasions when the government is in danger. Parliament has enacted neither proposal in the three decades since the first was made. The breadth of the whip ground is therefore a standing legislative choice, renewed by omission in every session, and it is the clearest point at which the Schedule's discipline exceeds the stability rationale that the Chavan record supports.

The confidence vote of 22 July 2008 in the Lok Sabha illustrates both faces of the provision. The United Progressive Alliance government of Manmohan Singh faced the vote after the Left parties withdrew support over the civil nuclear agreement with the United States, the rupture examined in [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) as a renegotiation of coalition commitments. Members crossed whips in both directions during that vote, and parties moved against their own defying members under Paragraph 2; the proceedings that followed, including the expulsion and disqualification disputes arising from the July 2008 divisions, showed the clause operating as designed on the highest-stakes vote a House conducts. A whip enforced on a confidence motion is the clause at its most defensible. The same clause, textually indistinguishable, governs a whipped vote on an ordinary bill in a quiet session, and it is the inability of the text to separate those two occasions, rather than any abuse by a particular party, that keeps Feature One at the centre of the reform argument.

## Feature Three: The Merger Door and the Record of Its Use

Paragraph 4 protects a member where the member's original political party merges with another political party, and Paragraph 4(2) deems the merger of the original party to have taken place if and only if not less than two thirds of the members of the legislature party have agreed to it. The text contains a puzzle that the Speakers of 2019 resolved, each time, in favour of the moving group. A merger, in ordinary party terms, is an act of parties: two organisations combine. Paragraph 4(2), however, measures the event inside the legislature party, so a two-thirds bloc of legislators can present its agreement as the party's merger even where the party organisation outside the House has neither merged nor consented. The distinction between the political party and its legislature party, provisionally answered by Speakers in 2019, returned at the centre of the Maharashtra dispute and was answered the other way by the Supreme Court in Subhash Desai in 2023, which held that the political party, not the legislature party, appoints the whip and the leader, a holding whose logic reaches back into how mergers ought to be recognised.

Goa supplied the cleanest instance. On 10 July 2019, ten of the fifteen members of the Congress Legislature Party in the Goa Legislative Assembly, led by the Leader of the Opposition Chandrakant Kavlekar, presented themselves to Speaker Rajesh Patnekar as a group constituting two thirds of the legislature party and claimed merger with the Bharatiya Janata Party. The Speaker accepted the claim the same evening, the Congress strength in the forty-member House fell to five, and the BJP government's strength rose from seventeen to twenty-seven, with ministerial berths following for members of the incoming group in the cabinet reshuffle days later. No determination was made that the Indian National Congress as a political party had merged with the BJP; the organisation continued, contested elections and held its remaining seats. The merger that Paragraph 4 recognised had occurred entirely within the legislature party, between its two-thirds majority and another party, over the stated objection of the party the members were leaving. Goa repeated the manoeuvre on 14 September 2022, when eight of the eleven Congress members then in the Assembly, exactly clearing two thirds, were recognised by Speaker Ramesh Tawadkar as merged with the BJP. Two performances of the same move in one state inside one assembly cycle establish that the 2019 recognition was not an improvisation. It was a reading of Paragraph 4 that any sufficiently large faction could rely on.

Telangana had run the same sequence a month earlier. In June 2019, twelve of the eighteen members of the Congress Legislature Party in the Telangana Legislative Assembly, after the party's state president Uttam Kumar Reddy resigned his Assembly seat on election to the Lok Sabha, sought recognition from Speaker Pocharam Srinivas Reddy of the merger of the Congress Legislature Party with the Telangana Rashtra Samithi. Twelve of eighteen is exactly two thirds; the Speaker recognised the merger the same day, 6 June 2019, the legislature secretary's bulletin recorded the twelve as deemed merged with the TRS legislature party under Paragraph 4, and the Congress lost its status as the principal opposition party in the House, its remnant of six falling below the AIMIM's seven. The arithmetic precision in both states deserves emphasis, because it is the fingerprint of the architectural feature rather than of spontaneous political movement. Groups assembled to the threshold, neither one member short nor wastefully above it: two thirds is a number a negotiator can count to, and Paragraph 4(2) tells the negotiator exactly what the number buys.

Arunachal Pradesh shows the merger door used in sequence with party re-formation. In 2016 the state's politics passed through the constitutional crisis that produced the Supreme Court's judgment in Nabam Rebia, after which the People's Party of Arunachal, a Congress breakaway vehicle, held the government; on 31 December 2016, thirty-three of the forty-three PPA legislators, including Chief Minister Pema Khandu, joined the BJP, carrying the government across parties while the members kept their seats, and the scale of the movement placed it beyond the reach of Paragraph 2 proceedings. The Arunachal sequence predates the 2019 cases and displays the same grammar: movement that would disqualify a member acting alone, or a faction acting at one third after 2003, is constitutionally protected when the moving bloc carries two thirds and a receiving party exists. M. R. Madhavan's PRS analyses of state assemblies document the resulting pattern across the post-2014 period, in which successful crossings are overwhelmingly wholesale rather than retail: the Schedule did not end defection as a practice, it set a minimum order size.

### Why did wholesale defection replace retail defection after 2003?

The deletion of Paragraph 3 in 2003 removed the one-third route, leaving Paragraph 4 as the only protected crossing. A lone member or small faction crossing faces disqualification under Paragraph 2, while a bloc carrying two thirds of a legislature party claims merger protection. Organisers assemble two-thirds blocs, as Goa showed in July 2019 and Telangana in June 2019.

The pattern explains why the strongest counter-argument to the architectural reading fails on the record. That argument holds that the 91st Amendment resolved the tension by decisively strengthening discipline: the split door closed, the ministerial prize capped, the defector barred from office pending re-election. If the resolution had worked as claimed, the post-2004 record would show crossings shrinking into insignificance. The record shows crossings changing form instead, consolidating at the two-thirds threshold and passing through the merger door, in Goa twice, in Telangana, in Arunachal Pradesh, and in the Karnataka crisis of 2019 by the alternative route of resignation, where seventeen coalition members resigned their seats, the Speaker's disqualifications under Paragraph 2 were upheld by the Supreme Court on 13 November 2019 in Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil v Speaker, Karnataka Legislative Assembly, but the Court struck down the Speaker's further order barring the disqualified members from contesting elections for the remainder of the term, leaving them free to seek fresh mandates in the December 2019 by-elections, several of which they won. Resignation plus by-election, merger at two thirds: the Schedule's post-2003 history is a history of movement rerouted through the openings the text leaves, which is what a tension operating through architecture looks like when it is observed rather than theorised.

## Feature Four: The Speaker as Tribunal and the Cost of Delay

Paragraph 6 vests the disqualification question in the Speaker or Chairman, whose decision Kihoto Hollohan characterised as the decision of a tribunal. The office's difficulty is structural and was named by the Kihoto minority in 1992: the Speaker is elected by the majority whose survival a defection dispute may decide, retains party membership in Indian practice, and can be removed by the same House. None of that makes any individual Speaker's decision corrupt, and the evenhanded treatment this article owes the record requires saying that many Speakers have decided promptly and against their own parties' interests. The structural point is narrower and stronger: the design gives the adjudicator an interest in the timing of adjudication, because a disqualification decided after a floor test, a session, or a government's fall is a different political act from the same decision delivered before it. Delay, in this architecture, is not a malfunction external to the design. It is one of the moves the design makes available, and the case law since 2007 is largely a record of courts trying to close the timing move without removing the adjudicator.

Manipur produced the judgment that states the problem in the Court's own words. Thounaojam Shyamkumar was elected to the Manipur Legislative Assembly in 2017 on a Congress ticket and took office as a minister in the BJP-led government of N. Biren Singh; disqualification petitions against him were filed before Speaker Y. Khemchand Singh and remained undecided for nearly three years, across repeated directions and the member's continued tenure in the council of ministers. In Keisham Meghachandra Singh v Hon'ble Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly, decided on 21 January 2020, the Supreme Court held that Speakers should decide disqualification petitions within a reasonable period, that three months from the filing of a petition is the outer limit the Court would ordinarily expect, and it went on to commend to Parliament a structural alternative: that Parliament consider substituting the Speaker with a permanent tribunal headed by a retired Supreme Court judge or a retired Chief Justice of a High Court, or some other independent mechanism, to decide defection questions. The Court itself removed Shyamkumar's ministerial tenure from the equation by restraining him from entering the Assembly pending the Speaker's decision, an interim remedy that concedes the design failure it could not itself repair, since the power to redesign Paragraph 6 belongs to Parliament acting by constitutional amendment. The Speaker decided the petition on 28 March 2020, disqualifying Shyamkumar days after the Court's restraint had already ended his effective tenure.

Karnataka in 2019 placed the same adjudicator under the opposite pressure, speed deployed against the moving members, and the Supreme Court's response completes the picture of review as calibration rather than replacement. Speaker K. R. Ramesh Kumar, faced with resignations tendered by seventeen Congress and Janata Dal (Secular) members during the collapse of the H. D. Kumaraswamy government in July 2019, disqualified all seventeen under the Tenth Schedule and ordered that they not be permitted to contest elections during the remaining term of the Assembly. In Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil, on 13 November 2019, the Court upheld the disqualifications, accepting that conduct including absence, public statements and refusal to attend the legislature party's processes could establish voluntary relinquishment, but struck down the term-long election bar as beyond the Speaker's power, because neither Paragraph 2 nor Article 191 confers on the tribunal a power to add disqualification from candidacy to disqualification from membership. Delay in Manipur, excess in Karnataka: the two errors are mirror images, and in both the reviewing court corrected the instance while leaving the office that produced it untouched, because Kihoto's settlement gives it no other instrument.

Arunachal Pradesh contributed the prior question of whether a Speaker facing removal can adjudicate at all. In Nabam Rebia v Deputy Speaker, decided on 13 July 2016, a Constitution Bench held that a Speaker against whom a notice of intention to move a resolution for removal is pending is restrained from deciding disqualification petitions, reasoning that the Speaker's own continuance depends on members whose fate the petitions would decide. The holding created a tactical symmetry that Maharashtra's lawyers pressed hard in 2022: members facing disqualification moved a removal notice against Deputy Speaker Narhari Zirwal, and the question whether that notice froze the adjudication became one of the doors into the Supreme Court's Maharashtra judgment. In Subhash Desai, on 11 May 2023, the Court held that the correctness of Nabam Rebia's proposition required reconsideration by a larger bench of seven judges, describing the reasoning underlying it as contradictory, and it referred the question accordingly. That reference remains the Schedule's largest open doctrinal file, and its existence confirms the architectural diagnosis from inside the Court: the identity, tenure and incentives of the adjudicator are not peripheral embarrassments of the system. They are the system, at the point where its decisions bite.

## Maharashtra 2022 to 2023 and Subhash Desai Stated by Holding

The Maharashtra crisis is the brief's mandated test of the whole architecture, and it must be read against the state's longer party history, the trajectory traced in [the post-2020 Sena-split-architecture](/2020/01/17/maharashtra-shiv-sena-evolution/) from Bal Thackeray's founding of the Shiv Sena in 1966 through the party's alliance decades. In June 2022 Eknath Shinde, a senior Sena minister in the Maha Vikas Aghadi government of Uddhav Thackeray, left Maharashtra with a bloc of Sena legislators that grew past two thirds of the legislature party, taking shelter first in Surat and then in Guwahati while the government counted its remaining support. On 29 June 2022 Governor Bhagat Singh Koshyari directed a floor test for the following day; Thackeray, facing the arithmetic, resigned that evening without facing the test, and on 30 June 2022 the Governor invited Shinde to form a government with Bharatiya Janata Party support, Devendra Fadnavis taking office as Deputy Chief Minister. Disqualification petitions multiplied in both directions, the Thackeray camp against the Shinde bloc for defying the party whip and the Shinde camp against the members who stayed, and the Election Commission of India took up the parallel dispute over which faction was the Shiv Sena for the purposes of the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order of 1968, awarding the party name and the bow-and-arrow symbol to the Shinde faction on 17 February 2023 on the test of legislative majority after finding the party constitution tests inconclusive.

The Constitution Bench judgment in Subhash Desai v Principal Secretary, Governor of Maharashtra, delivered on 11 May 2023 by a bench headed by Chief Justice D. Y. Chandrachud, is stated here by its holdings, in the order the Court's conclusions present them. The correctness of Nabam Rebia on the Speaker's power to adjudicate while a removal notice is pending stands referred to a bench of seven judges. The Court cannot ordinarily adjudicate disqualification petitions in the first instance; the Speaker must decide them, and must do so within a reasonable period, there being no extraordinary circumstances in the Maharashtra cases that justified the Court taking the petitions over. A member has the right to participate in the proceedings of the House while a disqualification petition is pending, and the validity of proceedings in the intervening period is not subject to the outcome of the petitions. The political party, and not the legislature party, appoints the whip and the leader of the party in the House, and the direction to vote or abstain is issued by the political party; the Speaker's recognition, communicated on 3 July 2022, of the whip and leader nominated by the Shinde faction was therefore contrary to law, and the Speaker was directed to recognise the whip and leader duly authorised by the Shiv Sena political party with reference to its constitution. The Speaker and the Election Commission are empowered to adjudicate concurrently, the Speaker under the Tenth Schedule and the Commission under Paragraph 15 of the Symbols Order, and the Commission, in deciding which faction constitutes the party, may apply the test best suited to the facts before it; the test of legislative majority is not a constitutionally impermissible test for that purpose. The Governor was not justified in calling upon Thackeray to prove his majority, because the material before the Governor, communications of discontent from legislators that did not state an intention to withdraw from the government, furnished no objective basis to doubt that the government held the confidence of the House. The Governor was justified, after the resignation, in inviting Shinde. And the Court could not restore the Thackeray government, because a government that resigns without facing a floor test cannot be reinstated by judicial order; the resignation was voluntary and its consequence irreversible by the Court.

Two consequences of the judgment deserve emphasis for the architectural reading. The holding that the political party appoints the whip cuts directly into the practice by which Speakers had recognised legislature-party mergers in Goa and Telangana in 2019, since it reasserts the original political party, the defined term the Schedule attaches to the member, as the source of binding direction inside the House; a legislature party majority is a fact about numbers, and after Subhash Desai it is not, without more, a fact about the party. The second consequence is the judgment's deliberate restraint on the disqualification question itself. The Court corrected the Governor, corrected the Speaker's recognition of the whip, referred Nabam Rebia onward, and then returned the disqualification petitions to the Speaker to decide within a reasonable period, because Kihoto's allocation of first-instance adjudication to the Speaker left it no ordinary route to decide them. Speaker Rahul Narwekar decided the Maharashtra petitions on 10 January 2024, holding that the Shinde faction was the real Shiv Sena for the purposes before him and declining to disqualify its members, while also declining to disqualify the Thackeray faction's members on the cross-petitions; the Bombay High Court and the Supreme Court were thereafter approached by the aggrieved camp, and the litigation continued past the judgment's second anniversary of the events. The sequence from June 2022 to January 2024, nineteen months from crossing to adjudication with a government formed, a symbol awarded and an assembly term largely spent in the interval, is the Speaker-tribunal feature operating exactly as its critics from 1992 onward predicted and exactly as its text permits.

## The Reform Proposals and the Positions Behind Them

Reform of the Schedule is unusual among Indian constitutional debates because the principal proposals have been on the table, in fully drafted form, for over three decades, and their authors span every institution that touches the subject. The Dinesh Goswami Committee on Electoral Reforms, a committee of political parties constituted by the National Front government, reported in 1990 and proposed two linked alterations: that disqualification for defying a direction be confined to votes on motions of confidence and no-confidence, so that the whip binds only where a government's life is at stake, and that the question of disqualification be decided not by the Speaker but by the President or the Governor acting on the advice of the Election Commission, on the model of Articles 103 and 192, which route other disqualification questions for legislators through exactly that channel. The Law Commission of India, in its 170th Report on the Reform of the Electoral Laws delivered in 1999, recommended deleting the provisions that exempt splits and mergers from disqualification, treating pre-poll electoral fronts as political parties for the purposes of the law, and confining the issuance of whips to occasions when the government of the day is in danger; Parliament enacted the deletion of the split provision in 2003 and left the rest. The National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution, chaired by Justice M. N. Venkatachaliah, himself the author of the Kihoto majority a decade earlier, reported in 2002 and recommended that a defector be barred from holding public office or any remunerative political post for the remainder of the term, and that the vote of a defector cast to topple a government be treated as invalid. The Election Commission of India has itself repeatedly proposed that decisions under the Tenth Schedule be made by the President or Governor on its binding advice, a position recorded in the Commission's own reform proposals across successive chief election commissionerships. The Supreme Court joined the list in Keisham Meghachandra Singh in January 2020 with its suggestion of a permanent tribunal headed by a retired judge.

### What would follow if defection decisions moved to the Election Commission?

Decisions would be taken by the President or Governor on the Election Commission's advice, the model Articles 103 and 192 use for other disqualifications. The Goswami Committee proposed that transfer in 1990, the Election Commission has endorsed it, and its defenders argue that an adjudicator outside the House has no stake in the majority its ruling may create or destroy.

The positions against transfer must be stated at their strongest, as the charter of this series requires for every side of a contested question. The first objection is jurisdictional and comes from the logic of Kihoto itself: disqualification for defection concerns the internal composition and discipline of a House, and the Constitution's framers of 1985 deliberately placed it with the House's own presiding officer as part of legislative privilege under Articles 122 and 212; moving it to an executive-channel decision, even one advised by the Commission, relocates a legislative function to the President or Governor, offices appointed by the Union government, and in the states that relocation imports the Governor's contested position into every defection dispute. The second objection targets the Commission's neutrality as an adjudicator of party disputes: the Commission already decides which faction of a split party is the party for symbol purposes under Paragraph 15 of the Symbols Order, as it did for the Shiv Sena in February 2023, and concentrating the symbol question and the disqualification question in one institution removes the check that the present separation, affirmed in Subhash Desai's concurrency holding, incidentally provides. The third objection is the asymmetry problem stated in [the architectural-asymmetry-question framework](/2020/06/30/tenure-security-election-commission/): the Election Commission's own tenure and appointment protections are the subject of a separate live controversy, and a transfer of Paragraph 6 adjudication to the Commission imports that controversy into defection law. Defenders of the transfer answer that the present adjudicator's conflict is not hypothetical but structural and repeatedly realised, that Articles 103 and 192 have operated the advised-decision model for office-of-profit and other disqualifications without the predicted disasters, and that a time limit, of the kind the Court gestured toward in Keisham Meghachandra Singh with its three-month expectation, could be attached to the transferred jurisdiction by the same amendment. No Parliament has carried the amendment. The full texts of the Goswami report and the 170th Report circulate in the standard documentary collections, and a researcher comparing the 2020 Keisham suggestion against the 1990 text, opening the older report through an [online document viewer for office files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), finds the proposal nearly unchanged across thirty years, which is itself a finding about the politics of reform: the beneficiaries of the adjudicatory status quo are the legislators who would have to amend it.

## Three Framings, One Text: Adjudicating the Disagreement

The brief for this article assigns a named disagreement among three readings of the Tenth Schedule, and the stance charter under which this series is written requires the disagreement to be adjudicated on primary evidence rather than merely displayed. The descriptive-historical framing tells the story as a sequence: instability in 1967 to 1971, the Chavan Committee in 1969, failed bills in 1973 and 1978, the Schedule in 1985, its tightening in 2003, and a series of state crises after 2014. The framing is accurate in every particular this article has checked, and its weakness is not error but exhaustion. A sequence explains that each stage followed the last; it cannot explain why the same conflict keeps reappearing at each stage in a fresh institutional costume, the whip in 1985, the split in the 1990s, the merger in 2019, the Speaker's diary in 2020 and 2023. The case-law-driven framing organises the subject by disputes, Kihoto Hollohan on review, Ravi Naik on relinquishment, Viswanathan on expulsion, Rana on the duty to decide, Keisham Meghachandra Singh on delay, Subhash Desai on the whip's source. Its weakness is the mirror image of the first: it knows exactly what each court held and cannot say, from inside any single holding, why the holdings keep needing to be supplemented by further holdings on adjacent conduct the text did not foresee. A judge decides the case in hand. The pattern across the cases is not a judicial artefact at all; it is the text's own structure surfacing case by case.

The architectural reading defended here starts where the other two stop. It holds that the Schedule has a built-in tension between two goods the Constitution elsewhere affirms, the discipline that makes a party's electoral mandate executable and the judgment that makes a representative a representative rather than a courier, and that the tension is located in identifiable features of the text: the whip ground's unlimited scope in Paragraph 2(1)(b), the conduct-based exit test that grew on Paragraph 2(1)(a), the merger threshold in Paragraph 4, and the interested adjudicator installed by Paragraph 6. The reading earns its keep by prediction and by fit. It predicts that closing one opening reroutes movement through the others, which is the observed sequence from split to merger to resignation-and-byelection between 1985 and 2019. It predicts that adjudicatory stress concentrates on timing, which is the observed Manipur and Karnataka pattern. It predicts that a factional dispute will force the system to say whether the party or its legislators own the whip, which is the question Subhash Desai answered in May 2023. The descriptive framing records each of those events accurately and predicts none of them; the case-law framing states each holding correctly and explains none of the transitions. On the evidence of the four features and the post-2014 trajectory, the architectural reading is the one that captures the Schedule's operative content, and this article adopts it not as a preference among vocabularies but as the conclusion the record supports.

The older party history sharpens the verdict by contrast. The Congress split of 1969, whose organisational consequences are traced in [the post-1969 organisational architecture](/2019/03/26/1969-congress-split-decisive-moment/), was fought and settled inside party forums, delegate counts and symbol disputes, because no anti-defection law existed to convert an organisational rupture into a question of seat tenure; Indira Gandhi's faction and the Syndicate contested the party, and members kept their seats whichever way the contest ended. Rajni Kothari's account of the Congress system, the framework set out in [the Congress System's four-component framework](/2019/11/18/kothari-congress-system-post-independence/), described a dominant party that processed internal dissent through factions operating as an internal opposition. The Tenth Schedule changed the venue of such contests permanently. After 1985, and decisively after 2003, an organisational rupture inside a legislature party is simultaneously a tenure dispute, adjudicated by the Speaker under constitutional text, with the Election Commission deciding the party's name and symbol in a parallel proceeding. The party did not disappear from the Constitution's field of vision in 1985; it entered the Constitution as a seat-holding fact, and the architectural reading is simply the discipline of reading the provisions that performed that entry.

## Conscience, Constituency and the Member's Mandate

The individual-conscience side of the tension is often argued sentimentally, and it deserves a harder statement. The case for the member's judgment rests on three grounds, each anchored in the constitutional text outside the Schedule. The first is the oath and the office: a legislator swears to bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution, not to a party constitution, and Articles 105 and 194 confer freedom of speech in the House as a privilege of the member's office, a freedom the Kihoto majority preserved for speech while permitting tenure to be conditioned on voting. The second ground is the constituency: the member is elected from a territorial seat and is the only representative that seat has, so a whip that binds the member against the seat's evident interest on a matter unconnected with the government's survival transfers the seat's voice to a party secretariat in which the constituency has no vote. The third ground is error correction inside parties. A legislature party that cannot be defied cannot be warned from inside; the backbench revolt is the mechanism by which parliamentary parties learn that a measure will fail in the country, and a text that prices every revolt at the rebels' seats suppresses the signal along with the rebellion. Suhrith Parthasarathy's essays on the Schedule press versions of the second and third grounds, arguing that the law inverts the accountability it claims to protect by making the member answerable upward to the leadership rather than outward to the electorate. Faizan Mustafa's essays press the institutional version, that a law administered by an interested adjudicator will in practice protect incumbents, since the Speaker's majority is usually the government's majority and delay usually serves the side holding office.

The discipline side, stated at its strongest rather than as a caricature of leadership greed, answers each ground. Against the oath argument, it observes that the member sought the seat on a party ticket and symbol, in most constituencies the decisive asset, and that the mandate theory of the Indian election, in which the voter chooses a governing party and its programme, makes the member a trustee of a collective promise rather than the owner of a personal one. Against the constituency argument, it replies that constituency service in the Indian system, the casework, the development funds, the access to ministers, flows through party and government channels, and that a member's effectiveness for the seat ordinarily depends on standing inside the party rather than against it. Against the error-correction argument, the discipline side offers the empirical claim that internal forums, legislature party meetings, parliamentary boards, the threat of collective resignation, carry warnings to leaderships without handing every dissatisfied member a marketable vote. M. R. Madhavan's PRS analyses add the comparativist caution that India is an outlier in the severity of its regime: most parliamentary democracies punish defiance through party consequences, deselection and loss of office within the party's gift, and reserve loss of the seat itself for the act of joining another party, if they punish it at all. The honest adjudication between these cases is the one the text itself performs. The Schedule accepts the mandate theory for tenure and rejects it for speech; it binds the vote and frees the voice; it prices exit at the seat and dissent, short of the division lobby, at nothing. Whether that allocation is wise is the standing argument of Indian constitutional politics. That the allocation is deliberate, and that its seams are the four features mapped in this article, is the finding the evidence supports.

## What the Record Shows: Stability Bought, Movement Rerouted

Measured against its founding purpose, the Schedule's record is mixed in a patterned way, and the pattern is the architectural tension expressing itself in outcomes. At the Union level, no government since 1985 has fallen because its members crossed the floor in the manner of the state assemblies of 1967 to 1971; the governments that fell, in 1990, 1991, 1997 and 1999, fell by withdrawal of coalition support, by electoral verdict or by a lost confidence motion, which are the exits the Constitution contemplates, rather than by purchased crossings of individual members. The 1993 episode marks the boundary of that achievement. On 28 July 1993 the Lok Sabha voted on a no-confidence motion against the minority government of P. V. Narasimha Rao; the government survived by a margin of fourteen votes, and Jharkhand Mukti Morcha members were later prosecuted on the allegation that bribes had purchased their votes, a prosecution that produced the Supreme Court's 1998 ruling in P. V. Narasimha Rao v State that a legislator who accepts a bribe and votes is shielded by the privilege in Article 105(2) in respect of the vote. A seven-judge bench overruled that immunity holding in Sita Soren v Union of India on 4 March 2024, holding that bribery is not protected legislative privilege. The episode belongs in this article because it is the whip system's limit case from the other direction: the Schedule can compel a member's presence inside a party's voting strength, and it can stop the member selling a departure, but the integrity of the vote inside the lobby is a matter for criminal law and privilege doctrine, not for Paragraph 2.

At the state level the rerouting documented in the merger and resignation cases continued through the period the brief's artifact covers. In Uttarakhand in 2016, nine Congress members rebelled against the government of Harish Rawat during the budget session; Speaker Govind Singh Kunjwal disqualified the nine under the Tenth Schedule on 27 March 2016, the Uttarakhand High Court upheld the disqualifications, President's rule intervened twice, and the government was restored only after a court-supervised floor test on 10 May 2016 which Rawat won with the disqualified members excluded from the count. In Madhya Pradesh in March 2020, twenty-two Congress members aligned with Jyotiraditya Scindia resigned their seats from Bengaluru, Speaker N. P. Prajapati accepted the resignations after a period of contest over their voluntariness, the Kamal Nath government resigned on 20 March 2020 ahead of a floor test it could no longer win, and many of the resigning members returned to the Assembly through by-elections later in 2020 as members of the Bharatiya Janata Party, several of them as ministers. Resignation is the one route the Schedule does not close, since it cannot: a seat vacated is a seat the member no longer holds, and the electorate in the by-election becomes the tribunal of the crossing. The Madhya Pradesh sequence is for that reason the most democratically defensible form the rerouted traffic takes, and the contrast with the merger route is instructive. The merger route asks the Speaker to certify arithmetic; the resignation route asks the voters to certify the defection. The Schedule's text, which aimed to subject crossings to the electorate's judgment, in practice reserves that judgment for the route it cannot reach and withholds it from the route it can.

The verdict this article returns on its assigned claim follows from that record. The Tenth Schedule has architectural-substantive tension between party-discipline and individual-conscience; the tension operates through the Schedule's features, the unlimited whip ground, the conduct-read exit test, the merger threshold and the interested tribunal; and the post-1985 trajectory, the 1992 settlement in Kihoto Hollohan, the 2003 tightening, the 2019 mergers, the Manipur delay, the Karnataka excess and the Maharashtra adjudication of 2023, is the tension working itself out in institutions rather than a series of accidents befalling a settled design. The descriptive-historical framing can narrate that trajectory and the case-law framing can state its holdings, and both are necessary labours. Neither explains why the trajectory keeps its shape. The shape is in the text, placed there in January 1985 by a Parliament that chose stability with its eyes open, amended in 2003 by a Parliament that chose more of the same, and read since by courts that have corrected its adjudicator's silences and excesses while leaving the architecture, as only a constitutional amendment can alter it, standing.

## Procedure Before the Speaker: Petitions, Evidence and the Missing Clock

The Schedule's eight paragraphs say who decides and on what grounds, and they are almost silent on how a case proceeds, a silence Paragraph 8 fills by delegation: the Speaker or Chairman of each House frames rules for giving effect to the Schedule, laid before and approved by the House. The Members of the Lok Sabha (Disqualification on Ground of Defection) Rules of 1985, and their state counterparts such as the Maharashtra Legislature Members (Disqualification on Ground of Defection) Rules of 1986, supply the procedural skeleton that the constitutional text omits. Under the Lok Sabha rules, a petition alleging disqualification is presented to the Speaker, ordinarily by a member of the House; the Speaker forwards a copy to the member complained against, calls for comments from the leader of the member's legislature party, and, if satisfied that a question under Paragraph 6 arises, refers the matter for inquiry, in the Lok Sabha's practice to the Committee of Privileges for preliminary investigation and report. The member complained against receives the material relied on, may file a written reply, and is heard, because Kihoto Hollohan's tribunal characterisation imports the rules of natural justice into a proceeding the 1985 framers had imagined as an internal act of the House. The state rules follow the same pattern with local variation in who may petition and whether a committee stage intervenes, and the variation itself is an architectural datum: a constitutional disqualification of identical text is processed through materially different procedures depending on the House in which the member sits.

Evidence before the tribunal has its own jurisprudence. Because Paragraph 2(1)(a) turns on inference from conduct, Speakers receive newspaper reports, television recordings, photographs of public meetings and the testimony of party functionaries, and the Supreme Court in Rajendra Singh Rana accepted that such material, including video recordings of a faction's public conduct, could ground a finding of voluntary relinquishment where the members did not dispute the events recorded. The standard is not the criminal standard; the tribunal weighs conduct on the preponderance of the material before it, subject to review for perversity, which in practice means a finding must have some evidence beneath it and must not fly in the face of the record. A second procedural doctrine limits the tribunal itself. In Ravi S. Naik's subsequent proceedings, the Supreme Court confirmed that a Speaker who has decided a disqualification question has no power under the Schedule to review the decision; the remedy for error lies in judicial review, not in a second hearing before the same office. The rule protects finality, and it also locks in the consequence of haste: a Speaker who decides quickly and wrongly can be corrected only by a court, on the Kihoto grounds, in proceedings that themselves take months or years, during which the decision ordinarily operates.

What the rules nowhere supply is a clock. The text of Paragraph 6 fixes no period within which the question must be answered, the Paragraph 8 rules prescribe periods for replies and references but none for the decision, and the gap is the single most consequential procedural fact in the entire Schedule, because every incentive identified in this article flows through it. A Speaker who wishes to protect a government delays the petitions against the government's new supporters; a Speaker pressed by the majority of the moment decides against the minority's members at speed; and the reviewing courts, confined by Kihoto to post-decision review save for the failure-to-decide complaint, have responded with exhortation, the three-month expectation in Keisham Meghachandra Singh, and interim restraints of the kind imposed on the Manipur minister in 2020, rather than with any doctrine that transfers the file. The Halim Committee of 1998, appointed to examine the Schedule's working, the Goswami Committee before it and the Law Commission after it all recorded the adjudicatory design as the system's weak joint, and the proposals they generated, transfer to the President or Governor on the Commission's advice, a statutory time limit, an independent tribunal, remain proposals. Procedure is where architecture becomes biography, in the life of a petition: filed in a week, answered in a month, and decided, in the cases that define the era, whenever the politics of the House made decision unavoidable.

## Defection Inside Parliament: The Union Record Under the Schedule

The Schedule binds Parliament as it binds the assemblies, and the Union record tests the architectural reading at the level where governments are largest and whips most consequential. The first great test came in November 1990, when the Janata Dal government of V. P. Singh fell on a confidence vote and Chandra Shekhar formed a government sustained by Congress support; sixty-four members of the Janata Dal legislature party in the Lok Sabha went with Chandra Shekhar and claimed protection as a split under the then Paragraph 3, the fraction comfortably exceeding one third, and Speaker Rabi Ray recognised the Janata Dal (Socialist) as a separate group, so that a government of India rested for its four months in office on the split exception operating exactly as drafted. The episode is the Paragraph 3 era in miniature: a crossing that the law's opponents called defection and its defenders called realignment, carried at a scale the text had chosen to protect, and it supplied part of the experience that the Law Commission weighed in 1999 when it recommended deleting the split and merger exemptions altogether. When the deletion came in 2003, Parliament kept the merger door, and the Rajya Sabha supplied its clearest modern instance. On 20 June 2019, four of the six Telugu Desam Party members of the Rajya Sabha resolved to merge their legislature party with the Bharatiya Janata Party; four of six is two thirds, the Chairman, M. Venkaiah Naidu, recognised the merger under Paragraph 4, and the members took their seats with the BJP without facing Paragraph 2, the TDP continuing as a party outside the House and in the Andhra Pradesh Assembly. The transaction was complete in a day, unaccompanied by any organisational merger between the two parties, and it followed the Goa and Telangana recognitions of the same season so closely in grammar that the three cases read as one case in three Houses.

The Lok Sabha's own disqualification record is thinner, and the thinness is informative. Petitions under Paragraph 2 have been filed against members in several Lok Sabhas, arising from confidence votes and from crossings between the smaller parties of coalition arithmetic, and Speakers have decided some and outlasted others with the House's dissolution; no Lok Sabha majority since 1985 has been manufactured by recognised crossings of the kind the Rajya Sabha saw in 2019, a fact the discipline side of the argument claims as the Schedule's success at the level that matters most. The architectural reading accepts the fact and disputes the inference. The Union government's stability after 1985 has many causes, among them the coalition discipline of the period from 1989 to 2014, in which allies held ministries and exits were negotiated between parties rather than executed by members, and the single-party majorities of 2014 and 2019, in which the treasury benches had no need of crossings. What the Schedule demonstrably governed at the Union level was the form of the crossings that did occur: the 1990 split at one third and the 2019 merger at two thirds track the text's thresholds with the same precision as Goa and Telangana, and a Lok Sabha Speaker's recognition of a whip, the controversy that reached the Supreme Court from Maharashtra in 2023, is a question the Union record had left untouched only because no faction with two thirds of a legislature party had yet needed the answer in Delhi. The architecture is national; its stress tests have so far been administered in the states and the upper House, and the text that will be applied when the test arrives in the Lok Sabha is the text this article has read.

## The Whip in Operation: Direction, Permission and Condonation

Paragraph 2(1)(b) turns on a document, the direction, and the case law has had to specify what counts as one. The direction must be issued by the political party or by a person or authority authorised by the party in that behalf, and it must reach the member; a party that cannot prove issuance and communication cannot sustain a disqualification, because the ground punishes disobedience of an instruction and an instruction never received cannot be disobeyed. In Jagjit Singh v State of Haryana, decided on 11 December 2006, the Supreme Court examined disqualifications of Haryana legislators who had been proceeded against after forming and supporting a breakaway group, and it set aside findings where the tribunal's conclusions outran the evidence, insisting that the inference of voluntary relinquishment and the fact of a binding direction are matters to be proved on material, not presumed from political circumstance. The judgment belongs with Ravi Naik in the architecture of Feature One and Feature Two alike: the exit ground is wide in the conduct it can reach and strict in the proof it demands, and Speakers' decisions have fallen in court more often on the proof than on the proposition. Parties have responded institutionally. The whip is issued in writing, served on members, published to the legislature party meeting and preserved, because the party secretariat knows that a Paragraph 2 petition will be tried on the paper trail of the direction.

The vocabulary of whips carries an imported grading that the constitutional text does not recognise. Parliamentary practice speaks of one-line, two-line and three-line whips, gradations of insistence inherited from Westminster procedure, in which the three-line whip marks the divisions on which attendance and obedience are demanded absolutely. The Tenth Schedule knows nothing of the grading: a direction is a direction, and defiance of a direction on an unwhipped-feeling occasion, if the party issued it in the authorised form and declines to condone, attracts the same consequence as defiance on a confidence motion. That flattening is the textual root of the Goswami Committee's complaint and of the Law Commission's recommendation that parties ration their whips; the reform those bodies proposed was directed at party practice as much as at constitutional text, since a party that whips only survival votes achieves by self-restraint the allocation the Goswami amendment would have imposed by law. No major party has adopted the restraint as a standing rule, and the reason is the tension again, visible this time from the leadership's chair: a whip is also an instrument of bargaining inside a coalition and of message discipline before the electorate, and a leadership that unilaterally narrows its own whip surrenders leverage that rival leaderships retain. The two safety valves the text does supply, prior permission and condonation within fifteen days, are both held by the same hand that issues the direction, which is why they function in practice as instruments of graded forgiveness for valued rebels rather than as a member's right: permission is sought before rebellions that the leadership is willing to licence, and condonation is extended after rebellions whose authors the leadership wishes to keep. The fifteen-day window has generated its own minor jurisprudence on when the period runs and whose condonation counts where a legislature party and a political party disagree, a disagreement Subhash Desai's holding on the source of the whip has partially ordered by locating the direction in the political party. A member reading Paragraph 2(1)(b) for guidance on a difficult vote finds, in the end, a single sentence of advice embedded in the text: the seat is held on the party's terms, the terms are the party's to soften in the particular case, and the Constitution offers no third party to the bargain. That is Feature One stated from the member's side of the lobby, and it is the human form of the architectural fact with which this article began.

## The Ledger of the Argument

Set the dated record in a row and the adjudication among the three framings makes itself. In 1969 the Chavan Committee counted the crossings and priced them in ministerial berths. In 1985 a Parliament with a four-hundred-seat majority wrote the response into the Constitution, choosing the unlimited whip, the insider adjudicator and the ouster clause in a single enactment, against objections recorded in its own debates. In 1992 the Supreme Court removed the ouster clause, converted the adjudicator into a tribunal and left both the whip's scope and the adjudicator's chair untouched; the minority's warning about the chair is dated the same day as the majority's settlement. In 2003 Parliament deleted the split door and capped the ministerial prize, and within sixteen years the traffic was passing through the merger door at exactly two thirds in Panaji, Hyderabad and the Rajya Sabha, and through resignation and by-election in Bengaluru's aftermath. In 2020 the Court, faced with a petition left undecided for three years, proposed that Parliament replace the adjudicator altogether, and Parliament did not. In 2023 the Court located the whip in the political party, corrected a Governor, declined to restore a resigned government, sent the Speaker's own disqualification problem to seven judges, and returned the petitions to the Speaker. Every entry in that ledger is a holding, a statute or a dated event; none requires the architectural vocabulary to be true. The vocabulary is required only to see that the entries are one story, the working of a text whose four features distribute advantage among leadership, member, faction and chair in proportions the text itself fixed.

The competing framings may keep their provinces. The descriptive-historical account remains the right way to teach the sequence to a reader meeting the subject for the first time, and the case-law account remains the right way to advise a client whose seat depends on the next petition. The architectural reading claims the remaining province, explanation: why a law amended in the direction of greater severity in 2003 produced crossings of greater scale rather than fewer crossings, why the litigation migrated from the merits of crossings to the conduct and timing of adjudicators, and why the system's own officers, a Chief Justice who had authored the 1992 majority among them, kept returning with proposals to redesign the adjudicator rather than the grounds. A reader who holds the four features in view can place the next defection crisis, in whichever state it arrives, inside the structure before its particulars are known: count the faction against two thirds, find the whip and ask which party organ issued it, watch the Speaker's calendar, and reserve judgment on the merits until the tribunal the Constitution appointed has spoken or failed to speak. The Schedule will supply the rest, as it has since 1 March 1985.

One file in the ledger remains open at the time of the record this article covers. The seven-judge bench contemplated by Subhash Desai has the Nabam Rebia question before it in principle: whether a Speaker facing a removal notice may decide the disqualifications of the very members who will vote on that removal. The answer, whenever it is delivered, will redistribute advantage between adjudicator and accused once more, and it will do so inside Paragraph 6, the feature the Kihoto minority marked in 1992 as the design's weakest joint. The architecture, characteristically, has kept the question for its own text.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the Tenth Schedule?

The Tenth Schedule is the part of the Constitution of India, inserted by the 52nd Amendment in 1985, that disqualifies legislators who defect from their parties. It has eight paragraphs. Paragraph 2 states the grounds, Paragraph 4 protects a merger agreed by two thirds of a legislature party, Paragraph 6 gives the decision to the Speaker or Chairman as a tribunal, and Paragraph 7, which barred the courts, was struck down by the Supreme Court in 1992. Articles 102(2) and 191(2) connect the Schedule to membership of Parliament and the state legislatures.

### Q: What is the anti-defection law?

The anti-defection law is the body of constitutional provisions, centred on the Tenth Schedule, that penalises a legislator for leaving the party on whose ticket the legislator was elected, for joining another party, or for voting against the party's direction without permission. Its stated purpose, drawn from the record of mass defections between 1967 and 1971 that the Chavan Committee documented, is to protect the electorate's verdict and the stability of governments from crossings purchased by office. Its working parts are the disqualification grounds in Paragraph 2, the merger protection in Paragraph 4 and adjudication by the Speaker under Paragraph 6.

### Q: When was the Tenth Schedule introduced?

The Tenth Schedule was introduced by the Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act of 1985. The bill passed the Lok Sabha on 30 January 1985 and the Rajya Sabha on 31 January 1985, received presidential assent on 15 February 1985 and entered into force on 1 March 1985. It was the Rajiv Gandhi government's response to the defection era that began with the 1967 general election, in which the Chavan Committee had counted 438 crossings in a single twelve-month span, and it followed two failed attempts, the bills of 1973 and 1978.

### Q: What is the Kihoto Hollohan verdict?

Kihoto Hollohan v Zachillhu is the Supreme Court Constitution Bench judgment of 18 February 1992 that settled the Tenth Schedule's validity. The majority, Justices Venkatachaliah, Reddy and Agrawal, upheld the Schedule but struck down Paragraph 7, the clause barring court jurisdiction, because it altered Articles 136, 226 and 227 without the state ratification that Article 368 requires, and held Paragraph 7 severable. The majority held that the Speaker deciding defection questions acts as a tribunal whose decisions are open to judicial review on grounds including mala fides, perversity and denial of natural justice. The minority would have struck down the whole amendment.

### Q: What did the 91st Amendment change?

The Constitution (Ninety-first Amendment) Act of 2003, in force from 1 January 2004, deleted Paragraph 3 of the Tenth Schedule, ending the protection for splits claimed by one third of a legislature party, and left the two-thirds merger under Paragraph 4 as the only protected collective crossing. It also capped the Union and state councils of ministers at fifteen per cent of the strength of the lower House through Articles 75(1A) and 164(1A), and it barred a member disqualified for defection from appointment as a minister, or to a remunerative political post under Article 361B, until re-election or the end of the House's term.

### Q: What is the Speaker's role in defection?

Under Paragraph 6 of the Tenth Schedule, the Speaker of a legislative assembly, or the Chairman of a council, and for Parliament the Speaker of the Lok Sabha or Chairman of the Rajya Sabha, decides every question of disqualification for defection referred to that office, and the decision is stated to be final. Since Kihoto Hollohan in 1992, the officeholder decides as a tribunal, not as a party functionary, and the decision can be reviewed by the High Courts and the Supreme Court on limited grounds. The office's party ties and the absence of a time limit in the text have made delay the most contested feature of the role.

### Q: How does the Maharashtra split case relate?

The Maharashtra crisis of 2022 arose when Eknath Shinde led a bloc larger than two thirds of the Shiv Sena legislature party out of the Maha Vikas Aghadi government, which then fell. The dispute put every part of the Tenth Schedule to work at once: competing whips, disqualification petitions before the Speaker in both directions, an Election Commission decision on the party's name and symbol, and a Governor's floor test. The Supreme Court's judgment in Subhash Desai on 11 May 2023 held that the political party, not the legislature party, appoints the whip, that the Governor had lacked objective material for the floor test, and that the Speaker must decide the petitions.

### Q: How is voluntarily giving up membership interpreted by the courts?

The Supreme Court has read the phrase in Paragraph 2(1)(a) widely. In Ravi S. Naik v Union of India, on 9 February 1994, the Court held that voluntarily giving up membership is not the same as resignation and can be inferred from a member's conduct, including speeches, associations and moves against the member's own government. In Dr. Mahachandra Prasad Singh's case in 2004, contesting or campaigning for another party supported the same inference. A formal letter of resignation is therefore unnecessary; the Speaker, as tribunal, assesses conduct, and the courts review that assessment on the Kihoto Hollohan grounds.

### Q: What is the two-thirds merger rule under Paragraph 4?

Paragraph 4 protects a member from disqualification where the member's original political party merges with another party, and Paragraph 4(2) deems such a merger to have taken place if and only if not less than two thirds of the members of the legislature party have agreed to it. The rule measures consent inside the legislature party rather than in the party organisation, which is how ten of fifteen Congress legislators in Goa in July 2019 and twelve of eighteen in Telangana in June 2019 were recognised as merged into other parties by their Speakers, while the parties they left continued to exist outside the House.

### Q: Can a disqualified member contest a by-election for the same House?

Yes, if the disqualification is only from membership. In Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil v Speaker, Karnataka Legislative Assembly, on 13 November 2019, the Supreme Court upheld the disqualification of seventeen Karnataka members under the Tenth Schedule but struck down the Speaker's additional direction barring them from contesting elections for the remainder of the Assembly's term, holding that the Schedule and Article 191 give the Speaker no power to add exclusion from candidacy to loss of the seat. Several of the disqualified members contested the December 2019 by-elections and returned to the Assembly on fresh mandates.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court say in Keisham Meghachandra Singh?

In Keisham Meghachandra Singh v Hon'ble Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly, decided on 21 January 2020, the Supreme Court addressed a disqualification petition that the Manipur Speaker had left undecided for nearly three years while the member concerned, elected on a Congress ticket, served as a minister in a BJP-led government. The Court held that Speakers should ordinarily decide such petitions within three months, restrained the member from entering the Assembly until the decision, and recommended that Parliament consider replacing the Speaker with a permanent tribunal headed by a retired Supreme Court judge or a retired Chief Justice of a High Court.

### Q: What question did Subhash Desai send to a seven-judge bench?

Subhash Desai referred to a bench of seven judges the correctness of Nabam Rebia v Deputy Speaker, the 2016 Constitution Bench ruling that a Speaker against whom a notice of intention to move a resolution for removal is pending is restrained from deciding disqualification petitions under the Tenth Schedule. The 2023 bench described the reasoning behind that restraint as contradictory, since it allows members facing disqualification to disable their adjudicator by filing a removal notice. Until the larger bench answers, the Nabam Rebia position continues to be cited by members on both sides of defection disputes, including in the Maharashtra proceedings themselves.

### Q: What reforms did the Dinesh Goswami Committee propose for defection law?

The Dinesh Goswami Committee on Electoral Reforms, reporting in 1990, proposed two linked changes. It would confine disqualification for defying a party direction to votes on motions of confidence and no-confidence, so that the whip binds a member only where the life of a government is at stake, leaving the member free on other business. And it would move the decision on disqualification away from the Speaker to the President or the Governor acting on the advice of the Election Commission, the channel that Articles 103 and 192 already use for other questions of legislative disqualification. Parliament has enacted neither proposal.

### Q: What did the Law Commission recommend in its 170th Report?

The Law Commission of India's 170th Report, Reform of the Electoral Laws, delivered in 1999, recommended deleting the provisions of the Tenth Schedule that exempt splits and mergers from disqualification, so that collective crossings would receive no protection at any threshold. It recommended treating pre-poll electoral fronts as political parties for the purposes of the anti-defection law, so that a member could not escape discipline by describing an alliance as the member's party. And it recommended that political parties limit the issuance of whips to occasions when the government is in danger. Parliament acted on the split deletion, through the 91st Amendment in 2003, and left the merger exemption in place.

### Q: Who was Gaya Lal and what does Aaya Ram Gaya Ram mean?

Gaya Lal was a member of the Haryana Legislative Assembly elected from Hasanpur in 1967 who crossed from the Congress to the United Front, back to the Congress and across again within a single day. At a Chandigarh press conference the Congress leader Rao Birender Singh presented the episode with the remark that Gaya Lal had become Aaya Lal, and the phrase Aaya Ram Gaya Ram, roughly the one who comes and the one who goes, entered Indian political language as the standing name for floor crossing. His day of switches became the emblem of the defection era that the Chavan Committee documented and the Tenth Schedule was eventually written to end.

### Q: What did the minority judges hold in Kihoto Hollohan?

Chief Justice Lalit Mohan Sharma and Justice J. S. Verma, the minority in Kihoto Hollohan, agreed with the majority that Paragraph 7 changed Articles 136, 226 and 227 and needed state ratification that had never been obtained. They parted on the consequence. Paragraph 7, in their view, was not severable, because the Speaker's finality under Paragraph 6 had been designed together with the exclusion of the courts, so the failure of ratification invalidated the entire 52nd Amendment and the presidential assent was of no effect. They also held that the Speaker is not an independent adjudicatory authority for such disputes, a defect they treated as offending a basic feature of democracy.

### Q: How does the Schedule treat independent and nominated members?

Paragraph 2(2) disqualifies an independent member, elected without a party, who joins any political party after the election; the independent member's mandate is personal, and acquiring a party afterwards is treated as a defection from it. Paragraph 2(3) gives a nominated member six months from the date of taking the seat to join a political party; joining within that window is permitted, and joining after it is a ground of disqualification. A nominated member who joins no party may sit unattached. The two rules together show the Schedule assigning every member a recorded party position, including the position of having none.

### Q: Why can a disqualified member not become a minister straight away?

Articles 75(1B) and 164(1B), inserted by the 91st Amendment in 2003, bar a member disqualified under the Tenth Schedule from appointment as a minister at the Union or in a state until the member is re-elected or the term of the House expires, whichever is earlier, and Article 361B extends the same bar to remunerative political posts. The bar attacks the reward that powered the defections the Chavan Committee counted in 1969, when 116 of 210 defectors in seven states received ministerial office. Before 2003 a disqualified defector could be reappointed a minister immediately upon re-election, and the amendment lengthened and hardened the exclusion.

### Q: Can a court review a Speaker's decision before the Speaker gives it?

Ordinarily no. The majority in Kihoto Hollohan held that judicial review of a Paragraph 6 decision is available after the Speaker decides, on grounds such as mala fides, perversity, violation of constitutional mandate and denial of natural justice, and that courts should not intervene at an interlocutory stage, because a petition pending before the Speaker is a proceeding before a tribunal. The Court recognised a narrow exception for exceptional situations where an immediate and irreversible consequence is threatened, described through the quia timet principle. In practice the bar on early review is why litigants press the distinct complaint that a Speaker has failed to decide at all, the Rajendra Singh Rana route.

### Q: What happens when a member is expelled by the party?

Expulsion does not free the member. In G. Viswanathan v Hon'ble Speaker, Tamil Nadu Legislative Assembly in 1996, the Supreme Court held that a member expelled by the party remains, for the purposes of the Tenth Schedule, a member of the original political party, and that if the expelled member joins another party the member is deemed to have voluntarily given up membership of the original party and is liable to disqualification. Critics answer that the holding lets a leadership expel a dissenter and still bind the dissenter's seat, while defenders reply that any other rule would let parties stage expulsions to free members for crossings with their seats intact.
