Tsubouchi Shōyō translated the lovers' tragedy in 1910, in the middle of the most consequential translation enterprise in the history of Japanese literature, and the date matters more than the play. His version of Romeo and Juliet belongs to the fourth of the five stages into which he later divided his own practice, the stage he called the mixture of literary and spoken styles, and it carries the marks of a method still being invented. The verdict of this article is that Tsubouchi's Romeo and Juliet should be judged as a national-literature act rather than as a finished translation: it is the play that taught Japan that Shakespeare could be Japanese literature, even though the version itself needed successors to become speakable on the modern stage. The evidence for that verdict is the enterprise around the single play, forty volumes of the complete works published between 1909 and 1928, a theatre society founded to stage them, a museum built to house them, and a quarrel about their language that lasted a century. The play's own translation note, recorded by the Shōyō association, is the most quoted sentence in Japanese Shakespeare translation studies: the play's translation begs stylistic license. That confession, read against the five stages, is the article's key. Tsubouchi admitted, in print, that the lovers' scenes resisted the dignified register he had built for the tragedies of state, and the admission is more instructive than any praise of his achievement, because it shows a translator learning, in public, what his language could not yet do.

## Tsubouchi the Translator Before the Translation

Tsubouchi Shōyō was born in 1859, in the last decade of the old order, and died in 1935, in the first decade of the militarist state that would ban much of what he stood for. His real name was Tsubouchi Yūzō, and he wrote criticism and fiction under the pen name Harunoya Oboro before the name Shōyō became, in the scholarship, the name of an institution. He entered the University of Tokyo and graduated in 1883, and he joined the staff of the Tokyo Senmon Gakkō, later Waseda University, where he taught for the rest of his working life and where he founded the literature department and the journal Waseda Bungaku. His first literary fame came not from Shakespeare but from theory. Shōsetsu shinzui, published in instalments in 1885 and 1886, argued that the novel was a serious art whose essence lay in human feeling and social manners rather than in moral instruction, and it attacked the didactic fiction of the old order for subordinating art to preaching. The companion novel, Tōsei shosei katagi, published in the same years, demonstrated the theory in the lives of contemporary students. The argument of Shōsetsu shinzui matters for the translation history because it states, before any Shakespeare translation existed in his name, the programme that the translations would serve: Japanese literature needed forms that could carry lived feeling without didactic machinery, and foreign models, studied rather than imitated, were the means of building them.

The move from the novel to the theatre followed the same programme. In 1886 Tsubouchi joined the Engeki Kairyōkai, the Theatre Reform Society, which argued that the Japanese stage needed dramatic coherence of the kind Shakespeare exemplified, and he spent the following decades arguing that reform meant discipline rather than decoration. His early Shakespeare work was classroom work. When he began teaching, he translated parts of Hamlet, the first scene of the first act, and parts of Macbeth, the first two acts, for his students, and he translated two novels by Walter Scott in his early twenties, The Bride of Lammermoor and The Lady of the Lake, under Japanese titles that advertised their strangeness as romance. The Scott translations show the young Tsubouchi already working at the boundary between foreign story and native idiom, and the classroom Shakespeare fragments show him testing, on students, how much of the English text a Japanese sentence could carry. He was, in the phrase later used by the scholarship, a man of the theatre from the start, and the translations were instruments of reform before they were monuments of literature. That ordering explains the shape of everything that followed: the complete works were not a retirement project but the central act of a reformer's life, and the Romeo and Juliet of 1910 was made inside a theatre project, for a stage that did not yet exist in the form its translator wanted.

## The Field He Entered: Shakespeare in Japan Before Tsubouchi

Shakespeare reached Japan as part of the great importation of Western learning that followed the Meiji Restoration of 1868, and the first encounters were oblique. The translator Nakamura Masanao, sent to Britain by the old Tokugawa government to study English, published Japanese versions of Samuel Smiles's Self-Help in 1871 and John Stuart Mill's On Liberty in 1872, and the first chapter of Self-Help contained a brief life of Shakespeare while its head quotations included Polonius's advice to Laertes, "Neither a borrower nor a lender be, for loan oft loses both itself and friend, and borrowing dulls the edge of husbandry," from Hamlet, act 1, scene 3. Shakespeare thus entered Japan as a successful man of the theatre who gave practical advice to young people, a figure of self-improvement rather than of poetry. The first lines of Hamlet's great soliloquy in Japanese print appeared in 1874, in the Yokohama periodical Japan Punch, under a sketch of a samurai in traditional costume standing in a pondering posture on a stage, the work attributed to Charles Wirgman, the painter sent to Japan by the Illustrated London News. The translation, in roman letters and in a word-for-word method later scholars have called childish and nonsensical, may have been parody or may have been limited command of Japanese; either way, it shows how far the field was from a usable Shakespeare in the first decade of contact.

The first attempts at whole plays followed the same fork that would define the tradition: render the foreign text word for word, or dress it in the native stage's clothing. An adaptation of Hamlet in kabuki style was attempted in 1875 by Kanagaki Robun and abandoned for unpopularity. The stories from Charles and Mary Lamb's Tales from Shakespeare were introduced by several hands in the early 1880s, and the anthology Shintaishishō of 1882, a landmark in the renovation of Japanese poetry, included four Shakespeare extracts, among them two versions of Hamlet's soliloquy. The first complete translation of a single play was Kawashima Keizō's Julius Caesar, serialized in 1883 in the newspaper of a democratic political party, the Kenseitō Shinbun, the translator working, according to the scholar Sasaburō Moriya, with Webster's English dictionary and the Schlegel-Tieck German edition at hand. That detail links the Japanese beginning to the German classic examined in the companion study of [Schlegel-Tieck: The German Romeo and Juliet](/2030/04/26/schlegel-tieck-german-translation/): the first Japanese Shakespeare was made with the German Shakespeare open on the desk. Kawashima rendered the lovers' tragedy in the mid-1880s in the jōruri manner, the chant-narrative style of the puppet theatre, under the floating-world title Shunjō ukiyo no yume, a romantic dream of the floating world, and the play entered Japanese print dressed in the verbal clothing of the native love-suicide repertory. A further version by Tozawa Kōya and Asano Wasaburō followed in 1905. The first production of a Shakespeare play by Japanese actors, as Tsubouchi himself recorded it, was an adaptation of The Merchant of Venice made from Lamb's Tales, staged in 1885 by the Nakamura Sōjūrō company at the Ebisuza Theatre in Osaka. Two scenes from Tsubouchi's own Caesar, the assassination in act 3, scene 1 and the forum speeches in act 3, scene 2, were staged in 1901 by the Ii company at the Meijiza Theatre in Tokyo. Kawakami Otojirō, the reforming entrepreneur, staged Japanized adaptations of Othello, The Merchant of Venice, and Hamlet in Tokyo in 1903, with his wife Sadayakko playing Desdemona and Ophelia. By the time Tsubouchi published his Caesar, the field had tested both methods, word for word and native manner, and had produced no complete text that could stand as both faithful and actable. That vacancy is the space his enterprise filled.

## Stage One: The Sword of Liberty and the Jōruri Solution

Tsubouchi's first complete Shakespeare translation, Julius Caesar, published in 1884, a year after Kawashima's version of the same play, announced its politics in its title. Where Kawashima had called his version plainly a European drama of Julius Caesar, Tsubouchi gave his the kabuki-style title Shīzaru kidan: Jiyū no tachi nagori no kireaji, a strange tale of Caesar, the remembered sharpness of the sword of liberty. The democratic movement of 1883 and 1884 made Caesar exactly the play for the time, and Tsubouchi's title advertised the early Japanese Shakespeare as a dramatist of freedom against feudal order, in the idiom of the popular-rights movement. The language of the translation was the traditional jōruri style, narrative verse and dialogue mixed in the manner of the puppet theatre, and Tsubouchi's preface defended the choice with a candour that became his signature. The original, he wrote, was only a rough script of dialogues without any songs to be chanted, very different from the traditional Japanese jōruri text, and he had therefore transformed the original into jōruri form. Differences and changes from Shakespeare's text would be found if the two were compared word by word or line by line, but his first aim was the convenience of his Japanese readers, so that they would have no difficulty in enjoying the play. Narrative verse style was used when it was effective and dialogue style when it was more appropriate, and the hardest efforts had been made to convey the meaning of the original text. The preface is a founding document of Japanese translation theory because it states, without apology, a hierarchy that Tsubouchi would spend fifty years revising: the reader's ease first, the original's meaning second, the original's wording nowhere in the ranking. The scholar Akiko Sano, whose chronology of Shakespeare translation in Japan remains the standard reference for the sequence, notes that Kawashima himself was influenced by Tsubouchi's example and rendered his next work, the lovers' tragedy, in the jōruri style, giving up the word-for-word method that might have seemed more appropriate. The jōruri solution, in other words, was contagious, and it set the terms of the problem for a generation: a literal rendering of blank verse into a language without the same metrical resources would produce a text neither actable nor readable, and every translator had to choose how much of the native stage to admit.

The choice carried a political charge that the later romance reputation of the lovers' play obscures. A story of young people crushed by an inherited order's marriage policy and street code was available, from the first Japanese printings, as an argument about the old house system itself, and the Caesar title shows that Tsubouchi understood Shakespeare as a weapon in the domestic argument about modernization before he understood him as a poet. The distinction matters for judging the 1910 Romeo and Juliet, because the play's Japanese career began as politics and only later became literature. Tsubouchi's Caesar was a free translation in seven-and-five-syllable verse, the metre of the jōruri chant, and he described it himself, in the retrospective he published in 1928, as a loose free translation in that metre, like the jōruri text. The description is retrospective self-criticism, and it opens the five-stage account that is the best evidence for how his method changed. The first stage solved the problem of access by surrendering the problem of form, and the surrender was honest, public, and temporary. What followed was a fifty-year attempt to take the surrender back, stage by stage, without losing the audience the surrender had won.

## The Five Stages: Tsubouchi's Own Account of His Method

In 1928, in the fortieth and final volume of his Shakespeare series, an introductory guidebook to Shakespeare study, Tsubouchi looked back over more than forty years of translation and divided his approach into five stages. The account, preserved in the standard scholarship, is the single most important document in the history of Japanese Shakespeare translation, because it is a translator's own audit of his changing method, and because the lovers' tragedy has a precise address inside it. The first stage was the Caesar of 1884, the loose free translation in seven-and-five verse in the jōruri manner. The second stage belonged to the middle of the 1890s, when his lectures on the plays were printed in the journals of Waseda University: for the benefit of students he tried word-for-word translation in prose with plentiful notes, in the archaic literary style then in fashion, and he later found that the style was not suitable to convey the sentiment of the original text. The third stage belonged to the productions of his theatre society in 1908 and 1909, when his idea of a good play was a renovated Japanese drama and he adopted the traditional languages of the stage, the seven-and-five metre, the kabuki text style, and the kyōgen speech style of the classical nō tradition. While still translating for the society, he learned that Lafcadio Hearn, the interpreter of Japan to the West and a lecturer at Tokyo University, used to say that a Japanese translation of Shakespeare should be in the spoken language, and he decided to try it. Because he could not give up completely the old delicate literary style, the result was a mixture of the two, the fourth stage, which he called the mixture of literary and spoken styles, and in order to harmonize the two he was obliged to choose rather new literary and rather old colloquial languages. Hamlet in 1909, Romeo and Juliet in 1910, and Othello in 1911 were translated in this style. The fifth stage followed: the old colloquial language of the mixture had reminded readers and audiences of the age when it was spoken, and he turned instead to the exact contemporary spoken language, used first for King Lear in 1912 and for the retranslated Julius Caesar, and eventually for everything after. He argued that this was no easy task, because the language of translation must not suggest any particular modern Japanese way of life, neither the middle-class family in the residential area nor the traditional townspeople downtown nor the fashionable streets of the Ginza at night, and he devised a special sort of spoken language for translation, fairly free from such associations.

The periodization is the strongest evidence the article possesses, and it disciplines every judgment that follows. The Romeo and Juliet of 1910 is a fourth-stage text, made before the fifth-stage solution existed, and it must be judged as the record of a problem being worked, not as a failed example of a solved one. The mixture was deliberate, not confusion: Tsubouchi chose a newer literary register and an older colloquial register and forced them to coexist, because neither alone could do the play's work. The literary register carried the sonnets, the aubades, the formal rhetoric of the fathers, and the public scenes of law and feud. The colloquial register carried the servants, the Nurse, the street brawls, and the bawdy. Shakespeare's play is itself a mixture of registers, the shared sonnet of the first meeting in act 1, scene 5 rising out of a hall full of servingmen carrying dishes, and Juliet's epithalamium in act 3, scene 2 answering Mercutio's Queen Mab in act 1, scene 4, so the translator's mixture answered a real property of the original rather than a weakness in his technique. The cost of the mixture was coherence: an audience could hear the join between the two languages, and the join was audible exactly where the play itself changes gear, which means the translation reproduced the play's structure at the price of sounding like two texts. That price is the heart of the idiom question the article examines.

### What did Tsubouchi mean by calling his fourth stage a mixture?

Tsubouchi meant that he had set two historical layers of Japanese inside one text, a newer literary language for the high passages and an older spoken language for the low ones, and had forced them to work together because neither could carry the whole play alone.

Hamlet in 1909, Romeo and Juliet in 1910, and Othello in 1911 are his own examples of the method, and he abandoned it for the contemporary spoken language of the fifth stage only after finding that the older colloquial reminded audiences of a bygone age. The mixture was a bridge built while he crossed it.

The five stages also explain why the Tsubouchi text kept changing. A translator who redefines his method every decade must revise or retranslate the early plays, and the Caesar that closed the circle, retranslated in the fifth-stage language and then revised again for the collected edition of 1933 to 1935, differs from the Caesar of 1884 so completely that the dramatist and translator Kinoshita Junji called the difference the record of a laborious journey. Kinoshita added that his own generation, the translators who came after the war, could start from where Tsubouchi had arrived, from the fifth stage itself. The sentence is the most generous and the most exact verdict in the literature: the enterprise is valued not for the texts it fixed but for the ground it cleared. Romeo and Juliet, standing in the fourth stage, is part of the journey rather than the arrival, and its defenders and its critics have always been arguing about which of those two things a translation ought to be.

## The Play in the Mixture Stage: Lyric, Bawdy, and Stylistic License

The lovers' tragedy was the hardest test the mixture stage faced, and Tsubouchi knew it. The Shōyō association's encyclopedia preserves his production notes on every play, dates of translation and first staging appended, and for this play the note is a single clause: the play's translation begs stylistic license. The sentence rewards unpacking. License, in a translator who had begun by confessing that his Caesar would not survive a word-for-word comparison, is not a confession of carelessness; it is a claim that the play demands freedom as a condition of fidelity. The reason lies in the play's verbal makeup. Romeo and Juliet is the most lyric of the tragedies, built from sonnets, aubades, and set speeches of desire, and it is threaded with the punning, prose, and bawdy that belong to the street and the household. The Prologue's sonnet, "From forth the fatal loins of these two foes a pair of star-crossed lovers take their life," fixes the story in fourteen lines of formal verse. The lovers' first exchange is itself a sonnet shared between two speakers, the pilgrim conceit in act 1, scene 5, "If I profane with my unworthiest hand this holy shrine," in which Juliet matches Romeo rhyme for rhyme and completes his quatrains. Juliet's soliloquy at the opening of act 3, scene 2, "Gallop apace, ye fiery-footed steeds towards Phoebus' lodging," is an epithalamium compressed into a command to the sun. Her line in the orchard scene, "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo," asks why he is Romeo, why he bears the name, and the rose speech that follows, "What is in a name? That which we call a rose by any other name would smell as sweet," builds the play's philosophy of language out of a garden metaphor. Against this lyric architecture stand the servants' opening bawdy in act 1, scene 1, the Nurse's garrulous prose in act 1, scene 3, and Mercutio's wit, which turns on English wordplay at the speed of improvisation. A Japanese translator in 1910 had no single register that contained both poles, because the Japanese literary language of the period had not finished deciding what modern speech was.

Tsubouchi's other notes show that he felt the problem as a property of the plays rather than of his own limits. Love's Labour's Lost, whose language games he thought made it the hardest of all the plays to translate, posed the same problem at maximum density. The Tempest, which he called hugely influential in his theories of translation, drew from him the principle that Shakespeare's uniqueness lies in his language rather than in what he actually says, and that the play is half like a musical drama, so that to disregard its musical qualities would be as good as killing it. That principle, applied to the lovers' tragedy, is the strongest defense of the stylistic license he claimed. The mixture stage tried to keep the music by assigning it to the literary register, while the spoken register took the commerce of the street and the house. The attempt succeeded as structure and failed as surface: the sonnet frame survived, the formal rhetoric survived, and the fathers' public grief in act 5, scene 3 had a language of adequate dignity, while the play's speed, its jokes, and its erotic urgency had to live in a colloquial that already smelled of an earlier decade. Later critics would call the result archaic. The archaism, as the translation scholar Daniel Gallimore has insisted, was intended: Tsubouchi had always intended his translations to sound archaic, a choice that made them classics in the receiving language by giving them the patina of classics, at the cost of a built-in expiry date as the language moved on.

### Why did the lovers' scenes resist the register he had built?

The register had been built for the tragedies and histories of state, where the dominant notes are public rhetoric, deliberation, and command, and the lovers' scenes run on different fuel: lyric compression, erotic impatience, and the intimate duet of two speakers completing each other's verse.

A dignified classicizing style could carry the Prologue's sonnet and the Prince's judgments, but the shared sonnet of the first meeting and Juliet's epithalamium asked for a language of desire that the public register could not supply, and the bawdy that frames those scenes asked for a street idiom the dignified register could not admit. Tsubouchi's own phrase for the situation, that the translation begs stylistic license, concedes the resistance and claims the freedom to answer it.

The grading of the 1910 text follows from the evidence. As a stage text for the modern theatre that Japan eventually built, the mixture-stage Romeo and Juliet was transitional, and the postwar record, examined later in this article, displaced it. As a reading text for a nation still assembling its modern literary language, it was a foundation document, because it proved that the whole play, not an adaptation of it, could exist in Japanese print at full length with its architecture intact. The article's verdict holds both halves together: the translation is a classic of Japanese literature whose classical status in the theatre expired, and the expiry was a function of its strategy, not a refutation of it. A translation that aims to sound like a classic will be superseded by the language it helped to create, and that is what happened to Tsubouchi's lovers between 1910 and 1960.

## The Theatre Project: The Bungei Kyōkai and the Staging of Complete Texts

The translations were made for a stage, and the stage was built to receive them. In 1906 Tsubouchi and his disciple Shimamura Hōgetsu established the Bungei Kyōkai, the Literary Arts Association, out of a drama club of Waseda University students, and the society became the first modern theatrical company in Japan. Its leader had begun with the reform of the drama as the goal and Shakespeare as the instrument, and the association threw its energy into training actors and improving the drama. For its first public performance at the Kabukiza Theatre, Tsubouchi translated the court scene of The Merchant of Venice and supplied two works of his own, the historical play Kiri hitoha, a paulownia leaf, and the musical play Tokoyami, the everlasting darkness, which required more than one hundred and twenty performers including a chorus of seventy singers. The programme for the following year consisted of Hamlet, in five acts with only some scenes cut, and a new musical play of his own. The Hamlet performance entered literature as well as theatre history, because the novelist Natsume Sōseki, the greatest Japanese prose writer of the age, put it into his novel Sanshirō, published in 1908, where the hero goes to see the production and is disturbed by exactly the problem the five stages describe: the language is good Japanese, with rhythm, fluency, and dignity, but Hamlet does not seem to be speaking like an ordinary Japanese man, and the discrepancy between the language and the action unsettles him. The sharpest early criticism of the mixture-stage method reached the public disguised as fiction, from the pen of the one contemporary whose judgment Tsubouchi could not dismiss.

In February 1909 Tsubouchi took direct charge of the association, building a theatre academy on the site of his own residence, a two-year coeducational programme with himself and Hōgetsu among the instructors, using the English text of The Merchant of Venice and an English translation of Ibsen's A Doll's House as textbooks. The academy embodied his vision of a national theatre as the association's ultimate goal. In 1911 the society produced his complete translation of Hamlet at the newly built Imperial Theatre in Tokyo, and the production is regarded as the landmark at which the modern Japanese theatre begins in earnest: the first complete production of a complete Japanese translation of a Shakespeare play, directed by the translator himself, with all the female parts played by actresses for the first time in the new drama, and Matsui Sumako, his Ophelia, becoming the first popular actress of the modern stage. The house was full through the week's run, and the production was a commercial success as well as an artistic manifesto. The historian Brian Powell, in the standard account of that production, sets its importance in the discipline it imposed: the faithful text, completely rendered, rehearsed as literature and played as realism. Sōseki saw the 1911 production too, and this time he wrote his criticism for a newspaper, under the title that named the combatants, "Dr. Tsubouchi and Hamlet," and the judgment was severe. It was essentially impossible, Sōseki wrote, to translate Shakespeare's blank verse into Japanese in spite of Tsubouchi's serious efforts, and a production of a Shakespeare play in Japanese would fail to give an audience artistic satisfaction. The quarrel is examined with the other critics later in this article; its place in the sequence is here, because the 1911 Hamlet was staged one year after the Romeo and Juliet translation of 1910, in the same fourth-stage language, and the lovers' play therefore belongs to the moment when the complete-text method won its public victory and received its most famous rebuke at the same time. The association dissolved in 1913, undone by differences of opinion among its members, and Hōgetsu left with Matsui Sumako to form a company of their own. Freed from the duties of leadership, Tsubouchi devoted himself to the translation itself, completing twenty plays by 1923 and then enlarging the project to cover the whole canon. The translators' theatre had lasted seven years; the translation it served lasted the rest of his life.

### How did the 1911 Hamlet change the terms on which the lovers' play would be judged?

The 1911 Hamlet proved that a complete Shakespeare translation could hold a metropolitan stage for a week, played by trained actors and actresses in a realistic manner, and it therefore shifted the question about any Tsubouchi text from whether a complete rendering was possible to how well the rendering spoke.

The Romeo and Juliet of 1910 stood or fell by the same standard the Hamlet had set in public, which is why Sōseki's attack on the blank-verse problem struck the lovers' play as directly as it struck the Danish tragedy: both were fourth-stage texts, and both had to answer for the mixture of literary and spoken language in front of an audience that had just seen what the mixture could and could not do.

## The Complete Works: Forty Volumes, a Museum, and a Life's Revision

The enterprise that gives the 1910 Romeo and Juliet its weight is bibliographical as much as theatrical. The complete works appeared in forty volumes between 1909 and 1928, the first volume published jointly by Fuzanbō and Waseda University Press in December 1909, the remaining volumes by Waseda University Press, the series titled first the masterpieces of Shakespeare and then, from the twenty-fourth volume, the complete works, the earlier volumes retitled to match. The fortieth and last volume, published in December 1928, was the introductory guidebook to Shakespeare study that contains the five-stage retrospective, which means the enterprise closed with its own audit, the translator standing at the end of forty volumes and explaining, stage by stage, how his method had changed on the way. The contents were the thirty-seven plays and three books of poetry, and well over half of the plays, the Shōyō association's record notes, were translated into Japanese for the first time by Tsubouchi himself. The chronology inside the enterprise is worth holding steady, because summary accounts compress it into a single feat. Hamlet opened the series in 1909, in the mixture style. The lovers' tragedy followed in 1910, Othello in 1911, King Lear in 1912 in the new spoken style, The Merchant of Venice reaching print in the series in 1914 after an earlier version in 1906, and the work ground on through the histories and the late romances until Henry VI and Henry VIII closed the canon in 1928. His purpose, stated in his own writings, reached beyond the national audience: Shakespeare in Japanese was a means of improving the skill of Japanese actors, and he hoped that a Japanese Shakespeare of sufficient standard would one day be studied by foreign researchers. The complete works were therefore designed as an export of competence as well as an import of literature.

The completion was commemorated in architecture. In October 1928 Waseda University opened the Tsubouchi Memorial Theatre Museum, known as the Enpaku, on its Tokyo campus, founded to mark the professor's seventieth birthday and the completion of the translation of all forty volumes, a task to which he had devoted half his life. The building, designed by the architect Kenji Imai, was modelled on the Fortune Playhouse in London, the playhouse of Shakespeare's own period, so that the enterprise received an Elizabethan shell in the middle of Tokyo, and the museum grew into the comprehensive theatre archive of Asia, holding his library, his collections of prints and costumes and puppets, and the records of the stage he had spent a lifetime reforming. The building argues the verdict of this article in brick: what Japan chose to commemorate in 1928 was not a play but a translation, and not a translation but an enterprise, the making of a national Shakespeare by labour over decades. Tsubouchi did not rest at the commemoration. He continued revising the translations until his death, and the revised edition, published play by play between 1933 and 1935 by Chūō Kōronsha in forty books, remains his final version; he died on 28 February 1935, with the revision essentially complete around him. The revising habit is the last piece of evidence for the article's judgment. A translator who believed his texts were finished does not spend his seventies redoing them, and the five stages were not a memoir but a working method: each play existed in the stage of the language he had reached when he made it, and the enterprise as a whole is the diary of a language being modernized by the act of translation itself.

### Why does the museum matter to a judgment of the translation?

The museum matters because it records what the receiving culture thought it had received: not forty volumes of foreign literature in Japanese dress, but the foundation of a national theatre, housed in a building copied from the playhouse of Shakespeare's London and filled with the archive of the Japanese stage.

A translation that gets a museum is being treated as an act of state in the republic of letters, and the judgment of Tsubouchi's Romeo and Juliet has to reckon with that institutional fact alongside the linguistic one. The version of 1910 sits inside the enterprise the museum commemorates, and its value cannot be separated from the shelf it stands on.

## The Quarrel Over His Language: Sōseki, Fukuda, and the Defense

The disagreement the article must adjudicate has three fronts, and each front has a named champion. The first is the contemporary attack. Natsume Sōseki, who had studied English literature in London on the first Japanese national scholarship in the field, judged the 1911 Hamlet production in a newspaper and concluded that Shakespeare's blank verse was essentially impossible to translate into Japanese, so that a Japanese production could not give its audience artistic satisfaction whatever the translator's efforts. The attack is the strongest form of the impossibility argument: not that Tsubouchi had failed, but that the task had. Sōseki's position in the translation debate of the period was consistent, that Shakespeare should be accommodated by Japanese culture rather than Japanese culture reshaped to accommodate Shakespeare, and the Sanshirō passage of 1908, in which the hero admires the diction's rhythm, fluency, and dignity while feeling that the speaker does not talk like a Japanese man, is the attack in embryo. The second front is the postwar repudiation. Fukuda Tsuneari, the critic and director whose own translations began with the Hamlet of 1955 at the Bungakuza company, built his practice on a declared difference from Tsubouchi, and Sano's account preserves his diagnosis in detail. Fukuda admitted that Tsubouchi's translation had good rhythm and vigour. The main reason it seemed outdated, he concluded, was that Tsubouchi had imitated the kabuki accent too much and had copied the plain colloquial language of the townspeople of his day. Against that double fault Fukuda set devices of his own: speeches are the most important element of a Shakespeare play, a conviction he took from watching Richard Burton's Hamlet at the Old Vic in 1954, and the translator's task was to reproduce the power of the lines, to raise up the Japanese language, which tends to lie flat. He preserved the word order of the original where Japanese idiom would reverse it, because a speech accompanies the speaker's physical movements and a changed word order changes the order of actions. He ended sentences with nouns for strength, borrowed the written style and even archaisms where they sounded more poetic on stage, and held that stage speech should differ from daily language. His translations, published play by play from 1955 to 1974, were used at most Shakespeare productions in Japan from the later 1950s into the early 1970s, and every one of them was performed at least once.

The third front is the scholarly defense, and it is as documented as the attacks. Daniel Gallimore, the translation scholar whose surveys of the Japanese tradition are the standard English-language accounts, argues that the archaism of the Tsubouchi texts was a deliberate strategy: he had always intended the translations to sound archaic, and they fell out of favour as the classical inflexions and vocabulary of Japanese were gradually replaced before and after 1945, until the differences had become impracticable, although archaism, Gallimore adds, may still be a valid strategy for a translator in context. Kinoshita Junji, the translator of the postwar generation, supplies the practitioner's version of the same defense: the Tsubouchi Shakespeare functioned as the standard translation for many years until about 1960, and his spoken style does not seem too outdated even in later judgment, probably because of the artificial elements he invented, the deliberate stage language free of any district's associations. Tetsuo Kishi, whose Shakespeare in Japan, written with Graham Bradshaw, opens the standard history of the subject with Tsubouchi's greatness, treats the enterprise as the founding act of the field: the translation and the criticism together created the discipline that could later criticize them. Anzai Tetsuo's account of the tradition adds the frame that reconciles the fronts: the history of Japanese Shakespeare translation is a dialectical series of responses to the circumstances of the receiving culture, one translator superseding another as those circumstances change, so that supersession is the normal metabolism of the tradition rather than a verdict of failure.

The adjudication follows. Against Sōseki, the record rules for Tsubouchi: the impossibility claim was falsified by the event, since the complete texts were made, staged, sold, studied, and argued with for a century, and a translation tradition that begins from his fifth stage, in Kinoshita's words, is the empirical refutation of the claim that the task could not be done. Sōseki is honored on the narrower point his fiction made better than his journalism: the language of the mixture stage did not sound like a Japanese person speaking, and the lovers' scenes, which live by sounding like two people speaking to each other in the dark, paid for that fault first. Against Fukuda's followers, where they treat the displacement of the Tsubouchi text as proof of its failure, the record rules for Gallimore and Kinoshita: the archaism was intended, the expiry was linguistic history rather than artistic collapse, and the artificial spoken style of the fifth stage was itself the platform the postwar translators inherited. Against Tsubouchi's celebrants, where they treat the enterprise as a finished monument, the record rules for Fukuda on the stage question that mattered most to the translator himself: speeches are the action, a text that actors cannot drive their bodies with has failed the purpose for which Tsubouchi made it, and his own five stages admit as much, since each stage revised the last in the direction of speakability. The Romeo and Juliet of 1910 stands exactly at the crossing of the three judgments: impossible by Sōseki's rule and existent in fact, displaced by Fukuda's rule and foundational by Kinoshita's, a classic by Gallimore's account of its intended archaism and a period piece by the same account's admission that archaism expires. The article's verdict is that the foundation judgment governs, because the other two judgments presuppose the ground the translation laid: there was a Japanese stage standard to displace, and a Japanese reading public to find the text dated, only because Tsubouchi had built both.

### What survives of Tsubouchi inside the translations that replaced him?

The ground plan survives: the expectation that a Japanese Shakespeare is a complete text rather than an adaptation, the discipline of translating for the stage and the study together, and the fifth-stage principle of a stage language built to carry no single district or class association, which postwar translators inherited as their starting point.

The specific diction does not survive, because Japanese changed around it, and the succession Fukuda, Odashima, Matsuoka is best read as maintenance of his institution under new linguistic conditions. Kinoshita's concession, that his generation started where Tsubouchi had arrived, is the precise measure of the survival: the method outlived the wording.

## Three Translators, Three Bargains: Schlegel, Pasternak, Tsubouchi

The Japanese case completes a triptych that the global reception framework has been assembling. August Wilhelm Schlegel's German Romeo and Juliet of 1797 made the play a German classic by smoothing it, planing the bawdy, regularizing the wit, and letting the receiving language's dignity stand in for the original's roughness, and the German tradition absorbed the bargain so completely that the translation itself became the classic. [Pasternak's Russian Romeo and Juliet](/2030/05/03/pasternak-russian-translation/), made in the early 1940s, turned the play into a Russian poem by freedom, dissolving the Prologue's star-crossed machinery and the servants' punning into a poet's own idiom, under a regime that treated translation as one of the few remaining rooms in which a poet could work. Tsubouchi's Japanese version made its bargain with a language under construction. His receiving tongue had no settled modern prose, no agreed stage speech, and no complete Shakespeare of any kind, so the translation had to be simultaneously a text, a theatre programme, and an argument about what Japanese literature was going to be. The three cases share the pattern the series has recorded in the debate over modern English versions: translation is celebrated where the alternative is absence, and interrogated where the alternative is the original itself, and in Japan the alternative was absence, which is why Tsubouchi's freedoms, like Schlegel's smoothing and Pasternak's liberties, were absorbed without a heresy trial. The difference is one of degree that becomes a difference of kind. Schlegel translated into a literary language at its classical height and Pasternak into one with centuries of poetry behind it; Tsubouchi translated into a language whose modern form his own generation was inventing, under the banner of the unification of spoken and written Japanese, and every one of his five stages is a negotiation with that unfinished business. Fukuda's later charge, that the unification movement was wrong for dramatic language and that stage speech must be allowed its own elevation, is the argument's continuation by other means, and it could only be made inside a tradition that Tsubouchi had founded. Where 425's frame has the Japanese translator standing as one of the foreign precedents for translation as a national-literature event, the record of the Romeo and Juliet of 1910 specifies the mechanism: the event was a forty-volume enterprise with a theatre society attached, and the play that begged stylistic license was the enterprise's confession that the event cost something at the level of the line.

## The Afterlife: Standard, Displacement, and the Verdict

The afterlife confirms the verdict at every checkpoint. Sano's survey records that Tsubouchi's Shakespeare was long the standard translation both for performance and for reading, and the proof she offers is theatrical and poignant: A Midsummer Night's Dream was performed at the Imperial Theatre in Tokyo in June 1946 as the first Shakespeare production after the Second World War, in his translation, and the scholar Rokurō Hidaka remembered the happiness of hearing the humorous speeches on stage and watching an elderly couple, not familiar with the plays, laugh heartily at them. The standard held, on Kinoshita's estimate, until about 1960, fifteen years into the postwar settlement and five years into Fukuda's ascendancy, and the overlap matters: displacement was a handover, not a collapse, with the older text still serving professional and amateur companies while the new acting texts took the metropolitan stages. Fukuda's translations, issued from 1955 to 1974, became the acting standard of the modern Japanese stage, exactly as the series' survey of the play on Japanese stages records. Odashima Yūshi, from the 1970s, adopted a more relaxed and contemporary style, first prepared for a small experimental theatre and soon spread into commercial theatres and the reading public. Matsuoka Kazuko, from the later 1990s, carried the tradition into the Ninagawa era with translations made inside the rehearsal process. The two successions repay a closer look, because they show the dialectic working at the level of practice rather than slogan. Odashima, who had trained as an engineer before the theatre claimed him, made his versions in the 1970s for the director Deguchi Norio and the small experimental stage, aiming at a language immediate and poetic enough to communicate the feeling of the original as well as its meaning, and the charge brought against him, recorded by the critic Anthony Pinnington, was the inverse of the charge against the founder: not that the text was too hard, but that it made Shakespeare too easy, leaving too little to the audience's imagination. Matsuoka, whose versions began appearing from Chikuma Shobō in 1998 under Ninagawa Yukio's Sai no Kuni Shakespeare Series, works inside the rehearsal room itself, attending rehearsals and adjusting the translation when it does not sound right in the actors' mouths, a method that answers, sixty years on, the exact complaint Sōseki's Sanshirō lodged against the mixture stage, that the words did not sit like speech in a speaker. She is also the first woman to attempt the complete works, after Oyama Toshiko's academic translations of nine plays in the 1960s and 1970s, and the line from the founder's classroom fragments to her rehearsal-room method is the tradition's whole history in one contrast: the text began as the professor's instrument and ended as the company's. Read through Anzai's dialectic, the succession is the tradition working as designed: each translator answers the linguistic circumstances of a changed Japan, and the founder's text retires into the library, the university syllabus, and the museum that bears his name. The Japanese Shakespeare kept its founder's shape, complete texts, stage discipline, national ambition, while replacing his words, which is what foundation means in translation history.

The final judgment on the Romeo and Juliet of 1910 can be stated with its evidence assembled. As a translation of the play's letter, it is a fourth-stage mixture, superseded within its own enterprise by the fifth-stage method its author reached two years later, and no honest account can call it Tsubouchi's best work. As a translation of the play's place in a culture, it has no rival in the Japanese record: it carried the lovers, complete and unadapted, into the first collected Shakespeare of a nation remaking its literature, it was made by the man who founded the theatre society that taught Japan to stage such texts, and it confessed its own central difficulty in a phrase that the scholarship still quotes. The cliche this series tracks keeps the play Western, an English jewel in foreign settings. Tsubouchi restores the counter-fact: between 1910 and 1928 the play became Japanese literature, bound in the same forty volumes as the rest of the canon, taught at Waseda, staged from the translated text, and housed at last in a museum shaped like the Fortune Playhouse. That is the sense in which his Romeo and Juliet is the strongest of the three great national versions as an institution and the most candid as a document, and it is the sense in which it survives its own supersession.

## The Hamlet Workshop: How the Method Was Made

The lovers' play was not translated cold. Behind the Romeo and Juliet of 1910 stands a decade and a half of work on a single proving text, and the Shōyō association's chronology records the sequence with unusual fullness: Hamlet appears in his record three times before the collected edition, in 1896, in 1907, and in 1909, with the staging following in 1911. The encyclopedia's note on the play explains its function in his practice: with its mixture of classical and modern elements, and its correspondence with the kabuki revenge tragedies such as Kanadehon Chūshingura of 1748, Hamlet was the prototype of his later Shakespeare translations. The sentence deserves emphasis, because it describes a method being reverse-engineered from a native classic. Chūshingura, the vendetta of the forty-seven rōnin, was the most famous revenge play in the Japanese repertory, and by reading Hamlet against it Tsubouchi could test which parts of the foreign tragedy already had Japanese equivalents and which parts, the soliloquy as thinking aloud, the ghost as metaphysical warrant, the play within the play as evidence, had to be built from new materials. The lectures of the middle 1890s, word for word in prose with plentiful notes, belong to this workshop phase, and so does the failure he recorded against them, that the archaic literary style of the lectures could not convey the sentiment of the original. The workshop produced the third stage's stage translations and then the fourth stage's mixture, and Hamlet was printed first in the collected edition, in December 1909, as the foundation stone of the whole series. Romeo and Juliet followed within the year, in the same style, which means the lovers' play was translated by a man who had just finished proving his method on the Danish tragedy and was applying it, at full stretch, to the most lyric text in the canon.

The workshop history also explains the otherwise puzzling confidence of the enterprise's scale. A translator who had spent fifteen years on one play's problems, verse against prose, classical against colloquial, page against stage, could plausibly project a forty-volume completion, because he had already paid the method's tuition. The connection runs the other way as well: the reason the Romeo and Juliet is a fourth-stage text rather than a fifth-stage one is chronological accident hardened into print, since the fifth stage was discovered through King Lear in 1912 and the retranslated Caesar, and the lovers' play, printed in 1910 and staged in 1918, was never retranslated wholesale in the later manner within the first edition, only revised. The revised edition of 1933 to 1935 gave the whole canon the benefit of his final judgment, and students of the text therefore read the Romeo and Juliet in strata: a 1910 mixture at the core, revised by an older man whose method had moved on. That stratigraphy is normal in long translation enterprises, Schlegel's texts were revised by other hands for a century, and it is one more reason the article judges the enterprise rather than the stratum.

## The Register Problem in Detail: Two Languages Inside One Text

The idiom question deserves a section of its own, because the slogan version, classical idiom versus modern speech, conceals the mechanics. Japanese literary prose in 1900 carried a double inheritance: the classical written language, with its own grammar, its Chinese-derived vocabulary, and its prestige, and the spoken language of the streets and households, which the genbun itchi movement, the campaign to unify speech and writing, was pressing into print as the medium of the modern novel. A stage text sat directly on the fault line, because the Japanese theatre's own classical forms, nō and kabuki and the puppet chant, were musical and formulaic, built on the seven-and-five syllable measure that organizes Japanese verse and prose rhythm, while the new drama Tsubouchi wanted was to be spoken by actors in a manner answerable to real life. His five stages are the record of a man negotiating that fault line with Shakespeare as his load. The jōruri Caesar put the load entirely on the classical side and admitted it. The Waseda lectures put it entirely on the philological side and failed by his own account to convey sentiment. The Art Society versions rebuilt it from the native stage's materials, metre, kabuki diction, kyōgen speech. The mixture stage split the load by register, high passages to a newer literary Japanese and low passages to an older colloquial, and the fifth stage finally engineered a spoken language with the local associations removed, a stage Japanese belonging to no district, class, or decade, which is why Kinoshita could say that its artificial elements are exactly what kept it from dating as fast as naturalistic speech would have done.

The play's hardest local problem inside this general one was Juliet. A doctoral study of the language of Shakespeare's heroines in Japanese translation, completed at the University of the Sacred Heart, has documented a persistent feature of the tradition the 1910 version founded: across the Japanese translations of the play, Juliet's speech has been rendered in an emphatically feminine Japanese, in the pronouns, sentence endings, and softening forms that Japanese marks as women's language, a register that narrows the character Shakespeare wrote. The Juliet of the text is the play's most competent rhetorician, the speaker who out-reasons Romeo in the orchard scene, who bargains with the Friar, who argues her parents down in act 3, scene 5 and then faces the tomb soliloquy alone in act 4, scene 3, and a translation that wraps her lines in decorative femininity trades that competence for charm. The observation belongs to the whole tradition rather than to one version, and the first woman to translate the play for the stage, Matsuoka Kazuko, made the avoidance of overused feminine marking a declared aim of her own version, which is the dialectic at work inside a single speech pattern. For the 1910 text the point is grading rather than blame: the mixture stage had no neutral spoken register for a young woman's voice that could carry both desire and argument, because the contemporary spoken Japanese of young women was itself socially coded in ways the translator was trying to escape, and the literary register he used for her lyric speeches solved the coding problem by elevation, at the price of the speed and nerve the character needs. The register problem, in short, was not a failure of talent. It was the exact shape of the unfinished modernization of Japanese itself, encountered at the point where the modernization hurt most.

### What did Hearn actually contribute to the method?

Hearn contributed the decisive nudge at the hinge between the third and fourth stages: the reported judgment, which Tsubouchi learned while translating for his theatre society, that a Japanese translation of Shakespeare should be in the spoken language of the stage.

Tsubouchi did not obey it outright, because he could not surrender the delicate literary style that carried the verse, and the disobedience produced the mixture of literary and spoken styles in which Hamlet, the lovers' tragedy, and Othello were made. Hearn therefore stands in the record as the external authority who pushed the enterprise off the classical stage languages and toward the spoken solution it reached two stages later.

## Rivals, Parallels, and the Road Not Taken

Tsubouchi's dominance was real, and it was contested from the start, which is part of its historical interest. Tozawa Kōya and Asano Wasaburō translated ten of the plays in the 1900s, including a version of the lovers' tragedy in 1905, five years before his, and well over half of the plays in the canon were nonetheless first translated by Tsubouchi, which measures both his speed and the thinness of the field. Mori Ōgai, the army surgeon and novelist who was the other great literary intelligence of the period, engaged him in the famous debate over hidden ideals in the early 1890s, Tsubouchi arguing the realist side and Ōgai the idealist, and Ōgai's own later Macbeth was praised by his old opponent, a reminder that the period's translation culture was an argument among a handful of men who read each other closely. The scholar Kohei Uchimaru, in a study of translation in early twentieth-century Japan, sets Tsubouchi beside Natsume Sōseki and Okakura Yoshisaburō as the three poles of the period's thinking: Tsubouchi's deference to the original, its names transliterated and its classical allusions carried across, which separated the foreign from the domestic; Sōseki's doubt that Shakespeare's authentic voice could survive into the vernacular at all; and Okakura's opposite practice in his 1916 translation of the Induction to The Taming of the Shrew, which made extensive use of Japanese equivalents for the original's language and imagery. The road not taken in 1910 was Okakura's: full domestication, the play re-costumed in Japanese equivalents, the method Kawashima's jōruri version had pioneered for the lovers' tragedy a generation earlier. Tsubouchi's whole career after 1884 can be read as a refusal of that road at the level of the complete text, combined with a stage-by-stage concession that the foreign text could only be reached by Japanese means. The concession is why his versions could be displaced by successors who inherited his foreignizing ambition with better linguistic tools, and why the domesticating alternative, for all its period charm, founded no tradition.

## The Stage Life of the 1910 Text

The translation waited eight years for its first staging, and the wait is itself evidence about the enterprise's order of priorities: print first, in the collected edition, with the stage following as the institution allowed. The first staging came in 1918, within the enterprise through which Tsubouchi pioneered the production of complete translated texts in place of the adaptation and selected-scene practice of the Meiji stage, and the lovers' tragedy then entered the most striking chapter of its Japanese stage life, the kabuki productions given in his translations between 1918 and 1925, alongside his Hamlet and his Othello, the last mounted at the Kabukiza with the form's reigning stars. The full production record, and the verdict of the performances, belong to the series' survey of [the Japanese film and stage adaptations of the play](/2029/04/06/japanese-film-stage-adaptations/), which records both the landmark and its cost: the fusion of the translated text with the hereditary stage was ridiculed as a distortion of both forms, and the historian James R. Brandon's research found that no kabuki actor appeared in any Shakespeare play for thirty-five years afterward. For the translation itself, the kabuki episode confirms the register argument from an unexpected side. The classicizing diction that made the text sound archaic to the new drama's audiences is precisely what made it playable by kabuki actors, whose art was built on inherited speech forms, so the same property of the language was a defect in one theatre and an enabling condition in another. The modern company's discipline took the text up as well: the Tsukiji Little Theatre, founded in 1924 by Osanai Kaoru and Hijikata Yoshi, produced several of Tsubouchi's translations as shingeki established itself as the modern genre, and his texts remained the working Shakespeare of the Japanese stage through the decades when the new drama was a minority art under increasing political pressure. During the war years Shakespeare in Japan became chiefly an object of scholarly study, as the critic Yoshiko Kawachi's introduction to the subject records, and the translation's life moved into the universities until the stage was free to want it again.

The postwar return, already noted in this article, closes the circle with a detail worth dwelling on. When A Midsummer Night's Dream reopened Shakespeare production at the Imperial Theatre in June 1946, the audience that laughed, elderly and unfamiliar, was hearing a text whose style had been engineered before the First World War, and hearing it as comedy that landed. The artificial stage language of the fifth stage, and the mixture of the fourth, had been designed for exactly that durability, to be nobody's period speech, and the 1946 performance is the design's best field test. The text then served until about 1960, overlapping its successor by half a decade, and its retirement was to the library and the syllabus rather than to oblivion. A translation's stage life of four decades, from the kabuki experiments of 1918 to the professional companies of the 1950s, is a success by any national standard, and it reframes the displacement narrative: Fukuda did not rescue the Japanese stage from an unusable text, he inherited a working institution and rebuilt its language for a new kind of actor.

### Why did the same diction fail in one theatre and work in another?

The diction failed, where it failed, in the new drama's rehearsal rooms, where actors trained in realism needed speech they could inhabit as their own feeling, and it worked in the kabuki theatres because kabuki acting is the virtuoso delivery of inherited forms, for which an elevated, classicizing text is raw material rather than an obstacle.

The property was one, the institutions were two, and the verdict on a stage translation always runs through the institution that speaks it. Tsubouchi, who wanted one text to serve both the reformed modern stage and the classical stage he refused to abandon, built his mixture for a double service that the theatres of his day could each only half receive.

## Tsubouchi the Institution: Waseda, the Classroom, and the Canon

The last piece of the adjudication is institutional, and it explains why the translation's authority outlasted its speakability. Tsubouchi spent his career at one university, and he built the infrastructure through which Japan studied what he translated: the literature department at Waseda, the journal Waseda Bungaku, the theatre academy attached to his society, and the professorship from which the complete works were issued by the university press. Generations of Japanese readers met the lovers first in his cadences because the university adopted its professor's text, and school and university study entrenched it as the standard account of the play. Canonization by syllabus is the quiet mechanism behind the loud one of the museum: the Enpaku monumentalized the enterprise in 1928, but the classroom had been ratifying it, volume by volume, since 1909. His hope, recorded in his writings, that a Japanese Shakespeare of sufficient standard would be studied by foreign researchers, has been fulfilled in a form he could not have predicted: Kishi's successors and Gallimore have written the standard English-language scholarship of his translation, and the lovers' tragedy in his version is an object of study in Birmingham and Tokyo alike. Researchers who set out to study his text as a document of translation history will find the archive he left behind him, his books and papers housed at Waseda beside the museum his completion inspired, the fullest single record any national Shakespeare in this series possesses. The play's educational afterlife, in the Japanese classroom and in classrooms worldwide that study the translation as a case, continues in the survey of [the play in global classrooms](/2030/06/28/play-in-global-classrooms/), and the pedagogical fact completes the biographical one: the professor's translation became the nation's textbook, and textbooks are displaced by curricula, not by refutation, which is another way of stating why the foundation verdict governs the displacement one.

The institutional reading also settles the question of what kind of greatness the record supports. Tsubouchi is sometimes praised as a solitary genius of translation, and the documents support a different and stronger claim: he was a builder of the conditions under which translation could matter, a theorist of the novel who reformed its status, a founder of a theatre society who trained its actors, a professor who founded the department and journal of his field, and a translator who gave the field its complete text. The Romeo and Juliet of 1910 is the play where all of those roles intersect at a single point of maximum difficulty, the lyric tragedy in the mixture stage, and its stylistic license note is the intersection's signature. Judged as the product of that intersection, the version is neither the flawless classic of the commemorative literature nor the stilted failure of the postwar polemic. It is the working document of a literature teaching itself to carry a foreign masterpiece, dated by the very archaism that made it teachable, and replaced, in the end, by the competence it created.

## Names, Pronouns, and Allusions: The Micro-Decisions

The register argument operates at the level of scenes, but translation is decided at the level of words, and three micro-decisions show the fourth stage working at close range. The first is names. The adaptation tradition Japanized freely, re-costuming foreign persons in Japanese settings, and Kawashima's floating-world title had pointed the play down that road. Tsubouchi's practice in the collected edition ran the other way: the scholar Kohei Uchimaru, comparing the period's translators, identifies his deference to the original as the distinguishing mark, extending to the transliteration of characters' names and the carrying across of Greco-Roman allusions, and judges that the deference crucially separated the foreign from the domestic. The lovers kept their foreign names in his text, as the mythological machinery of Juliet's epithalamium, Phoebus and Phaeton in act 3, scene 2, kept its classical freight, and the reader was asked to travel rather than to be visited. The second micro-decision is pronouns. The standard survey of the tradition's later history pauses, amid its account of Matsuoka Kazuko, on a problem it treats as the standing difficulty of the whole field since the Meiji period: Shakespeare's pronouns are more or less obligatory in the original, the intimate thou set against the formal plural, while Japanese tends to avoid pronouns altogether and encodes the relation between speakers in verb forms, honorifics, and sentence endings instead. Every line of dialogue in the 1910 text therefore carries social information that the English leaves to tone, and every soliloquy assigns its speaker a station-marked voice. The pressure runs in both directions: what Japanese forces the translator to specify, English forces the reader to infer, and the lovers' scenes, where the movement from formal address to intimacy is the plot in miniature, are exactly where the specification costs most. The third micro-decision is the crux of act 2, scene 2 in miniature: wherefore asks for a cause, not a place, and a receiving language must find a why that cannot be misheard as a where, then carry the rose argument that follows, in which a name is weighed against a scent and found to weigh nothing. The crux is the play's philosophy of language posed as a translation problem, since the speech argues that names are detachable from things in a language whose own writing system binds sound and meaning in characters of visible ancestry. None of these decisions can be audited from a summary; they are the level at which the stylistic license was spent, and the scholarship's concentration on the stages rather than the lines is a reminder of how much of the 1910 text still waits for its close readers. A fourth habit belongs to the same level: the director's hand inside the translator's text. His note on Othello, translated the year after the lovers' play, records that the stage directions he added were his own soliloquy, a confession that the man who had founded a theatre society could not keep his staging imagination out of the printed page. The mixture-stage texts were therefore working documents in the fullest sense, scored for production by their maker, and the habit helps explain both their stage utility in his lifetime and the postwar complaint against them, since a text pre-directed by a dead reformer leaves a new generation of directors less room than a bare one.

## What the Translation Did to Japanese Prose

The boldest claim in the literature, echoed in the series' own debate over modern translations, is that Tsubouchi's Shakespeare is simultaneously a translation and a foundation document of modern Japanese prose. The claim deserves grading rather than repetition. For Japanese prose fiction, the foundation was laid by several hands, and the colloquial experiments of Futabatei Shimei in the later 1880s have their own priority in the novel's history; Tsubouchi's own Shōsetsu shinzui had argued the theory of the modern novel in 1885, before he had translated a single complete play, and the translations are better understood as the theory's second act than as the prose's first cause. For Japanese dramatic prose, and for the translated canon as an institution, the claim stands at full strength. Before Tsubouchi, Japan had Shakespeare in fragments, adaptations, and prose retellings; after the forty volumes, it had a complete dramatic literature in Japanese, with a stage language engineered for the purpose and a university discipline to study it. His own plays belong to the same ledger: Kiri hitoha, the modern kabuki he supplied to his society's first programme, was written under the discipline he had praised since the Theatre Reform Society of 1886, Shakespearean drama as the model of dramatic coherence, and the traffic between his translating and his playwriting ran in both directions for fifty years. Kishi and Bradshaw open the standard English-language history of Shakespeare in Japan with his greatness, and the content of that greatness, on the documentary record assembled in this article, is institutional and methodological rather than verbal: a theory of the novel, a society for the stage, a department and a journal, a five-stage method, a forty-volume canon, a museum. The prose claim, graded to dramatic prose and translation culture, is the version of it the evidence supports, and it is strong enough without the exaggeration.

## The Record and Its Keepers: Reading a Translator's Self-History

A word of method belongs in the article, because so much of the case rests on the translator's own testimony, and self-history is a suspect genre. The five stages come from a memoir written at the end of the enterprise, in the fortieth volume of the work it describes, by a man being commemorated with a museum in the same year, and a reader is entitled to ask whether the stages are a tidied retrospect rather than a record. The check is bibliographical, and the record passes it: the dated texts exist at the stages the memoir assigns, the jōruri Caesar of 1884 against the spoken-style Caesar of the final revision, the lecture fragments of the 1890s, the society's stage versions, the mixture plays of 1909 to 1911, the Lear of 1912 in the new manner, and Kinoshita's comparison of the two Caesars is an independent practitioner's confirmation that the journey the memoir describes is visible in the artefacts. The production notes in the Shōyō association's encyclopedia, with their dates of translation and first staging for every play, supply a second, custodial record against which the memoir can be read, and the chronologies maintained by Sano and by the Shakespeare in Japan project agree with it at every point this article has tested. Where the records divide, the article has graded rather than chosen: Sano dates Kawashima's lovers' tragedy to 1886 in her survey and cites an 1884 Wakayama imprint in her bibliography, and the series' own survey of the Japanese stage settles the matter in prose as the mid-1880s; the association's chronology dates the retranslated Caesar to 1913 where Sano's account of the fifth stage gives 1914. These are the normal frictions of a record kept across a century in two languages, and they touch no load-bearing claim. What the keepers of the record have preserved, between the Waseda archive, the association's encyclopedia, and the scholarship in two languages, is the fullest documentary base any national version in this series can show, and the article's confidence in its verdict is a function of that base: the foundation judgment does not rest on the translator's say-so, but on a century of custodians who kept the evidence in order.

## The Lovers' Play Among His Other Translations

The 1910 Romeo and Juliet gains definition from the company it keeps in his record, because Tsubouchi's notes on the other plays show a consistent critical habit, and this play is where the habit breaks. Across the encyclopedia, his first instinct is comparison with the native repertory: Hamlet corresponds with the kabuki revenge tragedy Chūshingura; All's Well That Ends Well reminded him of kabuki in its plot's comparative simplicity; Cymbeline's frequent scene changes did the same; the depiction of evil in Titus Andronicus had some similarity with kabuki; the star-crossed lovers' companions in the canon of young love draw the same treatment, with The Taming of the Shrew's spirit recognized in the Osaka niwaka farce and A Midsummer Night's Dream's Puck matched to the kappa goblins of Japanese folklore, its mechanicals translated in rural dialect. His second instinct is formal measurement: Troilus and Cressida invites comparison with the medieval epic Heike monogatari; Measure for Measure carries something of the gritty realism of Ibsen, Shaw, and Brieux; the tragedies in particular, he wrote, seem composed with the meticulousness of the apocryphal sculptor of Buddha statues who bows three times each time he applies his chisel, and for that reason demand three or four times the effort to translate. Against that loquacious record, the entry for the lovers' tragedy is a single clause about stylistic license, the tersest note in the set. The terseness is eloquent. For play after play he had a native analogue or a formal verdict ready; for this play he had an admission, and the admission identifies the one point where his comparative machinery, the engine of his whole reception of Shakespeare, found nothing in the native storehouse that answered. Japan had love-suicide plays in abundance, and the jōruri repertory had taught Kawashima his solution, but Tsubouchi was no longer willing to solve the play by assimilation, and the unassimilated residue, the lyric duet as a form, is what the license was spent on. The note's brevity is therefore the most precise criticism of the play in his entire record: the tragedy that most resembled his native stage in story was the one that least resembled it in language.

## The Meiji Weather: Politics Inside the Translation

No part of the enterprise was politically innocent, and the article's verdict is incomplete without the weather. Shakespeare arrived under the slogan of the age, civilization and enlightenment, in the programme by which the Meiji state imported Western culture and technology for national wealth and military strength, and the first complete play translation was serialized in a democratic party's newspaper. The Caesar title of 1884, with its sword of liberty, belonged to the popular-rights movement's argument with the old order, and the lovers' tragedy, when Kawashima dressed it in the floating world, entered a culture arguing about the house system, the ie, the patriarchal household whose authority over marriage the play puts on trial in every act. Capulet's disposal of Juliet, "An you be mine, I'll give you to my friend; an you be not, hang, beg, starve, die in the streets," in act 3, scene 5, was not an exotic cruelty to a Meiji reader; it was the domestic constitution of the old Japan stated in verse, and the play's career in the new Japan ran along the seam between the household's duty and the individual's feeling, the giri and ninjo seam that the native drama had always worked. Tsubouchi's own formation sat on the same seam: the theorist of Shōsetsu shinzui had attacked didactic fiction in the name of human feeling as literature's essence, and the translator who followed him staged the argument in borrowed Verona dress. The scholar Kobayashi Kaori has examined his productions under the rubric of national identity, his authentic Shakespeare as a construction of the modern Japanese self, and the framing is just: by 1910, the year of the translation, Japan had annexed Korea and stood as an imperial power, and the completion of a national Shakespeare across the following eighteen years was an assertion of cultural parity as much as an act of homage, a claim that Japanese could hold the central canon of the West entire, in forty volumes, from its own university press. The museum of 1928, an Elizabethan playhouse rebuilt in Tokyo, is that claim in architecture, homage and appropriation in a single gesture, and the lovers' tragedy sits near the enterprise's front, the third play printed, as the demonstration that even the most lyric text of the canon could be naturalized without being surrendered.

## The Book Itself: Editions of the Tsubouchi Shakespeare

The enterprise can be read off its title pages, and the bibliography carries an argument of its own. The first volume of December 1909 was published jointly by the commercial house Fuzanbō and the Waseda University Press, and the remaining volumes by the university press alone, a partnership that states the work's double nature in imprint form: a trade book and a university monument at once. The series began under a modest title, the masterpieces of Shakespeare, and only from the twenty-fourth volume was it renamed the complete works, the earlier volumes retitled to match, so the canon's completeness was a claim the edition grew into as the end came in sight rather than a promise it had made at the start. The lovers' tragedy belongs to the modest phase, the third year of a series still calling itself a selection, which sharpens the article's point about accident and strata: the most consequential Japanese Shakespeare was not announced as one. The revised edition that followed, published by Chūō Kōronsha between September 1933 and May 1935 in forty books issued two to a box, was a thorough reworking, in places a new text; the reference literature notes that the Othello of the revision changed completely, and errata lists circulated for the publishing programme's faults, the ordinary friction of a large edition made by an old man at speed. After the war the revised text was kept in circulation by further editions, from Sōgensha and from Shinjusha among others, so the Tsubouchi that postwar readers and companies used was the final version of 1933 to 1935, not the texts of first printing. The layering matters for any judgment of the 1910 Romeo and Juliet: the play a student cites in the standard text is a 1910 fourth-stage core under a quarter-century of revision, by a translator whose fifth stage had superseded the method of the core and who nevertheless chose repair over retranslation for this play. The strata are the enterprise's honesty made visible, and they distinguish Tsubouchi's monument from Schlegel's, whose German text was revised by other hands after its maker's death of interest, while the Japanese text was revised, repeatedly and on the record, by its maker alone.

## The Two-Way Traffic: Scott, Dryden, and the Return Journey

Shakespeare was the largest, not the only, load Tsubouchi carried across the language boundary, and the smaller loads explain the method of the great one. In his early twenties he translated Walter Scott, The Bride of Lammermoor and The Lady of the Lake, under Japanese titles that sold the books as strange tales of the West, and the Scott work taught him the commercial half of the translator's craft, how a foreign narrative is packaged for readers who owe it nothing. At the other end of the career stands Dryden: Gallimore's study of Tsubouchi and Dryden records that he translated Dryden's ode "Alexander's Feast" in the years when the Shakespeare enterprise was forming, and that one of his last works, the pageant Prince Shōtoku and the Demon, published in the year of his death, was conceived as a Japanese oratorio on the poetic format of Dryden's ode. Gallimore's pairing is pointed: Dryden, who gave England its heroic Shakespeare and its theory of translation's kinds, and Tsubouchi, who gave Japan its complete Shakespeare, are the same figure in two literary histories, the translator whose versions acquire the cultural authority reserved for original authors, so that the receiving nation ends by sheltering its own classic inside the imported one. The return journey completes the pattern. Tsubouchi hoped, in his own words as the scholarship preserves them, that a Japanese Shakespeare of sufficient standard would be studied by foreign researchers, and the hope has been institutionalized in his university's partnership with the Shakespeare Institute at Birmingham, the collaboration agreement of 2016 under which Waseda and Birmingham scholars study Japanese Shakespeare together and publish its adaptations for English readers. The traffic he opened in 1884, with a German edition on Kawashima's desk and a jōruri chant in his own first version, runs in both directions in the scholarship of the present century, and the Romeo and Juliet of 1910, the play whose translation begged stylistic license, is one of its standard exhibits.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Tsubouchi Shōyō, and why does his Romeo and Juliet matter more as an enterprise than as a single text?

Tsubouchi Shōyō, born in 1859 and dead in 1935, was the Waseda professor who wrote Shōsetsu shinzui, founded the Bungei Kyōkai theatre society, and translated the complete works of Shakespeare into Japanese between 1909 and 1928. His Romeo and Juliet of 1910 matters more as an enterprise than as a single text because the single text is a transitional document, made in the fourth of his five stages of method, while the enterprise around it, forty volumes, a theatre society, a museum, and a century of successors, is what made Shakespeare a Japanese author. The play's importance is the place it holds on that shelf: the lyric tragedy, translated complete and unadapted, inside the first collected Shakespeare of a nation remaking its literature.

### Q: Why was Romeo and Juliet translated before King Lear if Lear marks the better method?

The order was set by the enterprise's momentum rather than by a ranking of difficulty. Hamlet opened the collected edition in 1909 in the mixture style, the lovers' tragedy followed in 1910 and Othello in 1911 in the same style, and only the work on King Lear in 1912 taught Tsubouchi that the old colloquial half of the mixture was the fault, when audiences heard in it the speech of a bygone age. The fifth stage, the engineered contemporary spoken language, was a discovery made by translating, not a plan made in advance, and the plays printed before the discovery kept the method of their moment. The Romeo and Juliet is therefore early work inside the collected edition, judged fairly only against the stages that produced it.

### Q: What does the note that the play's translation begs stylistic license actually claim?

The note, preserved in the Shōyō association's encyclopedia beside the play's dates, claims that this particular play demands freedom as a condition of fidelity. Tsubouchi had built his dignified register on the histories and tragedies of state, and the lovers' tragedy, with its shared sonnet, its epithalamium, its orchard duets, and its frame of servants' bawdy, would not fit inside that register without damage. License, in his usage, is not carelessness about the original but a refusal to let a single house style flatten a play whose own style is several. The sentence has become the most quoted line in Japanese Shakespeare translation studies because it compresses the whole fourth-stage problem into a concession: the method that worked for Caesar and Hamlet met, in the lovers' scenes, the material it could not carry unchanged.

### Q: Why did Tsubouchi begin with Julius Caesar, and what did he surrender in the 1884 version?

Caesar was the play of the political moment, the early 1880s of the popular-rights movement, when a drama of tyrannicide and republican oratory spoke directly to a Japan arguing about constitutions, and both pioneers, Kawashima Keizō in 1883 and Tsubouchi in 1884, reached for it first. What the 1884 version surrendered was the original's form: Tsubouchi recast the all-dialogue English script into the jōruri manner of the puppet theatre, narrative verse and dialogue mixed in seven-and-five syllables, and gave it a kabuki-style title invoking the sword of liberty. He surrendered wording, metre, and structure to buy his readers access, and said so in the preface. The surrender was the first stage of his method, and the rest of his career was the attempt to win the form back.

### Q: What did Tsubouchi's preface to the 1884 Caesar admit about his method?

The preface admits the transformation openly and ranks his loyalties. The original, he wrote, is only a rough script of dialogues without songs to be chanted, very different from a jōruri text, and he had therefore transformed it into the jōruri form. Differences from Shakespeare's text would appear under any word-for-word comparison, but his first aim was the convenience of his Japanese readers, so that they could enjoy the play without difficulty. Narrative verse was used where effective and dialogue style where appropriate, and the hardest efforts had gone into conveying the meaning of the original. The hierarchy is the founding document of his translation practice: the reader's ease first, the original's meaning second, the original's wording nowhere, a ranking he spent five stages revising until fidelity and speakability could stand together.

### Q: Why did the word-for-word lecture translations of the 1890s fail by his own account?

The lecture translations of the middle 1890s, prepared for his Waseda students and printed in the university's journals, rendered the plays word for word in prose with plentiful notes, in the archaic literary style then in fashion. Tsubouchi's retrospective judgment was that the style was not suitable to convey the sentiment of the original text. The failure is instructive because it is the opposite fault from the Caesar's: where the jōruri version had sacrificed the letter to the feeling, the lectures sacrificed the feeling to the letter, producing texts that were accurate as cribs and dead as literature. His mature method was built between the two failures, and the five-stage account is honest about both, which is why the scholarship treats the guidebook of 1928 as evidence rather than as self-promotion.

### Q: How did the retranslated Caesar of the fifth stage differ from the version of 1884?

The retranslated Caesar, made after King Lear in the new spoken style and revised again for the collected edition of 1933 to 1935, replaced the jōruri chant with the engineered contemporary stage language of the fifth stage, a Japanese built to suggest no particular district, class, or decade. The dramatist and translator Kinoshita Junji called the difference between the two Caesars the record of a laborious journey, and the phrase has governed later judgment. The 1884 text had dressed a Roman play in the clothing of the puppet stage and the slogans of the popular-rights movement. The final text stands in the language Tsubouchi had spent thirty years constructing for the purpose, and the distance between them is the distance his whole method travelled.

### Q: What was the special spoken language Tsubouchi devised for translation meant to avoid?

The special spoken language of the fifth stage was designed to avoid local association. Tsubouchi argued that a translation's language must not suggest any particular modern Japanese way of life, neither the middle-class family in the residential suburbs, nor the traditional townspeople of the downtown districts, nor the fashionable streets of the Ginza at night, because any such colouring would pin Shakespeare's Verona to one Tokyo neighbourhood and date the text with that neighbourhood's moment. The solution was a deliberate artificiality, a spoken Japanese fairly free from unnecessary associations, and Kinoshita Junji later judged that the artificial elements are precisely what kept the style from seeming too outdated decades on. The device is the linguistic counterpart of his museum: an Elizabethan shell built to hold any period's contents.

### Q: What role did Hamlet play in the making of his Romeo and Juliet?

Hamlet was the workshop in which the method was made. Tsubouchi worked on the Danish tragedy across fifteen years, in versions recorded in 1896, 1907, and 1909, and his encyclopedia note calls it, with its mixture of classical and modern elements and its correspondence with the kabuki revenge tragedy Chūshingura, the prototype of his later Shakespeare translations. The lectures, the stage experiments, and the fourth-stage mixture were all developed on Hamlet before the lovers' play was attempted, and Hamlet opened the collected edition in December 1909, one year before Romeo and Juliet followed in the same style. The lovers' play therefore inherited a tested method and its known limits, and the 1911 Hamlet production at the Imperial Theatre, staged a year after the translation appeared, fixed the public standard by which the 1910 text would be judged.

### Q: What did Natsume Sōseki argue against Tsubouchi's Shakespeare, and how has the record answered him?

Sōseki, the novelist who had studied English literature in London, reviewed the 1911 Hamlet in a newspaper under the title "Dr. Tsubouchi and Hamlet" and argued that Shakespeare's blank verse was essentially impossible to translate into Japanese, so that a Japanese production could not give artistic satisfaction whatever the translator's efforts. His earlier novel Sanshirō had already staged the narrower version of the charge, a hero admiring the diction's rhythm and dignity while feeling that the speaker does not talk like a Japanese man. The record answers the impossibility claim by the event: the complete texts were made, staged to full houses, kept as the national standard for decades, and inherited as a starting point by the translators who replaced them, which is the empirical refutation of a claim that the task itself could not be done.

### Q: What exactly did Fukuda Tsuneari blame for the datedness of Tsubouchi's Japanese?

Fukuda's diagnosis, preserved in Akiko Sano's account, was precise and double. He admitted that Tsubouchi's translation had good rhythm and vigour, and he concluded that the main reason it seemed outdated was that Tsubouchi had imitated the kabuki accent too much and had also copied the plain colloquial language of the townspeople of his day. The two faults are the two halves of the fourth-stage mixture seen from the postwar stage: the literary half had borrowed the classical theatre's music, and the spoken half had borrowed a Meiji street idiom that aged as the street changed. Fukuda's own practice answered the diagnosis with an elevated stage speech of his own, which is why his critique counts as a practitioner's succession rather than a dismissal.

### Q: How did Fukuda's own devices answer the faults he found in Tsubouchi?

Fukuda built his translations on the primacy of the speech: Hamlet's will and action, he concluded after watching Richard Burton at the Old Vic in 1954, are expressed mainly in the player's speeches, and the translator's task is to reproduce the power of the lines and raise up a Japanese language that tends to lie flat. His devices followed: he preserved the word order of the original where Japanese idiom would reverse it, because a speech accompanies the speaker's movements and reversing the words reverses the actions; he ended sentences with nouns for strength; and he admitted written and even archaic phrasing where it sounded more poetic on stage, holding that stage speech should differ from daily language. The result displaced Tsubouchi's text at most productions from the later 1950s into the early 1970s.

### Q: Why does Daniel Gallimore defend Tsubouchi's archaism as a strategy?

Gallimore, the translation scholar whose surveys anchor the English-language account of the Japanese tradition, argues that the archaism was intended from the start: Tsubouchi had always meant his translations to sound archaic, giving them the standing of classics in the receiving language by giving them a classic's patina. They fell out of favour not because the strategy failed but because the language moved, as the classical inflexions and vocabulary of Japanese were gradually replaced before and after 1945, until the differences had become impracticable. Archaism, Gallimore adds, may still be a valid strategy for a translator in context. The defense reframes the postwar displacement as linguistic history acting on a deliberate choice, and it restores agency to a translator too often treated as a victim of his period style.

### Q: What did Kinoshita Junji mean by saying his generation started from Tsubouchi's fifth stage?

Kinoshita, the dramatist and translator whose Macbeth became a postwar landmark, meant that the translators who followed Tsubouchi did not begin from nothing: they began from the method at which he had arrived, the engineered spoken stage language of the fifth stage, and their work was revision of his settlement under new conditions rather than a new foundation. He paired the concession with the evidence of the two Caesars, calling the difference between the version of 1884 and the final version the record of a laborious journey, and he judged that the Tsubouchi Shakespeare had functioned as the standard for performance and reading until about 1960. The sentence is the practitioner's verdict on the enterprise: the founder's achievement was a platform, and platforms are honoured by being built on.

### Q: Why is the complete works' fortieth volume a guidebook rather than a play?

The fortieth and last volume of the series, published in December 1928, is the introductory guidebook to Shakespeare study, Sheikusupiya kenkyū shiori, because the enterprise was pedagogical at its root: its maker was a professor, its publisher a university press, and its purpose the equipment of Japanese readers and actors for a literature they were still entering. The guidebook contains the five-stage retrospective in which Tsubouchi audited his own method across forty years, so the series closes with its maker's account of how it was made and what it cost. A canon that ends with its own manual presents itself as a national institution rather than a private feat, and the choice of the final volume is the enterprise's most compact statement of intent.

### Q: How did the Bungei Kyōkai change what a Japanese Shakespeare production could be?

The Bungei Kyōkai, the Literary Arts Association founded by Tsubouchi and Shimamura Hōgetsu in 1906, replaced the period's adaptation practice, selected scenes, Japanized settings, star vehicles, with the discipline of the complete translated text. Its academy trained actors and actresses for the new drama, its productions used his own translations entire, and its 1911 Hamlet at the Imperial Theatre, directed by the translator, played by actresses in the female parts for the first time in the new drama, and commercially successful across a week's full houses, is regarded as the landmark from which the modern Japanese theatre begins. After the association, a Japanese Shakespeare production could be a faithful staging of a whole play in Japanese, and every later company, including the ones that displaced his texts, worked inside that possibility.

### Q: Why did Juliet's Japanese voice become a problem for the tradition Tsubouchi founded?

A doctoral study of the language of Shakespeare's heroines in Japanese translation has documented that across the tradition, Juliet's speech was rendered in an emphatically feminine Japanese, in the pronouns, endings, and softening forms socially marked as women's language. The narrowing matters because the Juliet of the text is the play's most competent rhetorician, who out-reasons Romeo in the orchard, bargains with the Friar, defies her parents, and faces the tomb speech alone, and a register of decorative femininity trades that competence for charm. The mixture stage had no neutral spoken register for a young woman's voice that could carry both desire and argument, and the first woman to translate the play for the stage, Matsuoka Kazuko, made the avoidance of overused feminine marking an explicit aim of her version.

### Q: How does Tsubouchi's bargain with the Japanese language differ from Schlegel's and Pasternak's?

Schlegel translated into a literary language at its classical height and made the play a German classic by smoothing it. Pasternak translated into a language with centuries of poetry behind it and made the play a Russian poem by freedom, under a regime that left translation as a poet's remaining room. Tsubouchi translated into a language whose modern form his own generation was still inventing, amid the campaign to unify spoken and written Japanese, so his version had to be a text, a theatre programme, and an argument about the national literature at once. His bargain is the only one of the three in which the receiving language itself was unfinished, and his five stages are the record of the translator helping to finish it while he worked.
