The Spain vs Argentina World Cup 2026 final ended the way the whole match had been trending, with Spain finding the goal their control deserved and Argentina running out of ways to stop them. Ferran Torres, a substitute, drove the only goal of the game into the roof of the net early in the second period of extra time, and La Roja beat a ten-man Argentina side one-nil at the New York New Jersey Stadium to be crowned champions of the world for the second time. It was Spain's first world title since 2010 and the perfect companion to the European crown they already held, and it denied Lionel Messi and the defending champions the back-to-back triumph that only one nation has managed in the history of the competition. The scoreline reads as tight. The performance behind it was not.

For long stretches this looked like a final that Argentina were content to survive rather than win, and for more than a hundred minutes their goalkeeper made survival look plausible. Emiliano Martinez produced one of the great goalkeeping displays a World Cup final has seen, a night of eleven saves that kept the score level while his outfield teammates offered almost nothing going forward. Spain kept coming, kept probing, kept manufacturing the better positions, and the game turned decisively in their favor when Enzo Fernandez was sent off in stoppage time at the end of normal time. From that moment the outcome felt less like a question of whether Spain would score and more like a question of when. Torres answered it in the hundred-and-sixth minute, and Luis de la Fuente's players became world champions on a night that confirmed what the previous six rounds had already suggested: this Spain team was the most complete side in the tournament, and control, not chaos, was going to decide the World Cup 2026 final.

## Spain vs Argentina World Cup 2026 final result and the shape of the night

The final result was Spain one, Argentina nil, after extra time, with Ferran Torres scoring the only goal in the hundred-and-sixth minute. Spain therefore lifted the trophy at the end of a match they had dominated in almost every phase, and Argentina left the field having failed to defend the title they won in Qatar four years earlier. The bare facts of the scoreline hide how one-sided the contest actually was, because a one-goal margin usually implies a close game, and this was not a close game in any meaningful footballing sense beyond the number on the board.

Spain finished with twenty attempts on goal to Argentina's two, and the expected-goals count told the same story in a different language, with Spain worth one-point-nine-four expected goals against the barely-there zero-point-two-zero that Argentina managed from their rare forays forward. Argentina did not register a single shot in the ninety minutes of normal time, a statistic so extreme that it stands alone in the recorded history of World Cup finals since detailed shot data began in the nineteen-sixties. Their first attempt of the entire evening arrived through Messi in the hundred-and-seventeenth minute, deep into extra time, long after the game had been settled in Spanish gold and long after the contest had, in truth, been decided.

That is the shape of the night in its simplest terms. Spain controlled the ball, controlled the territory, controlled the tempo, and were denied again and again by a goalkeeper in the form of his life until the numerical advantage of the sending-off finally cracked the resistance. Argentina defended for their lives, leaned on Martinez, and hoped that Messi or Julian Alvarez might conjure one moment on the break. The moment never came. What follows is the full account of how the World Cup 2026 final unfolded, why Spain deserved to win it, where Argentina's plan succeeded and where it failed, and what the result means for both nations and for the players whose tournaments ended here.

### What decided the Spain vs Argentina World Cup 2026 final?

Two things decided it. Spain's superiority in possession and chance creation was constant from the first whistle, and the sending-off of Enzo Fernandez in stoppage time removed Argentina's last realistic hope of holding out. Once Spain had an extra man for the whole of extra time, their pressure became relentless, and Ferran Torres converted it into the only goal.

## How the World Cup 2026 final unfolded, minute by minute

The venue was the New York New Jersey Stadium in East Rutherford, the same MetLife bowl that had hosted the tournament's showpiece occasions, and the pre-match atmosphere carried the weight of a final that pitted the reigning European champions against the reigning world champions. Slavko Vincic of Slovenia took charge in the middle, and the storyline that had captivated the build-up, Lionel Messi against Lamine Yamal, the thirty-nine-year-old talisman against the nineteen-year-old prodigy, framed the opening exchanges even before a ball had been kicked in anger. For the record, Chris Martin of Coldplay performed at the interval in what was billed as the first dedicated halftime show at a men's World Cup final, a piece of tournament theatre that sat oddly alongside the tense, cagey football either side of it.

Spain settled first and settled quickest. De la Fuente had named a side very close to the one that had dispatched France in the semi-final, once again leaving Pedri on the bench and keeping Nico Williams, only recently recovered from injury, in reserve. The Spanish plan was familiar and it was ruthless in its patience: dominate the ball, pin Argentina deep, and wait for the gaps that a team defending a lead they did not yet hold would eventually leave. Argentina, for their part, lined up to absorb and to counter, with Messi and Alvarez the two men expected to punish any Spanish loss of concentration. Scaloni asked his midfield of Rodrigo De Paul, Enzo Fernandez, Alexis Mac Allister and Nicolas Gonzalez to screen the back four and to spring forward only when the moment was right.

The problem for Argentina was that the moment almost never arrived. Spain saw so much of the ball, and moved it with such control, that Argentina were pressed back onto their own eighteen-yard line for long passages and rarely escaped. Lamine Yamal was the game's most consistent source of danger down the Spanish right, wriggling free of his markers, drawing fouls, and delivering the crosses and cutbacks that repeatedly asked questions of the Argentine defense. Early on it was Yamal who created the game's first clear sight of goal, and it was Martinez who provided the first of the answers that would define his evening.

### How many shots did Argentina have in the World Cup 2026 final?

Argentina had two shots in the entire final, and neither came in normal time. They failed to register a single attempt across the ninety minutes, the first time any team has gone shotless through ninety minutes of a World Cup final in the era of recorded shot data. Messi's effort in the hundred-and-seventeenth minute was their first of the night.

The first half unfolded as a Spanish siege that lacked only a goal. Mikel Oyarzabal drew a rise from the crowd with a fierce effort around the half-hour mark, the sort of strike that in a looser game might have found the net, and Spain's corner count began to climb as Argentina conceded ground and set-pieces in equal measure. Marc Cucurella tried his luck from distance, catching Martinez fractionally out of position, and saw the ball roll just wide of the far post. Yamal continued to torment the Argentine right-back, and Dani Olmo and Mikel Merino found pockets between the lines that the Argentine midfield could not close quickly enough. Argentina, meanwhile, lost Lisandro Martinez to a first-half injury, the centre-back limping off after a challenge, and the reshuffle did nothing to steady a defense already living on the edge of its nerves.

Half-time brought no respite for the defending champions and no change in the fundamental pattern. De la Fuente made his adjustments around the hour, and in a decision that spoke to Spanish depth, he was able to remove his top scorer Oyarzabal and introduce fresh legs without weakening the press. The game grew more deliberate and, at times, more ill-tempered, exactly the kind of grinding, attritional second half that suits a side hanging on. The deeper the match went without a goal, the more Argentina's body language suggested belief that they might drag it to penalties, the arena in which they had made themselves masters in 2022. Spain's task was to keep their composure, keep the ball, and keep asking the question until Argentina finally gave the wrong answer.

## The turning point: Enzo Fernandez sees red and the final tilts

Every account of this match will return to the same three minutes at the end of normal time, because that is where the World Cup 2026 final was won and lost. Enzo Fernandez had already been walking a disciplinary tightrope. Earlier in the second half he had talked his way into a booking after a decision went his way, a caution for dissent that owed more to frustration than to any tackle, and it left the Argentine midfielder with no margin for error in a game that was slowly slipping away from his team. In the third minute of stoppage time at the end of the ninety, that margin disappeared.

The incident itself was avoidable. Pau Cubarsi, the teenage centre-back who had been so composed all tournament, carried the ball forward, and Fernandez upended him with a challenge that was late and unnecessary given the phase of play. Cubarsi went down, Vincic reached immediately for his pocket, and the second yellow followed by the inevitable red left Argentina to face the whole of extra time a man short. In the melee that followed, Messi remonstrated with Cucurella and tried to draw a matching card from the referee, but no further action was taken, and the numerical picture was set. Spain would attack the additional thirty minutes with eleven against ten.

The significance is hard to overstate. A team that had not managed a shot in ninety minutes now had to survive a further half-hour without the extra body in midfield that had allowed them to defend so deep and so narrow. Fernandez became the sixth player ever sent off in a World Cup final, and in a grim piece of national symmetry, half of those six dismissals have now been Argentine, with Pedro Monzon and Gustavo Dezotti both sent off in the 1990 final against West Germany. For a player whose tournament had already been shadowed by a red card in qualifying, it was a desperately timed loss of discipline, and it handed Spain the platform they had been building toward all night.

### Why was Enzo Fernandez sent off in the final?

Enzo Fernandez was shown a second yellow card in the third minute of second-half stoppage time for a late foul on Pau Cubarsi. He had earlier been booked for dissent, so the second caution brought an automatic red. Argentina played the entirety of extra time with ten men, and Spain used the advantage to win the final.

There was another moment, earlier in the second half, that deserves its place in the story even though it did not change the score. Spain thought they had broken through when Nico Williams, on as a substitute, fired the ball into the net, only for the goal to be ruled out after a foul on Nicolas Otamendi by Mikel Merino was spotted in the build-up. It was the correct call, and Spain accepted it without much protest, but it was one more instance of the game bending toward the Spanish and refusing, for a while longer, to break. A team can dominate a final for an hour and still walk away with nothing if the finishing touch will not come, and for a stretch it seemed possible that Argentina's resistance and Martinez's brilliance might combine to steal a night Spain had earned many times over.

## Extra time and the moment Ferran Torres crowned Spain

Extra time began with Argentina down to ten men and Spain sensing that the resistance in front of them was finally beginning to fray. The pattern of the previous hundred minutes did not change so much as intensify. Spain pushed men forward, worked the ball into wide areas, and forced Argentina to defend a penalty box that grew more crowded with every attack. Yamal, tireless, continued to beat his marker and get to the byline. The substitutes de la Fuente had introduced, Nico Williams and Martin Zubimendi among them, added fresh energy to a Spanish side that never looked like tiring even as the game entered its second hour.

The goal, when it came, arrived within the opening minute of the second period of extra time. It was made by the players Spain had brought off the bench and finished by the man who would become the story of the final. A flowing move down the right, with Nico Williams heavily involved, worked the ball across the face of the Argentine box, and Ferran Torres met it early and thumped his effort high into the net beyond Martinez, who for once could do nothing to keep his goal intact. The finish was emphatic, struck into the roof of the net from close range, and it broke a resistance that had held for a hundred and five minutes. New York New Jersey erupted in Spanish red, and Argentina, a man light and a goal behind, finally had to come out and chase a game they had spent the whole night trying to smother.

### Was Ferran Torres the match-winner in the World Cup 2026 final?

Yes. Ferran Torres came off the bench and scored the only goal of the final in the hundred-and-sixth minute, driving Spain to the title. He became just the second substitute to score the winning goal in a World Cup final, after Germany's Mario Gotze in 2014, a final that was also decided against Argentina. His strike settled a game Spain had dominated throughout.

There is a neat and slightly cruel symmetry in the identity of the scorer and the manner of the goal. The last time a substitute had settled a World Cup final was 2014, when Mario Gotze came off the German bench to break Argentine hearts in extra time in Brazil. Twelve years on, Torres did the same thing to the same nation, arriving from the sidelines to deliver the decisive blow in the additional period. For Argentina, it was a haunting echo. For Spain, it was the reward for a squad so deep that a forward who had started the final as a reserve could be the man to win the World Cup. De la Fuente had trusted his bench all tournament, and in the biggest moment of all, the bench repaid him.

Argentina's response was too little and far too late. Messi finally registered the team's first shot of the night in the hundred-and-seventeenth minute, a moment that only underlined how thoroughly they had been contained. Spain saw the remaining minutes out with the calm of a team that knew the trophy was theirs, and there was even a second Spanish goal chalked off for offside in the closing stages, a late flourish that made no difference to a result already beyond doubt. When Vincic blew the final whistle, Spain were world champions, and a tournament that had begun for them with a goalless draw against the debutants Cape Verde ended with them lifting the greatest prize in the sport.

## Why Spain won and Argentina lost: the tactical read

The temptation with a one-goal final decided in extra time is to reach for a single moment and call it the difference. The sending-off, the substitute, the goalkeeping display: each is a candidate for the decisive factor, and each played its part. The more honest reading of the Spain vs Argentina World Cup 2026 final is that Spain's control and structure, sustained over a hundred and twenty minutes, made them champions, and that no single incident so much as revealed the gulf that had been present from the first whistle. This is the namable truth of the night, the claim that the rest of the analysis supports: Spain did not win the final because of one moment, they won it because they were the better team by a distance and eventually forced the game to acknowledge as much.

Consider the fundamentals. Spain had twenty shots to Argentina's two. Spain generated one-point-nine-four expected goals to Argentina's zero-point-two-zero. Argentina failed to attempt a single shot in ninety minutes. Those numbers are not the fingerprints of a close contest that swung on a red card. They are the fingerprints of a team that spent the evening camped in the wrong half, unable to string together the passages of possession that a counter-attacking plan requires to function. Argentina's approach depended on winning the ball and breaking at speed through Messi and Alvarez, but they could not win the ball often enough or high enough up the pitch to launch those breaks, and when they did clear their lines, Spain's rest defense snuffed out the transition before it became a threat.

### Why did Argentina fail to create chances in the final?

Argentina were pinned back by Spain's possession and could not win the ball high enough to launch their counter-attacks. Messi and Julian Alvarez were starved of service, the midfield could not hold the ball long enough to relieve pressure, and the result was a final in which Argentina did not manage a shot in ninety minutes and finished with just two attempts overall.

Spain's press deserves particular credit. De la Fuente's side did not simply hoard possession for its own sake; they pressed with intelligence the instant they lost the ball, cutting off the outlet passes that Argentina needed to escape their own third. De Paul and Mac Allister, so influential in Argentina's run to the final, were denied the time and space to dictate, and without their platform, Messi drifted further and further from the game. It is a measure of Spain's containment that the greatest player of his generation, in what looks to have been his final World Cup appearance, went the whole match without a goal or an assist, the first time in his last twelve World Cup games that he had failed to contribute either. That was not an accident. It was the product of a Spanish plan executed with discipline for two hours.

Argentina's own plan was not without merit, and for long periods it nearly worked. Sitting deep, staying compact, and trusting Martinez to deal with what got through is a legitimate way to approach a final against a superior possession side, and it kept the game scoreless deep into extra time. But it was a plan with a low ceiling and a high dependence on one man in goal, and it left Argentina with no way of turning defense into attack. When Fernandez was sent off, the plan lost the extra body that made the deep block viable, and the whole structure came under a pressure it could not withstand. Scaloni's substitutions, including the introduction of the veteran Otamendi and a change at full-back, were aimed at shoring up the rearguard rather than changing the game at the other end, an admission in itself that Argentina had no route back into a contest they had never truly led.

## Player ratings and the man-of-the-match case

A final decided by a single goal in extra time can still produce clear winners and losers on the individual level, and this one did. The man-of-the-match debate really came down to two candidates who represented the two competing stories of the night: Emiliano Martinez, whose goalkeeping kept Argentina alive far longer than the run of play warranted, and the collective Spanish excellence embodied by Lamine Yamal, Rodri and the eventual matchwinner Ferran Torres. In most finals a goalkeeper who makes eleven saves is an automatic choice. In this one, the fact that he still ended up on the losing side, beaten by the weight of pressure he could not indefinitely resist, complicates the case.

Take Martinez first, because his performance was the individual highlight of the match regardless of the result. His eleven saves are the most by any goalkeeper on record in a World Cup final, a number that captures both his brilliance and Argentina's desperation. He denied Yamal from range, turned away efforts from Oyarzabal and the Spanish substitutes, and repeatedly bought his team the minutes they were living on. For a hundred and five minutes he was the only reason the scoreboard stayed level, and had Argentina somehow reached penalties, his record in shootouts would have made him favorite to win it for them again. That he was finally beaten by Torres, from a position where the finish was clean and the angle unforgiving, does nothing to diminish a display that will be remembered as one of the great losing performances in a final.

On the Spanish side, the excellence was shared, which is precisely why Spain won. Rodri, named the tournament's best player, was the metronome at the base of the Spanish midfield, dictating tempo and breaking up the rare Argentine attempts to build. Lamine Yamal, at nineteen, produced the most consistent attacking threat on the pitch, tormenting his marker down the right and creating the openings that Martinez spent the night repelling. Pau Cubarsi was composed beyond his years at the back and, in a fitting twist, it was the foul on him that reduced Argentina to ten. Marc Cucurella and Fabian Ruiz gave Spain control on the left and in the center, and Unai Simon, though he had almost nothing to do, ended the night with the clean sheet that sealed a tournament in which Spain conceded only one goal across eight matches.

The individual performance of the night belonged to Emiliano Martinez, whose eleven saves, a record for a World Cup final, kept Argentina level for more than a hundred minutes. Among the winners, Ferran Torres decided the game and Rodri controlled it. Martinez earned the man-of-the-match case on merit, even in defeat, for a goalkeeping display that defined the first hundred minutes.

Ferran Torres has the simplest ratings argument of all: he came on and won the World Cup. His overall involvement was brief, but the moment was decisive, and decisive moments are what finals are remembered for. If the award goes to the man who changed the game, it goes to Torres. If it goes to the man who played best across the ninety and beyond, it goes to Martinez. Both cases are strong, and the fact that they belong to opposing teams is itself a summary of the night: Argentina had the outstanding individual, Spain had the outstanding team.

For Argentina beyond Martinez, the ratings make for uncomfortable reading. Messi, starved of the ball, could not influence the game and finished without a shot until the last rites of extra time. De Paul and Mac Allister were overrun in a midfield that never gained a foothold. Julian Alvarez ran willingly but had nothing to run onto. Enzo Fernandez, whose dismissal defined the closing stages, will remember this final for the wrong reasons for the rest of his career. It was not a night on which many Argentine players could hold their heads high, and the honesty of that assessment is not cruelty, it is simply what a final in which a team fails to have a shot in ninety minutes produces.

## The roads that led both nations to the final

To understand how the final played out, it helps to remember how each side arrived at it, because the contrast in styles that defined the ninety minutes had been visible throughout the knockout rounds. Spain reached the final as the tournament's most controlled and defensively secure team, a side that had conceded just once in seven previous matches and that trusted possession to keep opponents at arm's length. Argentina reached it as the great survivors, a team that had learned to win ugly, to ride out storms, and to lean on the biggest names when the biggest moments arrived. The final was, in a sense, the collision of those two identities, and it was the Spanish version that prevailed.

Spain's semi-final had been a statement. Against a France side carrying Kylian Mbappe and a fearsome attacking record, de la Fuente's team won two-nil in Dallas, controlling the game in the manner that had become their signature. Anyone who watched that performance, or read the account of the France vs Spain semi-final in our preview of that tie at [our build-up to Spain's last-four meeting with France](/2026/07/14/france-vs-spain-preview/), would have recognized the template Spain brought to the final: patient build-up, a suffocating press, and the defensive solidity to make a single goal stand up. Before that, Spain had come through a demanding quarter-final against Belgium, a game we looked ahead to in [our preview of Spain against Belgium in the last eight](/2026/07/10/spain-vs-belgium-preview/), and the manner of that win had already hinted that this Spanish generation had the temperament for the closing stages of a World Cup.

Argentina's route was more dramatic and, in the end, more draining. Their semi-final against England in Atlanta was a comeback, a two-one win secured after they had fallen behind, the kind of resilient recovery that had come to define Scaloni's reign. The tension and swing of that last-four tie, which we set the scene for in [our preview of Argentina's semi-final with England](/2026/07/15/argentina-vs-england-preview/), spoke to a team that thrived on adversity and refused to be beaten even when the game turned against them. That resilience was Argentina's greatest weapon all tournament. In the final, against a Spain team that never gave them a moment of the adversity they fed on, it had nothing to work with.

The pre-match view, set out in [our preview of the World Cup 2026 final](/2026/07/19/world-cup-2026-final-preview/), had framed the tie as a meeting between the tournament's best defense and its most decorated talisman, and had asked whether Spanish structure or Argentine star power would decide it. The final answered the question emphatically. Structure won, and it won without ever really being threatened. ## What the result meant, and how it felt

A World Cup final is never only about the football, and the meaning of this one radiated out in several directions at once. For Spain, it was a coronation sixteen years in the making, the completion of a cycle that began with their first world title in South Africa in 2010 and that had passed through years of near-misses and rebuilding before this young, fearless side put it all together. Winning the World Cup as the reigning European champions confirmed Spain as the dominant force in the international game, and it did so with a style of play, controlled, technical, relentless, that felt like a vindication of everything the Spanish football culture believes about the sport. This was not a smash-and-grab. It was a team imposing its philosophy on the biggest stage and being rewarded for it.

For Argentina, the meaning was harder and heavier. They had come to this tournament chasing history of their own, the chance to become the first nation since Brazil in the late nineteen-fifties and early nineteen-sixties to win back-to-back World Cups, and, more than that, the chance to become the first team ever to win four consecutive major tournaments, having lifted the Copa America in 2021 and 2024 either side of their World Cup triumph in Qatar. To fall at the final step, and to fall so tamely without a shot in ninety minutes, made the defeat sting all the more. The dream of a repeat is over, and the long wait for a back-to-back champion goes on, still unbroken since that great Brazil side of 1962.

And then there was Messi. The final had been billed, in part, as a farewell, the last World Cup match of the greatest player of his generation, and the reality of it was quietly devastating. He was contained, marginalized, reduced to a peripheral figure by a Spanish plan built to smother him, and his tournament ended with a single late shot and no goal or assist in the final. There will be time to weigh what this means for his legacy, and the answer is that it changes almost nothing, because a career of his magnitude is not defined by a single quiet night. But the image of Messi trudging off at MetLife, denied a second world title in what looks to have been his last World Cup game, is one that will stay with the sport for a long time.

### Did Argentina retain their World Cup title in 2026?

No. Argentina lost the World Cup 2026 final one-nil to Spain and therefore did not retain the title they won in 2022. Their defeat means no nation has won back-to-back World Cups since Brazil in 1962, and it ended Argentina's bid to become the first team to win four consecutive major international tournaments.

The reaction on the night captured the two moods perfectly. Spain's players and supporters filled the stadium with the sound of a nation that had waited a long time for this, while Argentina's contingent absorbed a defeat that felt, in the cold light of the statistics, thoroughly deserved even as it was painful. The subplot of the two managers added its own layer. Luis de la Fuente and Lionel Scaloni share a history that predates their fame, the Spanish coach having once tutored his Argentine counterpart on a coaching course at the Spanish federation's academy years earlier, and de la Fuente had spoken warmly before the final of his relationship with the man in the opposite dugout, even saying he would be "delighted" to meet Argentina in the final. On the night, the teacher got the better of the pupil, though the respect between them survived the result intact.

## The individual awards and how they were shared

The closing ceremony at MetLife handed out the tournament's individual honors, and they broke down along lines that made sense given how the competition had played out. The Golden Boot, awarded to the leading scorer, went to Kylian Mbappe, who finished with ten goals and four assists across France's run to fourth place. In doing so, Mbappe became the first player ever to finish as the outright top scorer at two different World Cups, having also won the award in Qatar in 2022, a milestone that cements his standing among the great tournament goalscorers even as his France side once again fell short of the title. His total was boosted by France's deep run and by a productive knockout phase, and it edged out the finalists' marquee names in a race that had been close for much of the tournament.

The Golden Ball, awarded to the best player, went to Rodri, and it was a fitting choice. The Spanish midfielder had been the heartbeat of the champions, the player who set the tempo, controlled the pressing, and gave the whole side its rhythm across seven rounds. It is one thing to win a single tournament in a moment of peak form; it is another to be the best in your continent and the best in the world at the same time, a status that reflects sustained excellence rather than a fleeting surge. Spain now occupy that position, and they do so with a squad whose youth suggests the peak may not yet have arrived. The manner of the victory reinforces the point about the kind of football that is winning at the top level. That this approach proved good enough to overcome the reigning world champions, and to do so while conceding a single goal across an entire campaign, is a statement about the enduring value of a footballing philosophy that has sometimes been declared outdated. For the wider game, the outcome sets up a compelling four-year narrative heading toward the next edition. The emerging nations, encouraged by the expanded format that gave more of them a place at the table, will hope to push closer to the latter stages. Those questions will animate the international game until the answer begins to reveal itself, and they are the questions that a truly memorable World Cup leaves in its wake.
