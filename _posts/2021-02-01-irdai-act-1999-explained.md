The IRDAI Act 1999 did something no Indian statute had done for insurance before: it took the supervision of the industry away from a government office and gave it to a body corporate with its own fund, its own rule-making power and a statutory duty that points in two directions at once. The institution it created, the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority, licenses every insurer in the country, writes most of the operative law those insurers obey, and stands behind a grievance machinery that a policyholder can use without paying a fee or hiring a lawyer. A reader who finishes this profile should be able to answer three practical questions: what the regulator does, where an appeal from its orders goes, and which free door a policyholder with a rejected claim should try first.

The profile method used here is the one this series applies to every institution: begin with the founding statute read closely, then test the institution against its own text. The statute in question is short, running to thirty-two sections and three schedules, and almost everything about the Authority's character is visible in it: its composition in Section 4, its mandate in Section 14, its money in Sections 15 to 17, its subordination to government directions in Sections 18 and 19, and its power to make regulations in Section 26. The substantive law of insurance, meaning the obligations of insurers and the rights written into policies, sits largely in the older Insurance Act, 1938, whose own history and reform are treated in the companion profile of [the Insurance Act 1938 and sector reform](/2021/01/15/insurance-act-1938-reform-india/). This article stays on the regulator: who composes it, what it may do, how it is checked, and how an ordinary claimant uses the machinery it supervises.

One structural fact should be fixed at the start, because it explains most of what follows. The Authority was not built to be a pure policeman. Section 14(1) of its Act gives it the duty to regulate, promote and ensure orderly growth of the insurance business and re-insurance business. Regulation and promotion are yoked together in a single sentence, and the same statute arms the Authority to require insurers to write a stated percentage of their business in the rural and social sectors. The institution is therefore a prudential regulator, a market conduct regulator and a development agency in one legal person, and any fair assessment of it has to hold all three roles in view at the same time.

## Insurance before the Authority: a department, two nationalisations and a committee

For most of the twentieth century, Indian insurance was supervised by an official rather than an institution. Statutory supervision began with the life insurance legislation of 1912, worked through a government actuary and superintendent, and was generalised by the Insurance Act, 1938, which created the office of the Controller of Insurance, an officer of government who registered insurers, received their returns and enforced the Act's requirements from inside the administrative machinery of the state. The model fitted an industry that the state progressively absorbed: life insurance was nationalised in 1956 and the Life Insurance Corporation created under its own Act, and general insurance was nationalised in 1972, when the General Insurance Business (Nationalisation) Act brought the general insurers under public ownership through the General Insurance Corporation and its subsidiaries. A Controller of Insurance supervising state-owned monopolies was, in substance, one arm of government watching another, and the office carried neither the independence nor the rule-making apparatus of a modern regulator.

The intellectual break came with the economic reforms that began in 1991. The Government of India appointed a committee on reforms in the insurance sector in 1993 under the chairmanship of R. N. Malhotra, a former Governor of the Reserve Bank of India, and the Malhotra Committee reported in 1994. Its recommendations ran against the structure that had governed the sector for decades: private companies should be allowed to enter insurance, foreign participation should be permitted in a controlled form, the nationalised insurers should be restructured, and, centrally for this article, an autonomous regulatory authority should be set up to oversee the opened market, on the reasoning that a market with competing private insurers could not be supervised by a departmental officer in the old manner. The committee's case was that regulation had to precede and accompany liberalisation rather than follow it, because insurance sells a promise that falls due years or decades later, and a failed promise cannot be repaired by the ordinary law of contract after the event.

The legislative response took most of the decade. A first bill, introduced in 1998, lapsed when the Lok Sabha was dissolved, and the proposal returned with a change of government, completing its passage through both Houses in the winter session of 1999. In the interval, the executive acted on its own authority: by Resolution No. 17(2)/94-Ins.-V, dated 23 January 1996, the Central Government set up an Insurance Regulatory Authority as an interim body. That interim Authority is the "Interim Insurance Regulatory Authority" defined in Section 2(e) of the eventual Act, and its existence explains a whole chapter of the statute, Chapter III, which transfers its assets, liabilities, contracts and pending proceedings to the statutory body on the appointed day. An executive resolution can create an office; it cannot create offences, compel information with statutory force, levy fees under a statute or make regulations that bind courts. The interim body's four years of life are therefore best read as a rehearsal: the personnel, the consultative habits and the policy positions formed in that period passed into the statutory Authority, but the legal powers arrived only with the Act.

The Act itself, the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority Act, 1999, is Act 41 of 1999. It was enacted on 29 December 1999, the date it bears in the Gazette, and it was brought into force on 19 April 2000 by notification S.O. 397(E), which is also the appointed day on which the Authority stood established under Section 3. The statute did three things at once. It established the Authority as a body corporate. It amended the Insurance Act, 1938, the Life Insurance Corporation Act, 1956 and the General Insurance Business (Nationalisation) Act, 1972 through its three schedules, substituting the new Authority for the Controller of Insurance across the older law and rewriting the registration and capital provisions of the 1938 Act for an opened market. And it stated, in its long title, the purposes that the rest of this article keeps returning to: to protect the interests of holders of insurance policies, and to regulate, promote and ensure orderly growth of the insurance industry.

## What the IRDAI Act 1999 created

Section 3 establishes the Authority, to be called the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority, and Section 3(2) makes it a body corporate with perpetual succession and a common seal, with power to acquire, hold and dispose of movable and immovable property, to contract, and to sue and be sued in its own name. The corporate form matters more than it appears to. A body corporate can own its offices, employ its own officers under its own regulations, hold a fund of its own and appear in litigation as a principal rather than as a department of the Union. The Authority's head office is at Hyderabad, where it moved from the capital in its early years, and Section 3(4) empowers it to establish offices at other places in India, which it has done in the principal commercial centres. The first Chairperson of the interim body, and then of the statutory Authority, was N. Rangachary, whose tenure carried the institution from its executive rehearsal into its statutory life and through the first licensing of private insurers in the year 2000.

### Who was the first Chairperson of the IRDAI?

The first Chairperson was N. Rangachary, a former Chairman of the Central Board of Direct Taxes, who headed the interim Insurance Regulatory Authority set up by executive resolution in January 1996 and then became the first Chairperson of the statutory Authority when the IRDAI Act 1999 was brought into force on 19 April 2000.

Chapter III of the Act, which consists of Section 13 alone, is the bridge between the interim and statutory bodies. On the appointed day, all assets and liabilities of the interim Authority vested in the new Authority; contracts it had entered into were deemed to be contracts of the Authority; sums due to it were deemed due to the Authority; and suits and legal proceedings by or against it could be continued by or against the Authority. The drafting is the standard Indian technique for institutional succession, used whenever a regulator is re-founded on a statutory basis, and its presence in the Act is a reminder that the institution predates its statute by four years. The Authority also inherited a subject: a market in the act of opening. The schedules to the Act rewrote the threshold conditions of the 1938 Act for that market, including a requirement, in the substituted Section 6 of the Insurance Act, that an insurer be registered with paid-up equity capital of one hundred crore rupees, or two hundred crore rupees for an exclusive reinsurer, and a ceiling on foreign shareholding in an Indian insurance company, fixed at twenty-six per cent in the original scheme and raised by later amendments described below.

Two boundary provisions complete the picture of what was created. Section 28 of the Act provides that its provisions are in addition to, and not in derogation of, any other law in force, which is why the Authority's regime coexists with the consumer protection law, the competition law and the ordinary civil courts rather than displacing them. And Section 23A, inserted by the International Financial Services Centres Authority Act, 2019 with effect from 1 October 2020, carves the International Financial Services Centres out of the Authority's field: within an IFSC, the powers under the Act are exercised by the International Financial Services Centres Authority. The carve-out is a useful measure of the maturity of the Indian regulatory map, in which a sectoral regulator's territory can be reassigned by Parliament to a unified regulator for a defined enclave without disturbing the parent statute elsewhere.

The institution then had to build the market it was created to regulate, and its first year is the practical founding. The registration regulations were framed, applications invited, and in the course of 2000 and 2001 the first certificates of registration issued to private insurers in life and general business alike, several of them promoted by Indian business houses in joint venture with foreign insurers at the twenty-six per cent ceiling the First Schedule had written into the Insurance Act. Each certificate converted the Act from a scheme into a fact: a named company, with stated capital, approved promoters and an appointed actuary to follow, answerable to the Authority's returns, inspections and product filings. The speed of that first licensing round is part of the institution's self-understanding, and it was possible only because the interim Authority of 1996 to 2000 had done the consultative and drafting work in advance, which is the strongest argument the episode offers for rehearsing a regulator in executive form before founding it by statute.

## The people at the top: composition, tenure and the conditions of independence

Section 4 composes the Authority of three classes of member: a Chairperson, not more than five whole-time members, and not more than four part-time members, all appointed by the Central Government. Appointment is confined to persons of ability, integrity and standing who have knowledge or experience in life insurance, general insurance, actuarial science, finance, economics, law, accountancy, administration or any other discipline which, in the opinion of the Central Government, would be useful to the Authority. A proviso adds a distribution rule for the senior appointments: in appointing the Chairperson and the whole-time members, the Central Government must ensure that at least one person each has knowledge or experience in life insurance, general insurance and actuarial science respectively. The maximum strength of the body is therefore ten, and its design is that of a small expert commission rather than a representative assembly: the disciplines named in Section 4 are the disciplines an insurance supervisor needs, and the part-time members bring outside experience without executive responsibility for the Authority's daily work.

Tenure and its protections are in Sections 5 to 8, and they repay attention because the independence of a regulator is largely the sum of such details. The Chairperson and every whole-time member hold office for a term of five years and are eligible for reappointment, but no person may hold office as Chairperson after attaining the age of sixty-five years, and none as a whole-time member after attaining sixty-two. A part-time member holds office for a term not exceeding five years. A member may resign on three months' written notice. Removal is governed by Section 6: the Central Government may remove a member who has been adjudged an insolvent, who has become physically or mentally incapable of acting, who has been convicted of an offence involving moral turpitude in the Government's opinion, who has acquired a financial or other interest likely to affect his functions prejudicially, or who has so abused his position that his continuation in office is detrimental to the public interest; and in the two interest-and-abuse cases, removal requires that the member first be given a reasonable opportunity of being heard. Section 7 protects the salary, allowances and conditions of service of a member from variation to his disadvantage after appointment. Section 8 imposes a cooling-off bar: for two years after ceasing to hold office, the Chairperson and whole-time members may not, without the previous approval of the Central Government, accept employment under the Central or a State Government or any appointment in a company in the insurance sector.

### How long do the Chairperson and members of the IRDAI serve?

The Chairperson and whole-time members serve a term of five years and may be reappointed, subject to age limits of sixty-five years for the Chairperson and sixty-two years for whole-time members under Section 5. A part-time member serves a term not exceeding five years. Members may resign on three months' notice to the Central Government.

The remaining provisions of Chapter II are machinery, but machinery of a kind that shapes behaviour. Section 9 vests the Chairperson with powers of general superintendence and direction over the Authority's administrative matters. Section 10 leaves the time, place, quorum and procedure of meetings to be determined by regulations, gives the presiding member a casting vote on an equality of votes, and, with Section 11, protects the Authority's acts from invalidity merely because of a vacancy, a defect in appointment or a procedural irregularity not affecting the merits. Section 12 empowers the Authority to appoint its own officers and employees, whose conditions of service are governed by its regulations. Taken together, these sections describe a self-administering expert body: appointed by government, protected in tenure and salary, barred from immediate migration into the industry it regulated, and left to run its own establishment. Whether that amounts to independence in practice depends on appointment choices and on the direction and supersession powers examined later in this article, but the statutory design is the standard post-1991 Indian settlement for financial regulators, and insurance received it in the same form as the securities and banking regulators received theirs.

The appointment record is part of the institution's biography, and it has a pattern commentators remark on. The first Chairperson, N. Rangachary, came from the chairmanship of the Central Board of Direct Taxes. His successors have been drawn principally from two pools: the Indian Administrative Service and the public sector insurance industry itself. J. Hari Narayan, who led the Authority through the years of tariff dismantling and the health insurance expansion, was a career civil servant; T. S. Vijayan came to the office from the chairmanship of the Life Insurance Corporation; Subhash Chandra Khuntia was a civil servant of the Karnataka cadre; Debasish Panda, who took office in 2022, likewise came from the administrative service and the finance ministry. Defenders of the pattern argue that a young regulator supervising state-owned incumbents needed officers who understood both government and the industry from inside, and that the statutory qualifications in Section 4, which run from actuarial science to administration, expressly contemplate the generalist appointment. Critics answer that a chair drawn repeatedly from the regulated or from the sponsoring ministry softens the boundary the tenure provisions were drafted to harden, and point to the two-year cooling-off bar in Section 8 as an acknowledgement, inside the Act itself, that the traffic between regulator and regulated is the risk to be managed. The statute cannot settle that argument; it can only record, as it does, that Parliament chose protected tenure and a post-office bar rather than a bar on where appointees may come from.

## The duty in one sentence: Section 14(1) and the dual mandate

The whole of the Authority's legal personality is organised around Section 14. Sub-section (1) provides that, subject to the provisions of the Act and any other law for the time being in force, the Authority shall have the duty to regulate, promote and ensure orderly growth of the insurance business and re-insurance business. Three features of that sentence deserve emphasis. First, it is cast as a duty, not a discretion: the Authority is not merely permitted to regulate and promote; it is charged with doing so. Second, the objects are cumulative. Nothing in the sentence ranks regulation above promotion or promotion above regulation, and the statute supplies no tie-breaker for the cases in which they pull apart, as they do whenever a product that would spread insurance widely is also a product that lends itself to mis-selling. Third, the second limb is not decorative. The promotion duty is operationalised elsewhere in the same section, most concretely in the power to specify the percentage of business an insurer must write in the rural and social sectors, a power no purely prudential regulator would need or want.

The pattern is familiar from elsewhere in this series. The telecom regulator's statute joins regulation of the sector to its development, as the profile of the [TRAI Act and telecom regulation](/2019/07/15/trai-act-telecom-regulation-india/) records, and the competition statute was drafted against a background argument about whether a market regulator should also be a market builder. In insurance the combination is carried furthest, because the Authority's founding context was a market being created rather than merely supervised: when the first private licences were granted in 2000, the industry being regulated consisted of four public general insurers, one public life insurer and a queue of new entrants, and the state's policy object was simultaneously to police the new market and to make it grow. The dual mandate was therefore not a drafting accident. It was the institutional expression of a liberalisation programme that wanted a referee and a gardener in the same office, and the criticisms examined later in this article are best understood as arguments about how that office has divided its attention between the two roles.

Parliament's own reporting requirement shows that the second role was meant to be audited, not assumed. Section 20(2) requires the Authority's annual report to the Central Government to give a true and full account of its activities including, expressly, its activities for the promotion and development of the insurance business during the year, and the report is laid before both Houses. A purely prudential regulator accounts for soundness; this institution accounts, by statutory direction, for growth as well, and the annual report's growth chapters, penetration figures, new product counts and rural and social sector returns are the development limb rendered as a return. The obligation cuts both ways in the assessment of the institution: it disciplines the promotion duty by making it reportable, and it entrenches the duty by giving the institution a published record of expansion against which its members know they will be read.

## The powers, head by head: the Section 14(2) catalogue

Section 14(2) opens with the words "without prejudice to the generality" of the duty, and then lists the powers and functions of the Authority in clauses (a) to (q). The list is the closest thing the Indian statute book has to a job description for an insurance regulator, and it is worth walking through in groups rather than as a bare recital, because the groups correspond to the distinct kinds of power the Authority exercises.

The first group is the power over entry and exit. Clause (a) empowers the Authority to issue to an applicant a certificate of registration, and to renew, modify, withdraw, suspend or cancel that registration. Registration under the Insurance Act is the licence without which no person may carry on insurance business in India, and clause (a) makes the Authority the gatekeeper at both ends of an insurer's life: it decides who enters, on what capital and ownership conditions the Act prescribes, and it may terminate the registration of an insurer that ceases to qualify, subject to the hearing requirements the 1938 Act attaches to suspension and cancellation. The same gatekeeping extends, through the regulation-making power, to every class of intermediary, since agents, brokers, surveyors, loss assessors, third party administrators and corporate agents all hold their licences or registrations under instruments the Authority frames.

The second group is the protection of policyholders, and clause (b) is drafted with unusual specificity. It charges the Authority with protection of the interests of policyholders in matters concerning assigning of policy, nomination by policyholders, insurable interest, settlement of insurance claim, surrender value of policy and other terms and conditions of contracts of insurance. The list reads like a catalogue of the points at which an insurer's paperwork touches a family's money: who may take the policyholder's rights by assignment, who receives the proceeds on death by nomination, whether a claim is settled and on what terms, what a surrendering policyholder is paid, and the fairness of the contract's other conditions. Clause (b) is the statutory root of the large body of policyholder-protection regulation described later in this article, and its presence in the founding Act is the answer to the occasional suggestion that the Authority's concern with claim settlement and surrender values is an administrative accretion: Parliament wrote it into the second clause of the powers list in 1999.

The third group concerns the human infrastructure of the market. Clause (c) empowers the Authority to specify the requisite qualifications, code of conduct and practical training for intermediaries and agents; clause (d) does the same for surveyors and loss assessors, the professionals whose reports decide most property and motor claims; clause (e) charges it with promoting efficiency in the conduct of insurance business; and clause (f), read with clause (o), with promoting and regulating professional organisations connected with the insurance and reinsurance business, including specifying the percentage of premium income an insurer must devote to financing schemes for that purpose. The pattern is that of a professional regulator as much as a financial one: the Authority sets the entry standards, the ethics and the continuing obligations of the occupations through which insurance reaches the public.

The fourth group is money and information. Clause (g) empowers the Authority to levy fees and other charges for carrying out the purposes of the Act, which funds the institution in the manner Section 16 describes. Clause (h), the inspection power at the heart of supervision, empowers it to call for information from, undertake inspection of, and conduct enquiries and investigations including audit of insurers, intermediaries and other organisations connected with the insurance business. Clause (j) empowers it to specify the form and manner in which books of account are maintained and statements of account rendered. Clause (k) empowers it to regulate investment of funds by insurance companies, the power through which the pattern-of-investment rules reach insurers' portfolios, and clause (l) to regulate maintenance of margin of solvency, the capital buffer that stands between an insurer's promises and its assets. Clause (m) gives it a small adjudicatory jurisdiction of its own: adjudication of disputes between insurers and intermediaries. It is worth noticing what clause (m) does not do: it does not make the Authority a forum for policyholder disputes, which is why the grievance machinery described later in this article is a separate construction of ombudsman rules and consumer law rather than a head of Section 14.

The fifth group is price and distribution. Clause (i) empowers the Authority to control and regulate the rates, advantages, terms and conditions that insurers may offer in general insurance business, except where that business is controlled by the Tariff Advisory Committee under Section 64U of the Insurance Act; clause (n) makes it the supervisor of that Committee's functioning. The tariff system, under which general insurance prices were set collectively rather than competitively, was dismantled in stages in the years after 2006, and the Insurance Laws (Amendment) Act, 2015 dissolved the Tariff Advisory Committee altogether, so clauses (i) and (n) survive in the text largely as a record of a pricing regime the market has left behind. Clause (p), by contrast, is alive and distinctive: the power to specify the percentage of life insurance business and general insurance business to be undertaken by an insurer in the rural or social sector, examined in its own section below. Clause (q) is the residuary head, empowering the Authority to exercise such other powers as may be prescribed by the Central Government's rules.

### What happened to the Tariff Advisory Committee?

The Tariff Advisory Committee, the body that once fixed rates for general insurance under Section 64U of the Insurance Act, 1938, was dissolved by the Insurance Laws (Amendment) Act, 2015. Section 14(2) of the IRDAI Act still mentions the Committee in clauses (i) and (n), but the tariff regime it administered had already been dismantled in stages after 2006.

## The licensing machine in operation

Registration is where the Authority's abstract powers become a market. An applicant for registration as an insurer must satisfy the conditions of the Insurance Act: incorporation as an Indian insurance company, the capital thresholds the 1999 Act's First Schedule rewrote, one hundred crore rupees of paid-up equity capital for a life or general insurer and two hundred crore rupees for an exclusive reinsurer, a foreign shareholding within the ceiling the Act prescribes, and promoters and directors who pass the Authority's scrutiny of fitness and propriety. The application moves through stages prescribed by the registration regulations, from an initial requisition and in-principle scrutiny of the business plan, capital and ownership to the grant of the certificate of registration, and the Authority may refuse registration, a refusal the applicant may test on appeal. The register that results is organised by class: life insurers, general insurers, standalone health insurers, a category the system recognised as health business grew, and reinsurers, together with the branches of foreign reinsurers admitted after the amendment of 2015 and the Indian operations of the Lloyd's market admitted under the same reform. Each entry on the register is a continuing relationship rather than a completed transaction, because registration carries with it the obligation to maintain solvency, to file the returns the Authority prescribes, to submit to inspection, and to hold the certificate at the Authority's pleasure within the discipline of the hearing requirement.

The same machinery, at a smaller scale, licenses the market's intermediaries, and the numbers involved invert the usual picture of the institution: the Authority registers a few dozen insurers and licenses intermediaries in the lakhs. Individual agents are licensed under Section 42 of the Insurance Act on the qualifications Section 14(2)(c) lets the Authority prescribe; brokers, corporate agents, third party administrators, web aggregators, insurance marketing firms and surveyors and loss assessors each hold a licence or registration under a dedicated set of regulations, with capital, training, examination and code-of-conduct conditions of their own. The intermediary regulations are where the Authority's market conduct jurisdiction bites hardest in practice, because mis-selling, the field's persistent complaint, is overwhelmingly an intermediary event: the policy is sold by a person the Authority licensed, under a code the Authority wrote, for a commission the Authority's expenses-of-management framework caps. A profile of the institution that stopped at insurers would therefore miss the larger half of its licensing state, the distributed apparatus of permissions through which insurance is actually sold, serviced and assessed in India.

One line of business tests the machine in a form worth pausing on. Health insurance grew from a minor annexe of general insurance into one of the system's largest lines, and the register adapted around it: standalone health insurers, companies licensed to write health business alone, took their place beside the life and general insurers, and the Authority built the subject its own regulatory furniture, from the standardisation of health product definitions and waiting period disclosures to a standard health product, the Arogya Sanjeevani policy, which every general and health insurer was required to offer from April 2020 so that a first-time buyer could compare a common cover across the whole market. The standard product is the development and protection limbs working in a single instrument: it exists to spread cover, and it spreads cover by making one policy legible enough to be sold honestly. The health line also produced the system's largest grievance volumes, in claim repudiations over disclosure and waiting periods, which is why the ombudsman categories and the protection timelines in this article draw so heavily on health examples in practice.

## The product file: what an insurer may sell, and on whose say-so

Between registration and supervision sits a third machine, the control of products, and it is the part of the Authority's work a policyholder meets without knowing its name. An insurer may sell only insurance products that have been brought within the product framework the Authority maintains: for most of the institution's life that meant a file and use procedure, under which the insurer filed the product's terms, premium basis, benefit structure and sales literature with the Authority and could launch after the filing discipline was satisfied or approval given, the rigour varying by line of business. The consolidated insurance products regulations of 2024 recast the machine around the insurer's own governance: a board-level product management committee in each insurer carries responsibility for the design and review of products, filing obligations to the Authority continue, and a use and file procedure applies to classes of product the regulations specify, reserving closer prior scrutiny to the classes where policyholder detriment has historically concentrated. Around the product itself the framework wraps the sales literature: advertisements must conform to the advertising regulations, benefit illustrations for life policies must be given to the proposer on standard assumptions and signed for, and the protection framework gives every new policyholder a free look period of thirty days in which the policy may be returned and the premium recovered subject only to the deductions the regulations allow.

The product machine is the dual mandate at bench level. A file and use regime weighted towards scrutiny protects policyholders against toxic terms and unsustainable pricing, and delays the spread of simple covers into markets where absence of cover is the larger harm; a regime weighted towards insurer responsibility reverses both effects. The movement of the 2024 settlement, towards insurer governance with regulatory filing, is therefore not a technicality but a choice between the mandate's two limbs, taken in favour of speed and answered for, if the choice miscarries, by the conduct machinery of inspection and penalty described in this article. It is also the point at which the Authority's development programme acquires its instruments: standard and micro insurance products, designed for small ticket sizes and simple terms, exist because the product framework created categories for them, and the rural and social obligations described later in this article are discharged substantially through such products.

## Inspection, investigation and the direction power in practice

Section 14(2)(h) is a power to look. The coercive powers that follow from looking are found, in the main, in the Insurance Act as amended by the schedules to the 1999 Act and by the amendment of 2015, and a profile of the institution has to describe them together because in practice they form one supervisory sequence. The Authority calls for returns and statements from every insurer as a matter of routine, in the forms it prescribes, and conducts off-site surveillance on what those returns show. It may inspect an insurer's books, offices and records on site, and it may order an investigation into an insurer's affairs, including an investigation conducted through auditors it appoints, where the statutory grounds exist. An inspection that discloses defaults leads, in the recorded practice of the institution, to a show cause process and then to an order: directions to the insurer to take or desist from specified action, monetary penalties under the penalty provisions of the Insurance Act, directions for restitution to policyholders in appropriate cases, and, at the end of the scale, suspension or cancellation of registration. Section 34 of the Insurance Act empowers the Authority to issue directions to insurers generally or to an insurer in particular where it is satisfied that action is necessary in the public interest, in the interests of policyholders, or to secure proper management; that direction power, exercised through published orders, is the instrument by which much of the Authority's enforcement reaches the industry between one regulation and the next.

Two features of this enforcement practice matter for the assessment of the institution. The first is publicity: the Authority's orders are published, reasoned and indexed by the insurer concerned, so supervision produces a public record from which the industry and its advisers read the regulator's expectations. The second is grading. The penalty provisions of the Insurance Act, recast in 2015, attach monetary penalties to specified defaults and entrust adjudication to the Authority acting through designated officers, with the quantum guided by statutory factors including the gain made, the loss caused to policyholders and the repetitive nature of the default. The scheme is the insurance version of a movement visible across Indian financial law in the same period, away from criminal prosecution for regulatory defaults and towards administrative penalties imposed by the regulator itself, subject to appeal. That appeal, and the tribunal that hears it, are treated in the section on accountability below.

Beneath the inspection cycle sits a quieter apparatus on which the whole prudential structure rests: the actuarial function. Every insurer must have an appointed actuary, an officer whose appointment, qualifications and duties are prescribed by the Authority's regulations, and who certifies the valuation of the insurer's liabilities, advises on premium rates and bonus, and reports on solvency. The annual valuation, the audited financial statements in the forms Section 14(2)(j) lets the Authority prescribe, the investment returns and the solvency computation together form the off-site record from which supervision proceeds between inspections, and the appointed actuary is the point at which professional obligation runs inside the firm: the actuary's duty is owed to the integrity of the valuation and, through the regulations, to the policyholders whose claims the valuation must be able to meet, and the Authority may act on an actuary's report or qualifications to it as readily as on its own inspectors' findings.

## The public incumbents and the reinsurance account

The Authority regulates a market whose largest participants were, at its founding, owned by its own appointing government, and the statute book registers that fact carefully. The Second Schedule to the Act of 1999 amended the Life Insurance Corporation Act, 1956, and the Third Schedule amended the General Insurance Business (Nationalisation) Act, 1972, so that the Life Insurance Corporation and the public general insurers passed under the Authority's regulatory jurisdiction for the generality of insurance business, their founding Acts surviving for their corporate constitution. The settlement has consequences a profile should notice. The Authority licenses, inspects and may penalise the state-owned insurers on the same statutory footing as their private competitors, and its orders against public insurers are part of its published record; at the same time, the Government that appoints its members is also the owner of its largest regulatees, a duality the institution shares with much of Indian financial regulation and which the tenure and removal provisions of Sections 5 and 6 are designed, so far as drafting can, to neutralise.

Reinsurance belongs in the same account, because Section 14(1) names the reinsurance business alongside insurance in the Authority's duty. Every Indian insurer must retain a portion of its risks within the country and cede a portion in accordance with the reinsurance regulations, which prescribe an order of preference for placements that runs from the Indian reinsurer outward. The Indian reinsurer is the General Insurance Corporation's reinsurance successor, GIC Re, itself regulated by the Authority; a statutory obligatory cession, a fixed percentage of every general insurer's premium that must be offered to the Indian reinsurer, has been a feature of the framework since the market opened, set at levels the Authority revises from time to time. After the amendment of 2015, foreign reinsurers were admitted to establish branches in India and the Lloyd's market was given a route to operate onshore, and the reinsurance regulations were consolidated in 2024 with the foreign branches inside the same perimeter. The design is a compromise characteristic of the institution: the market is open, as the Malhotra Committee recommended, but the statute keeps a domestic retention spine inside it, and the regulator administers both the opening and the spine.

## Where the law actually sits: Section 26 and the age of regulations

A reader who confined attention to the two statutes would miss most of Indian insurance law. The Insurance Act, 1938 settles the frame: registration, capital, deposits, investment limits in outline, solvency margin in outline, the licensing of agents and surveyors, penalties and appeals. The IRDAI Act settles the regulator. The operative content, meaning the rules that decide what a policy may say, how quickly a claim must be decided, what an agent may earn, how an insurer's investments are patterned and what a policyholder must be told before signing, is found overwhelmingly in regulations made by the Authority, supplemented by master circulars and guidelines that organise and explain the regulations. That distribution of legal labour was deliberate. Section 26(1) empowers the Authority, in consultation with the Insurance Advisory Committee and by notification, to make regulations consistent with the Act and the rules to carry out the purposes of the Act, and Section 26(2) illustrates the field: the procedure and quorum of the Authority's own meetings, the conditions of service of its officers, the delegation of its powers to committees, and any matter required or permitted to be specified by regulations. The substantive catalogue lives in the Insurance Act itself, whose provisions repeatedly make the operative detail a matter "as may be specified by the regulations", from the manner of an application for registration to the fee for a duplicate certificate.

The Insurance Advisory Committee, established under Section 25, is the statutory consultative body in this system. The Authority may establish it by notification; it consists of not more than twenty-five members excluding ex officio members, drawn to represent the interests of commerce, industry, transport, agriculture, consumer fora, surveyors, agents, intermediaries, organisations engaged in safety and loss prevention, research bodies and employees' associations in the insurance sector; the Chairperson and members of the Authority are its ex officio Chairperson and members; and its objects, under Section 25(4), are to advise the Authority on matters relating to the making of regulations under Section 26, and on such other matters as may be prescribed. The design acknowledges that a regulator writing law for a whole industry ought to hear the industry's constituent interests before it writes, and the consultation requirement in Section 26(1) gives that acknowledgement legal form: regulations made without the prescribed consultation are vulnerable in a way that regulations made after it are not.

Every regulation is also laid before Parliament. Section 27 requires every rule and every regulation made under the Act to be laid before each House for a total period of thirty days, and gives the two Houses a power of modification or annulment, without prejudice to anything previously done under the instrument. Laying is a weak form of control in practice, since annulment is rare, but it marks the constitutional position accurately: the Authority's regulations are delegated legislation, made under a power Parliament granted, subject to a parliamentary override Parliament seldom uses, and reviewable by the courts on the ordinary grounds that the instrument exceeds the parent Act, offends the Constitution, or was made without a mandatory procedure. Within those limits, regulations have the force of law, and the courts enforce them as such against insurers and intermediaries.

The consequence for anyone using the law is practical, and the brief for this article asks that it be stated candidly. A practitioner working from a citation issued before the Authority's consolidation exercise may be citing a superseded instrument. That exercise, completed in 2024 after a full review of the regulatory framework, replaced thirty-four existing regulations with seven consolidated, principle-based regulations and added two new ones, covering among other subjects the protection of policyholders' interests and allied matters, insurance products, corporate governance for insurers, actuarial, finance and investment functions, registration and capital structure, rural, social sector and motor third party obligations, foreign reinsurers' branches, and an electronic insurance marketplace. The direction of travel is deliberate: fewer instruments, stated as principles with board-approved policies beneath them, in place of a proliferation of subject-specific regulations built up over twenty-five years. Consolidation reduces the search burden it was designed to cure, and it simultaneously illustrates the standing difficulty of a regulation-based system, which is that the law changes by notification rather than by enactment, and the citizen who wants the applicable rule must find the current instrument in a moving series. For a policyholder or a small intermediary, the statutes in the bookshop are the smallest part of the law that governs the policy in the drawer.

The instruments of the 2024 settlement repay a listing in prose, because together they are a table of contents of the regulator's mind. The first, notified in January 2024, consolidated the expenses of management of insurers, including the commission payable to intermediaries, into a single framework built on overall limits and board-approved policies in place of line-by-line caps. The remaining eight, approved at the Authority's meeting of 19 March 2024, covered the registration, capital structure, transfer of shares and amalgamation of insurers; the protection of policyholders' interests, operations and allied matters of insurers, which gathered the claim timelines, disclosures, advertisements and nomination rules into one instrument; insurance products, merging the product approval regimes of the life, general and health lines; corporate governance for insurers, which for the first time carried the governance requirements as regulations rather than guidelines; the actuarial, finance and investment functions of insurers; the registration and operations of foreign reinsurers' branches and Lloyd's India; the rural, social sector and motor third party obligations; and the Bima Sugam electronic marketplace. Beneath the regulations sit the master circulars, instruments by which the Authority gathers the operative directions on a subject into a single current document, a device that answers, in part, the search problem the regulations themselves create. The practitioner reader should take the moral the institution itself draws: in this field the Gazette notification and the master circular, rather than the bare Act, are the working texts, and diligence consists in confirming that an instrument is the current one before acting on it.

The lineage of a single subject shows how the strata accumulate. The Authority's first policyholder protection regulations were made in 2002, three years into its statutory life, and set the pattern of the subject: duties of insurers and intermediaries at the point of sale, the contents of the prospectus and proposal, the timelines for claim settlement and the grievance machinery every insurer must keep. Those regulations were replaced wholesale in 2017 by a recast instrument that tightened the timelines and widened the disclosures, and the subject was then carried into the consolidated protection regulations of 2024 and the master circular that organises them, each generation repealing and re-enacting the last with amendments that are individually small and cumulatively large. A claimant or adviser reconstructing the rule that governed a policy sold in a given year must therefore work in the instrument of that year, not the instrument of the year of the dispute, which is the research discipline the consolidation was designed to simplify and, by freezing the series into fewer and larger instruments, in part achieved. The strata are the price of a living framework: the statute of 1938 could not have specified the disclosure owed on a policy sold through a mobile application, and the regulation that specifies it can be remade in a season; the cost is that the law's address changes, and only the habit of checking the current instrument keeps the citizen and the regulator reading the same text.

A boundary inside the Authority's own output deserves a sentence, because litigation has turned on it. A regulation is made under Section 26, in consultation with the Advisory Committee, notified in the Gazette and laid before Parliament; it binds generally and prospectively. A direction, by contrast, is issued to a particular insurer or class of insurers under a power such as Section 34 of the Insurance Act, and a circular or guideline explains the Authority's administration of the law. The three instruments do different work, and the Authority cannot achieve by direction what the statute reserves to regulation, nor use a circular to amend a regulation. The distinction is the insurance form of a general principle of Indian administrative law, and it is the frame within which the appellate tribunal examines the Authority's orders.

## Money: Sections 15 to 17 and the economics of the institution

The Authority is funded by a mix that Section 16 defines. The Central Government may make grants to the Authority, after due appropriation by Parliament, of such sums as it thinks fit for the purposes of the Act, under Section 15. There is constituted, under Section 16, a fund called the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority Fund, into which are credited all Government grants, fees and charges received by the Authority, and all sums received by the Authority from such other source as the Central Government may decide. The Fund is applied to meeting the salaries, allowances and remuneration of the members, officers and employees of the Authority, and its other expenses in discharging its functions. Fees and charges, levied under Section 14(2)(g) on the regulated themselves, from registration fees to the annual fees insurers pay computed on their premium income, form the recurring core of the Fund in practice, so the institution is financed substantially by the industry it regulates, through a statutory fund, rather than by annual departmental budgets. Section 17 subjects the institution to public audit: the Authority must maintain accounts in the form prescribed by the Central Government in consultation with the Comptroller and Auditor-General of India, its accounts are audited by the Comptroller and Auditor-General, and the certified accounts and audit report are forwarded annually to the Central Government and laid before each House of Parliament.

The funding design has an independence argument inside it, which is worth stating because it recurs in the debate about the Authority. A regulator paid from fees on the regulated is insulated from the annual politics of budget allocation, and the insulation is reinforced here by the bar on varying members' service conditions to their disadvantage and by the statutory fund's separation from the Consolidated Fund's daily traffic. The counter-consideration is equally structural: an institution financed by levies on an industry has a fiscal interest in that industry's size, which is one more reason, beyond the words of Section 14(1), why the development limb of the mandate cannot be dismissed as rhetoric. The same funding pattern appears, in a sharper form, in the grievance machinery: the Insurance Ombudsman system is paid for by contributions from insurers, a point taken up in the policyholder section below, where the answer the design gives, that the ombudsman's award binds the insurer alone, is examined.

## The Authority among regulators

No financial regulator in India works alone, and the Authority's institutional profile includes the bodies it sits beside. At the apex of coordination stands the Financial Stability and Development Council, constituted by the Government of India in 2010 and chaired by the Union Finance Minister, whose members include the heads of the financial regulators; the Authority's Chairperson sits on the Council and on its sub-committee, chaired by the Governor of the Reserve Bank of India, which handles inter-regulatory coordination at the working level. The Council structure is the state's answer to the gaps that open between sectoral statutes: a product may be insurance in form and an investment in substance, a distributor may be a bank answerable to one regulator selling the products of an insurer answerable to another, and the bancassurance channel, in which banks distribute insurance, is supervised jointly in practice whatever the statutes say separately. Coordination also runs outward. The Authority is a member of the International Association of Insurance Supervisors, the global standard-setting body for insurance supervision, and a signatory to that association's multilateral memorandum of understanding on supervisory cooperation and information exchange, the instrument by which insurance regulators assist one another across borders when groups operate in several jurisdictions. The international membership is not ceremonial: the Association's insurance core principles are the benchmark against which the Authority's own framework is assessed in the periodic financial sector assessments conducted with the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank, and the solvency and group-supervision reforms of the institution's second and third decades track those principles closely.

The comparisons also run inward, to the two sister regulators whose statutes this series profiles separately. With the securities regulator the Authority shares an appellate tribunal, a funding model built on levies, and a founding decade; with the pension regulator it shares the dual mandate examined in this article; with the central bank it shares the problem of supervising conglomerates in which a bank, an insurer and a fund manager sit inside one group. What distinguishes the Authority's position in that company is the subject matter itself. Insurance is the only one of the four fields in which the regulated promise is typically bought by a household that cannot evaluate it, falls due on the worst day of the buyer's life, and is supervised, under the founding statute, by a body simultaneously charged with making the market for that promise grow. The institution's distinctiveness, and most of the argument about it, flows from that combination.

## The Government's hand: directions, supersession and reports

The Authority is autonomous in administration but not sovereign, and Chapter VI of the Act states the terms of its subordination with precision. Section 18 obliges the Authority, in the exercise of its powers and the performance of its functions, to be bound by such directions on questions of policy, other than those relating to technical and administrative matters, as the Central Government may give in writing from time to time; the Authority must, as far as practicable, be given an opportunity to express its views before a direction is given, and the Government's decision on whether a question is one of policy is final. Section 19 arms the Government with the ultimate sanction: where it is of opinion that the Authority is unable to discharge its functions by reason of circumstances beyond its control, that the Authority has persistently defaulted in complying with directions or in discharging its duties with damage to its financial position or administration, or that circumstances exist rendering supersession necessary in the public interest, it may supersede the Authority for a period not exceeding six months, after giving it a reasonable opportunity to make representations. On supersession the members vacate office, the powers and property of the Authority pass to the Controller of Insurance and the Central Government respectively, and the Government must reconstitute the Authority before the period expires and lay the notification and a full report of the action before Parliament. Section 20 completes the accountability chain by requiring the Authority to furnish returns and particulars to the Central Government as prescribed or directed, and to submit, within nine months of the close of each financial year, a report giving a true and full account of its activities, including its activities for the promotion and development of the insurance business, copies of which are laid before Parliament. Neither the direction power nor the supersession power has been used to displace the Authority in its history; the supersession clause has never been invoked against it, and the significance of the two sections is therefore disciplinary rather than operational, a standing reminder in the statute that the institution's autonomy is delegated and revocable, which is the standard constitutional position of every Indian sectoral regulator.

### Can the Central Government direct or supersede the IRDAI?

Yes, within limits fixed by Sections 18 and 19. The Central Government may give the Authority written directions on questions of policy, though not on technical or administrative matters, and may supersede it for up to six months on stated grounds after hearing its representations, whereupon the Controller of Insurance exercises its powers until reconstitution.

The remaining miscellaneous provisions fill out the institution's legal dress. Section 21 deems the Chairperson, members, officers and employees to be public servants when acting under the Act; Section 22 protects action taken in good faith from suit and prosecution; Section 23 permits the Authority to delegate its powers to the Chairperson, a member, an officer or a committee of members, which is the authority under which whole-time members pass the adjudication and enforcement orders that form most of the institution's visible output. None of these provisions is ornamental. Delegation in particular is what turns a ten-member commission into a working regulator: the Authority as a body reserves regulations and major policy to itself, and the statute's delegation power lets the rest of the machine, licensing, inspection findings, penalty orders and product approvals, move at administrative speed.

In operation, the delegation power produces the institution's visible anatomy. The whole-time members carry portfolios, life, non-life, health, finance and investment, distribution and enforcement among them, and the orders that fill the Authority's enforcement record, refusing a registration, imposing a penalty, directing restitution, warning an intermediary, are signed by a member or an authorised officer acting under delegation rather than by the Authority in plenary session. Beneath the members, the officers appointed under Section 12 staff departments organised by line of business and function, and the Authority's committees of members, constituted under the same section of the Act, settle classes of business between meetings. The design is worth stating because it corrects a common misreading of the institution as a committee that decides everything: the Act builds a two-storey regulator, a small statutory commission that owns the regulations and the mandate, and an administrative service beneath it that owns the files, and most encounters between the Authority and the regulated, or between the Authority and the public, happen on the lower storey under powers the upper storey has delegated and can recall.

## Checking the checker: the appellate route through the Securities Appellate Tribunal

An institution with power to cancel licences, levy penalties and direct the transfer of an insurer's business must itself be answerable somewhere, and for the Authority the answer has two tiers. The first is appellate and specialised. The Insurance Laws (Amendment) Act, 2015 substituted a new Section 110 in the Insurance Act, 1938, under which any person aggrieved by an order of the Authority made under the Insurance Act, its rules or regulations, or by an order of the Authority by way of adjudication, may prefer an appeal to the Securities Appellate Tribunal. The appeal must be filed within forty-five days of receipt of a copy of the order, though the Tribunal may entertain a later appeal where sufficient cause is shown; the Tribunal, after hearing the parties, may confirm, modify or set aside the order appealed against; it must send a copy of its order to the Authority and the parties; and it is directed to endeavour to dispose of the appeal finally within six months of receipt. The Tribunal is the same body, constituted under the securities law, that hears appeals against the securities regulator, whose founding statute is profiled in the article on the [SEBI Act 1992](/2013/04/01/sebi-act-1992-explained/), and its insurance jurisdiction makes it the meeting point of the two largest branches of Indian financial regulation. From the Tribunal a further appeal lies to the Supreme Court, within sixty days, on a question of law.

The second tier is the ordinary constitutional one. The Authority is a statutory body exercising public power, and its actions remain subject to the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts and the Supreme Court on the familiar grounds of illegality, irrationality and procedural impropriety; the existence of a tribunal does not oust that review, as the Supreme Court's tribunal jurisprudence, stated in L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India in 1997, establishes for statutory tribunals generally. In practice the two tiers divide the work by subject: the Tribunal hears the merits of regulatory orders, registration, penalties, directions, as the specialist appellate forum the 2015 amendment created, while the constitutional courts intervene on jurisdiction, procedure and rights.

The unified route of 2015 replaced a fragmented one, and the fragments explain why reform was wanted. Under the Insurance Act as it stood before the substitution, appeals against a small list of the Authority's orders, a cancelled registration, a direction to change an insurer's name, a cancelled agent's licence and orders in winding up among them, lay to the principal court of civil jurisdiction where the insurer had its main place of business, so that the country's insurance appeals were dispersed among district courts with no accumulation of expertise. Other orders, notably those arising from surveyor and loss assessor disputes under Section 64UM of the Insurance Act, went to an appellate authority located inside the finance ministry itself, a forum whose co-terminous powers the Delhi High Court examined in New India Assurance Co. Ltd. v. Union of India, and whose location in the sponsoring department sat oddly with the independence the rest of the framework was built to project. The substitution of Section 110 gathered the appellate business of insurance regulation into one specialist tribunal with a forty-five day limitation, a six-month disposal endeavour and a further appeal to the Supreme Court on law, completing for insurance the appellate design the securities field had worked with since the nineteen nineties.

The case that best illustrates the working of this discipline is the Sahara matter of 2017. The Authority, acting on findings about the financial condition of Sahara India Life Insurance Company, directed the transfer of that insurer's life insurance business, with its policyholders, to another insurer. The Securities Appellate Tribunal set the order aside and remitted the matter, because the Authority had passed a direction of that gravity without first giving the insurer an opportunity of being heard, in breach of the principles of natural justice. The episode is instructive in both directions. It shows the appellate route performing exactly the function the One Test for this article identifies: a regulated entity, and through it the policyholders whose contracts were to be moved, obtained an independent examination of the regulator's order and a correction of a procedural failure. It also shows the limit of the Authority's coercive powers even at their strongest: however well founded the prudential concern, an order that determines rights must be preceded by a hearing, and the tribunal will enforce that requirement against the regulator as firmly as the regulator enforces the statute against insurers. The Authority's regulation-making has been tested in the same spirit, and the sustainable statement of the case law is that regulations framed within the parent Acts and after the prescribed consultation bind the industry as law, while instruments that travel beyond the statute, or orders passed without the hearing the statute or natural justice requires, do not survive appeal.

The share transfer litigation of 2019 and 2020 shows the tribunal disciplining the Authority on substance as well as procedure. The Authority had held, by orders passed in December 2019, that a transfer and pledge of shares in a general insurance company effected through a trusteeship arrangement, without its previous approval under Section 6A of the Insurance Act, was void from the beginning, and had directed that the transfer not be given effect. On appeal, the Securities Appellate Tribunal set the orders aside, examining the approval requirement in the share transfer regulations and the structure of the transaction against the statute rather than accepting the regulator's characterisation of it. The point for this profile is not which side had the better of a difficult question about security interests in insurer shares; it is that the appellate route created in 2015 functions as a genuine second examination of the Authority's legal reasoning, available within forty-five days, before a tribunal that also polices the securities regulator and reads the Authority's orders with the same scepticism it applies there. A regulator whose orders are routinely tested in that forum drafts them differently, and the published, reasoned, hearing-based character of the Authority's enforcement output is in part the tribunal's work.

## The policyholder's doors: grievance machinery the Authority supervises

Most encounters between the Authority and the public do not involve the Authority at all, and that is by design. A policyholder with a rejected claim faces a ladder of forums, each free or nearly free, and the order in which the doors are tried matters because of one asymmetry that this article has already flagged: the Insurance Ombudsman's award binds the insurer and does not bind the complainant, so a policyholder can try that door without surrendering the right to try the next. The ladder begins inside the insurer. Every insurer is required, under the policyholder protection regulations, to maintain a grievance redressal mechanism with a designated grievance officer, to acknowledge a written complaint within three working days and to resolve it within two weeks, telling the complainant, where the complaint is not resolved to satisfaction, of the further avenues open. A complaint taken no further than this stage is, in the system's design, an unfinished complaint, because the ombudsman's door opens only after the insurer has been approached in writing.

The second door is the regulator's own grievance system. The Authority operates an integrated grievance management system, in its present form the Bima Bharosa portal, on which a policyholder may register a complaint against an insurer and track it; the complaint is routed to the insurer, whose handling is monitored by the Authority's grievance machinery, and an unresolved or unsatisfactorily resolved complaint can be taken further. The portal is a supervisory instrument as much as a postal one: the pattern of complaints is data, and the Authority uses complaint data in its supervision of insurers' conduct and in framing protection regulation. Behind the doors stand timelines the protection regulations fix for the claim itself, which a claimant should know because they convert delay from a grievance into a default: a life insurance claim is to be settled within thirty days of receipt of the last necessary document, an investigation the insurer considers necessary is to be completed within ninety days and the claim settled within thirty days thereafter, claims in the general and health lines run to survey and decision clocks of the same order, and an insurer that pays late owes interest at two percentage points above the bank rate for the period of delay. A repudiation, to be valid, must be a reasoned communication of the grounds and the evidence relied on, which is the document the rest of the ladder then examines. The third door is the Insurance Ombudsman, constituted under the Insurance Ombudsman Rules, 2017 made by the Central Government, and administered through the Council for Insurance Ombudsmen with offices across the country. The ombudsman is one door among several in the wider consumer landscape, and the full map of forums, ombudsmen in other sectors and commissions is drawn in the [complete guide to consumer law in India](/2020/11/01/consumer-law-india-complete-guide/); what follows is the insurance door in the detail a claimant needs.

The ombudsman may entertain complaints of defined kinds: delay in settlement of a claim, partial or total repudiation of a claim, disputes over premium paid or payable, misrepresentation of policy terms at the time of sale, disputes over the legal construction of a policy so far as they relate to a claim, grievances about policy servicing, the issue of a policy not in conformity with the proposal, and the non-issue of policy documents; an amendment of the Rules in 2021 brought insurance brokers within the scheme alongside insurers. Two preconditions govern entry: the complainant must first have made a written representation to the insurer and received a rejection, an unsatisfactory reply, or no reply within one month, and the complaint must be made within one year of the rejection or of the expiry of that month. Within the scheme, the ombudsman first attempts settlement by mediation where the parties consent in writing, the recommendation being made within a month of that consent; failing settlement, the ombudsman passes a reasoned award, to be made within three months of receiving all the requirements from the complainant. The award may not exceed the loss suffered as a direct consequence of the cause of action, and the total award is capped at the pecuniary limit of the scheme, which stood at thirty lakh rupees under the Rules of 2017 and was raised to fifty lakh rupees by an amendment that took effect on 9 November 2023. The complainant must communicate acceptance of the award in writing; on acceptance, the award binds the insurer, which must comply within thirty days and inform the ombudsman. If the complainant does not accept, the award falls away and every other remedy remains open, which is the sense in which the forum is risk-free for the claimant: the worst outcome of a complaint to the ombudsman is to be returned, with time spent, to the position occupied before it was filed.

The ombudsman's own institutional arrangements complete the picture. The offices are distributed across seventeen centres, each with a defined territorial jurisdiction, and the system is administered through the Council for Insurance Ombudsmen, the body of insurers through which the scheme is financed and staffed; the ombudsmen themselves are appointed under the Rules for fixed terms and are drawn, in the main, from the senior ranks of the insurance industry, the civil services and the judiciary's district cadre. The Rules empower the ombudsman, beyond deciding on documents, to call for the records the insurer holds on the dispute and to take expert opinion where the case requires it, which matters because the decisive material in a repudiation dispute, the proposal form, the agent's report, the investigator's findings, sits in the insurer's file and not with the claimant. A complainant without a lawyer thus acquires, through the office, a measure of the disclosure that litigation would have provided, and the disposal figures the council publishes each year, running to tens of thousands of complaints, measure the scale on which that substitution operates. The ombudsman is, in institutional terms, a creature of the same settlement as the Authority itself: industry-funded, rule-bound, and dependent for its legitimacy on a structural safeguard, in the Authority's case protected tenure, in the ombudsman's case the one-sided binding effect.

The fourth door is the consumer commission under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, before which a policyholder is a consumer complaining of a deficiency in service or an unfair trade practice. The commissions charge no fee for claims up to five lakh rupees of consideration and modest slab fees above that, work to a limitation period of two years, and may award the full range of consumer remedies, including compensation for harassment and mental agony of the kind the Supreme Court recognised in Lucknow Development Authority v. M. K. Gupta in 1994 for services under the predecessor Act. An order of a commission binds both parties, subject to the appeals the Act provides upward through the State and National Commissions to the Supreme Court. This article describes the machinery as legal information; it is not advice on any individual claim, and a claimant with a large or contested loss should take advice on the facts of the case before choosing a forum. The policyholder who does go forward should keep the file intact: the proposal form, the policy document, the claim papers and the insurer's letters are the record every forum on the ladder works from, and preserving that record in one secure place is the same discipline that leads journalists to keep source material in a protected store, described in [an account of how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/).

The commissions' own jurisdiction is arranged in the three tiers the consumer guide describes, and the figures matter to forum choice in insurance disputes because claim values cluster where the tiers divide. Under the jurisdiction rules made in 2021, a district commission entertains complaints where the consideration paid does not exceed fifty lakh rupees, a State Commission where it exceeds fifty lakh rupees and does not exceed two crore rupees, and the National Commission above two crore rupees, the measure being the premium and other consideration paid for the policy rather than the sum claimed, which prevents the inflation of a claim from carrying it into a higher forum. An insurance complaint of ordinary size therefore begins, if it goes to a commission at all, in the district where the policyholder resides or works, a venue rule of the 2019 Act that removed the older necessity of suing where the insurer's branch or head office sat. The commission route and the ombudsman route are alternatives, not stages: a complaint pending before a commission cannot be taken to the ombudsman, and the policyholder who wants the commission's fuller remedial range, including its power to award compensation for harassment beyond the ombudsman's direct-loss measure, should begin there, accepting the fee, the formality and the arrears that the commission system carries.

Two features of this ladder deserve explicit notice, because they are the points a rejected claimant most often gets wrong. The first is that the ombudsman's pecuniary cap applies to the award, and a dispute whose value exceeds fifty lakh rupees belongs in the consumer commission or the civil court from the start. The second is the funding of the ombudsman system itself: it is paid for by contributions from the insurance industry, collected through the council that administers it. That is the standard international model for industry ombudsmen, and it is also the standing ground of criticism, that a forum financed by insurers cannot be trusted against them. The design's answer is structural rather than rhetorical: the complainant is never bound, the insurer is bound on acceptance, the ombudsman is appointed under government-made rules for a fixed tenure, and the dissatisfied claimant loses nothing by the attempt. Whether that answer suffices is argued in the criticism section below; as a matter of machinery, the ladder's order is dictated by the asymmetry, and the claimant who starts at the ombudsman spends nothing and risks nothing.

## The rural and social sector obligations: a distributional condition on the licence

Section 14(2)(p) empowers the Authority to specify the percentage of life insurance business and of general insurance business to be undertaken by an insurer in the rural or social sector. The power has been exercised continuously since the first years of the opened market, through regulations that prescribe obligations by reference to the policies an insurer writes, the premium it collects and the lives it covers, rising in steps over the first years of an insurer's operations. The consolidated regulations of 2024 carry the subject under the head of rural, social sector and motor third party obligations, and measure rural obligations by the gram panchayat, an administrative unit small enough that compliance cannot be achieved by writing business in district towns alone. The social sector, as the regulations define it, reaches the informal and unorganised sectors and economically vulnerable classes, counted in lives covered rather than in premium earned.

The obligation is, on examination, an extraordinary feature for a prudential statute to carry, and the brief for this profile names it as part of the article's central claim. A licence to sell insurance in India is granted on solvency, governance and conduct conditions, as licences are everywhere; it is also granted on a distributional condition, that a stated share of the licensee's effort be directed to the parts of the population a purely commercial calculus would serve last, or not at all. No equivalent obligation sits on the licence of a bank, a securities firm or a mutual fund in Indian law, and few insurance regulators elsewhere possess a statutory power of this kind. Defenders of the power treat it as the price of entry to a market whose deepest failure is absence rather than misconduct: the harm the obligation addresses is not the bad policy but the policy never sold. Critics answer that obligations measured in policies and lives reward the sale of small, low-value covers that lapse quickly, and that the resulting statistics flatter penetration while delivering little protection. Both readings are live in the material, and both are really arguments about the dual mandate: the obligation is the promotion duty of Section 14(1) written as a quota, and it stands or falls with the case for that duty, which the next section takes up directly.

The same section of the 2024 consolidation carries the third obligation of the trio, motor third party, which differs in kind from the other two and shows the Authority administering a social insurance function inside a commercial market. Third party motor cover is compulsory under the Motor Vehicles Act, its premium is a regulated price rather than a market one, fixed through a process in which the Authority is the statutory consultee and was for many years the notifying authority, and every general insurer must write a prescribed share of the business notwithstanding that the class runs at an underwriting loss across the industry. The obligation survives because the alternative, insurers declining the compulsory class they are bound to offer, would leave accident victims compensated by nobody; the regulated price and the obligatory share together make the loss a levy on the industry's profitable lines, assessed and distributed by the regulator. Rural lives, social sector lives and the victims of road accidents are, in the statute's scheme, three versions of one idea: that the licence to write profitable insurance in India carries duties to the unprofitable, and that a regulator exists to state and enforce them.

## The dual mandate argued both ways

The case for the dual mandate begins with the condition of the market the Authority inherited. Insurance penetration in India, measured as premium against gross domestic product, stood in the region of four per cent in the years of the Authority's maturity, far below the levels of comparable economies, and the protection gap, the share of economic loss that falls on households and the state because no policy answers it, was wider still. A regulator charged only with solvency and conduct would have no statutory reason to concern itself with either figure. The development limb of Section 14(1) gives the institution that reason, and its products are visible: the rural and social sector obligations, product regulations that standardised simple and micro insurance covers, distribution reforms intended to lower the cost of reaching small policyholders, and the Authority's stated goal of insurance for all by 2047, which organises its regulatory programme around extension of cover. The pension regulator's statute carries the same double charge of regulation and development, as the profile of the [PFRDA Act 2013 and pension reform](/2021/02/15/pfrda-act-2013-pension-reform/) shows, and the recurrence of the pattern across Indian financial statutes suggests a considered legislative preference rather than a series of accidents: in markets the state is trying to build, Parliament has repeatedly declined to separate the referee from the gardener.

The case against begins from the incentive the combination creates. A regulator answerable for the growth of an industry has a reason, which it need never state, to approve products readily, to permit distribution practices that move volume, and to treat the industry's expansion as evidence of its own success; the costs of those choices fall on policyholders in the form of unsuitable policies, high surrender losses and claims friction, and they surface slowly. Mis-selling has been the persistent conduct complaint of the Indian insurance market across the Authority's whole life, in bank-led distribution of life policies in particular, and the critics' charge is that an institution with a growth mandate is poorly placed to discipline the selling that produces growth. The criticism received its most authoritative institutional form from the Financial Sector Legislative Reforms Commission, appointed by the Government of India and chaired by Justice B. N. Srikrishna, whose report of 2013 proposed a rebuilt financial regulatory architecture in which consumer protection and market conduct were organised as functions in their own right rather than as one limb of a sectoral regulator's divided duty. The commission's reasoning was structural: a regulator's objectives should be few and ordered, because an institution told to pursue everything will, under pressure, pursue what is measured, and growth is easier to measure than fairness.

The evidence supports a verdict narrower than either case at full strength, and this article takes it. The dual mandate has not been fatal: the prudential record across a quarter century, in which no Indian insurer's failure has left policyholders unpaid on the scale the solvency regime exists to prevent, is the work of the regulatory limb operating undistracted. Nor has the development limb been empty: the rural and social obligations and the standardisation of simple products are real extensions of cover that a purely prudential body would not have produced. Where the conflict has bitten is in the allocation of attention the series thesis predicts: conduct enforcement against mis-selling and claims friction lagged the prudential and developmental programmes for much of the institution's life, and the correction, when it came, came in the form of consolidated policyholder protection regulation and a grievance architecture whose centre of gravity sits in the ombudsman system rather than in the Authority's own adjudication. The mandate's two limbs are both in the statute; the institution's history is the record of which limb it reached for first.

## The statute in motion: 2015, the IFSC carve-out and the decriminalisation era

The Act of 1999 has been amended in ways that track the maturation of the institution. The most consequential amendment is the Insurance Laws (Amendment) Act, 2015, Act 5 of 2015, which took effect from 26 December 2014, the date of the ordinance it replaced. For the Authority itself, the 2015 Act inserted the words "of India" into its name, so that the body established by Section 3 is the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India, and the abbreviation IRDAI replaced IRDA in the institution's own usage. For the system around it, the same Act raised the ceiling on foreign shareholding in an Indian insurance company from twenty-six per cent to forty-nine per cent, dissolved the Tariff Advisory Committee, recast the penalty provisions of the Insurance Act with graded monetary penalties and an adjudication mechanism, and, as described in the appeals section above, substituted the new Section 110 that sends appeals from the Authority's orders to the Securities Appellate Tribunal. A later amendment, the Insurance (Amendment) Act, 2021, raised the foreign investment ceiling again, to seventy-four per cent, without disturbing the Authority's own constitution. In December 2025 Parliament enacted a further amendment of the three insurance statutes, the Sabka Bima, Sabki Raksha (Amendment of Insurance Laws) Act, 2025, raising the foreign investment ceiling to one hundred per cent; the institution's own sections were touched lightly in that exercise, and its detailed working belongs to the companion treatment of the Insurance Act.

### What is Bima Sugam?

Bima Sugam is an electronic insurance marketplace created under regulations the Authority made in 2024, conceived as a single digital platform on which policies may be bought, serviced and claimed against, and operated through a not-for-profit company owned by participants in the insurance industry. It is the development limb of the mandate expressed as infrastructure.

The decriminalisation programme of the Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2023, which converted imprisonment terms into monetary penalties across forty-two Central Acts, is part of the same legislative weather, and candour requires the insurance position to be stated exactly: the Schedule to that Act did not amend either the Insurance Act, 1938 or the Act of 1999, principally because the insurance statutes had already travelled the same road in 2015, when the penalty architecture of the Insurance Act was rebuilt around monetary penalties, adjudicating officers and appeal to the Tribunal. The era's direction is still visible inside the Authority's own practice, in the grading of enforcement towards penalties and restitution for regulatory defaults and the reservation of prosecution for the unauthorised conduct of insurance business, which remains an offence. The newest structural fact about the institution is the one Section 23A records: since 1 October 2020 the Authority's writ does not run in the International Financial Services Centres, where the unified IFSCA regulates insurance business in the enclave. The regulator of 1999, built to open a closed national market, operates inside a regulatory map that is itself being redrawn at the edges.

## What the profile adds up to

Read as a whole, the Act of 1999 built an institution of a recognisable Indian type and one unusual feature. The type is the expert statutory regulator of the reform era: a small appointed commission, protected tenure, its own fund fed by levies on the regulated, a large delegated rule-making power exercised in consultation and laid before Parliament, enforcement by published order, and appeal to a specialist tribunal with the constitutional courts behind it. The unusual feature is the content of the duty in Section 14(1), which makes the regulator a promoter of its industry and the author of distributional obligations on its licensees, and which no account of the institution can treat as surplusage because the institution itself does not: its stated ambition of insurance for all by 2047 is the promotion duty restated as a programme. The policyholder who will never read the Act meets the institution through the ladder of grievance doors described above, and by that test the design has a clear centre of excellence: a free ombudsman whose award binds the insurer alone, which is the single most claimant-favourable piece of machinery in Indian financial regulation. The industry reader meets it through Section 14(2) and the regulations, and by that test the standing difficulty is the one the consolidation of 2024 addressed without abolishing, that the law of insurance lives in instruments that change by notification and must be chased through them. Both readers are dealing with the same institution, and the statute that explains both encounters is thirty-two sections of institutional design wrapped around one sentence of divided duty.

A last measure of the institution is the one its founding committee would have applied. The Malhotra Committee asked for a regulator that would let a competitive market exist where a state monopoly had stood, protect the policyholder whom competition alone would not protect, and answer for the growth of a sector that reached a small fraction of the population. By the first two measures the record is strong: a licensed, inspected, solvency-tested industry of private and public insurers operates under law the Authority writes, and the policyholder's ladder of free remedies has no equivalent in the pre-1999 world. By the third measure the argument continues, and it is the argument Section 14(1) makes permanent, because a statute that orders its regulator both to police a market and to grow it has not resolved the tension between those orders; it has institutionalised the tension and appointed ten members to live inside it. That, more than any single power in the catalogue, is what the Act of 1999 created.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What functions does the IRDAI perform?

Section 14 of the IRDAI Act 1999 lists the functions in clauses (a) to (q) of sub-section (2). The Authority issues, renews, modifies, withdraws, suspends and cancels the registration of insurers; protects policyholders in matters of assignment, nomination, insurable interest, claim settlement and surrender value; sets qualifications and codes of conduct for agents, intermediaries, surveyors and loss assessors; levies fees; calls for information and inspects and investigates insurers and intermediaries; prescribes the form of accounts; regulates insurers' investments and their margin of solvency; adjudicates disputes between insurers and intermediaries; and specifies the percentage of business insurers must write in the rural and social sectors. Around that list sit its regulation-making power under Section 26 and its duty under Section 14(1) to regulate, promote and ensure orderly growth of the insurance and reinsurance business.

### Q: How is the IRDAI constituted?

Section 4 of the Act composes the Authority of a Chairperson, not more than five whole-time members and not more than four part-time members, a maximum strength of ten, all appointed by the Central Government. Appointments are confined to persons of ability, integrity and standing with knowledge or experience in life insurance, general insurance, actuarial science, finance, economics, law, accountancy, administration or another useful discipline, and a proviso requires that, among the Chairperson and whole-time members, at least one person each brings knowledge or experience of life insurance, general insurance and actuarial science. Section 3 makes the Authority a body corporate with perpetual succession and a common seal, able to own property, contract, and sue and be sued in its own name, with its head office at Hyderabad and power to open offices elsewhere in India.

### Q: What rural and social sector obligations does the IRDAI impose?

Section 14(2)(p) empowers the Authority to specify the percentage of life insurance business and general insurance business that an insurer must undertake in the rural sector and in the social sector, and the power is exercised through regulations that prescribe obligations rising in steps over an insurer's first years of operation, measured by policies written, premium collected and lives covered. The consolidated regulations of 2024 carry the subject as the rural, social sector and motor third party obligations, and measure rural business by the gram panchayat, so compliance cannot be built on district towns alone. The social sector reaches the informal and unorganised sectors and economically vulnerable classes, counted in lives covered rather than premium. The obligation is in substance a distributional condition written into the insurance licence itself.

### Q: Where does an appeal from an IRDAI order go?

Since the Insurance Laws (Amendment) Act, 2015 substituted Section 110 of the Insurance Act, 1938, an appeal from an order of the Authority, whether made under the Insurance Act, its rules or regulations or by way of adjudication, lies to the Securities Appellate Tribunal, the same tribunal that hears appeals against the securities regulator. The appeal must be filed within forty-five days of receipt of a copy of the order, though the Tribunal may condone delay for sufficient cause. The Tribunal may confirm, modify or set aside the order, and is directed to endeavour to dispose of the appeal within six months. From the Tribunal, a further appeal lies to the Supreme Court within sixty days on a question of law. The Authority's actions also remain subject to the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts on ordinary public law grounds.

### Q: What can the Insurance Ombudsman decide?

The Insurance Ombudsman, constituted under the Insurance Ombudsman Rules, 2017, decides complaints in defined categories: delay in settlement of a claim, partial or total repudiation of a claim, disputes about premium paid or payable, misrepresentation of policy terms at the time of sale, disputes over the legal construction of a policy so far as they relate to a claim, policy servicing grievances, a policy issued not in conformity with the proposal, and non-issue of policy documents; brokers were brought within the scheme by an amendment in 2021. Working first by mediation where both parties consent in writing, the ombudsman may otherwise pass a reasoned award on the dispute. The subject matter is the individual complaint against an insurer; the ombudsman is not a forum for challenging the Authority's regulations or an insurer's licence.

### Q: How much of insurance law sits in regulations?

Most of the operative law. The Insurance Act, 1938 supplies the frame of registration, capital, deposits, solvency and penalties, and the IRDAI Act 1999 supplies the regulator, but the rules that decide what a policy may contain, how fast a claim must be settled, what an intermediary may earn, how investments are patterned and what a buyer must be told sit in regulations made by the Authority under Section 26 and the regulation clauses of the 1938 Act, organised by master circulars. In 2024 the Authority consolidated thirty-four regulations into seven principle-based regulations and added two new ones, partly to cure the difficulty that a citation to a pre-consolidation instrument may point at superseded law. The statutes are the smallest part of the law a practitioner or policyholder actually uses.

### Q: How does a policyholder complain about an insurer?

In stages, and the order matters. The policyholder first complains in writing to the insurer's grievance officer; the policyholder protection regulations require acknowledgement within three working days and resolution within two weeks, with the insurer bound to point to the further avenues. If the answer is a rejection, an unsatisfactory reply or silence, the complaint may be registered on the Authority's grievance portal, Bima Bharosa, where it is routed to the insurer and tracked. The decisive free step is the Insurance Ombudsman, available once the insurer has been approached and within one year of its reply or silence, whose award binds the insurer alone. A policyholder may instead, or afterwards, file before a consumer commission as a complaint of deficiency in service.

### Q: What criticism does the IRDAI attract?

The standing criticism is structural and follows the dual mandate: a regulator charged by Section 14(1) with promoting the industry it polices is said to face an incentive to approve products and distribution practices that expand volume while their costs surface later as mis-selling, high surrender losses and claims friction, and mis-selling in bank-led distribution has been the market's persistent conduct complaint. The Financial Sector Legislative Reforms Commission under Justice B. N. Srikrishna argued in 2013 for organising consumer protection as a function in its own right rather than one limb of a divided duty. Other criticisms are practical: that the law's location in regulations makes it hard for policyholders and small intermediaries to find the applicable rule, and that conduct enforcement historically lagged the prudential programme. The Authority's defenders answer with its solvency record and the measurable extension of cover.

### Q: When did the IRDAI become a statutory body?

On 19 April 2000. The Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority Act, 1999, Act 41 of 1999, was enacted on 29 December 1999 and was brought into force by notification S.O. 397(E) dated 19 April 2000, which is the appointed day on which the Authority was established under Section 3. The institution had an earlier executive life: the Central Government had set up an Insurance Regulatory Authority by Resolution No. 17(2)/94-Ins.-V dated 23 January 1996, and that interim body worked for four years without statutory powers. Chapter III of the Act, consisting of Section 13, transferred the interim body's assets, liabilities, contracts and pending proceedings to the statutory Authority on the appointed day, so the institution's history runs from 1996 and its statutory powers from 2000.

### Q: What does the Insurance Advisory Committee do?

The Insurance Advisory Committee is the statutory consultative body established under Section 25 of the Act. It consists of not more than twenty-five members, excluding ex officio members, chosen to represent commerce, industry, transport, agriculture, consumer fora, surveyors, agents, intermediaries, organisations engaged in safety and loss prevention, research bodies and employees' associations in the insurance sector; the Chairperson and members of the Authority sit on it ex officio, the Chairperson presiding. Its statutory object, under Section 25(4), is to advise the Authority on matters relating to the making of regulations under Section 26, and it may advise on other matters prescribed. The committee matters legally because Section 26(1) conditions the regulation-making power on consultation with it: regulations are made in consultation with the committee, notified in the Gazette and laid before Parliament.

### Q: How is the IRDAI funded?

Through a statutory fund fed mainly by the industry it regulates. Section 15 permits the Central Government to make grants to the Authority after parliamentary appropriation. Section 16 constitutes the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority Fund, into which are credited all government grants, all fees and charges received by the Authority, and any other sums the Central Government decides; the Fund is applied to the salaries and remuneration of members, officers and employees and to the Authority's other expenses. The fees and charges are levied under Section 14(2)(g) and include registration fees and the annual fees insurers pay by reference to premium income, which form the recurring core of the Fund. Accounts are audited by the Comptroller and Auditor-General of India under Section 17 and laid before Parliament.

### Q: What is the difference between an IRDAI regulation and an IRDAI direction?

A regulation is delegated legislation. It is made under Section 26 of the Act, in consultation with the Insurance Advisory Committee, notified in the Gazette and laid before Parliament under Section 27; it binds the whole regulated field, generally and prospectively, with the force of law, and the courts enforce it as such. A direction is an exercise of supervisory power addressed to a particular insurer or a class of insurers, principally under Section 34 of the Insurance Act, 1938, requiring specified action in the public interest, the interests of policyholders or the interest of proper management. The distinction has legal consequences: the Authority cannot achieve by direction what the statute reserves to regulation, cannot use a circular to amend a regulation, and a direction that determines rights must be preceded by a fair hearing.

### Q: Can the IRDAI cancel an insurer's registration?

Yes. Section 14(2)(a) empowers the Authority to withdraw, suspend or cancel a certificate of registration, and the Insurance Act, 1938 supplies the grounds and the procedure, including defaults in complying with the Act, its regulations or the Authority's directions, failure to maintain the required solvency margin, and conduct prejudicial to policyholders. Section 3(5E) of the Insurance Act, inserted by the 1999 Act's First Schedule, empowers the Authority to suspend or cancel a registration in the manner determined by regulations, with a proviso that no such order may be made unless the insurer has been given a reasonable opportunity of being heard. Cancellation is the end of a graded ladder that runs through inspections, show cause proceedings, directions and monetary penalties, and an order cancelling registration is appealable to the Securities Appellate Tribunal under Section 110 of the Insurance Act.

### Q: What must a policyholder do before approaching the Insurance Ombudsman?

Make a written representation to the insurer first. The Insurance Ombudsman Rules, 2017 entertain a complaint only where the complainant has approached the insurer in writing and the insurer has rejected the complaint, given an unsatisfactory reply, or failed to reply within one month of receiving it. The complaint to the ombudsman must then be filed within one year of the rejection, the unsatisfactory reply or the expiry of that month of silence, as the case may be. The complaint must also not be pending before, or already decided by, a court, a consumer commission or an arbitrator, and it must fall within the categories the Rules define and the fifty lakh rupee pecuniary cap. Going to the insurer first is therefore not a courtesy but a condition of the ombudsman's jurisdiction.

### Q: Does an Insurance Ombudsman award bind the policyholder who receives it?

No, and the one-sidedness is the scheme's central design feature. The ombudsman passes a reasoned award, which the complainant may accept or decline; acceptance must be communicated in writing, and only on acceptance does the award become binding, and it binds the insurer, which must comply within thirty days and report compliance to the ombudsman. A complainant who declines the award, or lets it pass, is returned to the position held before complaining: the complaint to a consumer commission, or a civil suit, remains open on the same facts. There is no penalty for trying. The asymmetry is deliberate policy, because the alternative, a free forum whose outcome bound the claimant, would deter exactly the small complainants the scheme exists to serve, and it dictates the rational order in which a policyholder tries the available doors.

### Q: How long does the Insurance Ombudsman take to decide a complaint?

The Rules build the timetable in stages. Where both parties consent in writing to mediation, the ombudsman acts as counsellor and mediator and is to make a recommendation within one month of that consent. Where mediation is not attempted or does not settle the complaint, the ombudsman passes a reasoned award within three months of receiving all the requirements from the complainant. The complainant then communicates acceptance of the award in writing, and the insurer must comply with the award within thirty days of that acceptance and intimate compliance to the ombudsman. The scheme's speeds assume the complainant's own papers are complete: the three-month clock runs from the receipt of everything the ombudsman needs, which is one more reason the policyholder's file, proposal form, policy document and correspondence, should be kept together from the start.

### Q: Can an insurer refuse to comply with an Insurance Ombudsman award?

Not lawfully, once the complainant has accepted the award in writing. The Rules give the insurer no appeal against an award; the design binds the insurer alone, and compliance is required within thirty days of the acceptance, with intimation to the ombudsman. An insurer that fails to comply faces the consequences the framework attaches to default, including liability to penal interest on the awarded sum for the period of delay and the supervisory attention of the Authority, for which non-compliance with the ombudsman scheme is itself a regulatory default visible in inspection and enforcement. The insurer's protections lie earlier in the process: it is heard before the award, may press its case in the mediation stage, and remains free to contest a consumer complaint if the complainant declines the award and sues elsewhere.

### Q: What happens after a policyholder files a grievance on Bima Bharosa?

The complaint is registered on the Authority's integrated grievance management system and routed to the insurer complained against, which must deal with it under the timelines of the policyholder protection framework and report the outcome, while the complainant can track the complaint's progress on the portal. The Authority's grievance machinery monitors the handling; patterns of complaint feed into its supervision of the insurer's conduct and into the framing of protection regulation. The portal is a routing and monitoring instrument, not an adjudicatory one: it passes no award and decides no claim. Its place in the sequence is after the insurer's own grievance officer and before, or alongside, the Insurance Ombudsman, and an unsatisfactory outcome on the portal leaves the ombudsman and the consumer commission open, subject to the ombudsman's own preconditions and limitation period.

### Q: What solvency margin must an insurer maintain under the IRDAI framework?

Every insurer must maintain an available solvency margin of assets over liabilities at not less than the required solvency margin computed under Section 64VA of the Insurance Act, 1938 and the solvency regulations, and the framework fixes the control level of solvency at one and a half times that required margin: an insurer at or above one hundred and fifty per cent is within the control level, while a fall below it draws escalating supervisory intervention, from a financial plan and restrictions on business to, at the end of the ladder, action against registration. Section 14(2)(l) of the IRDAI Act gives the Authority the power of regulating maintenance of margin of solvency, and the prudential regulation of assets, liabilities and solvency was one of the subjects consolidated in the regulations of 2024.

### Q: Why was the interim Insurance Regulatory Authority replaced by a statutory body?

Because an executive resolution cannot carry the powers insurance regulation needs. The interim Authority, set up by Resolution No. 17(2)/94-Ins.-V dated 23 January 1996 on the Malhotra Committee's recommendation, could advise, consult and prepare the ground for an opened market, but it could not license insurers with statutory force, compel information, levy fees under a statute, make regulations binding on courts, or impose penalties; and the market it was preparing for, with private and foreign-participating insurers entering a field occupied by nationalised incumbents, required exactly those powers in a body that could also be made accountable for them. The Act of 1999 therefore re-founded the institution as a body corporate, transferred the interim body's assets, liabilities and proceedings to it under Section 13, and armed it under Sections 14 and 26, while subjecting it to government directions, parliamentary laying of its regulations and appeal to a tribunal.
