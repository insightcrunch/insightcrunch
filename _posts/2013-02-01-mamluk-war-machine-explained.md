For roughly two and a half centuries the Mamluk war machine was the standard against which soldiers in the eastern Mediterranean were measured. It stopped the Mongol advance at a moment when no other army had managed it. It removed the last Crusader holdings from the Syrian coast. It held Egypt and Syria as a single military space against threats from three directions, and it did so with a force that was small by the standards of the empires around it and better trained than any of them. Contemporaries who fought against Mamluk cavalry, and contemporaries who merely wrote about it, agree on the quality in a way that historical sources rarely agree on anything.

Then, in two engagements less than a year apart in 1516 and 1517, it was destroyed. The claim this article defends is what we can call the perfect-cavalry-wrong-century thesis: the Mamluks built the finest heavy cavalry of their age through a training system that took a boy and produced, over ten or more years, a horseman who could shoot, lance, and fight with a sword at a standard no conscript army could approach, and the completeness of that achievement is exactly what made adaptation impossible. A system optimized this hard for one form of war has no slack in it for another.

That framing corrects the usual account, which treats the collapse as evidence that the Mamluks had gone soft. They had not. The men who lost in 1516 were trained the same way as the men who won in 1260, they were individually formidable, and the sources on the Ottoman side say so. What had changed was that the thing they were superb at had stopped being the thing that decided battles, and the reasons they could not change with it are partly cultural, substantially institutional, and more fiscal than any account focused on stubbornness allows.

## What Made Them Feared

Reputation in military history is often inflated, so it is worth establishing what the Mamluk reputation actually rested on before explaining how it was built.

It rested first on individual skill at arms. A Mamluk trooper had spent his entire adolescence and early adulthood in continuous, supervised, technical training in horsemanship, archery, and the use of the lance and sword. Nothing comparable existed in the armies he faced. Feudal European knights trained hard but privately and unevenly. Mongol horsemen were superb riders and archers who learned by living the life rather than through a formal curriculum. Ottoman forces would eventually field highly trained infantry, but that is a different arm entirely. The Mamluk combination of institutional training, full-time professional status, and lifelong practice was unusual.

It rested second on equipment. Mamluk cavalry were heavily equipped by regional standards, with mail and lamellar armour, helmets, shields, and armoured horses in the heaviest formations, and they carried a full set of weapons rather than specializing in one. A single trooper was expected to shoot a composite bow accurately from the saddle, close with a lance, and then fight with sword or mace, which meant a Mamluk unit could function as missile cavalry and as shock cavalry in the same engagement without changing its composition.

It rested third on cohesion. Mamluks trained together, lived together in barracks through their formative years, and served in households built around personal loyalty to a commander and to one another. That produces a unit that holds together under pressure, and holding together under pressure is what usually decides a pre-modern battle.

### What actually decided a Mamluk battle?

Cohesion under sustained missile exchange, followed by a decisive charge delivered at the right moment. Mamluk armies rarely won by manoeuvre or surprise. They absorbed the enemy's arrows, wore him down with their own, waited for his formation to loosen, and then closed with lance and sword against a body already coming apart.

## The Raw Material

The Mamluk army was unusual before any training began, because of where its soldiers came from and what that arrangement was designed to achieve.

Recruits were purchased abroad as boys, principally from the Turkic steppe north of the Black Sea in the earlier period and increasingly from the Caucasus later, brought to Egypt, converted to Islam, trained, and then manumitted into the service of the man who had bought them. The full workings of that system, and the arguments about how it should be described, are set out in the account of [who the Mamluks were](/2012/08/01/who-were-the-mamluks/).

For military purposes three features of the arrangement mattered enormously.

The first is selection. Recruits were chosen young, bought for a purpose, and could be rejected. An army recruited by purchase can apply a standard at intake that an army recruited by levy or by feudal obligation cannot, and the men who arrived were selected for physical promise before a day's training had been done.

The second is that they arrived with nothing. A boy shipped from the steppe to Cairo had no family in Egypt, no local patron, no property, and no alternative career. His entire future ran through his master's household and through the corps of fellow recruits bought in the same batch, which produced attachments of extraordinary strength. The bonds within a purchase cohort and between a cohort and its patron are the social foundation of Mamluk battlefield cohesion.

The third is that many arrived already able to ride. Boys from steppe backgrounds had usually been on horseback since early childhood, which meant the training system was refining an existing competence rather than building one from nothing. That head start is easy to overlook and it shortens the road to a finished horse archer considerably.

Two features of the table set up the argument. The first is that every entry in the middle column describes a genuine strength, and the last entry is the only weakness, which is the shape of the whole story. The Mamluk system was not deficient across the board and then defeated; it was excellent across the board with one gap that turned out to be the one that mattered.

The second is that the evidence base is exceptionally good for a medieval military system. Mamluk warfare can be studied from a substantial technical literature written by and for practitioners, which is a rarity. Most medieval armies are reconstructed from chronicles written by clerics who never held a weapon. This one left manuals.

## Furusiyya: The Training System

Furusiyya is the term for the whole complex of horsemanship and martial skill that the training system existed to produce, and it covers both the practical disciplines and the written tradition that codified them.

The practical content had four principal branches. Riding came first and underlay everything, since a man who is not secure in the saddle cannot shoot or fight from it. Archery from horseback was the technical core and took the longest to master. The lance covered the couched charge and a repertoire of thrusts and parries at close quarters. The sword, together with the mace and other close-quarters weapons, covered the fight after the charge had spent itself.

Alongside these ran a set of supporting subjects: care and management of horses, veterinary knowledge, the maintenance of bows and the manufacture of arrows, the wearing and maintenance of armour, and elements of tactics and formation drill.

### What did a trainee's day involve?

Mounted drill in the riding grounds, repeated shooting at fixed and moving targets from a walk and then at speed, lance work against posts and rings, sword practice against fixed targets and opponents, care of his own horse and equipment, and religious and literacy instruction alongside the physical work.

The duration is what separates this from anything else in the region. A boy purchased at around ten to twelve years old might be manumitted and enrolled as a trooper in his early twenties, which means a decade or more of daily, supervised, progressive training under instructors whose job was nothing else. The barracks system kept trainees together, under discipline, and away from the distractions of a civilian life they did not have. It is closer to a modern military academy than to anything else in the medieval world.

The written tradition matters for a separate reason. A substantial body of furusiyya literature survives, covering archery technique in detail, lance exercises, cavalry drill, horse management, and the organization of training, some of it produced under Mamluk patronage. These texts are technical, specific, and clearly written by people who knew the practice, and they are why the details of Mamluk training can be described with confidence rather than inferred from battle outcomes.

## Archery From the Saddle

Mounted archery was the discipline that took longest to acquire and it was the foundation of Mamluk battlefield method, so it deserves close attention.

The weapon was the composite bow, built from a wooden core with horn laminated on the belly and sinew on the back, glued and bound and left to cure for a long period before it was serviceable. The construction stores far more energy for a given length than a self bow of wood alone, which is what makes a powerful bow short enough to use on horseback. It is also sensitive to damp, which is one reason Egyptian and Syrian conditions suited it and campaigning in wet weather did not.

The technique involved release with the thumb, protected by a ring, rather than with the fingers, which is standard across the Asian archery traditions and permits a fast draw and release from an awkward position. Manuals describe the correct grip, the draw, the anchor, the release, and the follow-through in detail, along with drills for shooting to either side, to the rear, and at targets on the ground.

The tactical point was volume and sustainability. Mamluk cavalry could deliver accurate arrows while mounted, at a rate the manuals treat as a measurable standard, for long enough to affect an enemy formation. That is a different thing from the single decisive volley of a massed infantry formation, and it produced a different kind of battle: a prolonged exchange in which the better-trained and better-supplied side gradually gained the upper hand, followed by a charge against a weakened opponent.

The requirement it imposed was logistical. Sustained shooting consumes arrows in quantity, and an army planning to fight this way must carry them by the tens of thousands and be able to resupply during an engagement. Arrow supply is an unglamorous constraint that shaped Mamluk campaign planning as much as water and fodder did.

## The Lance and the Charge

If archery softened an enemy, the lance finished him, and the Mamluk approach to shock action differed in an instructive way from the European one.

The lance was heavy, used couched under the arm for the charge and also employed in a range of thrusts, sweeps, and parries at close quarters that the manuals treat as a discipline in their own right. Training used rings suspended at height, posts, and mounted opponents, and the exercises progressed from control at a walk to accuracy at the gallop.

### Why did the charge usually come late in a battle?

Because charging an intact formation is expensive and charging a loosened one is decisive. Mamluk practice was to use missile fire to thin and disorder the enemy first, then commit the lance against a line already fraying, which converted a costly frontal action into a comparatively cheap exploitation.

That sequencing is the central difference from the western European pattern, where the charge was often the opening move and the aim was to break a line by impact alone. A Mamluk trooper carried both capabilities, which meant his commander could choose the moment rather than being committed to one method by the composition of his force. An army of knights must charge because that is what it does. An army of horse archers who are also lancers can wait.

The formation used for shock action was close order, delivered in successive lines rather than as one mass, so that a repulsed first line could withdraw through or around the second and reform. Repeated controlled charges are far harder to execute than a single all-in effort and they require exactly the drill and discipline the training system produced.

## Sword, Mace, and the Fight at Close Quarters

Once formations interpenetrated, the lance became a liability and the trooper drew what he could use in a press.

The primary sidearm was a single-edged curved sabre, well suited to cutting from horseback, and the manuals treat swordsmanship as a substantial discipline with its own progression of exercises against fixed targets, clay figures, and opponents. Mace and axe appear alongside it, and both had a specific virtue against armoured opponents, since a blow that does not cut through mail can still break what is under it.

A trooper also carried a shield, typically small and round in the later period, of leather or wood construction and manageable while holding reins and weapon. Daggers appear as a last resort.

The point worth drawing out is redundancy. A Mamluk was equipped to fight effectively at three ranges with three weapon systems and trained in all of them to a high standard. Most contemporary soldiers were specialists at one range. That versatility is expensive, since it multiplies training time, and it is the direct product of a system that could afford to spend a decade on each man because the men were a permanent professional corps rather than a levy raised for a season.

## Armour, Equipment, and What It Cost

Mamluk cavalry were heavily equipped, and understanding the equipment explains both the battlefield behaviour and the fiscal problem that eventually crippled the system.

The core defence was mail, sometimes combined with lamellar or with plates integrated into a mail garment in the later period, worn over padding. Helmets were of iron with mail aventails protecting the neck. Arm and leg defences varied by wealth and period. The heaviest formations armoured their horses as well, with barding of quilted or mail construction protecting the front and flanks, which is what allowed a formation to close through missile fire that would have shredded unprotected mounts.

All of this was individually owned and individually maintained. A trooper was responsible for his arms, his armour, and his horses, funded from his pay and from the revenue assignment attached to his position, and the sources record inspections and complaints about men appearing with inadequate equipment. This is a system that pushes cost down to the individual and therefore transmits any fall in revenue directly into a fall in military effectiveness within a few years.

### Where did the horses come from?

Egypt could not breed enough of them at the required quality, so warhorses were imported from Syria, Anatolia, North Africa, and the Arabian interior, alongside domestic breeding. Horses were a recurring rather than a one-time expense, since a trooper needed several and campaign losses were heavy.

The full cost of a single effective Mamluk trooper is therefore substantial and continuous: the purchase price of the boy, a decade of maintenance and instruction, arms and armour, several horses with fodder and replacement, and pay. Multiply by an establishment of many thousands and the annual figure explains why the trade revenue examined in the account of [how Mamluk Egypt controlled world trade](/2012/11/01/mamluk-egypt-world-trade/) mattered so much more than its share of total income would suggest. The military system consumed cash specifically, and cash came from commerce.

## How the Army Was Organized

The Mamluk army was not a unitary force with a chain of command in the modern sense. It was a federation of households, and this shaped everything it did well and everything it did badly.

At the centre stood the royal mamluks, the sultan's own purchased corps, trained in the barracks at the citadel and constituting the largest and best-equipped single body. Around them were the units of the amirs, senior officers who held ranks defined by the number of mamluks they were entitled to maintain, each running his own household with its own purchased recruits, its own training, and its own loyalties. Alongside both stood the halqa, a body of free non-mamluk cavalry that had been significant in the early period and declined in quality and importance over time. Auxiliary contingents of Bedouin, Turkmen, and other allied or subject groups supplied light cavalry and scouts.

The arrangement had real virtues. Each household was internally cohesive, its members bound by shared training, shared origin, and personal obligation to their patron. Household units fought together, knew each other, and did not disintegrate under pressure the way scratch formations do.

Its defect is the mirror image. An army made of private units bound by personal loyalty is an army whose components can be turned against one another, and Mamluk political history is largely the history of that happening. A sultan's death, a disputed succession, or a rivalry between senior amirs could split the force along household lines. The military structure that produced cohesion at the unit level produced fragility at the level of the whole, and the guide to [the Mamluk Sultanate of Egypt](/2012/07/01/mamluk-sultanate-of-egypt/) traces how that tension ran through the entire political system.

## The Career of a Trooper

Understanding the system from the inside means following one man's path, and the stages were formalized enough to describe.

A boy arrived by purchase, was housed in barracks with the cohort bought alongside him, and began a period combining religious instruction, basic literacy in Arabic, and progressively demanding physical training. Instructors were experienced soldiers assigned to the task, and the sources indicate a graded curriculum in which riding preceded weapons and each weapon had its own sequence.

Manumission came at the end of training and it was the pivot of the whole career. A trained mamluk was freed and enrolled as a soldier in the household of the man who had bought him, carrying an obligation of loyalty that outlasted the legal relationship and a set of horizontal bonds to the men freed alongside him. Those two attachments, vertical to the patron and horizontal to the cohort, are the social architecture of the army.

Promotion ran through household service and battlefield performance. A man might hold offices within his patron's establishment, and those offices carried the emblems that later appeared as blazons on the buildings of anyone who rose far enough. Advancement to the ranks of amir meant the right to maintain a specified number of mamluks of one's own, which converted a soldier into a patron and started the cycle again from the other end.

The ceiling was the sultanate itself, and it was genuinely reachable, since the throne repeatedly went to men who had arrived in Egypt as purchased boys. That openness at the top is the feature that most distinguishes this system from the hereditary military aristocracies of the same period, and it had a direct military consequence: a young trooper had a visible reason to excel that no feudal levy could offer.

The limit was equally sharp. Rank did not pass to sons, who were excluded from the mamluk corps and formed a separate and lesser status group. Every generation of the army was therefore recruited afresh from abroad, which kept the standard high and made the whole system dependent on a supply line running through foreign territory.

## Fortifications and Frontier Defence

A cavalry army needs fixed points, and the Mamluk approach to fortification was deliberate and coherent even though it produced less spectacular results than the Crusader castles it captured.

Coastal policy was the most striking decision. After clearing the Frankish holdings from the Syrian littoral, the Mamluks deliberately dismantled or refused to garrison many coastal fortifications, on the reasoning that a fortified port was an asset to whoever held the sea and that they did not hold the sea. A strong coastal castle that a returning crusade could seize and supply from ships was a liability, so the coast was left weak on purpose while the interior was held in strength.

That policy had a cost that arrived much later. A coast left without fortification is a coast open to raiding, and the great raid on Alexandria in 1365 exploited exactly that condition. The decision was rational against the threat it was designed for and it aged badly.

Inland the picture was different. Citadels in the major Syrian cities were maintained and strengthened, garrisons were kept in the interior strongpoints, and the routes between Egypt and Syria were secured with the water points and staging posts that made rapid movement possible. Damascus and Aleppo were the anchors of the northern frontier, and the Egyptian base was commanded from a fortified seat above Cairo.

The system's logic was mobile defence around fixed anchors: hold the interior points, keep the roads and the relay system working, and use the speed of a professional cavalry army to concentrate against whatever appeared. It worked well against threats coming overland from the north and east. It had no answer to a threat arriving by sea, and it was designed by people who knew that and accepted it.

## What a Campaign Involved

The gap between an army's paper quality and its performance is filled by the unglamorous business of getting it somewhere in a fit state to fight.

A campaign began with a decision at the citadel and a muster. Households were called up, equipment inspected, and the deficiencies that inspections revealed were a standing complaint in the sources, since a trooper responsible for his own kit could arrive with worn mail or an unserviceable horse. Provisions, arrows, spare mounts, tents, and the baggage train were assembled.

The march to the Syrian frontier followed the established route across northern Sinai, moving between water points at a pace set by the animals. This is the phase that consumed horses, and an army arriving at the frontier had already lost animals to exhaustion and injury before meeting an enemy. Grazing and grain determined how long a force could stay concentrated in one place.

In theatre the army operated from the Syrian cities, which supplied a base, a market, and fortified shelter. Operations were shaped by season, by the state of the water, and by what the relay system reported about the enemy's movements.

Return was as significant as departure. An army kept in the field is an army consuming revenue and not training, and the political risks of a long absence by the sultan were real, since Cairo without its ruler was Cairo available to a rival. Campaigns were therefore shorter than the geography might suggest, and the strategic pattern favoured a rapid concentration, a decisive engagement, and a return.

That preference for the short decisive campaign is worth noting because it aligns with everything else about the system. An expensive, small, professional army that cannot easily replace losses and whose commanders cannot safely be away for long is an army that wants the battle now, on ground of its choosing, and does not want a war of position.

## The Wars They Lost

An honest account of a celebrated army has to include the defeats, and the Mamluk record contains several that complicate the reputation.

The confrontation with Timur at the end of the fourteenth century went badly. Syrian cities were taken and Damascus was devastated, with much of its skilled population carried off, and the Mamluk response was ineffective at the moment it mattered. The episode showed that the army's performance depended heavily on political cohesion at the top, which was absent at that moment, and that a sufficiently formidable opponent operating with overwhelming force could not be stopped by quality alone.

Campaigns in northern Syria and Anatolia against emerging Turkmen powers in the fifteenth century produced mixed and sometimes poor results, and they consumed resources the treasury could not spare. The pattern of expensive campaigns yielding little is one of the clearer indicators of institutional decline before the Ottoman confrontation.

The naval effort against the Portuguese ended in comprehensive defeat at Diu in 1509, for reasons that were structural and have been described above.

Setting these alongside the victories produces a more accurate picture: an army that was genuinely superb in its own domain and against the kind of opponent it was designed for, and that struggled badly outside those conditions. That is not a criticism unique to the Mamluks. It is the normal condition of highly specialized military systems, and the reason specialization is a gamble on the future looking like the past.

## The Conduct of War

How an army treated the people it fought and the populations it moved through is part of what it was, and the Mamluk record is neither exceptionally brutal nor exceptionally restrained by the standards of the period.

Captured soldiers of standing were held for ransom, which was a normal and substantial part of the economics of medieval warfare across the region. Ordinary captives faced enslavement, which was routine practice on all sides of these conflicts. Cities taken by storm after refusing terms were subject to sack, and cities that surrendered on terms generally were not, which was the widely understood convention and was mostly observed.

The reduction of the Crusader states involved episodes of considerable violence, including massacres following storms, and it also involved negotiated surrenders under which populations were allowed to depart. Both appear in the record and an account that reports only one is misleading.

Treatment of the countryside on campaign followed the universal pattern: an army feeds itself from the land it moves through, and the population of that land suffers accordingly, whether the army is friendly or hostile. Complaints about the behaviour of troops toward Egyptian and Syrian civilians recur in the sources, and they increase in the later period as pay became less reliable, which is the expected relationship.

Stating this plainly is not moralizing at a distance. It is a necessary corrective to a genre of writing about elite medieval soldiers that describes training, equipment, and battlefield skill in loving detail and passes over what the skill was for. These were men trained from boyhood to kill efficiently on horseback, employed by a state that used them to hold territory and extract revenue, and the reputation for excellence should be read with that firmly in view.

## Numbers, and Why They Are Hard to Pin Down

Any statement about the size of a Mamluk army should be treated with caution, and the reasons are worth understanding because they recur across the whole subject.

Chronicle figures for medieval armies are notoriously unreliable, inflated for effect, and often formulaic. Administrative sources are better but incomplete and record entitlements rather than actual strengths, since an amir entitled to maintain a given number of mamluks might in practice maintain fewer. Field armies were smaller than paper establishments because garrisons had to be left, units were detached, and men were sick, absent, or without serviceable horses.

What can be said with reasonable confidence is comparative. The Mamluk field army was small relative to the populations and revenues it drew on, because a professional force trained for a decade each is necessarily small. It was considerably smaller than the Ottoman forces it eventually faced. And its quality-to-quantity ratio was its defining characteristic: an army that wins by having better soldiers rather than more of them.

That structural fact has a consequence that runs through the rest of this article. A small elite force cannot absorb heavy losses, because each casualty represents a decade of investment that takes another decade to replace and money the treasury increasingly did not have. Attrition is the enemy of an army built this way, and the plague waves examined in the account of [the Black Death in Mamluk Egypt](/2012/12/01/black-death-in-mamluk-egypt/) inflicted attrition that no battle did, killing recruits in barracks and serving soldiers alike, repeatedly, across a century and a half.

## Logistics and Movement

An army is only as good as its ability to arrive, and the Mamluk system was built around the specific geography of a state that spanned Egypt and Syria with a desert between them.

The backbone was a relay post system linking Cairo to the Syrian cities, with staged mounts allowing a rider to cover the distance at a speed that gave the sultan intelligence and gave commanders orders far faster than an enemy could move an army. Speed of information is an underrated military asset and the Mamluks invested in it heavily.

Movement of the army itself followed established routes with prepared water points, since the crossing between Egypt and Palestine is the constraining problem for any force operating in both. Water determined the route, the route determined the pace, and the pace determined how much warning a defender had. Fodder for horses was the other governing constraint, and a cavalry army of any size cannot move faster than its grazing and its supply of grain allow.

Campaign seasons were shaped by these facts. Operations clustered when water and grazing were available and when the Syrian frontier situation demanded, and the army wintered where it could be supplied. Sieges, which pin a force in place for months, imposed the heaviest logistical burden of all.

The Egyptian base was the system's great advantage. A secure, agriculturally rich, defensible core, with a fortified command centre described in the account of [the Citadel of Cairo](/2012/01/01/citadel-of-cairo-history/), supported operations in Syria without exposing the heartland. That relationship between a protected base and a contested frontier is the strategic logic the Mamluks inherited and refined, and it is why they could sustain campaigning that wore down opponents operating at the end of longer supply lines.

## The Tactical Pattern

Mamluk battles follow a recognizable shape often enough that the pattern can be stated as a method.

The army deployed in a centre and two wings, with the sultan or the senior commander and the best troops in the centre, and reserves held back. Light auxiliaries screened the front and probed. The engagement opened with missile exchange at range, both sides shooting from the saddle, with formations advancing and withdrawing to maintain distance and pressure.

This phase could last a long time. The aim was not to destroy the enemy with arrows but to disorder him: to kill horses, to thin ranks, to induce a wing to advance too far or to fall back, and to exhaust the discipline that holds a formation together. Superior training told here directly, since maintaining formation while shooting and manoeuvring under fire is precisely what the training system drilled.

When a section of the enemy line loosened, the charge went in. Successive lines of lancers closed against the weakened point, and the aim was penetration and collapse rather than a general push along the whole front. If the charge stalled, units withdrew, reformed, and repeated.

Pursuit followed a break, and this is where a defeated pre-modern army suffers most of its casualties. Mamluk pursuit was vigorous and it is one reason their victories tended to be decisive rather than merely successful.

The pattern's weakness was that it assumed an opponent who fought the same way, on open ground suited to cavalry, and who could be disordered by arrows. Against an enemy whose formation could not be loosened by missile fire, and whose own missile weapons outranged and outperformed the bow, the whole sequence broke down at its first step.

## Ayn Jalut and the Mongol Comparison

The battle that made the Mamluk reputation was fought in 1260 against a Mongol force in Palestine, and the comparison it invites is the most useful one available, because the two armies were closer relatives than the outcome suggests.

Both were steppe-derived cavalry traditions built around the composite bow, mobility, and disciplined formation work. Mamluk recruits came from the same regions that supplied Mongol manpower, and in a real sense the battle was fought between two products of the same military culture, one operating as a conquest empire and the other as a professional standing corps in a settled state.

The differences that mattered were institutional rather than technical. The Mamluks were full-time professionals maintained by a revenue system, better equipped, more heavily armoured, and fighting close to their base with secure supply. The details of the engagement, including the important qualification that the Mongol force present was not the main Mongol army, are examined in the account of [the Battle of Ayn Jalut](/2012/09/01/battle-of-ayn-jalut-mongols/), and that qualification should temper any claim that the Mamluks simply defeated the Mongols.

What the battle and the long frontier war that followed do establish is that the Mamluk system could match the best cavalry army in the world at its own game and sustain a war of attrition against it for decades. That is a genuine achievement and it is the foundation of everything the reputation rests on. The reign that consolidated it is examined in the profile of [Baybars, the greatest Mamluk sultan](/2012/10/01/baybars-greatest-mamluk-sultan/), whose reorganization of the army, the post system, and the frontier defences turned a battlefield victory into a durable strategic position.

## Intelligence, Signals, and Seeing the Enemy

An army that wins by choosing its moment must know what the enemy is doing, and the Mamluk investment in information was as deliberate as its investment in horsemanship.

The relay post system was the backbone. Staged mounts at intervals along the roads between Cairo and the Syrian cities allowed a rider to cover the distance at a speed no army could match, so a threat detected on the northern frontier was known in Cairo within days rather than weeks. The system was maintained at state expense with dedicated stations, keepers, and horses, and its upkeep is recorded as a standing charge.

Carrier pigeons supplemented it for the most urgent traffic, with lofts maintained at stations along the route and messages carried in a fraction of the time a rider needed. The birds were valuable, their handling was a specialized trade, and the sources treat the service as an instrument of state rather than a curiosity.

Signal beacons on high ground provided the fastest warning of all for a simple binary message, at the cost of carrying no detail. A chain of fires could tell the capital that something had happened on the frontier long before anyone could say what.

Human intelligence came from the auxiliaries and from the frontier population. Bedouin allies scouted, reported movements, and knew routes and water, which is one of the reasons the subsidy relationship with them mattered strategically and not merely politically. Merchants moving on the trade routes carried information as a matter of course, and a state that taxed those routes had ready access to the people using them.

The value of all this was concentration. A small professional army defending a large area cannot be strong everywhere and must instead be fast and well informed, arriving in force where the threat is before the threat has developed. Speed of information converts a small army into an effectively larger one, and the Mamluk system understood that clearly.

Its decay in the later period followed the general fiscal pattern. Relay stations that are not funded stop being maintained, tribal allies who are not paid stop reporting, and an army that had compensated for its size with information gradually lost that compensation.

## The Sultan in the Field

Command culture shaped Mamluk warfare as much as doctrine did, and the expectation that the ruler would lead in person is the feature that most distinguishes it.

A Mamluk sultan was expected to be a soldier and usually was, having come up through the same training and the same household system as the men he commanded. He took the field, he was present at the decisive engagements, and his visible participation was part of what held a coalition of household units together. An army assembled from privately loyal formations needs a focus that outranks the individual patrons, and the sultan in person was that focus.

The advantages were real. A commander who had trained as his men trained understood what could be asked of them. His presence at the centre of the line steadied formations. And the political authority to override a quarrelling amir was most effective when exercised on the spot.

The costs were equally real. A sultan on campaign was a sultan absent from Cairo, and the capital without its ruler was the standing opportunity for any senior amir with ambitions, which imposed a permanent time limit on operations. A sultan in the field was also a sultan at risk, and the death or capture of the ruler could convert a difficult battle into a political crisis. The last Mamluk sultan of the independent sultanate died on campaign in Syria, and the immediate consequence was exactly the disintegration that the arrangement always risked.

The deeper problem was succession. Because the throne was not securely hereditary, every commander in the army was a potential claimant, and every campaign brought the senior amirs together in arms with their households around them. That is a structurally dangerous configuration, and the frequency with which Mamluk campaigns were complicated by internal manoeuvring, defection, or outright treachery follows from it directly rather than from any failure of individual character.

## Siege Warfare and the Crusader Campaigns

Cavalry does not take fortresses, and the Mamluk record against the Crusader castles of the Syrian coast shows an army that was considerably more than a cavalry force when it needed to be.

The campaigns of the later thirteenth century systematically reduced the Frankish holdings, and the fortifications involved were among the strongest in the world, designed by engineers who had spent nearly two centuries learning what worked. Taking them required siege engines built on site, mining under walls with galleries propped and then fired to bring the masonry down, sustained bombardment by counterweight trebuchets, and the organizational capacity to keep a large force supplied in one place for weeks or months.

The Mamluks did all of this competently and repeatedly. They maintained corps of engineers, sappers, and artillerists in the pre-gunpowder sense, they transported and assembled machines over difficult ground, and they combined bombardment with mining and assault in a coordinated way. The earlier phase of the same long confrontation, from the Ayyubid side, is treated in the account of [Saladin and the Crusades](/2011/11/01/saladin-and-the-crusades/), and the Mamluk campaigns that finished the job were technically more accomplished.

This matters for the argument because it disposes of a simple version of the conservatism charge. An army that mastered mining, mechanical artillery, and combined siege operations was not incapable of technical adaptation as such. Whatever explains the gunpowder problem, it cannot be a general aversion to engineering or to weapons that were not swung from horseback.

## The Arm They Never Built

Naval power is the clearest gap in the Mamluk military system and the one that had the largest strategic consequence.

The sultanate maintained shipping and could assemble a fleet when required, and it conducted amphibious operations in the eastern Mediterranean, including the reduction of Cyprus to tributary status. What it never possessed was a standing navy of the kind that could contest control of a sea.

The reasons are structural rather than attitudinal. Egypt had almost no shipbuilding timber and had to import it, which made every hull expensive and made large programmes dependent on foreign suppliers who were periodically forbidden by their own authorities to sell. There was no large maritime population from which to recruit crews on the scale a fleet requires. And the entire prestige system of the military elite ran through horsemanship, so naval command offered no route to advancement for an ambitious officer.

The consequences arrived late and were severe. When the Portuguese appeared in the Indian Ocean at the end of the fifteenth century and began interdicting the trade on which the treasury depended, the sultanate had to build a fleet from a standing start, with imported timber and imported guns and Ottoman technical assistance, and send it to fight at the far end of the Red Sea. It achieved an initial success and was then comprehensively defeated at Diu in 1509. A cavalry state can buy ships. It cannot become a naval power in a decade.

## The Gunpowder Question, Part One: What They Did Adopt

The charge that the Mamluks refused gunpowder is the central claim about their decline and it is half right, which means stating it precisely matters.

They did adopt gunpowder weapons in several roles. Cannon appear in Mamluk service for siege work and for fortification defence, and the state maintained artillery establishments and personnel to serve them. Firearms were issued to certain formations. Late in the period a corps of infantry armed with firearms was raised, drawn from groups outside the mamluk military class, including black African troops and others of low status in the military hierarchy.

The adoption was therefore real but confined to categories the elite considered peripheral: static defence, siege operations, and units of low prestige. What did not happen, at any point, was the integration of firearms into the mamluk field cavalry itself or the development of a battlefield doctrine that combined guns and horsemen.

### Did they refuse firearms outright?

No. They used cannon in sieges and fortress defence and raised firearm-equipped infantry from groups outside the military elite. What they never did was arm the mamluk cavalry with firearms or build a field doctrine around them, so guns stayed at the edge of the system rather than at its centre.

The distinction matters because it changes the explanation required. A society that had never seen a gun would need one kind of account. A military class that had guns, used them in some contexts, watched its principal rival build an army around them, and still declined to reorganize needs a different and more interesting one.

## The Gunpowder Question, Part Two: Why the Cavalry Would Not Take Them

Several explanations have been offered and the honest answer combines them, with the fiscal one carrying more weight than it is usually given.

The status explanation is the most cited. Mamluk identity was built on furusiyya. A man's worth, his rank, his income, and his self-understanding rested on a decade of training in horsemanship and archery, and the firearm rendered that investment worthless by allowing a peasant with a few weeks of instruction to kill him at a distance. There are recorded incidents of mamluk troops reacting with contempt and hostility to firearm units, and the reaction is entirely comprehensible: adopting the weapon meant dissolving the basis of their own privileged position.

The technical explanation is real but partial. Early firearms were slow to load, unreliable in damp, dangerous to the user, and difficult to manage on horseback, and a trained horse archer could shoot far faster and, at close range, as accurately. For a period, the professional judgment that the bow was the better cavalry weapon was correct. It stopped being correct as firearms improved and, more importantly, as infantry formations learned to use them in massed disciplined volleys behind field fortifications, which is a tactical system rather than a weapon.

The institutional explanation is the most structural. The Mamluk army was a federation of households whose members were trained in a fixed curriculum and paid through revenue assignments. Reorganizing it around firearms would have required a new kind of recruit, a new training system, new pay arrangements, new units, and a new command structure, imposed on a body of men whose personal and factional loyalties made any centrally directed reorganization politically dangerous. Sultans who tried met resistance.

The fiscal explanation ties the others together. Building a gunpowder army is capital intensive: foundries, powder production, imported metal, specialist personnel, and the pay of a large infantry force alongside the existing cavalry rather than instead of it. The treasury of the later fifteenth century, working with an agricultural base reduced by a century and a half of plague and a commercial position about to be undercut by the Portuguese, could not fund a second army. The choice was not between cavalry and guns. It was between maintaining the force that existed and attempting to build a new one with money that was not there.

## The Frontier Where the Army Lived

Battles are the visible fraction of what an army does, and the Mamluk force spent most of its existence on a set of standing frontier commitments that shaped its character.

The northern frontier in Syria was the principal one and it was permanently live. Beyond it lay, in sequence across the centuries, the Mongol Ilkhanate and its successors, then a shifting collection of Turkmen principalities in eastern Anatolia and northern Mesopotamia, and finally the Ottoman state. The commitment involved garrisons in the Syrian citadels, patrols, punitive expeditions, the management of client and buffer relationships with border powers, and the periodic assembly of the field army when something larger developed.

The southern frontier in Nubia was a different problem, involving expeditions up the Nile, the management of relations with the kingdoms above the cataracts, and the security of the routes that brought gold and slaves north. It never threatened Egypt, and it consumed resources steadily.

The Red Sea and the Hijaz constituted a third commitment, involving the security of the pilgrimage, the protection of the ports through which the trade corridor ran, and the assertion of the sultan's guardianship over the holy cities. This was largely a matter of subsidy, escort, and prestige rather than of major operations, until the Portuguese arrived and turned it into a crisis.

The desert margins of Egypt itself were a fourth, quieter commitment. Control of the routes running through Bedouin territory, the security of the caravan crossings between the Red Sea coast and the Nile, and the policing of Upper Egypt all required a military presence and a working relationship with tribal populations.

The pattern that emerges is of a force stretched across several standing obligations by a state that could concentrate against only one of them at a time. That was manageable while the army was well funded and the frontiers were quiet in rotation. It became progressively less manageable as revenue fell, and the loosening grip on the desert margins that appears in the later sources is the first place the strain showed.

## Why the Reputation Outlasted the Reality

There is a puzzle worth addressing directly at the end, which is why the Mamluk military reputation remained so high through a period when the sources themselves record decline.

Part of the answer is that individual quality really did persist. A trooper trained in the household system in the fifteenth century was still a formidable soldier, and observers meeting one encountered exactly the skill the reputation described. The decline was in depth, in equipment standards across the whole establishment, in the supporting arms, and in the institutions that maintained all three, none of which is visible in a single man on a horse.

Part of it is that reputations are built on the memorable engagements and the memorable engagements were early. Stopping a Mongol force and finishing the Crusader states are the events that fixed the Mamluk image, and they belong to the thirteenth century. Nothing in the fifteenth century displaced them in anyone's memory, because losing a series of expensive inconclusive campaigns in northern Syria makes no impression on anybody.

Part of it is that their opponents had reasons to say so. An Ottoman account of the conquest gains rather than loses by describing the defeated enemy as formidable, and European writers who encountered Mamluk cavalry were describing something genuinely unlike anything at home.

And part of it is that the reputation was correct about the thing it described. The Mamluks were the finest heavy cavalry of their age until the end. The error was never in the assessment of the cavalry; it was in the assumption that having the finest cavalry meant having the strongest army, and that assumption was shared by the Mamluks themselves, which is the whole subject of this article.

## The Maydan and the Culture of Practice

Training needed space, and the ground on which it happened was a significant piece of urban infrastructure that shaped the military culture as much as any manual.

The maydan was a large open riding ground, maintained at state expense, used for mounted drill, archery practice at fixed and moving targets, lance exercises against rings and posts, formation work, and the mounted games that were both training and spectacle. Several were maintained around Cairo, and their upkeep, including the levelling and watering of the surface, appears in administrative sources as a recurring expense.

The games matter more than they look. Mounted exercises in which riders shot at targets set on poles, struck at rings at the gallop, or competed in ball games played from horseback with sticks were competitive, public, and watched by the sultan and the senior amirs. That combination made practice a route to visibility and promotion, which is a far more powerful incentive than an instructor's discipline. A young mamluk who performed well in front of the men who decided careers had a reason to train that no amount of compulsion supplies.

The public dimension also served a political function. Displays of horsemanship, reviews, and mounted games were part of how the regime presented itself, demonstrating to the population of Cairo that the men who governed them were formidable. A ruling class of foreign origin with no dynastic claim benefits from being visibly the best soldiers in the country, and the maydan was where that was demonstrated.

Decline in the maintenance of the training grounds is one of the indicators contemporaries themselves used when complaining that standards were slipping. When a riding ground was built over or allowed to deteriorate, the observation appears in the sources as a criticism, which tells us that the connection between facilities and military quality was understood at the time.

## Horses, Remounts, and the Limits of Supply

A cavalry army is a horse-supply problem with soldiers attached, and the Mamluk system operated permanently close to the edge of what its supply could sustain.

A trooper needed more than one animal. A warhorse trained to stand under noise, close with a formation, and respond in the press is not the same as a riding horse used for movement on campaign, and pack animals were needed besides. Campaign losses were heavy, since horses die of exhaustion, thirst, disease, and injury far more often than they die in combat, and every loss had to be replaced from a supply that Egypt could not generate internally at the required volume or quality.

Imported animals came from Syria, Anatolia, North Africa, and the Arabian interior, purchased with cash, and the trade in warhorses was a substantial commerce in its own right. Domestic breeding existed and the state maintained studs, but the arithmetic never closed without imports.

Two consequences follow. The first is another cash requirement layered on top of recruit purchase, which reinforces the point about why liquid revenue mattered so disproportionately to this state. The second is strategic vulnerability. A military system dependent on foreign horse supply can be squeezed by anyone who controls the routes, and a period of political disruption in the supplying regions translated into a Mamluk remount shortage within a season or two.

The care of horses was accordingly a serious professional discipline rather than a menial task. Veterinary knowledge, feeding regimes, conditioning, and the treatment of injury and disease are covered substantially in the furusiyya literature, and a trooper was expected to know them. That is what a society does when its principal military asset is expensive, perishable, and hard to replace.

## The Halqa, the Auxiliaries, and the Rest of the Army

The mamluk cavalry was the core and it was never the whole force, and the fate of the other components tells its own story about the system's trajectory.

The halqa was a corps of free cavalry, non-mamluk in status, which in the early period was a substantial and respectable body including men of good military background. Over the following centuries it declined markedly. Its pay and its assignments eroded, its membership became a matter of inheritance and purchase rather than of military capacity, and it came to be regarded as a secondary formation whose battlefield value was limited. By the later period the halqa was closer to a payroll category than a fighting arm.

Auxiliary contingents supplied capabilities the heavy cavalry lacked. Bedouin horsemen provided light cavalry, scouting, raiding, and knowledge of desert routes, and their cooperation was purchased with subsidies and privileges. Turkmen and other groups on the Syrian and Anatolian margins supplied additional light horse. Infantry existed for garrison duty, siege work, and the technical trades, drawn from populations outside the military elite.

The pattern across the period is a narrowing. The elite core remained elite while everything around it thinned, so an army that had once been a cavalry corps supported by a range of other capabilities became, increasingly, a cavalry corps with much less around it. That narrowing is a large part of why the confrontation with a genuinely combined-arms opponent went as badly as it did, and it happened gradually enough that no single decision can be blamed for it.

The subsidy relationship with the tribal auxiliaries also degraded as revenue fell, and the sources record deteriorating control of the desert margins and the routes running through them. An army that cannot pay its light cavalry loses its eyes.

## Pay, Discipline, and Mutiny

Professional soldiers must be paid, and the Mamluk pay system was a recurring source of instability that had direct military consequences.

Troopers received a combination of regular pay, revenue assignments, allowances for equipment and horses, and periodic bonuses. The most consequential of these was the accession payment, a substantial distribution to the troops on the elevation of a new sultan, which had become an expectation that no incoming ruler could refuse without immediate danger.

The consequences of that expectation were corrosive. A sultan's first act was to empty a large part of the treasury, which meant every succession imposed a fiscal shock, and in a period of frequent successions the shocks compounded. It also gave the troops a structural interest in changes of ruler, which is close to the worst incentive a state can create in its own army.

Discipline within a household was strong, resting on the personal bond between a mamluk and his patron and on the cohesion of the purchase cohort. Discipline across the army was weaker, because there was no single authority whose orders overrode household loyalty when the two conflicted. Riots by unpaid or aggrieved troops in Cairo are a recurring feature of the sources, and they were dangerous, since the rioters were the best-armed and best-trained men in the country.

The military effect of all this was cumulative rather than dramatic. An army periodically distracted by factional violence, whose fiscal position was shaken at every succession, and whose senior commanders were as often rivals as colleagues, cannot maintain the standards of an army that is left alone to train. Contemporary critics said as much, and their complaints about slipping standards should be read as observations about institutional decay rather than about the courage of individual men.

## The Arsenal: Making and Supplying the Weapons

An army of this kind consumes equipment continuously, and the industrial base behind it is a part of the system that battle narratives never mention.

Bows were the most demanding item. A composite bow takes a long time to build, since horn and sinew must be laminated and the glue allowed to cure over an extended period before the weapon is serviceable, and a bowyer's output per year is therefore limited. Bows also fail, from damp, from age, and from use, and a force that shoots in the volumes described above needs a steady replacement supply. That production could not be improvised in an emergency, which meant maintaining a standing craft capacity in peacetime.

Arrows were consumed faster than anything else. Shafts, fletching, and heads each required their own trades, arrows were manufactured in bulk, stored in state arsenals, and transported to campaign in quantity, and the running down of arrow stocks appears in the sources as a real constraint on operations. An army that fights by sustained missile exchange is an army whose logistics are dominated by ammunition long before gunpowder arrives.

Armour and edged weapons were produced by specialist workshops, some in Egypt and some in Syria, with Damascus in particular carrying a long reputation for blade production. Metal itself had to be imported, since Egypt was poorly supplied with iron ore, which added a strategic dependency and connects the arsenal directly to the commercial relationships discussed earlier.

State arsenals stored and issued equipment, maintained reserves, and handled the repair and refurbishment that any army generates. The administrative sources record these establishments as significant institutions with staff and budgets.

The vulnerability in all of this is the same one that appears everywhere in the Mamluk system. Imported metal, imported timber, imported horses, imported recruits: a military machine assembled largely from things that had to be bought abroad with cash. That is workable while the cash flows and it becomes a cascade of shortages when it stops.

## Reviews, Inspections, and the Measurement of Decline

The Mamluk state periodically inspected its own army, and the records of those inspections are among the most useful evidence available for how the system changed over time.

A review assembled troops with their horses and equipment before the sultan or his officers, who checked that a man had the mounts, arms, and armour his rank required and that his mamluks were present in the numbers his entitlement specified. The exercise was fiscal as much as military, since assignments were granted on the basis of maintaining a given establishment and the state had an interest in verifying that the establishment existed.

What the sources report about these reviews over time is a steady drift. Complaints appear about men presenting inadequate equipment, about amirs maintaining fewer mamluks than their rank required, about horses of poor quality, and about the general slippage of standards. In the later period the complaints become more frequent and more specific.

Reading these as straightforward evidence of decline requires some care, since every army in every century has produced officials complaining that standards were better before. What makes the Mamluk case more persuasive is the corroboration. The complaints track the independently documented fall in revenue, the recorded decline of the training grounds, and the observable narrowing of the army's non-elite components, and they come from within a system that had strong reasons to present itself favourably.

The picture that emerges is not sudden collapse but a long thinning, in which the elite core remained impressive while the depth behind it, the equipment standards, and the maintenance of the institutions that produced quality all eroded gradually. An army in that condition can still win engagements against opponents of its own type, which the Mamluks did, and will fail badly against a fundamentally different opponent, which is what happened.

## What the Ottomans Kept

A useful final test of how good this army was is what the power that destroyed it chose to do with the wreckage.

The Ottomans did not disband the Mamluk military tradition in Egypt. They incorporated surviving households into the provincial order, permitted continued recruitment by purchase from the Caucasus, and used Mamluk cavalry as a component of Egyptian provincial forces. Over the following centuries those households grew back into the dominant power in the province.

The Ottomans also did not adopt Mamluk methods anywhere else, and that combination is the informative part. They valued the cavalry for what it was, in a province where cavalry of that kind was useful, and they had no intention of restructuring their own army around it, because their own army had already solved the problem the Mamluk system could not.

The judgment implied is precise and it matches the verdict of this article. Mamluk heavy cavalry was a genuinely excellent instrument, worth preserving, worth continuing to produce, and worth deploying. It was not a military system capable of standing alone against a combined-arms opponent, and the people best placed to assess that, having beaten it, acted accordingly.

## What a Reader Should Be Able to Argue

Five propositions carry the argument of this article and separating them from the supporting detail makes the whole subject easier to hold.

The first concerns the training. Mamluk soldiers were produced by a decade or more of institutional, supervised, progressive instruction in horsemanship, mounted archery, the lance, and the sword, documented in a substantial technical literature that has no equivalent for most medieval armies. The quality claim rests on this and it is well supported.

The second concerns the method. Mamluk battles followed a pattern of prolonged missile exchange to disorder an enemy formation, followed by a charge delivered against a loosened line, with repeated controlled charges rather than a single mass effort. The sequencing, made possible by troopers who were both archers and lancers, is what distinguished them tactically.

The third concerns the cost. A trooper represented a purchase price, a decade of maintenance, imported horses, imported metal, and continuous pay, all of it requiring cash. That is why an army of this kind is small, why it cannot absorb attrition, and why the commercial revenue of the state mattered out of all proportion to its share of total income.

The fourth concerns the gunpowder gap and it needs the precise form. The Mamluks adopted gunpowder for sieges, fortress defence, and low-status infantry units, and never integrated it into the mamluk field cavalry or built a doctrine around it. Four factors explain the gap together: status, since firearms devalued the skill the elite was built on; technology, since early firearms genuinely were inferior cavalry weapons for a time; organization, since a federation of loyal households resists central reorganization; and money, since a second army could not be funded from a contracting revenue base.

The fifth is the verdict with its limits. The Mamluks were the finest heavy cavalry of their age and were destroyed by a combined-arms system, and the completeness of their optimization for one form of war is what made adaptation to another impossible. That is a claim about institutional design, not about courage, and anyone arguing it should concede that standards had also genuinely declined and that other cavalry powers did manage to adapt.

## What 1516 and 1517 Actually Showed

The two decisive engagements against the Ottomans, in northern Syria in 1516 and outside Cairo in early 1517, are usually summarized as guns beating horses. That is roughly right and it is worth being more precise, because the details identify which part of the Mamluk system failed.

The Ottoman army was a combined-arms force. It fielded janissary infantry armed with firearms, fighting in disciplined formation behind field fortifications and wagon barriers, supported by field artillery, with its own cavalry on the wings. It was also considerably larger and it was better supplied for a sustained campaign.

Against that combination the Mamluk method had no first move. The opening phase of a Mamluk battle depends on loosening an enemy formation with missile fire, and an entrenched infantry line behind obstacles does not loosen. The charge that followed had to be delivered against an unbroken position, into artillery and massed firearms, which is the most expensive thing heavy cavalry can be asked to do. The courage of the men is not in question in any account, Ottoman or Egyptian, and courage was not the variable.

Politics compounded the tactical problem. Defection and division within the Mamluk command played a significant part in the campaign, which is exactly the vulnerability that a federation of personally loyal households carries. The sequence of events and the specific circumstances of both battles are examined in the account of [how the Ottomans conquered Mamluk Egypt](/2013/04/01/ottoman-conquest-of-mamluk-egypt/).

The conclusion the engagements support is narrow and firm. The Mamluk soldier was not outfought as an individual. The Mamluk system was outmatched as a system, by a different system that had solved the problem of combining infantry firepower, artillery, and cavalry into one force, and that had the fiscal base to pay for all three at once.

## The Money Argument

The fiscal thread deserves to be pulled out and stated on its own, because it is the part of the explanation most often left out and it changes the character of the verdict.

Consider what the Mamluk military establishment cost. Purchase of recruits abroad, in cash, from foreign suppliers. A decade of maintenance and instruction for each. Arms, armour, and remounts, continuously. Pay, funded through assignments on agricultural revenue. Campaign costs. Accession payments to the troops on every change of sultan, which were substantial and unavoidable.

Now consider what happened to the income supporting it. Agricultural revenue declined across the later fourteenth and fifteenth centuries as the cultivated area contracted, for reasons rooted in demographic loss. Commercial revenue was pressed harder and harder, then undercut by the Portuguese route at the very end. Currency was debased. The domestic tax base shrank further as property migrated into religious endowment.

A state in that position has no capacity for a capital programme. Building a gunpowder army does not save money; it costs money for a generation before it repays anything, and it must be funded alongside the existing force during the transition, since disbanding the cavalry before the new army exists is suicide. The Ottomans built their combined-arms system on an expanding revenue base with a growing population. The Mamluks would have had to build theirs on a contracting one.

That does not excuse the conservatism, which was real, and it does not mean nothing better was possible. It does mean that framing the failure as a simple matter of proud horsemen refusing to learn is a story about attitudes when a large part of the explanation is a balance sheet.

## Were They the Best Cavalry of Their Age?

The question is worth answering directly because it is what most readers arrive wanting to know, and the answer needs to be split.

As individually trained heavy cavalry, the claim is strong. No contemporary force put its soldiers through a comparable curriculum for a comparable duration with comparable institutional support, and the combination of mounted archery and shock action in the same trooper was unusual and effective. Contemporary observers on every side of every conflict rated them highly, which is the closest thing to independent confirmation available.

As a cavalry army in the field, the claim is strong for the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries and weaker afterward. The Mamluks matched the Mongols in a long attrition war, destroyed the Crusader states, and dominated their region for a long period, which is a record few cavalry forces can match. Fifteenth-century performance is patchier, with campaigns that went badly and a general decline in the condition of the establishment that contemporaries themselves noticed and complained about.

As a military system, the claim fails by the end. Cavalry quality is one component of military capability, and a force that cannot combine arms, cannot field infantry firepower, and cannot project at sea has capability gaps that superb horsemen do not close. Judging an army by its best arm is like judging a football team by its best player.

The fair verdict is that the Mamluks were the finest heavy cavalry of their age and that being the finest heavy cavalry stopped being sufficient during their tenure. Both halves of that sentence are needed.

## After 1517: The Cavalry That Did Not Die

The conquest ended the sultanate and did not end the Mamluks, which is one of the more surprising facts about the whole subject and it bears on the assessment of their military value.

The Ottomans, having destroyed the Mamluk state, retained Mamluk households as a component of the provincial military and administrative order in Egypt. Recruitment by purchase from the Caucasus continued. Training continued. Over the following centuries these households recovered substantial power within Ottoman Egypt, to the point of dominating the province in practice, a story followed in the account of [the Mamluk beys of Ottoman Egypt](/2013/09/01/mamluk-beys-of-ottoman-egypt/).

Two conclusions follow. The first is that the military tradition was valued by the power that had defeated it, which is the strongest possible endorsement of its quality within its own domain. A conqueror who eliminates a rival army and then keeps recruiting and training it in the same way is making a judgment about its usefulness.

The second is that the same limitation reappeared. Mamluk cavalry in Ottoman Egypt remained excellent heavy cavalry and remained without a combined-arms system around it, and when a modern combined-arms force arrived at the end of the eighteenth century, the outcome was a repeat of 1517 in compressed form. The pattern held for another three hundred years, which suggests the problem was structural in the way this article has argued rather than a failure of one generation's judgment.

## How We Know: The Manuals and Their Limits

The evidence for Mamluk military practice is unusually rich, and knowing what each kind of source can and cannot support is what separates a careful account from a confident one.

The furusiyya literature is the distinctive body of material and it has no real equivalent for most medieval armies. These are technical treatises on archery, lance work, cavalry drill, horsemanship, and horse management, some written under Mamluk patronage, describing method in detail: how to hold, how to draw, how to release, what to practise, in what order, against what targets. Several are illustrated. They are written by people who plainly knew the practice.

Their limitation is that a manual describes an ideal. It tells us what the training system was supposed to produce, not what a given cohort in a given decade actually achieved, and a treatise composed at one moment may describe practices that had already lapsed or that were aspirational. Reading manuals as straightforward reportage overstates the uniformity of the system across two and a half centuries.

The chronicles supply the corrective. Egyptian historians record campaigns, reviews, complaints about standards, riots, equipment shortages, and the condition of the training grounds, which is exactly the material needed to check the manuals against practice. Their weakness is the usual one: numbers are unreliable and battle narratives are shaped by hindsight and by the politics of the writer.

Administrative sources record entitlements, assignments, and expenditures, which anchor questions of size and cost better than any narrative, while measuring paper strength rather than effective strength.

Material evidence, surviving arms, armour, and bows in collections, together with illustrated manuscripts, confirms construction and form. Its limitation is survival bias, since high-status pieces preserved as treasures are over-represented relative to what an ordinary trooper carried.

### How reliable is the picture we can build?

Strong on method, equipment, and organization, because the manuals and administrative records are specific and mutually corroborating. Weaker on numbers, on how far practice matched prescription in any given decade, and on the experience of the ordinary soldier, whose voice appears nowhere in the surviving material.

## Three Comparisons

Setting Mamluk cavalry against its principal contemporaries is the fastest way to see what was distinctive, and three comparisons do most of the work.

Against the European knight, the difference is versatility and institution. A knight was a shock cavalryman of high individual quality, trained privately from boyhood, whose tactical role was the charge. A mamluk was a shock cavalryman who was also a horse archer, trained in a state institution to a standardized curriculum, serving full time. The knight belonged to a social order that produced soldiers as a byproduct; the mamluk belonged to a system that existed to produce soldiers and nothing else. On open ground the Mamluk combination of missile and shock action gave a commander options that a force of knights did not have.

Against the Mongol horseman, the difference runs the other way. Mongol armies were built on light and medium horse archers operating with formidable strategic mobility, in enormous numbers by the standards of the region, sustained by a way of life rather than a budget. Mamluks were heavier, better armoured, better equipped, fewer, and much more expensive per man, and they fought close to a secure base. The Mamluk advantage was equipment, professional consistency, and supply; the Mongol advantage was numbers, mobility, and the ability to operate far from anywhere.

Against the Ottoman army, the difference is the one that decided everything. Ottoman forces combined disciplined firearm infantry, field artillery, and cavalry within a single command structure funded by an expanding revenue base. The Mamluks fielded one arm, superbly. Comparing a cavalry corps with a combined-arms army is comparing a component with a system, and the outcome of that comparison was demonstrated twice within a year.

The general lesson is that military quality is not a single scale. A force can be the best in the world at what it does and be beaten decisively by a force that is worse at that particular thing and better at combining several things.

## The Case Against the Thesis

The perfect-cavalry-wrong-century thesis needs testing against the serious objections, and there are three.

The first is that it flatters the Mamluks. Fifteenth-century performance was not uniformly excellent, contemporaries complained repeatedly about declining standards, the training system was not maintained at earlier levels, and campaigns went badly against opponents who were not fielding gunpowder armies. If the army was already degraded, then the story is one of ordinary institutional decay and the gunpowder question is a detail rather than the hinge.

The second is that it lets conservatism off too lightly. Other states with cavalry traditions and comparable status hierarchies did adapt. The Ottomans themselves were a cavalry power that built an infantry and artillery arm alongside their horsemen. If they could, the argument runs, so could the Mamluks, and the fiscal excuse understates how much of the resistance was a choice.

The third is evidential. The claim that firearms could have been integrated rests on a counterfactual that cannot be tested, and the claim that the fiscal position made it impossible rests on revenue figures that are reconstructions rather than accounts.

These objections deserve concessions. Standards did decline and the article should not present a static picture of unbroken excellence. The conservatism was a choice and the men who made it had reasons that were about status as much as about capability. The fiscal argument rests on inference.

What survives is the core. The Mamluks lost to a combined-arms force while remaining, by contemporary testimony on both sides, superb heavy cavalry, and the reason they had not become a combined-arms force is traceable to a training system, a status structure, an organizational form, and a revenue position that all pointed the same way. That is a stronger claim than the alternative, which is that they simply got worse, and it explains more, including why the same limitation reappeared for three centuries afterward under different rulers.

## The Verdict

The thesis holds in this form. The Mamluk military system was an extraordinary achievement in producing individual soldiers, sustained across two and a half centuries by a training institution with no contemporary equal, and it delivered results that justify the reputation: the Mongol frontier held, the Crusader states finished, a region dominated for a very long time.

The same system contained the reasons it could not evolve. A decade of training per man makes soldiers who are hard to replace and expensive to maintain, which makes an army small and attrition-intolerant. A status hierarchy built on horsemanship makes the weapon that devalues horsemanship unacceptable to the men who would have to carry it. A federation of personally loyal households makes centrally directed reorganization politically hazardous. And a revenue base eroded by demographic collapse and then by the loss of a commercial monopoly makes a capital programme unaffordable at exactly the moment it becomes necessary.

None of those four is a moral failing and all four point in the same direction, which is why the outcome looks overdetermined in hindsight and was not obvious at the time. The wider question of how a corps of purchased soldiers came to rule a country at all, and what that arrangement did and did not achieve, is taken up in the discussion of [how a slave army ruled Egypt](/2013/03/01/how-a-slave-army-ruled-egypt/).

The transferable point is worth naming. A military system optimized to the limit for one form of war has no slack for another, and the completeness of an optimization is a measure of how hard adaptation will be. That is not a lesson about horsemen. It is a lesson about what happens when an institution becomes very good at something and then the question changes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What made the Mamluk army so powerful?

Three things in combination. First, individual skill: each trooper spent a decade or more in institutional, supervised training in horsemanship, mounted archery, the lance, and the sword, at a standard no levy or feudal force could approach. Second, equipment: Mamluks were heavily armoured by regional standards, with mail and lamellar for the man and barding for the horses in the heaviest formations, and carried a full weapon set rather than specializing, so one unit could fight as missile cavalry and shock cavalry in the same engagement. Third, cohesion: recruits were purchased abroad as boys, lived and trained together in barracks, and served in households bound by personal loyalty to a patron and to the cohort bought alongside them, which produced formations that held together under pressure. Holding together under pressure is what usually decides a pre-modern battle.

### Q: How were Mamluk warriors trained to fight?

A boy purchased at around ten to twelve was housed in barracks with his purchase cohort and began a graded curriculum under full-time instructors. Riding came first, since a man insecure in the saddle cannot shoot or fight from it. Mounted archery was the technical core and took longest to master, drilled against fixed and moving targets at a walk and then at speed. Lance work used suspended rings, posts, and mounted opponents. Swordsmanship progressed from fixed targets to sparring. Alongside these ran horse care, veterinary knowledge, bow maintenance, arrow manufacture, formation drill, and religious and literacy instruction. Training ran a decade or more before manumission and enrolment as a trooper, which is closer to a modern military academy than to anything else in the medieval world. Mounted games watched by the sultan made practice a visible route to promotion.

### Q: What weapons did the Mamluks use?

A trooper was equipped to fight at three ranges. At distance he shot a composite bow, built from a wooden core with horn laminated on the belly and sinew on the back, glued, bound, and cured over a long period, which stores far more energy for its length than a plain wooden bow and is therefore short enough to use from horseback. Release was with the thumb, protected by a ring. For the charge he carried a heavy lance, used couched and also employed in thrusts, sweeps, and parries at close quarters. In the press he drew a single-edged curved sabre, and mace or axe appear alongside it, both useful against armour since a blow that fails to cut can still break what lies beneath. He also carried a small shield manageable while holding reins, and a dagger as a last resort.

### Q: Were the Mamluks the best cavalry in the world?

As individually trained heavy cavalry the claim is strong. No contemporary force put its soldiers through a comparable curriculum for a comparable duration with comparable institutional support, and the combination of mounted archery and shock action in one trooper was unusual and effective. Observers on every side of every conflict rated them highly, which is the closest thing to independent confirmation available. As a cavalry army in the field the claim holds for the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries and weakens afterward, since fifteenth-century performance was patchier and contemporaries themselves complained about slipping standards. As a military system the claim fails by the end, because a force that cannot combine arms, cannot field infantry firepower, and cannot project at sea has gaps that superb horsemen do not close.

### Q: What was furusiyya?

Furusiyya is the whole complex of horsemanship and martial skill that the Mamluk training system existed to produce, covering both the practical disciplines and the written tradition that codified them. Its four principal branches were riding, archery from horseback, the lance, and the sword together with other close-quarters weapons. Around them ran supporting subjects: care and management of horses, veterinary knowledge, the maintenance of bows and manufacture of arrows, the wearing and upkeep of armour, and elements of tactics and formation drill. The written tradition matters independently, since a substantial body of furusiyya treatises survives covering archery technique in detail, lance exercises, cavalry drill, and horse management, some produced under Mamluk patronage. These texts are technical, specific, and clearly written by practitioners, which is why Mamluk training can be described rather than inferred from battle outcomes.

### Q: How did the Mamluks win their battles?

By a recognizable sequence rather than by manoeuvre or surprise. The army deployed in a centre and two wings with the best troops and the commander in the centre and reserves held back, screened by light auxiliaries. The engagement opened with prolonged missile exchange, both sides shooting from the saddle, with formations advancing and withdrawing to maintain pressure. The aim was not to destroy the enemy with arrows but to disorder him: kill horses, thin ranks, draw a wing out of position, and exhaust the discipline that holds a formation together. Superior training told directly here, since maintaining formation while shooting and manoeuvring under fire is exactly what the curriculum drilled. When a section of the enemy line loosened, successive lines of lancers charged that point. Vigorous pursuit after a break is why their victories tended to be decisive.

### Q: Why did Mamluk military power decline?

Through a long thinning rather than a sudden collapse. Revenue fell as the cultivated area contracted after repeated plague, so the assignments that funded troopers, horses, and equipment produced less, and inspection records show men appearing with inadequate kit and amirs maintaining fewer mamluks than their rank required. The training grounds were less well maintained and contemporaries said so. The non-elite components narrowed: the halqa decayed from a real fighting arm into something closer to a payroll category, and subsidies to tribal auxiliaries became harder to pay, costing the army its light cavalry and its scouts. Accession payments to the troops emptied the treasury at every succession, and factional violence between households distracted an army that needed to be training. The elite core stayed impressive while everything around it eroded.

### Q: Did the Mamluks resist gunpowder weapons?

Partly, and the precise form matters. They did adopt gunpowder: cannon appear in Mamluk service for siege work and fortress defence, the state maintained artillery establishments, and late in the period a corps of firearm-equipped infantry was raised from groups outside the mamluk military class. What never happened was the arming of the mamluk field cavalry with firearms or the development of a battlefield doctrine combining guns and horsemen, so gunpowder stayed at the edge of the system rather than at its centre. Four factors explain that together: status, since firearms let a briefly trained man kill someone who had trained for a decade; technology, since early firearms genuinely were poor cavalry weapons for a time; organization, since a federation of loyal households resists central reorganization; and money, since a second army could not be funded from a contracting revenue base.

### Q: How large was the Mamluk army?

Smaller than the reputation suggests and harder to state than most accounts admit. Chronicle figures for medieval armies are notoriously inflated and often formulaic. Administrative sources are better but record entitlements rather than actual strengths, since an amir entitled to maintain a given number of mamluks might maintain fewer, and inspection complaints show that happening. Field armies were smaller still, because garrisons had to be left, units detached, and men were sick, absent, or without serviceable horses. What can be said confidently is comparative: the Mamluk field army was small relative to the population and revenue it drew on, because a force trained for a decade per man is necessarily small, and it was considerably smaller than the Ottoman armies it eventually faced. An army that wins by better soldiers rather than more of them cannot absorb heavy losses.

### Q: What horses did Mamluk cavalry ride?

A mixture, drawn from several sources because Egypt could not supply enough at the required quality. Warhorses came from Syria, Anatolia, North Africa, and the Arabian interior, purchased with cash, alongside domestic breeding and state studs that never closed the gap. A trooper needed more than one animal, since a warhorse trained to stand under noise and close with a formation is not the same as a riding horse for movement on campaign, and pack animals were needed besides. Losses were heavy and mostly non-combat, from exhaustion, thirst, disease, and injury. Horse care was therefore a serious professional discipline rather than a menial task, with feeding regimes, conditioning, and veterinary treatment covered substantially in the furusiyya literature. The dependence on imported bloodstock was a permanent strategic vulnerability as well as a permanent cash requirement.

### Q: How did Mamluk archers shoot from horseback?

With a composite bow drawn and released using the thumb, protected by a ring, which is standard across the Asian archery traditions and permits a fast draw and release from an awkward seat. The manuals describe grip, draw, anchor, release, and follow-through in detail, along with drills for shooting to either side, to the rear, and at targets on the ground, practised first at a walk and then at speed. The tactical point was volume and sustainability rather than a single decisive volley: accurate arrows delivered while mounted, at a rate the manuals treat as a measurable standard, for long enough to disorder an enemy formation. That imposed a logistical requirement most accounts skip, since sustained shooting consumes arrows by the tens of thousands and a force fighting this way must carry them in bulk and resupply during the engagement.

### Q: What armour did a Mamluk trooper wear?

Mail was the core defence, worn over padding and sometimes combined with lamellar or with plates integrated into a mail garment in the later period. Helmets were iron with mail aventails protecting the neck, and arm and leg defences varied with wealth and period. The heaviest formations armoured their horses too, with quilted or mail barding protecting front and flanks, which is what allowed a formation to close through missile fire that would otherwise have shredded unprotected mounts. All of it was individually owned and individually maintained, funded from a trooper's pay and from the revenue assignment attached to his position, and inspection records complain about men appearing with inadequate equipment. That arrangement pushes cost down to the individual, which means any fall in state revenue transmits into a measurable fall in military effectiveness within a few years.

### Q: How was the Mamluk army organized into units?

As a federation of households rather than a unitary force with a modern chain of command. At the centre stood the royal mamluks, the sultan's own purchased corps, trained at the citadel and forming the largest and best-equipped single body. Around them were the units of the amirs, senior officers whose rank was defined by the number of mamluks they were entitled to maintain, each running his own household with its own recruits, training, and loyalties. Alongside both stood the halqa, a body of free non-mamluk cavalry, with auxiliary contingents of Bedouin and Turkmen supplying light horse and scouts. The arrangement produced excellent cohesion within units, since household members shared training, origin, and personal obligation, and it produced fragility across the whole, since an army of privately loyal units can be turned against itself.

### Q: What was the halqa?

The halqa was a corps of free cavalry, non-mamluk in status, which in the early Mamluk period was a substantial and respectable body including men of good military background and served as a genuine second line alongside the purchased elite. Over the following centuries it declined markedly. Its pay and revenue assignments eroded, membership became a matter of inheritance and purchase rather than of demonstrated military capacity, and it came to be regarded as a secondary formation of limited battlefield value. By the later period it functioned closer to a payroll category than a fighting arm. Its decay is one of the clearest indicators of the wider narrowing of the Mamluk military system, in which the elite core stayed impressive while the depth behind it thinned, leaving a cavalry corps with progressively less around it.

### Q: Did the Mamluks ever fight on foot?

Yes, though it was never the identity of the force and never the arm the elite belonged to. Siege operations required men on the ground for mining, for working machines, and for assault, and the Mamluk reduction of the Crusader castles involved all of that conducted competently and repeatedly. Garrison duty in the Syrian citadels was infantry work. Technical trades attached to the army, sappers, artillerists in the pre-gunpowder sense, and engineers, operated dismounted. Late in the period firearm-equipped infantry units were raised, drawn from groups outside the mamluk military class. What did not exist was a prestigious, well-funded, tactically integrated infantry arm that a mamluk would want to join, and that absence is precisely the gap the Ottoman janissaries occupied on the other side.

### Q: How did the Mamluks supply an army on campaign?

Around water and fodder, which set the route and the pace. Movement between Egypt and the Syrian frontier followed established roads with prepared water points across northern Sinai, and a cavalry force cannot move faster than its grazing and grain allow. Provisions, arrows, spare mounts, tents, and baggage were assembled at the muster, and state arsenals held reserves of arrows and equipment for issue. In theatre the army operated from the Syrian cities, which supplied a base, a market, and fortified shelter. A relay post system linked Cairo to those cities, giving commanders intelligence and orders far faster than an enemy could move, which is an underrated asset. Campaigns tended to be short and decisive, partly because a sultan absent from Cairo for long risked finding it occupied by a rival.

### Q: How did a Mamluk cavalry charge actually work?

In successive lines rather than as one mass, and delivered late rather than as an opening move. The formation closed in close order against a specific weakened point in the enemy line, aiming at penetration and collapse there rather than a general push along the whole front, and if a charge stalled the line withdrew through or around the one behind it, reformed, and went in again. Repeated controlled charges are far harder to execute than a single all-in effort and require exactly the drill the training system produced. The timing is the distinctive part: charging an intact formation is expensive while charging a loosened one is decisive, so Mamluk practice was to thin and disorder with missile fire first, which converted a costly frontal action into a comparatively cheap exploitation.

### Q: Why did Ottoman guns beat Mamluk horsemen?

Because the Mamluk method had no first move against them. The Ottoman force was a combined-arms army: firearm infantry fighting in disciplined formation behind field fortifications and wagon barriers, supported by field artillery, with cavalry on the wings, and it was larger and better supplied for a sustained campaign. A Mamluk battle depends on loosening an enemy formation with sustained missile fire, and an entrenched infantry line behind obstacles does not loosen. That forced the charge to be delivered against an unbroken position into artillery and massed firearms, which is the most expensive thing heavy cavalry can be asked to do. Defection and division within the Mamluk command compounded the tactical problem, which is exactly the vulnerability a federation of personally loyal households carries. Courage was not the variable in any account, Ottoman or Egyptian.

### Q: Did the Mamluks ever use cannon themselves?

Yes. Cannon appear in Mamluk service for siege work and for the defence of fortifications, and the state maintained artillery establishments with personnel to serve them. The sultanate also imported guns and technical assistance, notably from the Ottomans, when building a Red Sea fleet to contest the Portuguese presence in the Indian Ocean at the start of the sixteenth century. The adoption was real and it was confined to roles the military elite considered peripheral: static defence, sieges, naval use, and units of low prestige. Guns never entered the mamluk field cavalry and no doctrine was developed to combine them with horsemen on the battlefield. That distinction changes the explanation required, since a military class that had guns, used them in some contexts, and still declined to reorganize needs a more interesting account than simple ignorance.

### Q: How do we know what Mamluk training involved?

Chiefly from a substantial surviving technical literature, which is a rarity for any medieval army. Furusiyya treatises cover archery technique, lance exercises, cavalry drill, horsemanship, and horse management in specific practical detail, some produced under Mamluk patronage and several illustrated, and they were plainly written by people who knew the practice. Their limit is that a manual describes an ideal rather than what a given cohort actually achieved in a given decade. The Egyptian chronicles supply the corrective, recording campaigns, reviews, equipment shortages, riots, and complaints about slipping standards. Administrative records anchor questions of size and cost by documenting entitlements, assignments, and expenditure. Surviving arms, armour, and bows in collections confirm construction and form, with the usual caveat that high-status pieces preserved as treasures are over-represented.
