The Places of Worship Act 1991 politics begins with a single legislative act of unusual architectural ambition: on 18 September 1991 the Parliament of India received presidential assent for a short statute of eight sections that froze the religious character of every temple, mosque, church, gurdwara, monastery and other place of public worship in the country at the character each held on 15 August 1947, barred the conversion of any such place from one denomination to another or from one section of a denomination to another section, extinguished pending lawsuits that sought such conversion, forbade fresh lawsuits of that kind, and then carved out exactly one great exception, the disputed site at Ayodhya, which the statute named and released from its own prohibitions so that a litigation older than the Republic could run a course Parliament chose not to interrupt. The statute was the first major legislative initiative on religious place politics taken by the minority government of Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao, formed in June 1991 after the assassination of Rajiv Gandhi during the general election of that year and after the fall of the V. P. Singh government in November 1990 and the brief Chandra Shekhar government that followed it, and it was carried through the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha in September 1991 with support that crossed party lines and against a walkout by the Bharatiya Janata Party, whose Ayodhya movement stood at the height of its mobilisation. This article defends one claim about that statute, and the claim is architectural rather than biographical or electoral: the 1991 Act established a status quo architecture for religious places in India, a designed arrangement of four load bearing features consisting of a character freeze dated to Independence, a jurisdictional bar that removed character questions from the courts, a single named exception for Ayodhya, and a set of carve outs for monuments and settled disputes, and the litigation that opened at Varanasi in 2021, at Mathura in 2022 and at Sambhal in 2024 tests whether that architecture can withstand a procedural mechanism its drafters did not name, the court ordered survey conducted to ascertain historical character without formally changing character at all.

## A Minority Government Builds the Places of Worship Act 1991

To see why a government without a Lok Sabha majority spent its first parliamentary season on a statute about shrines, the sequence of the preceding twenty four months has to be held in view, because the Act answered a mobilisation rather than a courtroom file. The Vishva Hindu Parishad campaign for a Ram temple at the disputed site in Ayodhya, carried into national politics by the Bharatiya Janata Party under L. K. Advani, had by 1990 converted a title suit pending since the 1950s into the organising question of north Indian electoral competition, a transformation traced in the account of [the religious-political-mobilisation-substantive reading](/2020/04/25/ayodhya-movement-reanalysed/) of that movement's method. Advani's rath yatra from Somnath in September 1990, his arrest at Samastipur on 23 October 1990 on the orders of Bihar Chief Minister Lalu Prasad Yadav, the withdrawal of Bharatiya Janata Party support that brought down the V. P. Singh government on 7 November 1990, and the police firings on kar sevaks at Ayodhya on 30 October and 2 November 1990 together demonstrated that a dispute over one shrine could dissolve governments, and the Chandra Shekhar government that held office from November 1990 to March 1991 with Congress support negotiated over Ayodhya without settling it. The Rao government that took office on 21 June 1991 inherited that demonstration, and its legislative answer was preventive rather than remedial: if Ayodhya was to remain an exception, the exception had to be fenced, and the fence had to be built before any litigant or movement treated the Ayodhya dispute as a transferable precedent for Kashi, Mathura or any other contested site. The Rao government's wider method in that season, a minority administration legislating structural settlements in economics and in religious place politics within the same quarter, is set out in the account of [the Rao government's institutional-political context](/2019/05/26/1991-rao-government-silent-reforms/), and the Places of Worship Bill belonged to the same governing instinct, settling by statute a question that negotiation had failed to settle.

The legislative timetable was compressed. The Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha in August 1991 by the Union Home Minister, Shankarrao Chavan, passed both Houses in September 1991, received the assent of President R. Venkataraman on 18 September 1991 as Act 42 of 1991, and was deemed to have come into force on 11 July 1991, a retrospective commencement that closed any window in which anticipatory litigation might have been filed between the government's formation and the statute's enactment. The Act carried a long title that stated its purpose without ornament: an Act to prohibit conversion of any place of worship and to provide for the maintenance of the religious character of any place of worship as it existed on 15 August 1947, and for matters connected therewith or incidental thereto. Every element of the design followed from that long title. The prohibition looked forward, forbidding conversion after commencement. The maintenance clause looked back to a fixed date and held character constant from that date. The incidental machinery, abatement of suits, bars on jurisdiction, penalties and an overriding clause, existed to make the first two elements enforceable rather than declaratory. A reader who treats the Act as a press statement issued by a frightened government misses the drafting discipline visible in that sequence, because each section performs a distinct mechanical function inside a single design, and the sections interlock: the forward prohibition would leak without the bar on suits, the bar on suits would be unjust without the exceptions, and the exceptions would swallow the design without the penalties and the overriding effect that Sections 6 and 7 supply.

The political authorship of the Act was wider than the Congress party, and that breadth matters to the architectural reading because architecture claims durability only when more than one builder accepts the plan. In the September 1991 debates the Janata Dal and the parties of the Left supported the prohibition while pressing the government on the Ayodhya exception and on enforcement, the Bharatiya Janata Party opposed the Bill and its members walked out of the Lok Sabha during the proceedings, and the government defended the exception on the stated ground that the Ayodhya litigation predated the statute and involved questions Parliament would leave to the courts and to negotiation. Home Minister Chavan's speech in the Lok Sabha on 10 September 1991, quoted at length twenty eight years later in the Supreme Court's Ayodhya judgment of 9 November 2019, framed the Bill as a healing measure in the government's own words: a measure to provide and develop the country's traditions of love, peace and harmony, set within a post Independence endeavour of healing the wounds of the past and restoring traditions of communal amity and goodwill, with the candour to admit setbacks and the resolve to take lessons from them for the future. That framing was advocacy by the Bill's sponsor, and the opposition's counter framing, that the statute legislated amnesia about conquest era destructions and denied Hindu, Jain, Buddhist and Sikh worshippers a judicial remedy available to every other class of claimant, was stated in the same debates and has been restated in the constitutional challenges filed since 2020; both framings are examined on their own terms in later sections of this article, because the architectural reading does not require either framing to win, and requires only that the design be described accurately before it is praised or condemned.

### Which shrines did Parliament actually place under the freeze?

Section 2 of the Act defines a place of worship as a temple, mosque, gurdwara, church, monastery or any other place of public religious worship of any religious denomination or any section of a denomination, by whatever name called, and the freeze therefore covers living shrines of every faith in public worship, not a list of named disputed sites.

The definitional choice deserves emphasis because later litigation has turned on it repeatedly. Parliament did not schedule particular mosques or temples for protection, as a dispute settlement statute might have done after the manner of a boundary award, and it did not protect buildings as heritage fabric, which the Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains Act 1958 already did for notified monuments. It protected a legal attribute, religious character, wherever that attribute attached to a place of public worship, and it fixed the attribute at a date chosen for constitutional symbolism rather than for antiquarian convenience: 15 August 1947 was the day on which British sovereignty ended and on which the Constituent Assembly's India began, the same settlement moment examined in [the contested-settlement framework](/2020/04/09/indian-secularism-contested-settlement/) for the wider religion and state bargain of the founding. By attaching the freeze to Independence Day, the drafters tied the statute to the Republic's founding premise that citizenship and worship would be ordered without regard to the century in which a claimant's ancestors arrived or a conqueror ruled, a premise the Supreme Court's five judge bench would quote back to the nation in November 2019. A freeze dated to 1528, to 1669 or to any year of construction or destruction would have required the courts to conduct exactly the historical inquiries the statute was drafted to foreclose. A freeze dated to 1947 required only one inquiry, what a place was on that day, and then forbade even that inquiry from being used to disturb possession and worship, which is why the date is the architecture's foundation stone rather than an administrative detail.

## The Statute Section by Section: Conversion Barred, Character Frozen

Section 3 of the Act, headed as a bar of conversion of places of worship, provides that no person shall convert any place of worship of any religious denomination or any section of the same religious denomination into a place of worship of a different religious denomination or of a different section of the same religious denomination. The drafting reaches further than a prohibition on turning a temple into a mosque or a mosque into a temple, because the intra denominational limb forbids converting a shrine of one section of a faith into the shrine of another section of that same faith, a limb that speaks to Shia and Sunni waqf disputes, to denominational contests within Hindu temple traditions, and to church property schisms as much as to Hindu and Muslim contestation. Section 3 is the Act's operative prohibition, the conduct rule against which the penalty in Section 6 is aimed, and it is deliberately framed in terms of conversion, a completed alteration of character, rather than in terms of inquiry, excavation or belief about a site's past. That verbal choice, conversion rather than ascertainment, became thirty years later the hinge on which the Gyanvapi litigation turned, when a Supreme Court bench observed in May 2022 that the Act does not prohibit ascertaining what the religious character of a place is, and the whole post 2022 trajectory of the statute flows through the gap between those two verbs.

Section 4 carries the design's centre of gravity, and it repays slow reading because summaries of the Act, including the deep brief from which this series works, have misassigned its subsections often enough that precision here is a substantive and not a pedantic matter. Section 4(1) declares that the religious character of a place of worship existing on 15 August 1947 shall continue to be the same as it existed on that day. Section 4(2) supplies the jurisdictional consequence: if, on the commencement of the Act, any suit, appeal or other proceeding with respect to the conversion of the religious character of any place of worship existing on 15 August 1947 was pending before any court, tribunal or other authority, the proceeding abates, and no suit, appeal or other proceeding with respect to any such conversion shall be instituted or commenced in any court, tribunal or other authority, subject to a proviso that a proceeding may still be instituted where the conversion complained of took place after 15 August 1947 and before the Act's commencement. The Ayodhya exception does not sit in Section 4 at all. It sits in Section 5, which provides that nothing contained in the Act shall apply to the place or place of worship commonly known as Ram Janma Bhumi Babri Masjid situated in Ayodhya in the State of Uttar Pradesh and to any suit, appeal or other proceeding relating to that place. The carve outs for monuments and closed disputes sit in Section 4(3), which disapplies the abatement and the bar for a place of worship that is an ancient and historical monument or an archaeological site or remains covered by the Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains Act 1958, for a suit, appeal or proceeding finally settled or disposed of by a court, tribunal or other authority before the Act's commencement, for a dispute settled by the parties before commencement, and for a conversion effected by acquiescence before commencement. Getting that map right matters, because the post 2022 litigants and courts have argued subsection by subsection: the Gyanvapi mosque committee invoked Section 4(2), the Hindu plaintiffs answered with the ascertainment distinction, and the monument carve out in Section 4(3)(a) framed the Archaeological Survey of India's role at Gyanvapi and at Sambhal, where the protected status of the Jama Masjid under the 1958 Act entered the pleadings directly.

Sections 6, 7 and 8 complete the machinery. Section 6(1) makes contravention of Section 3 punishable with imprisonment for a term that may extend to three years, together with liability to a fine, and Sections 6(2) and 6(3) extend the same punishment to attempt, abetment and criminal conspiracy, punishable whether or not the offence is committed in consequence of the abetment or in pursuance of the conspiracy, a drafting severity that signals Parliament's expectation that conversions would be collective enterprises rather than solitary acts. Section 7 gives the Act overriding effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent contained in any other law in force or in any instrument having effect by virtue of any law, a clause whose practical consequence is that a claimant cannot route around the bar through a differently framed statute, a waqf instrument, a trust deed or a municipal record. Section 8 amended Section 8 of the Representation of the People Act 1951 to add conviction under Section 6 of the 1991 Act to the offences carrying disqualification from electoral office, welding the shrine freeze into the law of democratic representation itself. Read as a whole, the eight sections describe a legislature that intended finality: a date, a prohibition, an extinguished docket, a penalty, an override and an electoral consequence, and the thirty years in which almost no litigation tested the Act suggest that the machinery worked as machinery, whatever judgment history finally passes on the politics that built it.

### How does Section 4 stop a lawsuit before it begins?

Section 4(2) works in two directions at once: every suit, appeal or proceeding about conversion of a shrine's religious character that stood pending on 11 July 1991 abated automatically, and no fresh proceeding of that description may be instituted in any court, tribunal or other authority, except a claim that a conversion occurred between 15 August 1947 and the Act's commencement.

The proviso inside Section 4(2) is the subsection's most litigated sentence in the post 2022 cases, and it is routinely misdescribed as a general invitation to sue over any shrine with a contested past. Its words permit proceedings only where the conversion of religious character is alleged to have taken place after the freeze date and before commencement, that is, where a shrine that was one thing on 15 August 1947 was made another thing during the Republic's first forty four years; a claim that a mosque of 1947 stands over a temple destroyed in 1528 or 1669 alleges a conversion completed centuries before the freeze date and falls outside the proviso on the plaintiffs' own pleading. The district courts at Varanasi and Mathura nevertheless allowed the Gyanvapi and Mathura suits to proceed on a different theory, that ascertaining character is not converting character, and the Allahabad High Court sustained that theory in December 2023 and August 2024 rulings examined later in this article. The distinction between those two theories, the proviso theory and the ascertainment theory, is the distinction on which the Act's survival as an effective bar depends, and it is a distinction Parliament never debated in terms, because no speaker in September 1991 foresaw the survey as the instrument through which character would be investigated and, the mosque committees argue, effectively adjudicated in public before any decree could pass.

## Four Features: The Status Quo Architecture Named

The first feature of the architecture is status quo protection fixed at 15 August 1947. Legislatures asked to quieten shrine disputes have three broad instruments available: they can award each disputed site to a claimant after inquiry, they can divide sites between claimants, or they can freeze possession and character at a chosen date and forbid inquiry from disturbing either. The 1950 settlement of the Ayodhya site's internal arrangement, locks placed inside the Babri Masjid on the night of 22 and 23 December 1949 and worship administered thereafter by state appointed receivers, had already shown Indian administrators reaching for the third instrument at a single site, and the administrative handling of that night and its aftermath supplies [the religious-place-state-administration template](/2020/04/14/1949-babri-masjid-lock-up-politics/) against which the 1991 statute can be measured as the same instinct written at national scale. What Parliament did in Section 4(1) was to generalise the receiver's lock into a rule of law for every shrine in the country: character as on Independence Day continues, full stop. The substantive generosity and the substantive harshness of the Act are the same property viewed from opposite sides: generosity, because every community in possession of its shrines in 1947 holds them under statutory protection against majoritarian revision; harshness, because a community that lost a shrine before 1947, in the centuries of conquest, conversion and temple destruction that fill the pleadings in the Mathura and Varanasi suits, receives no remedy at all, and is told by Section 4(1) that the loss is constitutionally invisible. Faizan Mustafa, writing on the post Gyanvapi trajectory in essays published after 2022, has defended precisely this harshness as the price of a secular state's refusal to arbitrate sacred history, arguing that a Constitution which promises equal citizenship cannot make rights depend on the archaeology of the sixteenth century. Ashwini Kumar Upadhyay, the petitioner in the lead constitutional challenge filed in 2020, states the opposing position at its strongest in his own pleading: that the Act legislates a cut off that denies Hindus, Jains, Buddhists and Sikhs the judicial forum every other rights holder retains, and thereby abridges Articles 25 and 26 of the Constitution by protecting possessions acquired, on his account, through invasion era destruction. The architecture description accommodates both: the freeze is genuinely neutral between communities in possession, and genuinely indifferent to losses suffered before the freeze date, and any honest adjudication has to hold those two truths together rather than select one.

The second feature is the jurisdictional bar: the removal of the character question from judicial resolution. Section 4(2) does not merely declare a policy and invite courts to respect it; it abates pending proceedings by operation of law and forbids the institution of fresh ones, converting a substantive rule into a docket rule, so that a civil judge confronted with a character suit in 1992 or 2002 had no discretion to exercise and no evidence to record. For three decades the bar functioned almost invisibly, which is the strongest evidence of its effectiveness: the Ayodhya title suits proceeded because Section 5 lifted them out of the Act, a handful of proceedings survived under the Section 4(3) carve outs, and the flood of Kashi, Mathura and analogous claims that polemicists on every side predicted in 1991 did not reach the courts, because registry officials and district judges could point to a subsection and refuse the file. The bar also had a constitutional dimension that the Supreme Court would later articulate: by legislating non retrogression, Parliament had not usurped the judicial function but exercised the legislative function of settling the legal consequences of a historical date, the same kind of settlement legislatures enact in limitation statutes, land ceiling laws and abolition of privy purses. Petitioners challenging the Act answer that a bar which prevents a court from even examining whether a shrine was converted by force is not a limitation rule but an ouster of judicial review, and judicial review, on the doctrine settled after Kesavananda Bharati in 1973 and restated in Minerva Mills in 1980, forms part of the basic structure that Parliament cannot abrogate even by constitutional amendment, let alone by ordinary statute. That exchange, ouster versus settlement, is the doctrinal core of the pending challenge, and it cannot be resolved by describing the architecture; it can only be framed accurately, which the architectural reading does by identifying the bar as a feature with a purpose, protection against serial litigation, and a cost, the foreclosure of claims that a court has never heard on merits.

The third feature is the Ayodhya exception in Section 5, the architecture's single deliberate opening. Its drafting is absolute: nothing in the Act applies to the place commonly known as Ram Janma Bhumi Babri Masjid, or to any suit, appeal or other proceeding relating to it. The exception is often described as a concession extracted by the Ayodhya movement's strength, and the parliamentary record supports a colder reading: the Ayodhya suits were forty years old in 1991, a receiver held the site, a state government had acquired land around it, and a statute that extinguished those suits by abatement would have detonated the very conflict the Bill existed to contain, while handing the movement a martyrdom by legislation. The exception therefore quarantined the one dispute too advanced to freeze and froze every other dispute in the country, trading completeness for containment. The demolition of the Babri Masjid on 6 December 1992, fifteen months after the Act, occurred inside the excepted zone and is treated in this series as [the Ayodhya-specific exception's post-2019 consummation](/2020/05/01/1992-demolition-liberhan-commission/), culminating in the Supreme Court's title judgment of 9 November 2019; the architectural point is that the demolition, catastrophic as it was for the exception's own site, did not breach the architecture elsewhere, and no second demolition followed at Kashi or Mathura in the 1990s although both sites were named in the same movement slogans. Critics answer that the quarantine taught a lesson opposite to the one intended: that mobilisation is rewarded with exceptions while patience receives a bar. Defenders answer that the counterfactual, a 1991 statute abating the Ayodhya suits outright, would have produced confrontation on a scale the Republic was fortunate to avoid, and that the exception was priced into the consensus that carried the Bill through a hung Parliament. The architectural reading records the exception as a designed feature with a defensible rationale and a real moral cost, and notes that every post 2022 plaintiff has sought, in effect, a judicially granted Ayodhya exception for a new site, which is precisely the transfer the exception's narrow drafting, one named place and no general principle, was built to refuse.

The fourth feature is the set of carve outs in Section 4(3), of which the monument carve out in Section 4(3)(a) is the most consequential for the post 2022 cases. A shrine that is also an ancient and historical monument or an archaeological site or remains covered by the Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains Act 1958 stands outside the abatement and the bar, because Parliament legislated in 1991 against the background of a 1958 statute that already committed protected monuments to the Archaeological Survey of India for preservation, documentation and regulated access. The distinction embedded in the carve out is between a living shrine, whose character the 1991 Act freezes, and a protected monument, whose fabric the 1958 Act commits to archaeological custody, and many of India's contested structures are both at once: the Gyanvapi mosque stands within a complex of intense archaeological interest, the Sambhal Jama Masjid is a protected monument under the 1958 Act, and the Shahi Idgah at Mathura adjoins the Katra Keshavdev complex whose excavation history runs back to the nineteenth century. The carve out's interaction with the survey question is subtle and has been underargued in public commentary: an Archaeological Survey of India study of a protected monument is an ordinary statutory function under the 1958 Act, while a court ordered survey of a living shrine in a character suit is an evidentiary step inside litigation the 1991 Act purports to bar, and the post 2022 orders at Gyanvapi and Sambhal blurred that line by commissioning survey machinery, advocate commissioners first and the Survey itself at Gyanvapi in 2023, inside suits rather than under the monuments statute. Alongside the monument carve out, Section 4(3) preserves suits finally decided before commencement, disputes settled by the parties, and conversions effected by acquiescence before commencement, three closure respecting exceptions that show the drafters distinguishing between open wounds, which the Act closes by force, and closed files, which it leaves closed by consent or by judgment. A statute bent on pure amnesia would not have bothered with such distinctions; the carve outs are the signature of draftsmen who expected their text to be read closely by courts, as in the event it has been, subsection by subsection, three decades later.

The four features can be set out as a citable map, which this article names the Four-Feature Status Quo Map, so that the pillar's later articles and any reader's notes can refer to the design by its parts rather than by slogans for or against it.

## The September 1991 Debates: Consensus, Walkout and a Quoted Speech

The parliamentary debates of September 1991 are the Act's under cited primary source, preserved in the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha debate transcripts held by the Parliament Library, and they matter to the architectural reading because the builders explained the building while building it. Home Minister Shankarrao Chavan, moving the Bill for consideration in the Lok Sabha on 10 September 1991, presented the measure as preventive statecraft in the passage the Supreme Court later chose to reproduce: the government saw the Bill as a measure to provide and develop the country's glorious traditions of love, peace and harmony, within an endeavour after Independence of healing the wounds of the past and restoring traditions of communal amity and goodwill to their past glory, acknowledging setbacks and drawing lessons from them for the future. The Statement of Objects and Reasons accompanying the Bill used plainer language about the mechanism, recording that the government proposed to freeze the status of places of worship as on 15 August 1947 in the interest of communal harmony, and the debate speeches returned repeatedly to the fear that had organised the drafting: that the Ayodhya dispute, if treated as a method rather than as a file, would be replicated at every site where medieval destruction could be alleged, which in India means hundreds of sites, and that the Republic would spend its courts and its police budgets on the archaeology of grievance. Speakers for the Janata Dal and the Left supported the freeze while criticising the government for weakness on the Ayodhya exception and for the acquisition politics of the Uttar Pradesh government of Kalyan Singh, whose administration had taken land around the disputed site in 1991 and whose assurances of protection for the Babri Masjid were being tested in real time as the debate proceeded. The debate therefore joined two questions that public memory has since separated: the freeze, which commanded a wide consensus, and Ayodhya, which commanded none, and the statute's final shape, freeze plus exception, is the arithmetic of that division preserved in law.

The opposition's case against the Bill was stated most forcefully by the Bharatiya Janata Party, whose members walked out during the Lok Sabha proceedings and whose leader L. K. Advani had made the Ayodhya movement the axis of his party's rise from two Lok Sabha seats in 1984 to 120 in 1991. The party's stated objections, recorded in the debates and restated in its later manifestos, were threefold: that the Act penalised the victims of medieval temple destruction for the acts of destroyers, that a legislature elected in 1991 had no moral warrant to extinguish claims rooted in wrongs that worshipping communities had preserved in memory for centuries, and that the Ayodhya exception conceded the very principle, that the site of a destroyed temple could be restored to worship, which the Act denied at every other site. Those objections were not answered in 1991 by refutation so much as by majority, and they survive intact in the pleadings of the 2020s, which is one reason the post 2022 litigation reads like a continuation of the September 1991 debate by forensic means. A. G. Noorani, whose The Babri Masjid Question 1528-2003, published in 2003, assembled the documentary record of the Ayodhya dispute from the title suits onward, treated the 1991 Act as the one legislative achievement of the Rao season that deserved defence on secular grounds, and his post 2022 essays returned to the statute to argue that the survey litigation was an attempt to achieve by procedure what the Act forbade by substance. The scholarly division visible in the debate chamber in 1991, between a healing consensus that regarded the past as a closed account and a restorative opposition that regarded closure as legislated injustice, has not narrowed since; it has changed venue, from the Lok Sabha to the district courts of Varanasi, Mathura and Sambhal and to a Constitution Bench's pending file in the Supreme Court, and the architectural reading's task is to show how a design built for the first venue behaves under the pressures of the second.

One further element of the 1991 record deserves extraction, because the post 2022 argument about surveys depends on what the drafters did and did not foresee. The September 1991 debates contain no discussion of court ordered surveys, scientific investigations or advocate commissioners as instruments for probing a shrine's past; the forensic imagination of 1991 ran on documents, waqf records, revenue entries and oral testimony, and the archaeological dimension of shrine litigation had been present at Ayodhya since the court ordered excavation debates of the 1970s without entering the Bill's text. The Act therefore neither authorises surveys nor forbids them in words, and that silence, unremarkable in 1991, became the statute's most consequential gap: the plaintiffs of 2021 and 2022 argued that silence is permission, that a court which cannot decree conversion can still find facts; the mosque committees argued that silence is irrelevant, that a survey ordered to find whether a mosque was once a temple is a character proceeding in substance whatever its label, and that Section 4(2)'s bar on proceedings with respect to conversion covers proceedings whose only conceivable decree would prepare a conversion claim. Both arguments are genuinely available on the text, which is why the architectural reading, rather than either party's advocacy, best describes the position: the design's prohibitions were drafted against conversion and against character proceedings, and the survey arrived as a mechanism parallel to both, testing whether a prohibition on outcomes also prohibits the evidentiary road toward those outcomes. Legislatures usually repair such gaps by amendment; the politics of the 2020s, in which amendment in either direction carries electoral costs that governments have declined to pay, has instead left the repair to courts, and the courts' answers, interim order by interim order, form the trajectory traced in the sections that follow.

## Three Decades of Quiet: The Architecture at Work, 1991 to 2019

Between the demolition of December 1992 and the Supreme Court's Ayodhya judgment of November 2019, the Places of Worship Act performed its function in the manner of a locked door in a corridor nobody photographs: the absence of events was the event. The Ayodhya suits, exempted by Section 5, proceeded through the Allahabad High Court's Lucknow bench, through the court ordered Archaeological Survey of India excavation of 2003, and through the three judge judgment of 30 September 2010 that divided the disputed site three ways, while at Kashi and Mathura, where movement rhetoric had paired the names with Ayodhya in the slogan that Ayodhya was only a glimpse and Kashi and Mathura remained, no title suit survived the registry. A suit filed at Varanasi in 1991 by devotees associated with the Kashi Vishwanath temple, asserting that the Gyanvapi mosque stood on the plinth of a destroyed Vishvanath temple, was itself caught in procedural suspension for over two decades, stayed by the Allahabad High Court in 1998 on the question whether the Act barred it, and that stay became emblematic of the era: the file existed, the grievance was recorded, and the architecture held the file motionless. The Liberhan Commission, appointed ten days after the demolition and reporting on 30 June 2009 after seventeen years and forty eight extensions, documented the Ayodhya catastrophe in prosecutorial detail, and its very remit confirmed the design's logic, since the commission inquired into a demolition at the excepted site, while no commission of inquiry was ever needed for a second site, there being no second demolition to inquire into. Historians of the coalition era note that governments of every complexion between 1996 and 2014, including the Bharatiya Janata Party led governments of Atal Bihari Vajpayee from 1998 to 2004, left the Act unrepealed and unamended, although repeal had figured in the movement's demands; the Vajpayee government's coalition arithmetic and its National Agenda for Governance excluded the temple agenda items, and the statute's survival under a government formed by the party that had walked out against it in 1991 is a datum the restorative argument has to absorb, just as the defence has to absorb the datum that quiescence owed something to the Ayodhya suits' own unfinished state, which kept the movement's hopes invested inside the exception rather than against the freeze.

The deeper explanation of the quiet decades lies in the interaction between the Act and the electoral system. A shrine dispute frozen by statute yields no litigation milestones around which mobilisation can be scheduled, no hearings to photograph, no verdicts to celebrate or mourn, and political entrepreneurs rationally invested elsewhere: in the Ayodhya exception while it lasted, and after 2014 in the instruments of a single party majority government whose religious place politics ran through citizenship law, conversion law and temple administration rather than through character suits, a renegotiation of the founding tensions described in [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/). The technique of legislating religious identity categories returned to Parliament in December 2019 with the Citizenship Amendment Act, whose own architectural reading, religion as a statutory classification tested against the equality code, is developed in [the architectural-substantive question reading](/2020/05/17/citizenship-amendment-act-debate/) of that statute's debate; the contrast instructs, because where the 1991 Act used classification to freeze conflict, the 2019 Act used classification in a manner its critics describe as distributing conflict, and the courts' handling of each has differed accordingly. What ended the quiet was not a political campaign against the Act but the exhaustion of the exception: once the Supreme Court decided Ayodhya on 9 November 2019 and the temple construction began under the trust the judgment mandated, the mobilisation energy and the forensic method refined at Ayodhya over seventy years, inscriptions, excavation, survey, title, became available for redeployment, and the Act's bar stood between that energy and its next objects. The judgment itself, however, had spoken about the Act in terms that raised the bar's constitutional stature even as the exception closed, and the terms it chose frame every post 2022 courtroom exchange.

## The Ayodhya Judgment Reads the Act: Non-Retrogression as Constitutional Value

The Constitution Bench judgment in M. Siddiq (deceased) through legal representatives versus Mahant Suresh Das, delivered unanimously on 9 November 2019 by a five judge bench headed by Chief Justice Ranjan Gogoi, was a title judgment about one site at Ayodhya, and the Places of Worship Act was not under challenge before the bench, a limit the judges themselves recorded. Within that limit, the bench devoted a sustained passage to the Act, reproduced Section 5 in its reasoning, and read the statute as a legislative expression of constitutional commitments that outran the Ayodhya file. The judgment held that the Act's express prohibition on conversion speaks to the future by mandating that the character of a place of public worship shall not be altered, and that the statute imposes a positive obligation on the state to maintain the religious character of every place of worship as it existed on 15 August 1947, when India achieved independence from colonial rule. It described the Act as an affirmation of the solemn duty cast upon the state to preserve and protect the equality of all faiths as an essential constitutional value, a norm with the status of a basic feature of the Constitution, and it characterised the statute in the sentence that defenders have quoted in every subsequent proceeding: the Places of Worship Act is a legislative intervention which preserves non-retrogression as an essential feature of India's secular values. Non-retrogression, borrowed from the vocabulary of human rights law where it names the prohibition on rolling back realised rights, here named the principle that the Republic would not re-open the religious geography it inherited at Independence, and the bench anchored the principle in the Home Minister's September 1991 speech, quoting Shankarrao Chavan's healing words from the Lok Sabha record of 10 September 1991 as evidence of the statute's animating purpose.

### What did the Supreme Court say about the Act on 9 November 2019?

The five judge bench held that the Act speaks to the future by forbidding alteration of a shrine's character, imposes a positive duty on the state to preserve every shrine's character as on 15 August 1947, and preserves non-retrogression as an essential feature of secular values and a basic feature of the Constitution.

The same passage disposed of the deepest version of the restorative claim, the claim that courts should entertain grievances arising from the acts of medieval rulers against the shrines of another faith. The bench held, in sentences that the mosque committees at Varanasi and Mathura have since read aloud in court, that the court cannot entertain claims that stem from the actions of rulers of the past against places of worship in a court of law in the present, that for any person who seeks solace or recourse against the actions of any number of ancient rulers the law is not the answer, and that the adoption of the Constitution marked a watershed at which the people of India departed from the determination of rights and liabilities on the basis of ideology, religion, ancestry or the century of arrival, and submitted instead to the rule of law. Hindu plaintiffs answer, with textual accuracy, that these passages are observations in a judgment that expressly left the Act's validity unexamined, that observations about a statute's spirit cannot decide a later challenge to its letter, and that the title method the bench itself applied at Ayodhya, weighing centuries of evidence about worship and possession, is the method they seek at sites the Act covers. Both readings of the 2019 passage are professionally respectable, and the architectural reading accommodates the tension by classifying the passage correctly: it is a constitutional benediction pronounced over the architecture by the highest court, obiter in form and weighty in effect, which raised the political and doctrinal cost of disturbing the freeze without legally foreclosing the challenge that Ashwini Kumar Upadhyay had already prepared. The benediction and the challenge thus proceeded on parallel tracks from 2020 onward, the benediction quoted by defenders as near settlement, the challenge pressed by petitioners as the unexamined question, and between them the district courts of Uttar Pradesh began, in 2021 and 2022, to receive the suits that turned benediction and challenge into a live procedural war over surveys.

## Gyanvapi: The Survey Becomes the Argument

The Gyanvapi mosque stands against the Kashi Vishwanath temple in Varanasi, in a complex whose layered history the pleadings contest at every point: the plaintiffs' case, descending from devotees' claims recorded since the eighteenth century and pleaded in the suit of 1991, holds that the Vishvanath temple on the site was destroyed on the orders of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb in 1669 and the mosque raised over its plinth, with the temple's western wall and pillars retained in the mosque's fabric; the Anjuman Intezamia Masajid, the committee that manages the mosque, answers that the mosque has stood and been prayed in for over three centuries, that its character on 15 August 1947 and continuously since is that of a mosque, and that the 1991 Act forecloses the entire inquiry. Maharani Ahilyabai Holkar rebuilt the Kashi Vishwanath temple beside the mosque in 1780 under Maratha patronage, and worship at the temple and prayer at the mosque proceeded in enforced adjacency through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, punctuated by the communal violence of 1809 and by post Independence arrangements of barricading and police protection that made the complex one of the most heavily guarded shrine sites in India. In August 2021 five women devotees filed a suit in the Varanasi civil court seeking a declaration of their right to worship the deities Shringar Gauri and other visible and invisible deities within the complex through the year, an apparently modest claim about access to shrines located on the mosque's outer wall, which nevertheless required the court to look inside the complex, and the procedural history that followed converted that modest claim into the vehicle for the survey question. In parallel, the revived 1991 suit, in which the deity Swayambhu Jyotirlinga Bhagwan Vishweshwar sought a declaration that the mosque complex was temple property, was held by the Allahabad High Court on 9 September 2021 to require determination of whether the Act barred it, keeping both files alive as the access suit advanced.

The survey entered through an application for local investigation. On 8 April 2022 the civil judge (senior division) at Varanasi appointed an advocate commissioner, Ajay Kumar Mishra, to inspect and videograph the Gyanvapi complex in the women devotees' suit; the mosque committee's objections that the exercise was a character inquiry in disguise were rejected, the survey began on 6 May 2022 amid protests and was stalled, and on 12 May 2022 the court ordered the survey to resume and to cover the basement rooms the committee had resisted opening. On 16 May 2022, the final day of the resumed survey, the plaintiffs announced the discovery of a structure they identified as a shivling in the mosque's ablution tank, the wazukhana; the civil court sealed the area on the plaintiffs' application the same day; the committee answered that the object was a fountain mechanism of the ablution tank, an attribution dispute, fountain or shivling, that remains unresolved on the merits and that this article records without adjudication, since the parties' characterisations are the procedural facts and the object's identity is the substantive question no court has finally decided. The Supreme Court, approached by the committee, ordered on 17 May 2022 that the sealed area be protected without restricting Muslim worshippers' access to the mosque, and on 20 May 2022 transferred the suit to the district judge at Varanasi on account of its sensitivity and complexity. In that 20 May hearing came the observation around which the entire post 2022 trajectory pivots: Justice D. Y. Chandrachud, speaking for the bench, observed orally that ascertaining the religious character of a place is not barred by Sections 3 and 4 of the Act, a distinction between finding character and changing character that the bench framed as consistent with the statute's text, since Section 3 prohibits conversion and Section 4(1) commands continuity, and neither verb prohibits knowledge. The mosque committees and the Act's defenders answered in print and in later hearings that the observation, oral and interim in form, had the practical effect of an authorisation, because district courts across northern India took it as a signal that survey applications in character suits would survive the Act's bar; Suhrith Parthasarathy, writing in the Economic and Political Weekly after the Gyanvapi orders, argued that the ascertainment distinction dissolves on inspection, since a suit whose only prayers seek declarations about a mosque's temple past has no purpose except to unsettle the character the Act froze, and a survey inside such a suit is a proceeding with respect to conversion within Section 4(2)'s meaning whatever the order sheet calls it.

The forensic escalation continued on a judicial timetable. On 12 September 2022 the district judge at Varanasi rejected the mosque committee's application under Order 7 Rule 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure and held the women devotees' suit maintainable, reasoning that the Act does not bar a suit that seeks worship rights and factual determination rather than a declaration converting the mosque. On 21 July 2023 the district court ordered a scientific survey of the complex by the Archaeological Survey of India, excluding the sealed wazukhana area; the Allahabad High Court upheld the survey on 3 August 2023; the Supreme Court on 4 August 2023 declined to stay it while recording the Survey's assurance that no excavation or invasive method would be used and the structure would not be harmed; and the Archaeological Survey of India's report, submitted in December 2023 and unsealed to the parties in January 2024, concluded on the basis of inscriptions, sculptural fragments and architectural members that a large Hindu temple had existed at the site before the mosque's construction, a conclusion the mosque committee contested as exceeding the Survey's brief and as incapable in law of altering the mosque's protected character. On 19 December 2023 the Allahabad High Court, deciding the batch of petitions around the 1991 suit, directed that the suits proceed to trial and rejected the contention that the Act disposed of them at the threshold, while the trial of the access suit and the title suit continued at Varanasi without final decree. The procedural state at the close of the period this article covers is therefore precise and unsatisfying to every party: surveys ordered, conducted and reported; maintainability upheld at two levels; no decree altering the mosque's character, possession or worship; the Act neither held to bar the suits nor held inapplicable to them; and the Supreme Court's interim discipline of December 2024, arising from the Sambhal violence and the constitutional challenge, superimposed over the whole field, as the Mathura and Sambhal sections and the challenge section of this article record. What Gyanvapi established by accumulation rather than by ratio is the survey's career as a parallel mechanism: a procedure that yields findings of immense public consequence, an Archaeological Survey conclusion read on television as a verdict, while formally deciding nothing, and the architectural question the mechanism poses is whether a design that prohibits outcomes can survive a procedure that manufactures the outcomes' factual predicates in public, one commission at a time.

## Mathura: The Shahi Idgah Suits and the Maintainability Turn

The Mathura litigation replicated the Varanasi pattern with a local history of its own, and its paper trail begins earlier than the standard post 2022 framing suggests. The Shahi Idgah mosque at Mathura stands beside the Krishna Janmasthan temple complex at Katra Keshavdev, on land whose twentieth century title history includes a compromise: in 1968 the Sri Krishna Janmasthan Seva Sangh and the Shahi Idgah management committee settled their dispute by an agreement under which the mosque continued at its site and the temple complex developed around the remaining land, a settlement later embodied in a compromise decree of the Mathura civil court in 1974. On 25 September 2020 a suit filed in the name of the deity Bhagwan Srikrishna Virajman and devotees including the advocate Ranjana Agnihotri sought to annul that compromise decree and to remove the mosque as an encroachment on the deity's birthplace; the civil judge dismissed the suit on 30 September 2020 on locus and maintainability grounds; a Mathura district judge admitted the appeal and revived the suit on 19 May 2022; and a succession of further suits by devotees, temple trusts and advocates followed, thirteen or more in the count placed before the Allahabad High Court, each seeking declarations about the Idgah's character and possession that the mosque committee and the Sunni Central Waqf Board answered with Section 4(2): barred at the threshold, and barred additionally by the parties' own 1968 settlement, which Section 4(3) instructs courts to respect where a dispute was settled before commencement. The plaintiffs' answers, stated at their strongest in the Mathura pleadings, are that a compromise procured in the political conditions of 1968 cannot bind the deity or future worshippers against a mosque that, on their case, occupies the sanctum of Krishna's birthplace following destruction ordered under Aurangzeb in 1670, and that the Act cannot constitutionally protect the product of that destruction against the birthplace claim of a living faith. The committee's answers, also at their strongest, are that the settlement was a lawful compromise between competent parties, acted on for half a century, that the Idgah's character as a mosque on 15 August 1947 is undisputed, and that the Act and the settlement together close the file twice over.

The survey mechanism arrived at Mathura by the Varanasi road. On 14 December 2023 the Allahabad High Court, in the transferred batch of Idgah suits, allowed the plaintiffs' application for a court monitored survey of the Shahi Idgah premises by an advocate commissioner, on the ascertainment reasoning the Varanasi orders had normalised; the mosque committee carried the order to the Supreme Court, which on 16 January 2024 stayed the survey's implementation, observing a need to examine the vagueness of the application and the legal basis of the order, and the survey has not been executed. On 1 August 2024 the Allahabad High Court dismissed the mosque committee's Order 7 Rule 11 applications across the suit batch and held the suits maintainable, reasoning that the plaints, read as a whole, raised questions including the validity of the 1968 compromise that the Act does not dispose of at the threshold, and that the bar of Section 4 would be tested at trial against the prayers actually pressed. The committee's challenge to that maintainability ruling travelled to the Supreme Court, where it met the freeze imposed by the December 2024 interim order in the Act challenge, leaving Mathura, like Varanasi, in procedural suspension: suits alive, surveys stayed, trial halted by the apex court's discipline, title undisturbed. For the architectural reading, Mathura contributes a distinct datum that Varanasi cannot supply, because Mathura tests the bar against a settled dispute rather than an unsettled one: if the architecture cannot protect even a compromise decree that Section 4(3) names as a closure ground, the bar's weakness lies not in the survey device alone but in the threshold doctrine of maintainability, which allows a plaint artfully framed around a settlement's validity to carry a character claim into court inside a contractual envelope. The defenders of the Act, including the Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind in its Supreme Court intervention seeking the Act's enforcement, cite Mathura for the proposition that threshold discipline is the whole game: a bar that can be pleaded around is a bar repealed by drafting technique, without Parliament ever voting.

### What penalty follows a conversion under the Act?

Section 6(1) punishes contravention of Section 3 with imprisonment that may extend to three years and liability to a fine, and Sections 6(2) and 6(3) extend the same punishment to an attempt, to abetment and to membership of a criminal conspiracy aimed at conversion, whether or not the offence is actually committed in consequence.

The penalty's history is almost entirely theoretical, and the fact deserves recording plainly: across three decades, prosecutions under Section 6 have been vanishingly rare, because the conversions the section targets did not occur at covered shrines, the one catastrophic exception, the demolition at Ayodhya in December 1992, having occurred at the excepted site and having been prosecuted under other provisions of penal law through the special CBI court that acquitted all accused on 30 September 2020. A deterrent that is never invoked can be read two ways, as a success so complete that invocation became unnecessary, or as a dead letter whose presence in the design flatters the architecture's strength, and the evidentiary record supports the first reading for the period to 2019 and leaves the question genuinely open thereafter, since the post 2022 suits test the Act civilly rather than by physical conversion, and Section 6 has no application to a survey, a plaint or a televised claim. The criminal limb of the architecture, in other words, guards the gate against crowds and says nothing to litigants, which is a further sense in which the survey route operates parallel to the design: every coercive instrument in the Act is aimed at the mosque or temple committee that changes a shrine by hand, and none is aimed at the party that changes a shrine's public meaning by litigation while its stones and its namaz continue undisturbed.

## Sambhal: A Survey in a Day, Four Deaths, and a Supreme Court Pause

The Sambhal Jama Masjid litigation compressed the Varanasi sequence of years into a single week in November 2024, and the compression itself became the scandal. On 19 November 2024 a suit was filed in the court of the civil judge (senior division) at Chandausi by plaintiffs including the advocate Hari Shankar Jain, asserting that the Jama Masjid at Sambhal had been raised in 1526 under the Mughal emperor Babur over a demolished Harihar temple, and seeking access and declarations; the court ordered an ex parte survey by an advocate commissioner on the day the suit was filed, and the first survey was conducted that evening, within hours. A second survey on the morning of 24 November 2024, conducted on short notice to the mosque committee, drew a crowd, protests, stone pelting and police firing in the lanes around the mosque, and four men were killed, with officials and the mosque committee disputing the sequence of provocation and response in terms this article records as disputed and does not adjudicate. The Committee of Management of the Shahi Jama Masjid approached the Supreme Court directly, and on 29 November 2024 a bench headed by Chief Justice Sanjiv Khanna directed the trial court to take no action on the suit until the Allahabad High Court heard the committee, ordered the advocate commissioner's survey report kept in a sealed cover and not opened without the Supreme Court's leave, and directed the Uttar Pradesh administration to maintain peace and harmony at Sambhal, language whose plainness conveyed the bench's displeasure at the hot haste, the committee's phrase, with which a sensitive shrine suit had been converted into a street confrontation. The mosque is a protected monument under the Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains Act 1958, so the Sambhal file also presented, in its rawest form, the Section 4(3)(a) question: whether a survey inside a character suit can be justified where the monument's custody already lies with the Archaeological Survey under a statute the 1991 Act itself preserved.

Sambhal changed the national procedural weather in a way Gyanvapi and Mathura had not, because the deaths attached a human cost to the survey mechanism that interim orders had until then treated as a lawyers' question. On 12 December 2024, a three judge bench headed by Chief Justice Sanjiv Khanna, hearing the batch of petitions challenging and defending the 1991 Act, directed that while suits may be filed, no fresh suits shall be registered or proceedings ordered until further orders of the Supreme Court; that in pending suits courts shall pass no effective interim or final orders, including orders of survey; and that the Union government file its affidavit in the challenge within the time the bench allowed. The bench recorded that eighteen suits concerning ten religious places were pending across the country, a census that itself testified to the survey route's replication within three years of the first Varanasi order. The December 2024 direction is an interim discipline rather than a ruling on validity, and both camps claimed it: defenders, because the bench stopped the mechanism the Act's spirit forbids; challengers, because the bench did not dismiss their petitions and instead pressed the Union for the affidavit that governments had delayed filing since the first notice in the lead challenge in March 2021. With that order the trajectory the brief for this article calls the architecture test reached its provisional resting point: the design of 1991 suspended between its own text, which district courts had read permissively, and its author's intent, which the Supreme Court's benediction of 2019 had described in near constitutional terms, awaiting the two decisions that will settle its fate, the constitutional challenge itself and the trials at Varanasi and Mathura whose eventual decrees will test whether ascertainment can be contained short of conversion.

### What did the Supreme Court order on 12 December 2024?

The three judge bench directed that no fresh suits concerning religious places be registered and no proceedings ordered until further directions, that courts in pending suits pass no effective interim or final orders including orders of survey, and that the Union government file its long awaited affidavit in the constitutional challenge to the Act.

## The Act on Trial: Challenge, Repeal Advocacy and the Defence

The constitutional challenge that frames the December 2024 order was filed before the survey litigation matured, and its lead petition, Ashwini Kumar Upadhyay's public interest litigation of 2020, attacks Sections 2, 3 and 4 of the Act as violative of Articles 14, 15, 21, 25, 26 and 29 of the Constitution, as an arbitrary and irrational retrospective cut off that denies Hindus, Jains, Buddhists and Sikhs the right to approach courts for the recovery of places of worship taken, on the petition's account, by invaders, and as a legislative abrogation of judicial review, a basic feature of the Constitution. The Supreme Court issued notice to the Union on 12 March 2021, and the Union's affidavit remained unfiled through repeated extensions across 2022, 2023 and 2024, a delay that acquired its own significance: the government that declined to defend the Act in court also declined to repeal it in Parliament, leaving a Congress era statute in a condition of official orphanhood under a Bharatiya Janata Party majority whose 1991 predecessors had walked out against it. Further petitions accumulated from devotees, seers and advocates, some seeking the Act's invalidation in whole, some attacking the 15 August 1947 cut off alone, and interventions accumulated on the other side: the Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind sought the Act's strict enforcement and the dismissal of the challenge, the All India Muslim Personal Law Board intervened in defence, and in January 2025 the Indian National Congress, the statute's original author, applied to intervene in its defence, describing the Act in its application as essential to secularism and warning that the challenge was a motivated attempt to undermine the settled principles of the Republic. Repeal advocacy outside the courtroom has run on a parallel track: the Vishva Hindu Parishad and aligned organisations have demanded repeal or radical amendment for Kashi and Mathura since the Ayodhya judgment, Bharatiya Janata Party legislators have introduced private members' bills and raised the demand in debate without government sponsorship, and no repeal bill has been taken up by any Union government, including the majority governments after 2014, a legislative silence that the defence reads as tacit ratification and the challengers read as judicial outsourcing of a decision politicians prefer courts to take.

The substantive arguments deserve statement at full strength on each side, as the charter of this series requires. For the challenge: a right to worship at a site a community holds sacred is a constituent of Articles 25 and 26; a statute that forecloses even the determination of whether a shrine was seized by force privileges possession however acquired over faith however documented; the 1947 cut off is arbitrary because it protects the results of every pre Independence conversion, including conversions effected in living memory of 1947 itself, while barring inquiry into them; and a legislature cannot extinguish causes of action in a manner that ousts judicial review of constitutional injury. For the defence, as argued by counsel for the interveners and by scholars including Faizan Mustafa in his post 2022 essays: the Act classifies by date, not by religion, protecting mosques, temples, churches and gurdwaras in identical terms, and a classification by a founding date is the opposite of religious discrimination; Articles 25 and 26 confer rights to practise and manage religion, not a right to recover real property lost to medieval sovereigns, and no fundamental right is infringed by a limitation that every property system imposes through repose; the equality of faiths and the non-retrogression principle pronounced in the Ayodhya judgment place the Act inside the basic structure rather than against it; and the practical alternative to the freeze is a rolling archaeological audit of the Republic's sacred geography, district by district, with the Sambhal week as its demonstrated price. Suhrith Parthasarathy's Economic and Political Weekly essays add the institutional corollary the defence presses hardest: that the survey litigation itself, whatever the challenge's fate, is converting the Act's breach into a technique, and that a court which permits ascertainment suits while validity is undecided is deciding the substance by procedure. Against that corollary the plaintiffs' counsel answer that evidence gathering is the ordinary first step of any civil justice system, that the Ayodhya excavation of 2003 was itself ordered by a court in a title suit and produced findings the Supreme Court relied on, and that a rule forbidding the state to learn facts about its own monuments would be a rule against knowledge, which no secular value requires. The architectural reading does not split these differences; it observes that both sides argue as if the Act's meaning were the question, when the record shows its mechanism is the question, since every argument for the defence assumes threshold enforcement and every argument for the challenge assumes a hearing the threshold exists to refuse.

## The Ascertainment Distinction: Anatomy of a Parallel Mechanism

The distinction on which the post 2022 trajectory rests deserves anatomical precision, because it is neither frivolous nor self evidently evasive, and the architectural reading is credible only if it states the distinction's best case before judging its effects. In ordinary civil procedure, a court seised of a dispute may ascertain facts through commissions, inspections and expert surveys without granting the decree those facts might support; evidence and relief are distinct stages, and a plaintiff who loses at trial has nonetheless lawfully obtained findings. Section 3 prohibits conversion, a relief and a physical act; Section 4(1) declares continuity of character, a rule of substantive law; and Section 4(2) bars proceedings with respect to conversion, a phrase the Gyanvapi and Mathura benches read as attaching to proceedings whose object is conversion, not to proceedings whose object is knowledge that might one day inform a conversion claim the Act would then bar at the decree stage. On that reading, the system behaves coherently: surveys may run, facts may be found, and the Act interposes at the moment a decree would alter character, possession or worship, which is why the Archaeological Survey at Gyanvapi proceeded under recorded assurances against excavation and harm, and why the Supreme Court in May 2022 coupled its ascertainment observation with protection of the sealed area and of Muslim worship. The mosque committees' counter anatomy is equally precise: a plaint that prays only for declarations about a mosque's origin, filed by plaintiffs who announce in public that the findings will ground recovery of the site, is a proceeding with respect to conversion in substance; the survey is not neutral evidence gathering but the suit's central event, conducted under police protection, reported in real time, and productive of exactly the public recharacterisation of the mosque, from a place of worship protected by statute to a disputed structure awaiting verdict, that Section 4(1) exists to prevent. On that reading the Act is breached not at the decree stage but at the moment of public meaning, and the breach is irreversible by any later dismissal, because a community cannot unhear the announcement that the Survey found its mosque to have been a temple.

Between these anatomies lies the empirical record, and the record shows the mechanism's ratchet. Each procedural step at Varanasi, commission in April 2022, finding in May 2022, maintainability in September 2022, Survey in July 2023, report in January 2024, high court direction for trial in December 2023, was individually defensible under the ascertainment reading and cumulatively transformative under the committees' reading, converting a barred title claim into an advanced forensic proceeding without any single order ever holding the Act inapplicable or invalid. The comparison with the Shah Bano sequence of 1985 and 1986, where a Supreme Court judgment on maintenance was answered by a parliamentary statute that reset the legal position, instructs by inversion: there, a judgment moved first and legislation answered; at Gyanvapi, district procedure moved first and the Supreme Court's eventual word on validity will answer, with legislation absent from the field altogether. The mechanism also migrated, which parallel mechanisms do when they succeed at a prominent site: Mathura adopted it within months, Sambhal adopted it in accelerated and catastrophic form, and the Supreme Court's December 2024 census of eighteen suits over ten religious places measures the migration's reach before the apex court suspended it. A design audit must therefore grade the architecture not only on its text but on its environment: the 1991 drafters built against mobs and ministerial caprice, the threats of their decade, and built well; the threat model of the 2020s, patient litigation by well resourced plaintiffs through forums of first instance, was not in the design brief, and the architecture's joints, the threshold bar and the undefined space around fact finding, are exactly where the new pressure concentrates. Whether that verdict damns the design or merely dates it is the question the adjudication between framings must answer.

## Roots in the Founding Settlement and the Movement Decade

The freeze of 1991 had a genealogy inside the founding settlement itself, and tracing it disciplines the temptation to treat the Act as an improvisation of one frightened September. The Constituent Assembly, debating religious freedom between 1947 and 1949, had refused both a theocratic state and a wall of separation on the French pattern, settling instead on equal respect and regulated intervention, the settlement reconstructed in [the Constituent Assembly's secularism debates](/2018/12/28/secularism-constituent-assembly-debates/) from the Assembly's own proceedings. Within that settlement the state acquired and exercised an administrative power over shrines from the Republic's first years: the idols placed inside the Babri Masjid in December 1949 were managed by executive magistracy and receivership rather than by judicial decree, the Somnath temple's reconstruction was blessed by the President of India at its consecration in May 1951 against the Prime Minister's reservations about state identification, and temple entry and administration statutes across the states normalised legislative supervision of religious endowments. The 1991 Act is legible as the settlement's late codicil: where the founders had trusted administration and courts to manage shrine conflict case by case, the Parliament of 1991, having watched case by case management at Ayodhya consume two governments, withdrew one class of question from management altogether. Challengers answer that the genealogy proves the opposite, that the founders conspicuously declined to enact any general freeze although the Partition's shrine losses were fresh, and that a settlement built on adjudication cannot be amended by exhaustion into a settlement built on foreclosure without constitutional cost. The genealogy thus supplies both camps with a founding warrant, which is precisely what the architectural reading predicts for a statute that codified one tendency within the settlement against the other.

The movement decade supplied the second root. The Vishva Hindu Parishad's Ekatmata Yatra of 1983, the Ram Janmabhoomi liberation campaign from 1984, the shilanyas of November 1989 permitted by the Rajiv Gandhi government, and L. K. Advani's rath yatra of September and October 1990, reconstructed in [the rath yatra and the Ayodhya movement's expansion](/2019/10/10/advani-rath-yatra-ayodhya-movement/), converted shrine grievance into the most efficient mobilisation technology in the Republic's electoral history, raising the Bharatiya Janata Party from 85 Lok Sabha seats in 1989 to 120 in 1991 and making Kashi and Mathura household names as the movement's declared next stations. The Act's drafters legislated inside that demonstration, and the statute's religion blind generality is best explained as a direct reply to the technology: a mobilisation that feeds on named sites and scheduled litigation events is starved by a rule that names only one site, the excepted one, and schedules no events at all, because a registry refusal generates no photograph, no procession and no verdict day. The thirty year famine of shrine litigation events after 1991, broken only when the survey device manufactured events that the Act had not scheduled, measures how accurately the drafters had diagnosed the technology they faced. The diagnosis is also the defence's strongest historical argument in the constitutional challenge: that the Act is not an antiquarian preference for 1947 but a considered legislative answer to a demonstrated mechanism of communal mobilisation, assessed by a Parliament that had buried two governments under that mechanism within twenty four months, and entitled to the deference courts ordinarily give a legislature's choice among instruments against a documented public order threat.

The third root is fiscal and administrative, and it is the least discussed in commentary. Every contested shrine in India is also a charge on the police budgets of its district, a standing deployment around the Gyanvapi and Kashi Vishwanath complex being among the largest permanent shrine security commitments in the country, and the freeze of 1991 capped that liability at the sites already contested while forbidding its multiplication. District administrations understood the Act in those terms immediately: a character suit admitted is a decade of bandobast, a survey ordered is a curfew risk, and the Sambhal week of November 2024, in which a survey conducted on hours of notice ended with four deaths and a town under restriction, is the administrative nightmare the freeze existed to prevent, arriving through the courtroom door the freeze had left unguarded against fact finding procedure. When the Supreme Court's bench on 29 November 2024 directed the Uttar Pradesh administration to maintain peace and harmony in terms of unusual directness, and when the bench of 12 December 2024 halted fresh registrations nationally, the apex court was speaking the administrative language of 1991 as fluently as the constitutional language of 2019, and the convergence of those two languages, harmony as governance and non-retrogression as doctrine, is the strongest available evidence that the architecture's purposes remain legible to the institutions charged with its fate.

## Three Framings Judged: Context, Cases and Architecture

The immediate political context framing, dominant in the Act's first decade of commentary, reads the statute as the Rao government's defensive reflex: a minority Congress administration, frightened by the rath yatra's demonstration that Ayodhya dissolved governments, legislating a freeze to protect itself and the Babri Masjid's remaining months, and failing even at that as the demolition of December 1992 proved within fifteen months. The framing explains the timing completely and the text hardly at all. It cannot explain why a frightened government chose a general, religion blind, permanently drafted statute with penalties, an override clause and an electoral disqualification, when a narrow Ayodhya protection law would have served the fright; it cannot explain the statute's survival under the Vajpayee governments, whose party had authored the fright; and it reads the demolition as the Act's failure when the demolition occurred at the one site the Act expressly excluded, an error of attribution equivalent to blaming a sea wall for a flood inside the harbour it was never built across. What the framing captures, the minority government's fear and the movement's pressure, is real but belongs to the biography of the statute's passage rather than to an account of what the statute is.

The post 2022 specific case framing, dominant in commentary since the Gyanvapi survey, reads the Act backwards from Varanasi, Mathura and Sambhal, treating the statute as a prelude to its own litigation crisis and each shrine suit as the unit of meaning. Its strength is concreteness: it alone follows the paper trail of commissions, stays and interim orders through which the Act is actually being decided in the 2020s, and this article's own case sections borrow its method gratefully. Its weakness is synecdoche: it takes three contested complexes, of the lakhs of shrines the freeze covers without incident, as the statute's truth, and it cannot see the architecture's ordinary operation, the thousands of registries that refused files, the movements that found no procedural purchase, the decades in which the most contested sites in the Republic generated no forensic events at all. A framing that notices a law only when it is litigated will always conclude that the law is failing, because litigation is the selection effect of conflict, and the post 2022 framing's implied verdict, a statute overwhelmed, mistakes the visibility of the test for the generality of the result.

The status quo architecture reading defended here subsumes both rivals' evidence and corrects both distortions. Against the context framing it insists that the text, four features in interlocking design, is the statute's identity, and that the design's subsequent career under hostile, friendly and indifferent governments is data about the design rather than about 1991. Against the case framing it insists that the unit of analysis is the arrangement, freeze, bar, exception and carve outs, operating across the whole sacred geography and across three decades, with the post 2022 suits registering as a stress test concentrated at the design's two unbuilt joints: fact finding procedure, which the drafters never addressed, and threshold maintainability doctrine, which the carve outs complicate at Mathura. The reading also absorbs the strongest counter to itself, which the brief for this article names honestly: that the post 2022 cases have proceeded through court orders the architecture ought to have prohibited, leaving the framework apparently tested and found permissive. The answer turns on the distinction between a design and its enforcement environment. The prohibitions the drafters wrote, against conversion and against character proceedings, have not been judicially nullified; no decree has converted a covered shrine, no bench has struck the Act, and the Supreme Court's December 2024 discipline reimposed the threshold at the national level. What the trajectory shows is a design whose enforcement was delegated to threshold doctrine in trial courts, and trial courts, presented with the ascertainment distinction by a Supreme Court bench's oral observation, enforced permissively for three years. An architecture can be sound and under enforced, and the judgment between tested and restrictive or tested and permissive therefore awaits the two decisions that remain outstanding: the Constitution Bench's answer on validity, which will grade the design's constitutionality, and the trial decrees at Varanasi and Mathura, which will grade whether ascertainment can be held short of conversion in practice. Until those decisions arrive, the accurate verdict is the architectural one: the framework stands, its text unamended and its benediction of 2019 unwithdrawn, while its enforcement oscillates, and oscillation under test is a different historical fact from collapse.

The scholarly literature divides along the same seams, and anchoring the division to named works keeps the adjudication honest. A. G. Noorani's documentary method in The Babri Masjid Question 1528-2003 (2003) and his essays after 2022 defend the Act as the Republic's considered refusal to litigate sacred history, and his authority rests on the archive he assembled across the title suits. Suhrith Parthasarathy's Economic and Political Weekly essays after the Gyanvapi orders supply the mechanism critique in its most exact legal form, arguing that the ascertainment distinction fails the Act's own text. Faizan Mustafa's essays and lectures after 2022 supply the constitutional defence in its most accessible form, grounding the freeze in equality of faiths and in the Ayodhya judgment's non-retrogression passage. On the challengers' side, the Upadhyay petition's grounds, arbitrariness of the cut off, ouster of judicial review and abridgement of Articles 25 and 26, state the restorative case in justiciable form, and sympathetic commentary adds the comparative point that no other class of property claim in Indian law is barred by the claimant's ancestors having lost possession before a twentieth century date. The architectural reading rules between these bodies of argument on one narrow point and declines the rest: on the description of what the Act is and how it is being tested, the architecture reading is correct and the rivals are partial; on whether the Act deserves to survive its constitutional challenge, this article's charter requires the arguments to stand at full strength side by side, as they have been set out, because that question belongs to the Constitution Bench seised of it and not to a series whose contribution is to make the object of the dispute visible in its true shape.

## Conclusion: A Design Awaiting Its Two Verdicts

The Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act 1991 is the Indian Republic's most consequential statute about religion that most citizens never read: eight sections, passed in a frightened September by a minority government, which between them froze the sacred map of the country at the moment of Independence and held that map undisturbed through three decades of coalition governments, single party majorities, a demolition at the excepted site and a unanimous constitutional benediction from the Supreme Court that decided the excepted site's fate. Its politics, the subject of this article's title, is the politics of a design: the claim defended throughout is that the 1991 Act established status quo architecture, and that the post 2022 trajectory tests whether the architectural framework can withstand parallel mechanisms, the survey that finds without converting, the plaint that ascertains without decreeing, the interim order that suspends without deciding. The test's provisional results are already legible. At the level of text and title, the architecture holds: no covered shrine has changed hands, character or worship by force of law, and the Supreme Court's order of 12 December 2024 restored the threshold discipline the design presupposes. At the level of public meaning, the test has already scored against the design at Varanasi, where a mosque protected by statute is discussed by millions as a structure awaiting archaeological verdict, which is a change of character in the register of reputation that Section 4(1) cannot reach. The two outstanding verdicts, on validity and at trial, will determine whether that reputational change remains a commentary upon the architecture or becomes its epitaph, and the discipline this series applies to itself, recording the parties' positions at their strongest and the procedural record exactly, applies equally to the statute's chroniclers: journalists who document contested shrines under legal threat work with the same care for sealed records and sourced claims that a tool such as the VaultBook guide on [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) describes for source protection, because the paper trail of 1991, of 2019 and of the survey years will be the evidence from which the next generation reconstructs what the architecture was and what the test proved. The freeze was a promise the Republic made to every worshipping community at once: that the map of 15 August 1947 would not be redrawn by crowd, by court or by the archaeology of grievance. Whether the promise survives its fourth decade depends on decisions that, as this article closes, remain exactly where the design's defenders and challengers both, for once, agree they belong: before the Supreme Court of India, undecided.

Each of the two outstanding verdicts can be specified in advance, and specifying them is the architectural reading's final service. If the constitutional challenge fails and the Act stands, the trial courts at Varanasi and Mathura will still have to decide whether ascertainment suits can conclude in any decree the Act permits, and a validity verdict that leaves the survey route open would preserve the architecture as a text while licensing its circumvention as a practice, the permissive outcome the brief for this article names as the strongest counter to the design's defenders. If the challenge succeeds in whole or against the cut off date alone, Parliament will face the choice it has avoided since 1991 under governments of every party: legislate a new settlement for shrine character in full public view, with the electoral costs itemised in advance, or accept a judicially reopened field in which the eighteen pending suits of the December 2024 census become the first tranche of a national docket. If the trials at Varanasi and Mathura conclude first, with decrees that either confine the Survey's findings to history or convert findings into possession, the constitutional challenge will be argued in the shadow of an accomplished fact, as the Ayodhya litigation so often was. The sequence in which the verdicts arrive may therefore matter as much as their content, and the December 2024 discipline, suits unregistered and orders suspended, reads in retrospect as the Supreme Court's attempt to control that sequence, holding every file still until the largest question is answered first. A design audit cannot grade the future; it can record that the architecture of 1991 has already survived the demolition of its excepted site, the electoral triumph of the party that opposed its birth, thirty years of executive indifference and three years of forensic siege, and that its survival through each test was produced by the same property: a short text, a fixed date, and a bar that any registry clerk could apply without discretion. Whatever the verdicts hold, the statute has set the terms on which India argues about its shrines, and the argument itself is the proof that the design, and not any single case within it, is the object worth understanding.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the Places of Worship Act 1991?

The Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act 1991, Act 42 of 1991, is a short central statute of eight sections that received presidential assent on 18 September 1991 and was deemed in force from 11 July 1991. It prohibits the conversion of any place of worship from one religious denomination to another, or between sections of one denomination, and it declares that the religious character of every place of worship existing on 15 August 1947 shall continue as it existed on that day. It exempts the Ram Janma Bhumi Babri Masjid site at Ayodhya, carries penalties of up to three years imprisonment for conversion, and gives its provisions overriding effect over inconsistent laws.

### Q: Why was the 1991 Places of Worship Act passed?

Parliament passed the Act to prevent the Ayodhya dispute from becoming a transferable method for contesting other shrines. The rath yatra of 1990, the fall of two Union governments within a year and the firings on kar sevaks at Ayodhya had shown the Rao government that a single shrine dispute could dissolve administrations and ignite violence across states. Home Minister Shankarrao Chavan, moving the Bill on 10 September 1991, framed it as a healing measure for communal harmony, and the Statement of Objects and Reasons proposed freezing the status of places of worship as on 15 August 1947. The design was preventive: fence the Ayodhya exception by statute before imitation suits reached Kashi, Mathura and other contested sites.

### Q: Why is Ayodhya excluded from the 1991 Act?

Section 5 of the Act provides that nothing in the statute applies to the place commonly known as Ram Janma Bhumi Babri Masjid at Ayodhya or to any suit, appeal or proceeding relating to it. The exclusion reflected the dispute's procedural maturity in 1991: title suits filed from 1950 onward were pending, a receiver administered the site after the locks and idols controversy of 1949 and 1950, and extinguishing those suits by abatement would have inflamed the very conflict the Bill existed to contain. Parliament therefore quarantined the one dispute too advanced to freeze, froze every other shrine dispute in the country, and left the Ayodhya suits to run to the Supreme Court judgment of 9 November 2019.

### Q: What is the Gyanvapi case?

The Gyanvapi case groups the Varanasi suits concerning the mosque adjoining the Kashi Vishwanath temple. A devotees' suit of 1991 sought declarations that the mosque stands on a destroyed Vishvanath temple site; a suit filed by five women devotees in August 2021 sought worship rights at shrines on the complex's outer wall and produced the court ordered surveys of May 2022, the sealing of the ablution tank area after a claimed shivling discovery, a district court maintainability ruling of 12 September 2022, and an Archaeological Survey of India scientific survey ordered in July 2023 whose report concluded that a large Hindu temple had preceded the mosque. The mosque committee answers throughout that the Act bars the suits and that the mosque's character is protected.

### Q: What is the Shahi Idgah Masjid Mathura case?

The Mathura case groups the suits filed from September 2020 onward against the Shahi Idgah mosque beside the Krishna Janmasthan complex at Katra Keshavdev. Plaintiffs seek annulment of the compromise between the temple Seva Sangh and the Idgah committee recorded in 1968 and embodied in a 1974 decree, and removal of the mosque from land they plead is Krishna's birthplace. The Allahabad High Court allowed a survey application on 14 December 2023, which the Supreme Court stayed on 16 January 2024, and on 1 August 2024 the High Court held the suits maintainable against the mosque committee's threshold objections under the Act and the settlement.

### Q: What happened at Sambhal in November 2024?

On 19 November 2024 plaintiffs filed a suit at Chandausi asserting that the Jama Masjid at Sambhal was raised in 1526 over a demolished Harihar temple, and the civil judge ordered an advocate commissioner survey the same day; the first survey was conducted that evening. A second survey on the morning of 24 November 2024 drew crowds, protests, stone pelting and police firing around the mosque, and four men were killed. The mosque committee approached the Supreme Court, which on 29 November 2024 directed the trial court to take no further action until the Allahabad High Court heard the committee, ordered the survey report kept sealed, and directed the administration to maintain peace and harmony.

### Q: Can court-ordered surveys violate the 1991 Act?

The question is undecided and is the pivot of the post 2022 litigation. Plaintiffs and several district and high court benches rely on the distinction drawn orally by Justice D. Y. Chandrachud on 20 May 2022, that Sections 3 and 4 do not bar ascertaining a place's religious character, since the Act prohibits conversion and character change rather than fact finding. Mosque committees and the Act's defenders answer that a survey ordered inside a character suit is a proceeding with respect to conversion under Section 4(2) in substance, whatever its label. The Supreme Court's order of 12 December 2024 suspended survey orders in pending suits while the constitutional challenge is heard, without deciding the question.

### Q: What does Section 3 of the Act prohibit?

Section 3 bars the conversion of any place of worship of any religious denomination, or of any section of a denomination, into a place of worship of a different denomination or of a different section of the same denomination. The intra denominational limb extends the prohibition beyond Hindu and Muslim contestation to disputes between sections of one faith, such as denominational contests within a temple tradition or between mosque management traditions. Contravention is punishable under Section 6 with imprisonment extending to three years and liability to a fine, and the same punishment reaches attempt, abetment and criminal conspiracy. Section 3 is the Act's operative conduct rule; the remaining sections supply its declaration, its jurisdictional bar, its exceptions and its enforcement machinery.

### Q: What happens to lawsuits that were pending when the Act commenced?

Section 4(2) abated them by operation of law. Every suit, appeal or other proceeding with respect to the conversion of the religious character of a place of worship existing on 15 August 1947, pending before any court, tribunal or other authority at the Act's commencement on 11 July 1991, stood extinguished without any judge needing to pass an order, and no fresh proceeding of that description could be instituted thereafter. A proviso preserves proceedings where the conversion complained of occurred after 15 August 1947 and before commencement. The abatement is the mechanism that gave the freeze its quiet first three decades, because registries and district judges could refuse character suits at the threshold by pointing to the subsection.

### Q: Which places and disputes fall outside the bar under Section 4(3)?

Section 4(3) disapplies the abatement and the jurisdictional bar for four classes. A place of worship that is an ancient and historical monument, an archaeological site or remains covered by the Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains Act 1958 stands outside, because archaeological custody of protected monuments runs under the 1958 statute. A suit, appeal or proceeding finally settled or disposed of before the Act commenced stays closed on its own terms. A dispute settled by the parties themselves before commencement is respected as a settlement. A conversion effected by acquiescence before commencement is left undisturbed. The carve outs show the drafters distinguishing open conflicts, which the Act closes by force, from closed files, which it leaves closed.

### Q: Why did Parliament choose 15 August 1947 as the freeze date?

The date is the architecture's foundation rather than an administrative convenience. Fifteen August 1947 is the day British sovereignty ended and the Constituent Assembly's India began, so freezing religious character at Independence tied the statute to the founding premise that citizenship and worship would be ordered without regard to the century in which a claimant's ancestors arrived or a conqueror ruled. A freeze dated to a year of construction or destruction, 1528 or 1669 for the pleaded cases, would have required courts to conduct precisely the historical inquiries the Act was drafted to foreclose. The 1947 date required only one factual inquiry, what a place was on that day, and then forbade using even that inquiry to disturb possession and worship.

### Q: How did the Supreme Court describe the Act in the Ayodhya judgment?

The five judge bench in M. Siddiq versus Mahant Suresh Das, delivering the Ayodhya title judgment on 9 November 2019, held that the Act speaks to the future by mandating that the character of a place of public worship shall not be altered, imposes a positive obligation on the state to maintain every shrine's character as it existed on 15 August 1947, and affirms the solemn duty to preserve and protect the equality of all faiths as an essential constitutional value and a basic feature of the Constitution. The bench called the Act a legislative intervention preserving non-retrogression as an essential feature of secular values, quoted the Home Minister's 1991 speech, and recorded that the Act's validity was not under challenge before it.

### Q: What did the Allahabad High Court decide about the Gyanvapi suits in December 2023?

On 19 December 2023 the Allahabad High Court dismissed the mosque committee's petitions seeking to dispose of the Gyanvapi suits at the threshold and directed the Varanasi courts to proceed with the suits, including the revived devotees' suit of 1991, rejecting the contention that the Places of Worship Act ended the litigation without trial. The ruling followed the district court's maintainability decision of 12 September 2022 in the women devotees' suit and the court ordered Archaeological Survey of India scientific survey conducted during 2023. The committee's position that Section 4(2) bars the proceedings remains its defence at trial; the December 2023 ruling decided maintainability, not title, character or possession, and no decree altering the mosque's status has been passed.

### Q: On what grounds has the Act been challenged in the Supreme Court?

The lead petition by Ashwini Kumar Upadhyay, filed in 2020 and noticed by the Supreme Court on 12 March 2021, attacks Sections 2, 3 and 4 as violative of Articles 14, 15, 21, 25, 26 and 29 of the Constitution. The grounds state that the 15 August 1947 cut off is arbitrary and irrational, that the Act abridges the rights of Hindus, Jains, Buddhists and Sikhs to worship at and recover places taken by invaders, and that a legislative bar on judicial proceedings abrogates judicial review, a basic feature of the Constitution. Companion petitions press overlapping grounds. The Union government's affidavit remained unfiled across repeated extensions until the bench of 12 December 2024 pressed for it.

### Q: Who has intervened in court to defend the Act?

The Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind filed in the Supreme Court seeking the Act's strict enforcement and the dismissal of the constitutional challenge, and the All India Muslim Personal Law Board intervened in defence of the statute. The Indian National Congress, whose government carried the Act in 1991, applied in January 2025 to intervene in its defence, describing the Act as essential to secularism and the challenge as a motivated attempt against settled principles. Mosque committees at Varanasi, Mathura and Sambhal defend the Act case by case as respondents, and scholars including Faizan Mustafa have defended it in essays and lectures on the equality of all faiths and the non-retrogression principle pronounced in the Ayodhya judgment of 2019.

### Q: Why did the district courts allow the surveys if the Act bars character suits?

The benches relied on a distinction between ascertaining character and converting character. The Varanasi civil judge's commission of April 2022, the district judge's maintainability ruling of September 2022 and the survey order of July 2023 reasoned that the suits sought worship rights and factual determination rather than a decree changing the mosque's character, and that Sections 3 and 4 prohibit conversion and character alteration, not fact finding. Justice D. Y. Chandrachud's oral observation of 20 May 2022, that ascertaining religious character is not barred by the Act, supplied the distinction's apex court authority, though it was made in interim proceedings. The mosque committees answer that the distinction is formal, since the surveys' findings are deployed publicly as character determinations.

### Q: What did the Archaeological Survey of India conclude in its Gyanvapi report?

The Archaeological Survey of India's scientific survey of the Gyanvapi complex, ordered by the Varanasi district court on 21 July 2023, upheld by the Allahabad High Court on 3 August 2023 and not stayed by the Supreme Court on 4 August 2023, was conducted without excavation under recorded assurances that the structure would not be harmed. Its report, submitted in December 2023 and unsealed to the parties in January 2024, concluded from inscriptions, sculptural fragments, architectural members and the western wall fabric that a large Hindu temple had existed at the site before the mosque's construction. The mosque committee contested the report's scope and legal effect, arguing that evidence of a predecessor structure cannot alter the character the Act froze in 1947.

### Q: Did any government repeal or amend the Act between 1991 and 2019?

No Union government repealed or amended the Act during that period, including the Bharatiya Janata Party led governments of Atal Bihari Vajpayee from 1998 to 2004, although the party had opposed the Bill in 1991 and its members walked out of the Lok Sabha during the September 1991 proceedings. The Vajpayee government's National Agenda for Governance set aside the temple agenda items around which the Ayodhya movement had mobilised, and coalition arithmetic discouraged reopening a statute whose disturbance promised litigation at hundreds of sites. After the Ayodhya judgment of November 2019, repeal demands from the Vishva Hindu Parishad and aligned organisations and private members' initiatives by individual legislators proceeded without sponsorship from any Union government, leaving the statute unrepealed and unamended.

### Q: What is the difference between the Act's treatment of living shrines and protected monuments?

The 1991 Act freezes the religious character of living places of public worship, defined in Section 2 as temples, mosques, gurdwaras, churches, monasteries and other places of public religious worship of any denomination. The Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains Act 1958 separately commits protected monuments to the Archaeological Survey of India for preservation, documentation and regulated access. Section 4(3)(a) of the 1991 Act keeps protected monuments outside its abatement and bar, preserving that archaeological custody. Many contested structures, including the Sambhal Jama Masjid, are both living shrines and protected monuments, and the post 2022 litigation turns partly on whether a survey belongs to archaeological custody under the 1958 statute or to a character suit under the 1991 Act's prohibition.

### Q: What was the Statement of Objects and Reasons of the 1991 Bill?

The Statement of Objects and Reasons accompanying the Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Bill declared the government's proposal in plain mechanism language: to prohibit conversion of any place of worship and to freeze the status of places of worship as it existed on 15 August 1947, in the interest of communal harmony, with provision for the Ayodhya dispute's exclusion and for penalties against conversion. The statement belongs to the Bill's parliamentary record preserved in the Parliament Library, and the Supreme Court's Ayodhya judgment of 9 November 2019 drew on the accompanying debate, quoting the Home Minister's speech of 10 September 1991 about healing the wounds of the past, when it characterised the statute as a legislative intervention for non-retrogression.
