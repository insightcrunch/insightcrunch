Althusser wrote the most influential Marxist account of ideology of the twentieth century, and it is usually encountered in a form he would barely recognise. What circulates in lecture slides and revision guides is a list of institutions, a policeman calling out in a street, and a conclusion that schools exist to keep capitalism running. The list is real, the policeman is real, and the conclusion is not entirely wrong, but the three fragments together omit almost everything that makes the essay an argument. Missing is the question the essay exists to answer, which concerns the reproduction of the relations of production. Missing is the thesis that ideology exists materially in practices and rituals rather than in heads. Missing is the strange claim that ideology has no history. Missing, above all, is any sign that the famous text is an extract, cut from a longer manuscript that stayed unpublished for a quarter of a century and that answers, in part, the objections the extract provoked.

![Althusser on ideology and the ideological state apparatuses, interpellation and the hailed subject - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-42.webp)

This profile restores the argument in the order Althusser built it. It begins with reproduction, because he did. It then separates the repressive apparatus from the ideological apparatuses, states the theses on ideology in general with the precision they demand, and only then turns to interpellation, the mechanism by which ideology is said to recruit the very subjects who live it. The profile also states the strongest objection to the whole construction, the charge of functionalism pressed by historians within a decade of publication, and it gives that objection the space it earned. A reader who finishes should be able to explain interpellation without leaning on the policeman, to say why the essay must be cited as an extract, and to state the circularity problem that the theory has never fully escaped. Those three abilities mark the distance between knowing the slogans and knowing the text.

Louis Althusser was a philosopher at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris, the institution that trained much of the French intellectual elite, and by the time the ideology essay appeared in the journal La Pensée in 1970 he was already the most debated Marxist thinker in France. For Marx and Reading Capital, both published in 1965, had proposed a structural reading of Marx that set a generation of students alight and drew furious replies. The essay on ideology and ideological state apparatuses carried a subtitle that summaries almost never reproduce: notes towards an investigation. The phrase matters. Althusser presented the text as exploratory, a set of theses advanced for testing, and he flagged its gaps himself. His biography, including the illness that shadowed his later years and the death of his wife Hélène in 1980, after which he was judged unfit to stand trial, belongs to the separate [louis althusser intellectual biography](/2015/12/15/louis-althusser-intellectual-biography/) in this series, and this profile takes from the life only what the ideas require. What the ideas require is chiefly context: a thinker inside the French Communist Party, arguing against both the humanist Marxism of the party's official philosophers and the economism that treated ideology as a decorative reflection of the economy.

## Althusser and the Question of Reproduction

Every society, Althusser argued, must reproduce the conditions of its own production if it is to survive beyond a single cycle, and this platitude becomes an argument the moment its components are separated. The forces of production must be replenished: raw materials replaced, machines repaired, infrastructure maintained. Labour power must also be reproduced, and here the analysis sharpens, because labour power is not a machine part. Workers must be fed, housed, rested and replaced across generations, which is the material side of the matter, and wages figure in the essay largely as the means by which that material reproduction is secured. But labour power must also arrive at the point of production in a usable form. It must carry skills, and the distribution of skills across a workforce is not natural. Somebody teaches reading, numeracy, technical procedures, professional routines. Above all, labour power must arrive in a form that accepts the organisation of production it enters: punctual, rule governed, responsive to instruction, and disposed to treat the hierarchy of the workplace as the ordinary furniture of the world.

That double requirement, competence and submission, is the hinge of the whole essay. Marx had noted that capital needs labour power to be reproduced; Althusser pressed the question of where the reproduction happens and by what means. It does not happen inside the factory, mostly. Skills are formed in schools over years before a worker meets an employer. Submission is formed earlier still and more diffusely, in families, in classrooms, in the routines of institutions that teach a child what authority sounds like and what happens to those who ignore it. If that is right, then the institutions usually classified as superstructure are not ornamental. They perform work without which production itself would stop, and a theory of ideology that begins here will look very different from one that begins with false beliefs. It will be a theory about institutions and practices first, and about ideas only second. The parent concept behind the essay is set out in the series guide to the [marxist theory of ideology](/2012/02/01/marxist-theory-of-ideology-guide/), against which Althusser's version can be measured.

A distinction inside the concept does quiet work throughout the essay and repays extraction. Reproducing the forces of production is a technical and material affair: materials consumed, equipment worn out and replaced, labour power restored by food and rest. Reproducing the relations of production is a different affair, because relations are not things that wear out; they are patterns of command, ownership and expectation that persist only by being continuously enacted and recognised. A factory's machines do not need to believe in the factory. Its people do, or at least must enact the recognitions, the clocking in, the acceptance of the order and the wage, in which the relation consists. That is why the second kind of reproduction, unlike the first, is necessarily ideological in Althusser's sense: relations exist in the practices and recognitions of subjects, and a theory of their reproduction must therefore be a theory of subject formation. The essay's architecture follows from this single distinction with unusual rigour. Because relations are reproduced in recognition, the institutions that organise recognition are load bearing; because recognition is enacted in rituals, ideology is material; because the rituals address individuals as subjects, interpellation is the mechanism; and because the mechanism must already be running for any individual to be reached by it, the always already waits at the end of the chain. Readers who grasp the distinction can reconstruct the essay from it, and readers who miss it will keep wondering why a text about ideology spends so long on everything except ideas.

### Why does the essay begin with reproduction rather than with belief?

Because reproduction explains what ideology is for before asking what it says. A society must renew its labour power daily and across generations, with skills attached and with a disposition to accept the rules of the production process, and that renewal happens largely outside the workplace, in institutions whose output is people ready to take their places.

Reproduction also reframes the base and superstructure relation that cruder readings of Marx had left mechanical. If the superstructure merely reflected the base, ideology would be a shadow with no causal weight, and its content would be the only interesting thing about it. Althusser's earlier work had already replaced that picture with relative autonomy and reciprocal action, the framework examined in [base and superstructure explained](/2010/02/01/base-and-superstructure-explained/), and the ideology essay gives the picture an institutional address. The state, on his account, is not a single instrument but a set of apparatuses, and those apparatuses secure the political conditions under which relations of production persist. Reproduction is therefore not a metaphor borrowed from biology and applied loosely to society. It names a set of identifiable processes, carried out by identifiable institutions, whose interruption would be visible within a generation in the form of a workforce that could not or would not do what production requires.

Notice what this starting point does to the concept of ideology itself. In the tradition Althusser inherited, ideology named a system of ideas, distorted representations that served ruling interests by disguising them. The German Ideology had pictured ideology as a camera obscura image, inverted because the material world it reflected was inverted. Later usage often softened the concept into a synonym for any worldview, or hardened it into a label for the opponent's propaganda. Starting from reproduction cuts across both usages. The question becomes functional and material at once: which practices, in which institutions, form the agents a mode of production needs, and how do those practices secure consent to the relations within which the agents will work? Beliefs matter in such an account, but they matter as the lived surface of practices, not as freestanding errors that better information could correct. That is why the essay spends so little time refuting anybody's opinions. Its subject is the machinery that makes certain opinions feel like the obvious description of the world.

There is a further consequence that readers often miss, and it explains the essay's peculiar tone of calm system building. If reproduction is continuous and largely successful, then the persistence of capitalism needs no conspiracy and no continuous deception campaign. Institutions do the work by doing their ordinary work: schools teach, families raise children, broadcasters fill schedules, churches hold services, courts process disputes. Each activity has its own internal purposes, sincerely pursued by the people who staff it. The ideological effect, on Althusser's account, does not require anyone to intend it, because it is carried by the form of the practices themselves and by the places they assign to participants. A teacher who believes nothing but the value of literacy still sorts children by attainment, still rewards punctuality and penalises disruption, still sends most pupils out of the school gates at different ages with different certificates and different expectations of what work will ask of them. The theory's coldness comes from this impersonality. It describes a reproduction that runs through well meaning hands, which is precisely why later critics found it so difficult to argue with at the level of intention, and so necessary to attack at the level of structure.

## Reproduction Has a History

The reproduction framework gains credibility when it is turned on the past, because the sites where labour power is formed have visibly migrated, and each migration rearranged the ideological system. In the artisanal world the workshop performed the whole task in miniature: the apprentice lived in the master's household, learned the trade at the bench, absorbed the hierarchy of master, journeyman and apprentice as a natural order, and was formed as much by the household's table and the guild's ceremonies as by the tools. Skill and submission were a single fabric because a single institution wove them. The factory tore the fabric apart. Production left the household, children entered employment as small operatives rather than as apprentices, and the formation of labour power split into fragments that no single institution supervised: the family kept the early years, the employer took the working day, and the skills specific to a trade decayed or were rebuilt inside the factory's own discipline. The gap showed quickly, in a workforce whose habits, literacy and time sense industrial production found wanting, and the nineteenth century's answer to the gap is the material prehistory of Althusser's dominant apparatus.

Compulsory schooling was that answer, assembled piecemeal across the industrialising world by reformers whose motives were mixed in ways the theory predicts and forgives: philanthropy, anxiety about crime and disorder, the churches' competition for souls, employers' demand for literate and punctual operatives, and the nation state's demand for citizens and soldiers. Whatever the motives in each locality, the system's convergent result was a universal apparatus, taking every child, sorting by attainment, and certifying the sort. The theory does not require the reformers to have intended the reproductive function; the materiality thesis was built precisely to handle functions that run ahead of intentions. What the historical view adds is a check on the theory's timeless tone. The school's dominance is itself a historical product, younger than the factory system it serves, and the church's earlier dominance was equally a product, of a formation in which production was agricultural, literacy scarce and the parish the only universal institution. Apparatuses rise and fall with the mode of production they reproduce, and a reader alert to that rhythm will ask the question the essay leaves hanging: which apparatus is being assembled, in the digital reorganisation of communications and education, to inherit the school's position, and what would count as evidence either way. The essay gives no answer, since its author's media world was broadcast and print, but it gives the criteria by which an answer could be argued: compulsory reach, duration of exposure, intimacy with the family, and responsibility for certifying the sorted population.

## One Repressive Apparatus, Many Ideological Ones

The essay's most quoted distinction separates the repressive state apparatus from the ideological state apparatuses, and the distinction is more delicate than the summaries suggest. The repressive state apparatus, singular and unified, comprises the government, the administration, the army, the police, the courts and the prisons. It functions, in Althusser's formula, primarily by violence and secondarily by ideology: even armies and police forces cultivate esprit de corps, ceremonies and internal doctrines, but their characteristic instrument, the one held in reserve and used when others fail, is physical repression. The ideological state apparatuses are plural, distinct and relatively autonomous: the religious apparatus of churches and missions, the educational apparatus of schools and universities, the family apparatus, the legal apparatus, the political apparatus of parties and parliaments, the trade union apparatus, the communications apparatus of press, radio and broadcasting, and the cultural apparatus of literature, arts and sport. These function primarily by ideology and secondarily by violence, a qualification that matters. Schools expel, churches excommunicate, families punish; the repression is real, but it is not the main instrument, and it usually arrives dressed in the institution's own moral vocabulary.

Two clarifications protect the distinction from its commonest misreadings. The first concerns the word state. Althusser states plainly that most ideological apparatuses belong to the private domain, not the public one, and that the distinction between public and private is a distinction internal to bourgeois law, irrelevant at the level of function. A private school, a commercial broadcaster and a family are not run by ministries, yet they count as state apparatuses in his terminology because the term marks a function within the reproduction of the relations of production, under the horizon of state power, not a line on an organisational chart. Readers who object that the family is not part of the state have usually imported a liberal definition of the state that the essay explicitly sets aside. The second clarification concerns unity. The repressive apparatus is one body, centrally commanded. The ideological apparatuses are many, uncoordinated in detail, and frequently in conflict with one another; churches denounce broadcasters, unions fight governments that fund schools, parties quarrel with courts. Their unity, such as it is, lies not in command but in the horizon of the ruling ideology they variously realise, and even that unity is an achievement of struggle rather than a design feature. The apparatuses contradict one another constantly, and Althusser says so; what he claims is that their contradictions are played out beneath a dominant ideology whose broad outlines they share.

### How can an institution count as a state apparatus without being run by the state?

Because the term state in the essay marks a function rather than an owner. Althusser treats the public and private distinction as a formality: what makes an apparatus a state apparatus is its role in securing the conditions under which the relations of production are reproduced, and private schools, churches and families perform that role as reliably as ministries do.

The distinction also carries a strategic implication that Althusser drew explicitly and that his critics later turned against him. If ideology works through a plurality of institutions dispersed through civil life, then a politics aimed only at seizing the repressive apparatus, the ministries, the army, the broadcasting headquarters in the old coup d'état picture, would leave the machinery of reproduction untouched. The schools would open on Monday with the same timetables. This was one point of contact with Gramsci, whose theory of hegemony had located the defence of a social order in the trenches of civil society, although the two thinkers built very different theories on the shared intuition, as the comparison in [gramsci vs althusser](/2015/04/15/gramsci-vs-althusser/) shows in detail. For the moment it is enough to register that the RSA and ISA distinction is not a classification exercise for its own sake. It redistributes attention: away from the palace and the barracks, toward the classroom, the living room and the screen, as the places where a social order is actually renewed.

A last feature of the distinction deserves emphasis because examination answers so often drop it. The two kinds of apparatus are not sealed systems. No repressive apparatus operates by violence alone, since soldiers and police officers must themselves be formed, motivated and held together by doctrines and rituals; and no ideological apparatus operates by ideology alone, since every school and church keeps sanctions in reserve. The terms primarily and secondarily do the analytical work. They also give the theory a way to describe change: an institution whose balance shifts, a church that leans harder on excommunication or a police force that invests in community legitimacy, is moving along a spectrum the distinction defines. Critics who treat the RSA and ISA as boxes into which institutions are sorted once and for all are criticising a diagram of their own manufacture. The essay offers a spectrum with two poles and a claim about which pole dominates in which kind of institution, and that claim, unlike the diagram, can be argued with case by case.

## The State Behind the Apparatuses

Why call the whole dispersed system state apparatuses at all, if most of it is private, much of it is uncoordinated, and some of it opposes the government of the day? The answer lies in Althusser's distinction, developed at length in the longer manuscript, between state power and the state apparatuses. State power is what is at stake in political struggle: the capacity to direct the whole apparatus system, held by a class or alliance of classes. The apparatuses are the machinery through which that power is exercised and reproduced, and they have a stubborn material persistence that governments do not. Ministries change hands, constitutions are rewritten, parties fall, while schools keep their timetables, courts their procedures, churches their calendars and families their forms. On this account, a revolution that seizes state power without transforming the apparatuses has seized the helm of a ship whose engines, charts and crew loyalties remain what they were, which is the strategic lesson Althusser drew for the movements of his own camp: the long work is in the apparatuses, because that is where the formation actually lives between elections and insurrections.

The conception also explains the essay's otherwise odd serenity about the opposition of particular apparatuses to particular governments. A church hierarchy denouncing a ministry, a judiciary frustrating an executive, a press savaging the party in office: none of this troubles the classification, because the apparatuses serve the reproduction of the relations of production under the horizon of state power, not the convenience of the cabinet. Their unity is the unity of a function distributed across institutions that may quarrel about everything else, and the function tolerates, even uses, the quarrels, since a formation whose legitimacy is debated inside its own institutions is being reproduced in the debating. Critics have found this the slipperiest point in the system, and they have a case: a unity that survives every disagreement risks becoming a unity by definition, immune to the evidence of fragmentation. The strongest defence available is empirical modesty. The claim is not that the apparatuses form a single subject with a single will, but that their separate operations converge, statistically and over time, on the renewal of the relations they inhabit, as separate trades converge on the building none of them designed. Whether convergence of that kind needs the word state to describe it is a fair question, and readers who answer no can keep the apparatus analysis while rebaptising it, as much of the secondary literature quietly does.

## The Apparatuses One by One

The list of ideological apparatuses is usually reproduced as a bare inventory, which wastes it. Each apparatus performs the reproductive work in a distinctive register, and the differences among them explain why the system is resilient: a failure in one register does not interrupt the others. The family apparatus works first and longest. Within it the child learns the elementary grammar of place: who may command, who must wait, what a promise is, what shame feels like, how authority sounds when it is affectionate and how it sounds when it is angry. The family's ideology is carried in practices so small they escape notice, the distribution of food and space, the stories told about relatives who prospered and relatives who fell, the division of tasks between parents that teaches a sexual division of labour before the phrase could be understood. Because the family delivers its charges to the school already formed in the rudiments of recognition, Althusser treats the family and the school as a couple, an alliance in which the second apparatus receives what the first has prepared and the first receives back, years later, adults whose expectations of work and marriage were set in its classrooms.

The legal apparatus occupies a peculiar double position, belonging in part to repression and in part to ideology, and Althusser uses the doubleness deliberately. Law commands with sanctions behind it, yet its daily operation consists of forms, procedures and recognitions: the contract signed by parties who meet as legal equals, the title deed that makes an owner, the marriage register, the courtroom ritual in which everyone rises. Legal ideology, the representation of persons as free and equal bearers of rights, is not a decoration on the legal machine; it is the medium in which property relations become livable by those they disadvantage, since the wage contract's formal equality is the very shape in which unequal exchange is experienced as fairness. The political apparatus works a similar register at longer intervals: elections interpellate the citizen as sovereign, parties interpellate the member as militant or supporter, and the ceremony of the vote confirms each participant in the identity of a chooser among options the chooser did not set. That the options are real and the choosing genuine does not touch the analysis, which concerns the form of the position rather than the sincerity of the occupant.

The communications and cultural apparatuses complete the circuit by filling the hours the other apparatuses leave free. Press, broadcasting and their successors address audiences as publics, as fans, as the nation gathered around an event, and the address is renewed daily so that its positions never cool. Althusser, writing when broadcasting meant a handful of channels, stressed the ritual character of the schedule itself: the news at its hour, the match on its day, the serial returning to its slot, a liturgy of attention that sorts its congregation into those who belong to the conversation and those who do not. The cultural apparatus, literature, the arts and sport, performs the same work in the register of admiration and identification, supplying the figures in whose recognition audiences rehearse their own. Sport deserves the sentence Althusser's readers often give it: few practices interpellate so efficiently, since the supporter does not merely watch a contest but answers to a name, wears the colours, and experiences the assigned identity as the freest choice of the week.

The trade union apparatus is the list's most instructive entry, because it shows the classification following function through contested territory rather than respecting the self description of institutions. A union defends workers against the consequences of the relations of production, and in the same daily practice it negotiates the terms on which those relations continue: it processes grievances into procedures, converts anger into claims with forms and deadlines, and trains its officials in the arts of the possible. Althusser does not call unions fraudulent; he classifies the apparatus by its structural position, as a machine for channelling struggle into forms the formation can absorb, while noting that the same machine can be seized by the struggle it channels. The religious apparatus, finally, is the historical template: for centuries the church held the educational, cultural and much of the political function in one organisation, and its rituals, confession, sermon, feast and fast, remain the clearest exhibit of ideology's material existence, which is why the essay keeps returning to it even while announcing its displacement. Read as a set of registers rather than a list of buildings, the inventory becomes an argument about redundancy. The formation does not depend on any single apparatus, because the hail that fails in church may succeed in the stadium, the classroom or the sitting room, and the subject is the sum of the turns.

## The Inheritance the Essay Revises

Althusser's theses were written against a concept with a crowded past, and the essay's originality shows most clearly when the inheritance is laid out. The word ideology began respectably, as Destutt de Tracy's proposed science of ideas at the end of the eighteenth century, and acquired its sneer from Napoleon's contempt for the ideologues whose abstractions resisted his rule. Marx and Engels took the term up in The German Ideology, where ideology names the inverted representations produced by a divided material life: as in a camera obscura, men and their circumstances appear upside down because the process generating the image is itself inverted. The metaphor carries a theory: representations are distorted because the reality they reflect is distorted, and the distortion will end when the reality is set right. In the later Marx the concept recedes, surfacing in the analysis of commodity fetishism, where the misrecognition is no longer in heads at all but in the form of the commodity itself, a social relation between men that assumes the fantastic form of a relation between things. Althusser's materiality thesis is best read as the generalisation of that late, scattered insight: the fetishism chapter is his true point of departure, the place where Marx himself located a necessary misrecognition in a practice rather than in an error.

Between Marx and Althusser stand two great intermediaries, and the essay silently revises both. Lukács, in History and Class Consciousness, had made reification the universal form of objectivity under capitalism and class consciousness the standpoint from which the totality could be known, reserving to the proletariat an epistemological privilege: its position in production, properly understood, grants access to the truth of the whole. Mannheim's sociology of knowledge then generalised ideology into the thought of all groups, dissolving the Marxist asymmetry into a general relationism. Althusser refuses both inheritances at the decisive points. Against Lukács, there is no class standpoint exempt from ideology, since the working class is interpellated like every other population and its spontaneous self understanding is an ideological product, not a science in embryo; the knowledge of the mechanisms must be produced theoretically, not read off a position. Against Mannheim, ideology is not a synonym for situated belief but a specific structure with a specific function, the constitution of subjects for reproduction, and levelling it into the general condition of thought destroys exactly what the concept explains. These refusals give the essay its severity and its loneliness: it offers the dominated no epistemological consolation, and it refuses the analyst the comfort of a general relativism in which every account, including the essay's own, is merely another group's worldview. The severity is why the inheritance matters for assessment. Many criticisms of the essay are, without knowing it, defences of Lukács or of Mannheim against Althusser, and stating the quarrel at that level, as a choice among three theories of what ideology is, clarifies what evidence and argument could possibly settle it.

## Ideology in General Has No History

The essay's second major movement distinguishes ideologies, in the plural, from ideology in general, and the distinction organises two of its boldest theses. Particular ideologies, religious, legal, moral, political and the rest, plainly have histories. They are born in specific class conditions, they borrow from one another, they decay and are replaced. Nobody disputes this, and Althusser builds it into the account: a particular ideology is the expression of class positions, formed in the class struggle, and it changes as that struggle changes. Ideology in general is something else. It names the structure by which human beings, in any society, live their relation to their conditions of existence through an imaginary representation of those conditions. Structures of that kind, Althusser argues, do not have histories in the way their contents do. His formulation is deliberately provocative: ideology has no history, which he glosses as the claim that ideology in general is eternal, present in the same form throughout history, where history means the history of class societies. He reaches for an analogy with Freud, who had called the unconscious eternal in the sense of standing outside temporal succession. As the unconscious persists while dreams change nightly, so the ideological structure persists while ideologies rise and fall.

The analogy is easy to mock and important to understand correctly. Eternal does not mean unchanging in content, and Althusser is emphatic that the contents, the specific representations of specific societies, vary enormously and demand historical study. What persists is the form: the constitution of individuals as subjects who experience their relation to the world as a free, self transparent relation, lived through representations that misrecognise the real conditions producing them. A medieval peasant and a modern commuter inhabit different ideologies in every detail of content, yet on this theory both are constituted as subjects by the same general mechanism, and both misrecognise their conditions in structurally similar ways. The eternity thesis is therefore a claim about the level at which the theory operates. It tells the reader that interpellation, when it arrives, will not be a description of capitalist manipulation but of a permanent feature of social life as such, which class societies bend to the work of reproduction. Ideology in general would persist, on Althusser's own reasoning, even in a classless society, though the ideologies through which it operated would no longer serve domination.

### What does it mean to call ideology eternal rather than unchanging?

Eternal describes the form, not the contents. Particular ideologies are born, borrow, decay and are replaced with the class conditions that produce them, while the general structure, individuals constituted as subjects living an imaginary relation to real conditions, persists across those changes. The claim borrows Freud's sense of an unconscious that stands outside time.

The second thesis of this movement defines what ideology represents, and the definition is the essay's most quoted sentence. Ideology, in Althusser's formulation, is a representation of the imaginary relationship of individuals to their real conditions of existence. The sentence repays slow parsing, because every term is loaded. Ideology does not represent the real conditions themselves, directly, as a faulty map might misdraw a coastline. It represents the relationship of individuals to those conditions: how people stand toward their work, their rulers, their prospects and their place. Nor is the relationship imaginary in the sense of being a deliberate fiction or a lie told by rulers to the ruled. Imaginary here carries a Lacanian register: the relation is lived as an image of wholeness and self presence, the way a child in the mirror phase takes an image of bodily unity for its own identity. The representation is imaginary because the relation it figures is itself constituted in that register, prior to any proposition that could be checked against the facts. This is why arguing people out of ideology so rarely works on the theory's own terms. A misrecognised relation is not a false belief waiting for correction; it is the form in which a life is lived, and corrections are absorbed as new content within the same form.

The parsing also dissolves a lazy question that has dogged Marxist theory: if ideology distorts, why do people not simply compare it with reality and discard it? On Althusser's account there is no standpoint of unmediated comparison available to the subject, because the subject's very sense of being a self who could do the comparing is an ideological effect. Science, in his vocabulary of the period, names the theoretical practice that can know the mechanisms of ideology, but knowing them does not exempt the knower from living them. The theorist analysing interpellation over breakfast is also a father, a citizen, a consumer and an employee, hailed in each capacity and turning around each time. That reflexive turn, the theory applying to its own author, is either the theory's deepest strength or the symptom of its unfalsifiability, and the objection section below takes the charge seriously. For now the point is architectural: the eternity thesis and the imaginary relation thesis together set up the need for a mechanism. If ideology is a permanent structure that represents relations rather than things, then the theory owes an account of how the structure gets its grip on actual individuals. Material existence and interpellation are that account.

## Ideology Has a Material Existence

The third thesis is the one summaries most consistently drop, and Althusser regarded it as the essay's most original contribution. Ideas, in the idealist picture he opposed, exist in minds, and a person's beliefs are the spiritual furniture from which actions flow: first the conviction, then the deed. Althusser inverts the order by way of Pascal. The wager passage in Pascal advises the unbeliever who cannot force belief to behave as believers behave, to take the holy water and have masses said, because practice will manufacture the conviction that argument could not. Althusser seizes on the sequence. An individual's beliefs, he argues, are his acts inserted into practices, and the practices are governed by rituals, and the rituals are defined by the material ideological apparatus to which they belong. A person who kneels in prayer, attends the service, observes the fast and confesses according to the schedule is not expressing a prior inner state so much as producing and renewing one. The subject's ideas are therefore his material actions, inserted into material practices, governed by material rituals, defined by a material apparatus. Strip the apparatus away and the belief, deprived of its ritual support, does not float free as pure conviction; it withers or transforms.

This inversion does heavy work in the theory. It explains why ideology is so durable: it is anchored not in fragile opinions but in the timetables, buildings, ceremonies and routines that organise ordinary life, and those outlast any individual's doubts. It explains why ideology survives refutation: one can lose every argument about a practice and still be formed by performing it, as the sceptical parent at the school gate discovers each morning. It also disciplines the analyst's method. To study an ideology, do not begin with its doctrines and manifestos; begin with its apparatuses and their rituals, the admission procedures, the examinations, the promotions, the commemorations, the formats. The doctrine will be found sedimented in the practice, usually in a form simpler and more effective than the theologians' version. In media research this move proved especially fertile, because it redirected attention from the content of messages to the forms of address and the rituals of consumption, a redirection traced in the series article on [marxist media theory explained](/2012/05/15/marxist-media-theory-explained/). The message matters less, on this view, than the position the audience is practised in occupying while receiving it.

### What changes when ideology is treated as practice rather than as ideas?

The location of belief moves. Conviction stops being a private state that causes action and becomes an effect of action performed inside rituals: the kneeling, the attendance, the examination entry, the signature. Analysis follows the move, studying apparatuses, timetables and ceremonies first and treating doctrines as the sediment those practices leave behind in propositional form.

Material existence also clarifies what the theory denies. It denies that ideology is a screen of lies manufactured by a ruling group and projected onto passive minds, since the practices that constitute ideology are performed by everyone, rulers included, and form the rulers' own subjectivity as surely as the ruled. A minister educated in the same schools, formed by the same rituals of merit and deference, does not stand outside the machinery operating it; the machinery operates through him. This impersonality, again, is what separates Althusser from every propaganda model of ideology, and it is what his critics seized on from the other side: a machinery operated by nobody, serving a function nobody intends, begins to look like a system that explains everything by its own persistence. The tension is genuine and the essay does not resolve it. What the materiality thesis contributes, even for readers who reject the larger system, is a research instruction of permanent value: follow the rituals. Where a practice is compulsory, repeated and ceremonially marked, there ideology is being manufactured, whatever the participants say they believe.

## Ideology at the Scale of the Body

The materiality thesis has a smallest scale, and it is reached before any doctrine is uttered: the body. Rituals are bodily before they are verbal. The congregation kneels, stands, processes and sings in unison; the classroom sits in rows, raises the hand, stands when the teacher enters, writes within margins under a watched clock; the parade ground turns a hundred torsos as one. Althusser's Pascal example is a bodily prescription, kneel and belief will follow, and the generalisation runs through every apparatus: posture is pedagogy. A child who has spent ten thousand hours seated, silent and facing forward under an authorised voice has learned the central lesson of the dominant apparatus in the muscles, whatever the lessons on the board contained, and has learned it in a form no argument can reach because no argument installed it. Discipline of this kind is not experienced as ideology by its subjects, which is the mark of its success. It is experienced as normality, as the way rooms are, the way time behaves, the way a person holds himself when serious matters are under way, and its transfer to the workplace, where the same postures are resumed under other authorised voices, requires no instruction at all.

Dress and address extend the bodily register into every encounter. Uniforms assign places visibly and are answered by the body's own adjustments, the straightened back of the inspected and the ease of the inspector. Forms of address do the same work acoustically: the title required, the surname permitted, the Christian name presumed, each hail calibrating the distance between the parties before any content is exchanged. Even handwriting, drilled for generations as a school discipline, carried the double product in miniature, a skill every clerkship required and a submission rehearsed in every copybook line, the hand kept inside the ruling as the citizen would be kept inside the law. Analysts who find the apparatus theory too institutional, too fond of buildings and systems, can recover its point at this scale: the formation reproduces itself in the angle of a back and the name a person answers to, and an account of ideology that cannot descend to the body has not yet reached the material existence the essay insists on. The descent also suggests the theory's most practical research instruction. When studying an institution, watch what it makes bodies do repeatedly and in common, and the ideology will be found there first, with the doctrines arriving later as the practice's commentary on itself.

## Against the Sovereign Subject

Interpellation lands with its full force only against the background of the position Althusser had spent a decade defending: theoretical anti-humanism. The Marxism he inherited from the postwar French party celebrated man as the maker of history, and the early writings of Marx, with their language of alienation and species being, supplied the celebration with texts. Against that reading, For Marx had argued that the mature Marx broke with the humanist problematic altogether, replacing the subject of history with structures and relations whose bearers are men but whose logic is not man's self expression. The claim scandalised because it seemed to expel human agency from a doctrine of emancipation. The ideology essay is where the scandal becomes a theory. If the subject is not the origin of the social process, then the feeling of being an origin, the ordinary certainty that one's acts flow from a self that precedes them, itself requires explanation, and ideology is the name of the explanation. Anti-humanism is therefore not a contempt for human beings, whatever its critics said; it is the refusal to take the subject's self understanding as the foundation of social theory, on the grounds that the self understanding is one of the things social theory must explain.

This background also clarifies the essay's quarrel with two opponents who rarely meet each other. Against the humanist Marxist, Althusser denies that alienation names a fall from an essence that history will restore; there is no essence beneath the assigned places, only the places and the structures that assign them. Against the liberal theorist of the social contract, he denies that society is founded by subjects who pre exist it and agree to enter; the contracting parties are themselves constituted by the order their contract supposedly founds, legal subjects manufactured by law before they can sign anything. Both denials follow from a single methodological rule that runs through his work of the period: a science of society must begin from relations and processes without a subject, and must then explain why the process nevertheless appears, to everyone inside it, as the deed of subjects. The rule is austere and its austerity is the point. Every concept in the essay can be read as an application of it, and every major objection to the essay can be read as a protest against the rule itself, which is why the quarrel over interpellation has always been, at bottom, a quarrel over whether social theory may start where Althusser starts.

The austerity had a biographical and institutional edge that readers of the period felt even when they could not name it. To tell a generation of militants that history is a process without a subject was to tell them that the party, the class and the militant himself were not the sovereign authors they felt themselves to be, and the doctrine cut against the self image of the movement that housed its author. Althusser accepted the wound as the price of rigour. His critics inside the movement replied that a Marxism unable to say who makes history will in practice leave history to whoever claims the knowledge of its structures, and that the theoretician who exempts his own science from ideology becomes a new kind of priest. That internal quarrel, between the anti-humanist and the militants formed by his teaching, produced one of the sharpest books written against the essay's whole approach, by a former student, and it is considered in its place below. For the exposition, what matters is the rule and its consequence: the subject must be an effect in this theory, never a premise, and interpellation is the machinery proposed to manufacture the effect.

## Interpellation: How Ideology Recruits Its Subjects

The category of the subject, Althusser argues, is the constitutive category of all ideology, and the sentence is built to be unfolded slowly. In ordinary philosophy the subject is the free, self originating centre of action: the person who thinks, chooses and answers for choices. Althusser takes the word's other sense, the sense alive in grammar and in law, where a subject is one who is subjected, placed under an authority, the subject of a monarch or of a verb. His claim is that the two senses are one operation seen from two sides. Ideology constitutes individuals as subjects who experience themselves as free precisely in the act of submitting to the order that constitutes them. The worker who signs the contract as a free agent, the citizen who votes as a sovereign chooser, the student who enters the examination as the author of her results: each performs freedom inside a form that assigned the role, set the options and will grade the performance. Subjection and selfhood arrive together, as a package, and the feeling of freedom is not a disguise laid over the subjection but the very mode in which it is lived.

The mechanism that performs this constitution is interpellation, a word Althusser borrows from the vocabulary of hailing and formal address. Ideology, he writes, interpellates individuals as subjects: it addresses them, names them, assigns them a place, and in their recognition of the address as meant for them, they become the subjects it described. The theory therefore turns on recognition, and recognition of a peculiar kind, since what is recognised is an identity that the act of recognising brings into being. Before the mechanism can be assessed, it must be stated exactly, and the next heading does that work in the compressed form the essay itself invites.

### What does interpellation mean?

Althusser's term for the way ideology addresses individuals so that they recognise themselves as its subjects. His illustration is a police officer calling out in the street; the person who turns around has, in that turn, accepted the identity the hail assigned them.

The illustration is famous enough to have eaten the theory, so its details deserve the care usually denied them. A police officer calls out, hey, you there, to a figure in the street. The figure turns around. By that mere physical conversion of a turn, Althusser argues, the individual becomes a subject: because he has recognised that the hail was addressed to him, that he was its target and not another, he has accepted a place in a structure of address whose other pole is authority. The scene is deliberately minimal, a little theatre staged, as Althusser concedes, for expository convenience, and he immediately complicates it. The hail need not be a policeman's; most hails are not. They arrive as names called across playgrounds, as letters addressed to the householder, as examination results addressed to the candidate, as the greeting of a form that begins by asking who you are. Nor need the turn be physical. What matters is the structure: an address, a recognition, and an identity confirmed by being recognised. The scene also contains a quiet asymmetry that commentary often flattens. The officer does not argue, persuade or deceive. Nothing propositional passes between the two figures at all. Interpellation is not a theory of persuasion, and treating it as one, as though ideology talked people into their places with clever messages, misses the mechanism's austerity: the place is occupied in the act of answering to a name.

To keep the argument visible beneath the anecdote, the interpellation walkthrough below breaks the scene into its analytical steps and attaches to each step the objection it attracts and the reply available within the theory. The interpellation walkthrough is the article's findable artifact, presented as a table, and readers pressed for the argument's skeleton can reconstruct the whole essay from it, which is more than the anecdote alone permits. Read the rows in sequence and the theory's logic becomes difficult to mistake: nothing in the scene persuades, nothing deceives, and yet a subject is produced, because the production happens in the recognition rather than in any message the recognition carries. Read the objection column with equal care, since it previews, in miniature, every major criticism the later sections develop at length, the circularity of recognition, the smuggled theology of the guarantee, and the puzzle of conduct that feels free while following a script. The table therefore does double duty in this profile, as a summary of the mechanism and as a map of the controversy, and readers who return to it after the objection sections will find that the replies column, adequate inside the theory, is exactly where the theory's jurisdiction ends.

| The hail | An officer calls out to a figure in the street | Ideology addresses individuals, assigning a place before any argument begins | A call is just communication, not constitution | The claim concerns recognition, not information: nothing is communicated except a position |

The mirror structure, which the table's fourth row compresses, is the essay's most intricate piece of machinery and the one that connects interpellation to the imaginary. Taking religious ideology as his model case, Althusser describes a central Subject, written with a capital letter, who interpellates individuals in a doubled mirror relation. The Subject addresses the individual, as God addresses Moses; the individual recognises himself in the address and recognises the Subject as the centre from which the address comes; subjects recognise one another as fellow subjects of the same centre; and the whole structure carries a guarantee, the promise that everything will be well for those who keep their assigned place, sealed in the formula of a covenant. Recognition thus runs in a circle of mirrors: the subject sees himself in the Subject, the Subject sees and ratifies the subject, and each pole confirms the other's reality. The structure explains a feature of ideology that persuasion models cannot, its self sealing quality. To doubt one's place is already to occupy the position of the doubter the structure provides, the sinner, the failing student, the difficult employee, positions with their own rituals of correction and return.

### Why is recognition in this theory also a misrecognition?

Because what the subject recognises is an imaginary version of his real position. The turn toward the hail confirms an identity, free, self originating, answerable, that feels like the most obvious fact in the world, while the conditions that assigned the place and scripted the turn disappear from view. Recognition of the self is purchased by misrecognising the mechanism.

The mirror machinery also explains why the theory treats ideology as having no outside for the subject who lives it. A mirror structure cannot be stepped out of by an act of the same recognition that constitutes it, since the stepping out would be recognised, by the subject and by others, through the categories the structure supplies: rebellion itself is interpellated, given names, costumes and careers. Althusser's own escape route is not experiential but theoretical: a science of the mechanisms, of the kind he believed historical materialism could become, knows ideology without ceasing to live it. Whether that exemption can be sustained is one of the deepest questions the essay leaves open, and it returns in the discussion of the circle below. What should not be missed is the elegance of the problem the mirror structure solves. Ideology must explain why dominated relations are lived by the dominated as natural, meaningful and often as the very medium of their selfhood. A structure in which the subject's identity is guaranteed by a centre, mirrored among fellows, and confirmed in daily ritual answers that demand without postulating either stupidity or deceit, the two postulates that disfigure lesser theories.

## The Debt to Lacan

The mirror structure is not Althusser's invention, and he does not pretend otherwise: it is borrowed from Jacques Lacan, whose account of the mirror stage had described the infant's jubilant identification with an image of its own bodily unity, an image that gives the child an ego at the price of alienation, since the unity is outside, in the mirror, and the self is founded on a misrecognition. Althusser takes the machinery and socialises it. Where Lacan's infant meets its image, Althusser's individual meets the gaze and voice of the ideological order, embodied in the Subject whose recognition promises coherence. The borrowing explains several of the essay's distinctive choices. It explains why the imaginary is a register rather than a synonym for the false. It explains why recognition and misrecognition are the same act described twice, once from inside the experience and once from the standpoint of the mechanism. And it explains the theory's indifference to propositional content: a mirror does not argue with the child, and an ideology, in the decisive moment, does not argue with the subject either. Both present an image in which the subject already appears, completed, and the presentation does the work that arguments cannot.

The debt also imports Lacan's difficulties, and intellectual honesty requires naming the exchange rate. Psychoanalytic concepts carry their own contestations, and a social theory that builds its central mechanism from the mirror stage inherits the standing of that stage within psychoanalysis, where it is illuminating to many clinicians and contestable to many others. Althusser's use is structural rather than clinical: he needs a model in which identity is conferred from outside and experienced from inside as self evident, and the mirror supplies it with a precision no sociological vocabulary of his moment could match. Readers who resist the psychoanalytic frame can translate the mechanism into the language of recognition developed by other traditions, the Hegelian struggle for recognition, the sociology of labelling, the interactionist account of the self as seen through others' eyes, and the translation preserves much of the machinery. What it loses is the guarantee structure, the Subject with a capital letter whose recognition seals the circuit, and with it the explanation of ideology's peculiar consolation: the promise, renewed by every hail answered, that somewhere the accounts of who we are are kept and balanced. Secular formations keep the promise in secular currencies, the career that will vindicate, the nation that will remember, the record that will show, and the persistence of the guarantee across theologies is among the strongest evidence that Althusser's borrowing, whatever its provenance, touches something real in the material.

## The Always-Already Circle

Every exposition of interpellation reaches the same bump in the road, and honest ones slow down for it. If interpellation constitutes subjects, then before the hail there is an individual who is not yet a subject. But the scene requires that individual to recognise the hail as addressed to him, and recognition is an act only a subject can perform: it involves understanding an address, taking it as meant for oneself, and answering from an identity. The mechanism therefore presupposes the very thing it claims to produce. Althusser saw the bump clearly, and his response gives this article its namable claim. The always-already circle is the name used here for his answer and its cost: individuals, he argues, are always already subjects, interpellated before birth by the familial configuration that expects them, names them in advance, assigns them a sex, a place in a lineage and a set of anticipations into which they are born. There never was a first hail for any actual person, because the ideological structure into which each person arrives precedes them, and the temporal sequence of the street scene is a theoretical fiction, a way of displaying a structure that in reality has no beginning for the individual concerned.

The answer is partly a defence and partly a concession, and the two parts should be priced separately. As a defence, it corrects a misreading: the street scene was never a claim about chronological origins, any more than a diagram of a circle claims the circle was drawn starting at the top. Interpellation describes how subjectivity is continuously reproduced, hail by hail, across a life that begins inside an already functioning familial ideology. The child addressed by name, corrected, praised and positioned from infancy does not need a founding moment of constitution; constitution is the medium, not an event. As a concession, however, the answer gives up exactly what a theory of the subject might be wanted for: an account of where subjectivity comes from, and with it, an account of how subjectivity could fail to form or form otherwise. If individuals are always already subjects, the theory describes the reproduction of subjectivity with great power and its origin not at all, and it struggles to say what would count as a person escaping interpellation rather than being reinterpellated under a new name. The circle is not a refutation, since many respectable theories are circular in their foundations, but it fixes the theory's jurisdiction. Interpellation is a mechanics of maintenance. Questions of genesis and of rupture must be answered from elsewhere, and the later sections show Althusser's readers dividing over whether the longer manuscript supplies the missing account.

The circle also has a second turn that critics have pressed with relish. If recognition is guaranteed by the structure, what happens when the hail fails: when the figure does not turn, or turns laughing, or answers to the name in order to mock it? The essay's machinery has little to say about failed hails beyond reclassifying them, since the heckler and the refuser are themselves recognisable positions, bad subjects in Althusser's own revealing phrase, who provoke the repressive apparatus where good subjects work by themselves. That phrase deserves the attention it has received. A theory that sorts humanity into good subjects, who reproduce their subjection freely, and bad subjects, who are handed over to repression, has built its exceptions into a police category, and the sorting is done by the theory's own criterion of success. Failed interpellation becomes evidence of bad subjectivity rather than evidence against interpellation. The objection does not destroy the concept, which retains great descriptive power for the ordinary run of institutional life, but it marks the point where description hardens into a closed system, and the functionalism objection below generalises exactly this worry.

The doctrine's most concrete illustration is also its most unsettling, and it deserves more than the passing mention it usually receives. The child, Althusser argues, is interpellated before birth: the familial ideological configuration awaits it with a name already chosen or narrowed, a sex anticipated and prepared for, a place in the sequence of siblings, a set of resemblances to be remarked and futures to be projected. The surname assigns a lineage before the bearer can speak; the given name, chosen by others for reasons steeped in family memory, religion and fashion, is answered to before it is understood. Nothing in this is sinister in itself, and the theory does not require it to be: the point is structural, that the positions precede the person, and that by the time a person can ask who assigned the place, the asking is done in a language and a name the assignment supplied. Parents who choose a name precisely to refuse the family's traditions illustrate the structure as faithfully as those who obey them, since the refusal too is a position inside the configuration, legible to everyone in it. The illustration earns its place because it is the one case where the circle cannot be dismissed as a philosopher's trick. Nobody remembers choosing to be the person their name prepared, yet nobody experiences the name as an alien imposition either. The hail has been answered so early and so continuously that the answering feels like the self, which is the theory's whole claim, stated at the scale of a single biography.

One more feature of the always already doctrine should be registered before leaving it, because it separates Althusser from the socialisation theories with which he is often shelved. Socialisation, in the standard sociological sense, takes a biological individual and adds social content over time, as a vessel is filled. The always already claim denies the vessel. There is no pre ideological individual in the theory, not as a stage and not as a residue: even the needs and drives with which a child arrives are taken up, from the first feeding schedule onward, into practices saturated with meaning and place. This is why Althusser's Freud analogy matters to him. The unconscious, for Freud, is not acquired at some datable point in development as a possession; it is the condition under which a human life is lived at all. Ideology in general claims the same status in the social register. Readers who find the claim grandiose can still use the concept in its weaker, defensible form: for any actual person, the hails that formed them precede their capacity to examine them, so self knowledge always arrives late to its own constitution. That weak form, shorn of eternity, is what most working researchers quietly take from the essay.

## Good Subjects Who Work by Themselves

The essay's most quoted sentence after the definition is its observation that the subjects, in their vast majority, work by themselves. The phrase completes the theory's central transaction. An order that had to coerce every act would collapse under the cost of its own policing; a durable order arranges for the policing to be done by the policed, who experience their compliance as their character. The good subject, in Althusser's chilling vocabulary, is the one in whom the hail has been so thoroughly internalised that the external voice is rarely needed: the employee who feels the deadline as a personal obligation, the student who experiences a grade as a verdict on the self, the citizen who queues, files and declares without being watched. Against these stand the bad subjects, the minority who do not turn, or who turn only to refuse, and for them the system keeps its other apparatus in reserve. The proportion matters to the argument. Repression, on this account, is not the normal cement of the social order but its backstop, and the size of the backstop a formation requires is an index of its ideological health: the more the good subjects multiply, the quieter the barracks can afford to be.

Althusser supports the picture with a taxonomy that summaries usually skip, his division of the agents whom reproduction requires. A formation needs agents of repression, who operate its coercive machinery; agents of exploitation, who organise production and appropriate its surplus; and professionals of ideology, priests, teachers, journalists, officials of doctrine, who elaborate and administer the representations under which the whole is lived. Each category must itself be reproduced, trained, certified and kept in post, and each is formed inside the apparatuses it serves, so the system maintains its maintainers through the same machinery it maintains everyone else. The taxonomy is worth recovering because it blocks a persistent misreading, the idea that the theory posits a conspiracy of the knowing against the duped. The professionals of ideology are not exempt from interpellation; they are its most thoroughly formed products, which is why they can administer it with clean consciences. The priest believes, the teacher believes in merit, the editor believes in the news values that structure the bulletin. Sincerity is not the opposite of ideological function in this theory. It is the function's preferred fuel, and an apparatus staffed by cynics would work far worse than one staffed by the convinced.

There is a hard question buried in the good subject doctrine, and the essay's defenders should not bury it deeper. If working by oneself is the signature of successful interpellation, then the theory risks treating every competent, conscientious act as evidence of subjection, including the acts of its own adherents teaching the theory in universities. The schoolteacher of Althusserianism, punctual, examined, credentialed, reproducing the doctrine through the dominant apparatus, is either a counterexample or the theory's most elegant confirmation, and seminar rooms have enjoyed the paradox for decades. Althusser's answer, implied by his doctrine of science, is that knowing the mechanism changes one's relation to it without abolishing the relation: the formed subject who studies formation does not cease to be formed, but the knowledge is a real difference in what the formation can do with him unexamined. Whether that difference is large enough to bear the weight the theory loads onto it is, once again, the unfalsifiability question in biographical dress, and it is best left standing where the objection section puts it: as the price of admission to a theory that refuses, on principle, to exempt its own readers.

## The Working Day Presupposes the School

Reproduction is easiest to see from the standpoint of the working day itself, because the working day consumes, hour by hour, exactly what the apparatuses supply. An employer who hires labour power does not receive a generic capacity to work. He receives a person who can read the instructions or operate the terminal, who arrives at the appointed hour in the appointed place, who accepts the foreman's direction and the inspector's correction as features of the landscape, who treats the wage as the price of the day and the week's end as the natural horizon of effort. None of these dispositions is taught by the employer, though all are enforced by him; they arrive with the worker, installed by years of prior formation, and the employer's discipline merely maintains an installation it did not build. The division of labour in reproduction is thus a division between institutions: the apparatuses form, the enterprise consumes and polices the form, and the wage pays for the upkeep of the formed capacity between its consumptions, food, rest, housing, and the raising of the next cohort of the formed.

Marx had already located one ideological mechanism inside the wage relation itself, and Althusser's readers do well to reconnect the essay to it. The wage form, Marx argued, extinguishes every trace of the division of the working day into necessary and surplus labour: paid by the hour or the piece, the worker's whole day appears as paid labour, and the unpaid portion vanishes from the form in which the exchange presents itself. No belief needs to be implanted for the misrecognition to work; it is built into the shape of the payment, repeated at every payday, and lived by both parties as the obvious meaning of fairness. Here is ideology with a material existence in its purest instance, a misrecognition that no amount of correct opinion dissolves, since the worker who has read the analysis still receives the wage in the same form and still experiences the day through it. The essay's apparatus theory generalises this insight from the wage form to the whole institutional environment: forms hail, rituals confirm, and the representations in heads are the least load bearing part of the structure. That generalisation, from a single form inside production to the architecture of a whole formation, is the essay's true scale, and it is why the article on reproduction had to precede the articles on schools: the school is the wage form's logic, extended backward into childhood and outward into every institution that prepares a person to find the working day natural.

## The School as Dominant Apparatus

Which of the ideological apparatuses dominates in mature capitalist formations? Althusser's answer is the essay's most consequential empirical claim and the one that education research took up first. In medieval formations, he argues, the church was dominant, fusing religious, educational, cultural and much political function in a single apparatus served by a specialist clergy. The bourgeois order progressively displaced it, and the apparatus that inherited its position is the school. The displacement is not measured by noise or visibility. The communications apparatus shouts louder, and the political apparatus stages grander ceremonies. Dominance is measured by reach and by compulsory intimacy: no other apparatus gathers the entire child population, holds it for years of daily attendance, for hours a day, across the most formable period of life, with the legal and moral authority of the family delivered into its hands each morning. The school family couple, Althusser argues, replaced the church family couple as the load bearing alliance of the formation, and everything else in the apparatus system is arranged around the school's long, quiet work.

What the school delivers, on the essay's analysis, is a double product matched to the double requirement of reproduction. It delivers know how: literacy, numeracy, technical and professional competences, distributed in quantities and kinds corresponding to the posts the division of labour must fill. And it delivers that know how wrapped in the ruling ideology, or in forms that rehearse submission to it: respect for the division of labour itself, for its hierarchies of qualification, for the ethos of the well performed task, and, for those ejected early, for an account of their ejection as the measure of their own gifts. The school's sorting is therefore not a malfunction of its educational mission but the junction where the two products are combined. Pupils leave at different ages, through different doors, carrying certificates whose exchange value differs and carrying also an explanation of why the door they left by was the right one for them. Those destined for the posts of command learn, alongside advanced know how, the arts of commanding and of speaking to the commanded; those destined for the bench and the counter learn, alongside basic know how, the arts of being commanded without experiencing the command as an affront.

### How does the school reproduce both skills and submission at once?

By fusing them in a single process. The timetable that teaches numeracy also teaches punctuality, deference to the bell and acceptance of assessment by superiors, and the sorting that certifies different levels of skill simultaneously certifies different expectations about who will direct work and who will be directed, with the outcome explained to each leaver as personal merit.

The examination is the school's master ritual, and it concentrates every element of the theory into a single ceremony. It is compulsory and universal within its cohort, so its reach matches the apparatus itself. It is performed bodily, in silence, in rows, under surveillance, within a time discipline announced and enforced by bells and invigilators. It interpellates each candidate as the sole author of a result, addressing the script to a name and a number, returning the outcome as a property of the person rather than of the preparation, the household or the school, so that the sorting the system requires is experienced by the sorted as self knowledge. And it carries the guarantee structure in secular form: the certificate promises that the place it assigns is the place the candidate's own qualities earned, a promise the successful and the unsuccessful are equally formed to believe, since both sat the same paper in the same silence. No textbook teaches this lesson. The ritual teaches it, annually, at scale, which is why the school's ideological work would survive the rewriting of every syllabus in the country, and why reforms aimed at content alone leave the apparatus, in Althusser's sense, exactly as it was.

The claim should be stated with its proper scope, because defenders and critics both inflate it. Althusser does not say that teachers conspire to indoctrinate, nor that the content of lessons is ruling class propaganda; the materiality thesis would make such an account crude, since the work is done by the practices, the examinations, the streaming, the discipline, rather than by slogans in textbooks. Nor does he say the school only reproduces: the same essay that crowns the school also insists that the ideological apparatuses are sites of class struggle, and teachers figure among his examples of those who work, sometimes heroically, to turn the apparatus against its reproductive function. What he claims is structural and statistical: taken as a system, across a whole formation and a whole generation, the school reliably delivers labour power in the forms production requires, and it does so with an efficiency no church ever matched, because its compulsion is universal and its legitimacy, the belief that education is the neutral road to self realisation, is the most widely shared ideology of the societies it serves. The belief is not false, which is the point the slogans miss. Education does realise selves. The theory asks which selves, sorted how, for which places, and at whose direction the sorting criteria were set.

## Rival Machines: Correspondence and Cultural Capital

The school thesis did not develop in a vacuum, and measuring it against its two great contemporaries sharpens what is distinctive in it. In the same decade, the American economists Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis argued, in Schooling in Capitalist America, that education reproduces the relations of production through a correspondence between the social relations of the classroom and those of the workplace: hierarchy answering hierarchy, external rewards answering wages, fragmentation of tasks answering fragmentation of knowledge. The resemblance to Althusser is obvious and the difference is instructive. Correspondence is a claim about structural resemblance and its effects on personality; it requires no theory of the subject, and its agents remain the choosing individuals of ordinary social science, formed by incentives rather than constituted by recognition. In France, Pierre Bourdieu and Jean Claude Passeron had published their own account of reproduction in 1970, centred on cultural capital and on pedagogic action as symbolic violence: the school consecrates the culture of the dominant class as universal culture, and children unequally endowed with that culture at home are sorted by examinations whose neutrality is the mechanism's disguise. Bourdieu's machinery is empirical where Althusser's is structural; capitals can be measured, inherited, converted, and the theory generates the research programmes that filled journals for decades.

Set side by side, the three accounts divide the explanatory labour. Bourdieu explains the differential: why children of one class harvest the school's rewards so much more reliably than children of another, a question Althusser's essay, for all its talk of sorting, treats only in outline. Bowles and Gintis explain the workplace fit: which dispositions production actually orders from the school and how the order is transmitted through organisational resemblance. Althusser explains the experience: why the sorted and the sorters alike live the process as the free unfolding of their own natures, and why the explanation they give themselves never mentions reproduction at all. A researcher choosing among them is therefore not choosing between a true theory and two false ones, whatever the polemics of the period claimed. The choice is among questions, and the questions stack: endowment, correspondence and constitution operate at different levels of the same institution, and the literature's later syntheses quietly use all three while citing the quarrels. What Althusser alone contributes is the insistence that the subject is an effect to be explained rather than a premise to be assumed, and that insistence, exported from the essay into fields its author never entered, is the deepest channel of his influence.

## An Extract, Not a Testament

The single most underreported fact about the essay is bibliographical, and it changes the obligations of anyone citing it. The text published in 1970, and reprinted in Lenin and Philosophy and Other Essays, the collection through which most English readers meet it, is an extract. Althusser cut it from a longer manuscript devoted to reproduction, a book length study in which the ideology essay formed one movement of a larger argument. The manuscript itself was not published in his lifetime. It appeared in French in the mid 1990s, several years after his death, under the title Sur la reproduction, and an English translation, On the Reproduction of Capitalism, followed in the mid 2010s. For roughly a quarter of a century, therefore, the most influential statement of Marxist ideology theory in the world was a fragment, read as a whole, taught as a whole, and criticised as a whole, while the whole from which it was cut sat in an archive.

The fuller text matters for substance and not only for piety toward sources. In the manuscript, the discussion of the apparatuses is embedded in an extended treatment of the state, of law, and of class struggle, and the reproductive machinery is repeatedly interrupted by the resistance it must overcome. Readers of the extract complained, reasonably on the evidence before them, that Althusser's system ran by itself, that struggle appeared as a decorative concession in a closing paragraph. Readers of the manuscript meet a different balance: reproduction there is a stake fought over, the apparatuses are arenas before they are instruments, and the dominance of an ideology is an outcome to be explained rather than a premise to be assumed. Whether the fuller text answers the functionalism objection or merely surrounds it with more material is a genuine scholarly question, and honest exposition should present it as open. What is not open is the citation practice the extract's history imposes. To cite the 1970 essay as Althusser's theory of ideology, full stop, is now a methodological error of the same kind as citing a chapter as a book. The extract remains the canonical doorway, and its formulations remain the ones to quote, but they should be quoted as formulations of 1970, positioned within a project whose complete statement exists and can be consulted.

### What did the longer manuscript restore to the argument?

Chiefly, class struggle at the centre of the machinery. The book length study from which the essay was cut treats law, the state and the apparatuses at far greater length and presents reproduction as a stake fought over inside the institutions, not a result they deliver automatically, which softens the extract's most criticised feature without removing it.

The extract's history also explains some of its peculiarities of tone. A text cut from a larger whole tends to overstate its theses, because the qualifications lived in the chapters left behind. The eternity thesis, the always already subjects, the apparatuses functioning with machined reliability: each reads, in the manuscript's neighbourhood, more like a provisional extreme, a position stated at full stretch so that its logic can be inspected. Althusser's own subtitle, notes towards an investigation, signals exactly this provisional status, and his later self criticism, in which he conceded a theoreticist deviation in his work of the period, confirms that he did not regard the formulations of those years as final doctrine. None of this licences the lazy reading that treats the essay as a sacred text whose every sentence must be defended. The author's own trajectory points the other way: toward treating the essay as the sharpest draft of an investigation that its author continued, revised and in places abandoned. A profile that restores the argument must restore that provisional status too, because a theory presented as beyond revision is being misrepresented even by its admirers.

## Reception: Screens, Classrooms and Historians

The essay's afterlife ran along three main tracks, and each track took a different part of the machinery. Film studies took interpellation. Through the journal Screen and the current known as Screen theory, Althusser's account of the subject became the foundation of apparatus theory in cinema: the spectator, seated in the dark, addressed by the film's organisation of looks, was analysed as a subject constituted in the viewing position, recognising himself in the camera's gaze much as the figure in the street recognises himself in the hail. The names attached to that development, among them Colin MacCabe and Stephen Heath, built a research programme on the borrowed mechanism, and for a generation the humanities seminar on cinema was an Althusserian seminar whether or not the syllabus said so. Education research took the dominant apparatus thesis, and the sociology of schooling spent years testing, refining and contesting the claim that the school's reproductive work is done by its practices and sortings rather than by its declared curriculum. Media studies took the materiality thesis and the communications apparatus, and learned from them to study formats, schedules and modes of address with the same seriousness as content.

The third track was hostile, and it produced the essay's most important criticism. Historians, above all E. P. Thompson in The Poverty of Theory, published in 1978, read the Althusserian system as an offence against their discipline and against politics. Thompson's polemic is a large book with many targets, theoreticism, structuralism, the expulsion of agency and experience from history, but its pressure converges on the ideology essay's machinery. History, for Thompson, is a record of people making their own lives under inherited conditions, surprising every system built to contain them; a theory in which apparatuses reproduce relations and subjects work by themselves leaves the historian with nothing to study except the system's self confirmation. The polemic's ferocity reflected the stakes as Thompson saw them: a Marxism that explained away the agency of actual people had, in his judgement, ceased to be a theory of liberation and become a theology of the process. Whatever verdict a reader reaches on the quarrel, it fixed the terms in which the essay has been debated ever since. Defenders answer Thompson by pointing to the struggle passages and to the manuscript. Critics answer the defenders by noting that the struggle passages must do all the work the system forbids. The next section states that objection in its strongest analytic form, stripped of polemic, because the essay deserves to be tested at its hardest point rather than its weakest.

Within France the reception had a party political charge that the English language debate often loses. Althusser was the most prominent philosopher inside the French Communist Party, and his anti-humanism was aimed, in part, at the party's own official humanists, who had made the young Marx of alienation into the doctrine's public face. To argue, from inside, that the mature Marx had broken with humanism, and that ideology theory must begin from structures rather than from man's essence, was to contest the party's self presentation while remaining its most famous intellectual asset, a position maintained for years at considerable personal and institutional cost. The ideology essay belongs to that internal war as much as to the seminar room: its insistence that the school and the family, not the factory gate, are where the order renews itself carried a strategic rebuke to a movement still picturing revolution as the seizure of the commanding heights. Readers who know only the academic afterlife miss this first context, in which the essay was an intervention in a living organisation's argument about where change could possibly begin.

The reception also had a quieter, institutional dimension worth naming. The essay became a set text, which is to say it became part of the very machinery it describes: an examination syllabus is an ideological practice too, with its rituals of quotation, its approved formulations, its good subjects who reproduce the interpellation paragraph on demand. Althusserian concepts survived in the academy partly because they were teachable, examinable and endlessly applicable, qualities that guarantee reproduction in the educational apparatus regardless of a theory's truth. Noting this is not a refutation; true theories are also teachable. But it explains a feature of the essay's presence that puzzlement about its difficulty cannot: the text is hard, allusive and unfinished, yet it colonised syllabuses across several disciplines within a decade. Ideas travel on institutions, as the essay itself would predict, and its own itinerary is a case study in its thesis that practices, not propositions, do the heavy lifting of intellectual history.

## After Screen: Audiences Answer Back

The media reception of the essay has its own second act, which complicates the first in ways that mirror the larger debate. Screen theory's spectator, constituted by the apparatus of cinema and positioned by its looks and cuts, was a figure of great theoretical elegance and almost no empirical curiosity: nobody had asked actual audiences whether they occupied the positions the theory assigned. The correction came from within the same broad tradition. Stuart Hall's encoding and decoding model, developed through the 1970s at the Birmingham centre for cultural studies, kept the insight that texts propose positions for their readers while insisting that audiences may take those positions up in dominant, negotiated or oppositional modes, decoding the message against the grain of its preferred reading. David Morley's audience research then supplied the missing empiricism, showing viewers of the same current affairs programme producing systematically different readings patterned by their social positions, though rarely by simple refusal. The sequence is the functionalism debate replayed inside media studies: a machinery of positioning, an objection from the side of agency and evidence, and a settlement in which the machinery survives as a map of pressures while the outcomes are returned to research. Hall's settlement is of particular interest to readers of the essay because it was built from Gramsci as much as from Althusser, hegemony supplying the contest that interpellation lacked, and it stands as the most influential practical demonstration that the two frameworks of the comparison section can be made to work together when a researcher, rather than a system, is doing the joining.

## The Feminist Reading

No body of readers tested the essay against more recalcitrant material than the feminist scholars who took it up in the decade after publication, and their divided verdict is among the most instructive in the reception. The attraction was real. Here was a Marxist theory in which the family was not a private refuge outside the analysis but a named apparatus with reproductive work to do, in which subjectivity itself was constituted rather than natural, and in which the sexual division of labour could be treated as an ideological formation rather than a biological residue. Michèle Barrett's book length study of women's oppression under capitalism, the most careful of these engagements, drew on the essay's machinery to refuse both the reduction of women's subjection to capitalism's needs and its treatment as an eternal patriarchy, insisting instead on the historically specific ideological constructions, of femininity, of domesticity, of the family form itself, through which subjection is organised. The concepts travelled: interpellation gave a generation of feminist film theory its account of how cinema positions women as spectacle and men as bearers of the look, and the analysis of the gaze that followed, associated above all with Laura Mulvey's intervention in Screen, is unthinkable without the Althusserian machinery it adapted.

The criticisms from the same quarter cut as deep as the borrowings. The essay's family apparatus is described from the standpoint of its function for capital, and the specific subjection of women inside it, the unpaid labour, the legal and economic dependence, the violence the private form shelters, appears as an instance of a general reproduction rather than as a structure with its own history and its own beneficiaries among men of every class. The subject of interpellation, moreover, arrives strangely unsexed for a theory that elsewhere insists on concreteness: the hail that constitutes a daughter differs from the hail that constitutes a son in content, in ritual and in the places each is prepared for, and a machinery that cannot register the difference has abstracted away one of the oldest assignments of place there is. Barrett's settlement, refined across the subsequent literature, keeps the framework and disciplines it: the apparatuses are real, the functions are real, but the analysis must be rewritten at each point where the general subject turns out to be two subjects formed for unequal places. That settlement, like Hall's in media studies, shows the essay working as its best readers have always made it work, not as a closed system to be believed but as a set of questions sharp enough to survive their own answers.

## Cases That Strain the Machinery

A theory earns trust by facing the cases that fit it worst, and the ideology essay's worst cases are not obscure. The school, crowned as the chief reproductive machine, produced in the author's own country and decade a student movement that filled the streets in May 1968 and very nearly brought down the state the school supposedly served. The church, the template apparatus of submission, sheltered in Latin America a liberation theology whose priests organised the poor against the orders the church had blessed for centuries. The army, the core of the repressive apparatus, dissolved into mutiny and soldiers' councils in Russia in 1917 and in Portugal in 1974, turning its weapons and its loyalties against the formation it existed to defend. Broadcasting systems built to unify national audiences have repeatedly furnished the images and the information by which governments were discredited. Each case is an apparatus performing the opposite of its assigned function, and together they form the empirical core of the objection stated in the next section: whatever these institutions are doing, reliably reproducing the relations of production is a poor description of it at the moments that matter most.

The theory's handling of such cases deserves a precise verdict, neither dismissal nor surrender. Three of its resources are genuine. The plurality of the apparatuses means that contradiction between them is predicted, not anomalous: a church formed by a universalist doctrine carries resources its reproductive employment cannot fully control, and a school that teaches literacy and criticism hands its pupils instruments that outrun the sorting. The class struggle strand, thin in the extract and thick in the manuscript, treats the apparatuses as occupied territory rather than as property, and occupation can be contested from inside by the occupied, as the liberation priests and the mutinous soldiers were insiders before they were rebels. And the theory of the conjuncture, developed in Althusser's work on overdetermination, denies that reproduction is ever the only process under way: ruptures condense where multiple contradictions fuse, and no apparatus analysis was meant to predict them from its own resources. Against these resources stands a stubborn remainder. The theory explains the failures of reproduction retrospectively, by reaching for the struggle strand, and it explains the successes prospectively, by the machinery strand, and a reader is entitled to ask whether the choice of strand is ever dictated by anything but the outcome. The honest position, adopted by the best of the essay's later users, is to treat the machinery as a map of the pressures acting on institutions in quiet times, and to reach for other instruments, organisational, biographical, accidental, when the quiet times end. That division of labour concedes the objection's force while keeping the map, and the map, for quiet times, has no serious rival.

## The Functionalism Objection

The objection must now be given at full strength, in the form its most serious proponents would recognise. It runs as follows. The essay explains institutions by the function they perform for the reproduction of the relations of production: schools exist, in the analysis, because capital needs sorted and submissive labour power; families, churches and broadcasters are gathered into the system because each contributes to the same renewal. But explaining a thing by the need it satisfies is not explaining it at all unless a mechanism connects the need to the thing's existence and maintenance, and the essay supplies none. Needs do not summon institutions into being. Worse, the structure of the explanation immunises itself against evidence. Where an institution reproduces the relations, the theory is confirmed. Where an institution obstructs, disrupts or simply bumbles along indifferent to them, the theory reclassifies the case: contradiction within the apparatuses, uneven development, a site of struggle, a bad subject provoking repression. Every possible observation has a drawer prepared for it, and a theory that can accommodate every outcome predicts none. The historical record, moreover, is not a record of smooth reproduction. It is a record of apparatuses failing at their supposed function: schools that produced revolutionaries by the cohort, churches that sheltered rebellion, armies that mutinied, broadcasters that discredited the governments that chartered them. A functionalism of reproduction must treat the most consequential events in its domain, the moments when reproduction broke down and history lurched, as noise at the margin of a system whose essence is continuity. That is the objection, and it is the same defect analytical critics identified in vulgar historical materialism: the effect is named as a cause, the outcome is read back into the origin, and the agents whose struggles actually decide outcomes vanish from the explanation.

The reply available to Althusser begins in the essay itself and strengthens in the manuscript. The closing movement of the extract insists that the ideological state apparatuses are not only the stake but also the site of class struggle, and that the resistance of the exploited classes finds means and occasions to express itself there, either by turning the ideologies of the apparatus against its function or by exploiting the contradictions between apparatuses. The dominated ideology, on this strand of the text, is not manufactured by the dominant one; it forms in struggle, in unions, parties and movements that inhabit the apparatuses without belonging to their reproductive design. The manuscript expands this strand considerably, treating the apparatuses as arenas in which the outcome is contested rather than delivered. Defenders add a methodological point: functional language in social theory can be shorthand for a selection process, in which institutions that destabilise a formation are reformed, defunded or suppressed over time, so that survival itself filters for reproductive compatibility without any designer. On that reading the essay sketches a research programme, identify the practices, trace their effects, find the selection pressures, rather than a closed metaphysic.

The concessions owed to the objection should be stated without varnish. The extract as taught, which is the text the world received for twenty five years, invites the functionalist reading on almost every page: its examples run in one direction, its struggle passages are brief relative to its system building, and its author later conceded the theoreticist cast of the work. The mechanism connecting reproductive need to institutional form is gestured at, through state power and class dominance, but never developed with the patience the objection demands. The theory is also asymmetrical in its evidence: it is far better at explaining the ordinary Tuesday, the timetable kept and the form signed, than the extraordinary rupture, and a reader whose questions concern rupture will find the machinery describing everything except the event. One refinement of the objection, from analytical Marxism, deserves separate statement because it converts the complaint into a testable demand. Jon Elster argued that functional explanation in social science is legitimate only where a feedback loop can be demonstrated, a mechanism by which the beneficial consequences of an institution for some group act, without anyone's intending them, to maintain the institution. Biology has such a loop in natural selection; society must find its equivalents case by case, in funding decisions, in the suppression of disruptive experiments, in the career incentives of the professionals who staff the apparatuses, in the quiet non renewal of whatever fails to fit. Judged by that standard, the essay is a promissory note: it identifies the beneficiary of reproduction clearly enough and gestures at state power as the enforcer, but the loops that would cash the note are specified for no single apparatus. The demand is fair, and the research the essay inspired can be read as a generation's attempt to pay it, study by study, mechanism by mechanism, which is perhaps the most productive relation a grand theory can have to its critics: to be converted by them from a system into a workload.

The balanced verdict is therefore jurisdictional. As an account of how social orders persist through the practices of institutions and the constitution of subjects, the essay remains the strongest statement in its tradition, and its concepts earn their keep in research that has nothing to do with its author's system. As an account of how social orders change, break or are overthrown, it is incomplete by its own structure, and it must be supplemented, by the theory of struggle its author kept in the drawer and by the historical work its critics insisted on. Stating the verdict in that divided form is not fence sitting; it is the accurate map of where the machinery works and where it stops.

## The Revolt of the Students and the Author's Self Criticism

The functionalism objection came from historians and analysts; the most wounding objection came from inside the school. Jacques Rancière, who had been Althusser's student and a contributor to Reading Capital, published Althusser's Lesson in 1974, a reckoning that charged the master's theory with reproducing, inside Marxism itself, the very division the apparatuses enforce outside it: on one side the science, held by the theoretician, on the other the ideology in which the masses are sunk and from which only the science can describe them. A theory that begins by denying the subject ends, Rancière argued, by installing one subject exempt from its law, the knowing philosopher, and by treating the revolts of actual workers and students as raw material for a knowledge produced elsewhere. The charge had biographical voltage, since the students of 1968 had been told by the party's philosophers that their movement misunderstood its own meaning, but its analytic core survives the voltage: it is the circularity objection turned political. A theory of interpellation that cannot interpellate its own author is incomplete at the exact point where it claims completeness, and the exemption of science is the seam where the system can be pulled.

Althusser's own later writings pulled at the seam themselves. In the self critical texts of the mid 1970s he conceded that his work of the previous decade had carried a theoreticist deviation, an overvaluation of theory's autonomy and a corresponding thinness about the class struggle his concepts were meant to serve, and he revised the definition of philosophy accordingly, toward philosophy as, in the last instance, class struggle in the field of theory. The concessions do not retract interpellation, and he never disowned the essay, but they withdraw the protective claim that surrounded it: the formulations of 1970 are repositioned by their own author as moments in an investigation marked by an error he named. For the reader, the self criticism completes the extract problem from the other side. Not only is the canonical text a fragment of a larger manuscript; it is a fragment its author subsequently surrounded with retractions of emphasis, so that citing it as settled doctrine misrepresents the author's own final relation to it. The mature way to hold the essay, the way this profile has tried to model, is as a powerful, partial, self acknowledgedly provisional construction, strongest where it describes the machinery of persistence and weakest where persistence gives way to change, and most valuable when its concepts are put to work rather than recited.

## Althusser Beside Gramsci

No comparison has organised the essay's reception more than the one with Gramsci, and the pairing is instructive precisely because the two thinkers agree on the terrain and disagree on almost everything built there. Both reject the picture of domination as force plus fraud. Both locate the stability of a social order in the institutions of civil life, in schools, churches, associations and media, rather than in the barracks. Both therefore hand political strategy the same unwelcome message: the order is defended in depth, in the everyday, and taking the ministries changes less than the insurrectionary imagination supposed. The overlap is real, and the essay's readers in the Italian tradition were quick to note that the ideological apparatuses redraw, in structural vocabulary, the map Gramsci had drawn with the concepts of civil society and hegemony. Althusser acknowledged the proximity and insisted on the differences, and the differences are where the theories become testable against each other.

Hegemony, for Gramsci, is a relation continuously made and remade: a class leads because it has built alliances, made concessions, educated its own intellectuals and woven its worldview into the common sense of allied groups, and the weaving can fray. Consent in Gramsci is an achievement with authors, strategies and failures, and his prison notebooks are a manual for the counter achievement, the war of position by which a subaltern class builds its own hegemony inside the same institutions. Interpellation has no equivalent workshop. The subject is constituted by structures whose operation does not depend on anyone's strategy, and the dominated appear in the essay mainly as the bearers of places assigned elsewhere. Where Gramsci's theory is a theory of leadership, Althusser's is a theory of machinery, and the contrast explains their different political temperatures: the notebooks breathe with the possibility of reversal, since what was built can be outbuilt, while the essay's air is colder, since machinery does not care who admires it. The neighbouring concept of [overdetermination explained](/2016/01/01/overdetermination-explained/), drawn from Althusser's earlier work, complicates the coldness by insisting that contradictions never arrive singly and that ruptures condense from many uneven strands, but even there the accent falls on the structure of the conjucture rather than on the will of the actors within it.

### Where do Althusser and Gramsci genuinely converge?

On the location of power's daily work. Both place the defence of a social order inside the institutions of civil life, schools, churches, press and associations, rather than in coercion alone, and both conclude that political change must therefore be won on that terrain over long periods, not seized in a single assault on the state.

The comparison also decides a practical question that students of both thinkers inherit: where would change begin? A Gramscian strategy builds counter hegemony patiently, forming organic intellectuals, contesting common sense, and treating every institution of civil society as a trench to be taken and held. An Althusserian diagnosis agrees about the terrain and despairs more deeply about the troops, since the counter hegemon's own cadres arrive interpellated by the apparatuses they set out to capture, and the capture of an apparatus by new personnel leaves its rituals available for the old work. The history of reforming governments supplies melancholy evidence for the despair: administrations change, curricula are rewritten, broadcasters are rechartered, and the sorting, certifying and hailing continue through the new letterheads. The Gramscian reply is that this is exactly why hegemony names a war of position rather than an event: the rituals themselves must be rebuilt, not merely restaffed, and rebuilding them is the work of the organic intellectuals the movement forms for the purpose. On the practical question the two theories thus converge again, at the level of patience, while diverging at the level of hope's justification, Gramsci grounding hope in the demonstrated capacity of subaltern classes to build worlds, Althusser permitting it mainly as a wager the theory cannot underwrite. Movements have usually lived on the Gramscian grounding while thinking with the Althusserian diagnosis, and the combination, unstable as it is, describes the actual intellectual practice of the tradition better than either orthodoxy.

The fairest summary of the comparison is distributive. For questions about how consent is organised, contested and rebuilt by identifiable actors, Gramsci's framework gives the researcher more handles, and its concepts travel into empirical work on parties, media and movements with less friction. For questions about how individuals come to experience assigned places as their own identities, about the rituals that form subjects beneath the level of anyone's leadership, Althusser's framework asks the sharper question and Gramsci's barely asks it at all. The two theories are often taught as rivals for the same office, the Marxist theory of ideology, and generations of students have been examined on choosing between them. The better lesson of the comparison is that they describe different storeys of the same building: hegemony names the politics conducted among already formed subjects and blocs, interpellation names the formation of the subjects who then conduct it. A complete account would need both storeys and a staircase between them, and the absence of the staircase, in both authors, is the honest place to end the comparison.

## The Successors: Conscience, Cynicism and the Turn

The strongest evidence for a theory's life is the quality of the repairs it provokes, and interpellation has been repaired by two successors whose amendments now travel with the concept. Judith Butler, in The Psychic Life of Power, took the street scene as her starting point and asked the question Althusser's theatre suppresses: why does the figure turn with guilt already prepared, as though the law's accusation found an accomplice inside the accused? Her answer, drawing on the theory of conscience, is that the subject is formed through a passionate attachment to its own subjection, a turning back of aggression and desire that precedes any particular hail and makes the hail's success likely rather than miraculous. The amendment preserves the machinery while supplying the missing psychology, and it converts the always already from a concession into a researchable claim about how attachment to authority is laid down in early life. Readers who find Althusser's subject too empty, a place without an interior, will find in Butler the interior the place acquires, and with it an explanation of the phenomenon the essay noted and passed by: that subjects cling to their assigned identities even when the identities injure them, because the identity is also the condition of being recognised at all.

Slavoj Žižek's amendment cuts the other way, against the misrecognition thesis rather than the missing interior. The classical model, including Althusser's, assumes that ideology works because subjects do not know: they misrecognise their real conditions. The cynical subject of developed consumer societies, Žižek argues, knows very well: knows the advertisement is manipulation, the ceremony is theatre, the official story is a story, and acts exactly as the order requires none the less. On this amendment, the illusion has migrated from knowledge to practice; it is not that they do not know, but that their knowing makes no difference to their doing, because the belief is embodied in the act itself, in the money exchanged, the form signed, the ritual performed with a private smirk. The amendment is less foreign to Althusser than its presentation suggests, since the materiality thesis always located ideology in acts rather than in convictions, and a cynical act is still an act inserted into a practice governed by a ritual. What Žižek adds is the demonstration that the turn toward the hail does not require the innocence the street scene implies. The figure may turn ironically, knowingly, even contemptuously, and the machinery loses nothing by it, since the turn, not the inner state, was always the theory's datum. Together the two amendments map the concept's living frontier: Butler deepening the subject the hail presupposes, Žižek thinning the belief the hail requires, and both confirming that the scene in the street remains the place where theories of subjection go to be tested.

## Domination Without a Dominator

The theory poses a moral problem that its technical vocabulary can conceal, and a complete profile should bring it into the open. If reproduction runs through well meaning hands, if the teacher, the priest, the parent and the editor are themselves interpellated and sincere, then the ordinary language of blame loses its address. Nobody designed the machinery, nobody operates it from outside, and yet its outcomes are radically unequal and, on the theory's own premises, serve the dominance of a class. Althusser's framework handles this by relocating responsibility from intentions to positions: what can be judged and changed is not the soul of the functionary but the practice of the apparatus, its rituals, its sortings, its exclusions, and political action accordingly becomes the slow transformation of institutions rather than the exposure of villains. The relocation has real attractions. It explains why systems survive the replacement of their personnel by reformers of undoubted goodwill, a fact that purely moral accounts of domination chronically fail to explain, and it directs energy toward the points where change is mechanically possible: the examination's form, the ritual's script, the apparatus's funding and mandate.

The cost of the relocation should be counted with equal honesty. A politics without villains can decay into a politics without agents of change either, since the same argument that acquits the functionary of design also dissolves the subject who might redesign, leaving transformation to be accomplished by nobody in particular through processes nobody directs. The history of the movements that read Althusser is partly a history of attempts to escape that paralysis: the turn to Gramsci, whose hegemony restores strategists and alliances; the turn to the manuscript, where struggle is written back into the apparatuses; the turn, in the author's own late work, toward an aleatory materialism in which encounters and swerves, rather than structures alone, decide what a conjuncture becomes. Each escape concedes the problem while trying to keep the analysis, and the pattern of concessions is itself the most reliable guide to the essay's balance sheet. As a theory of how domination persists without a dominator, it remains without an equal in its tradition, and the persistence it describes is the part of the phenomenon that moralised accounts cannot see. As a theory of how the dominated become the authors of a different order, it is a beginning that mistakes itself, in its coldest pages, for an end, and its author's own trajectory, restless to the last, is the standing correction to that mistake.

## What Would Count Against the Theory?

A fair hearing for the essay should end by specifying its defeaters, the observations that would genuinely damage it, because a theory whose friends cannot name its defeaters is in the condition its critics describe. Several can be named. If the practices of the dominant apparatus were shown to have no systematic relation to the dispositions workplaces require, if leavers emerged from a decade of schooling measurably indifferent to punctuality, hierarchy and certification, the reproduction thesis would lose its central case. If the sorting performed by examinations proved, on careful study, to track nothing but the skill it certifies, with the certified and the rejected sharing identical expectations about their places, the submission component of the double product would be refuted in its strong form. If subjects formed under one ideological configuration routinely and effortlessly reconstituted themselves under another, treating identities as costumes rather than as selves, the interpellation account of recognition would be weakened at its core, since its whole point is that assigned places are lived as identities and defended as such. And if the historical record showed apparatuses changing their social function as readily as they change their personnel, churches becoming engines of permanent revolution and schools of permanent refusal, the convergence claim behind the word state would collapse into the fragmentation its critics allege.

None of these defeaters has been cleanly delivered, which is part of the theory's survival story, but none has been cleanly excluded either, and the research record is honestly mixed in instructive ways. Studies of schooling find strong effects on credentials and earnings, weaker and more contested effects on dispositions, and persistent evidence that pupils read the institution against its grain in numbers no machinery model would predict. Ethnographies of workplaces find consent manufactured, as the theory expects, but also find it manufactured locally, by the games and bargains of the shop floor, rather than imported ready made from the school, which suggests the apparatuses hand over a less finished product than the essay's system implies. The mature assessment treats the essay as a framework with specified exposure to evidence, strong where institutions are compulsory, prolonged and ritualised, weak where encounters are brief, optional and ironised, and that gradient, between the boarding school and the passing advertisement, is itself a finding the theory makes possible. Stating the gradient is the difference between using Althusser and believing in him, and the essay is better served by users.

There is a final, quieter test the theory passes more often than its rivals, and it belongs in the record before the verdict closes. Ask of any institution the essay's characteristic questions, which rituals form its members, which identities its addresses assign, which centre guarantees the recognition it offers, which sanctions wait behind its persuasions, and the questions generate descriptions that surprise even the institution's own staff, who rarely see their ceremonies as a system. A theory that reliably produces new descriptions of familiar material has earned the pragmatic standing that working researchers grant it, whatever its metaphysical debts. The defeaters listed above mark where the descriptions would stop surprising and start failing, and the fact that the failures remain partial, local and contested, after five decades of motivated scrutiny by historians, feminists, analysts and former students, is the strongest available evidence that the machinery describes something real about how social orders hold their members. Holding is not everything a theory of society must explain, and this profile has insisted on the remainder at every stage, but holding is the thing the essay set out to explain, and it explains it still.

## A Note on Words: Translation and Terminology

The essay's key terms arrive in English carrying losses that a careful reader should price in. The French appareil, rendered apparatus, names any organised contrivance, from a camera to a party machine, and its faint suggestion of equipment is part of the argument: institutions as devices that do work. Interpellation itself is a term of French institutional life before it is a term of theory: a deputy interpellates a minister, formally calling him to account in the chamber, and a police officer interpellates a suspect, calling on him to stop and identify himself. The word thus joins the parliamentary and the police senses, formal address by authority, in a single term, and the English hailing, though vivid, keeps only the street half of the meaning. Most consequential is the pair subject and subjection. French, like English, lets sujet carry both the grammatical agent and the king's subject, and Althusser's argument depends on the double sense being one word's ordinary burden rather than a pun imposed by the theorist. Ben Brewster's translation, through which the English speaking world received the essay, made choices that hardened into doctrine: renderings debated by later translators, and a generation of commentary built on them, remind the reader that even the citation of this text is an institutional practice with its own rituals and authorities. The lesson generalises beyond translation. Every summary, syllabus and profile, this one included, is a small apparatus interpellating its reader as the kind of subject who understands Althusser in a particular way, and the defence against the process is the one the essay itself teaches: return to the practices, in this case to the text, and check who is being hailed to believe what.

## Can Ideology Be Known?

Behind the essay stands a claim about knowledge that its author later softened and its critics never forgave: the distinction between science and ideology. In the work of the 1960s, Althusser held that historical materialism, refounded by Marx's epistemological break with his own humanist beginnings, could know ideology as an object, from a standpoint not itself ideological, much as psychoanalysis knows the unconscious it interprets. The ideology essay presupposes that standpoint every time it describes a misrecognition: someone must be positioned to call the recognition false to its conditions, and that someone is the theorist armed with the science. The claim explains the essay's tone of serene exteriority, its description of rituals the author himself performed daily, written as though from outside the aquarium. It also explains the fury of the replies. Thompson's polemic reserved its deepest contempt for this exteriority, for the implication that the historian's archive and the militant's experience count as ideology while the philosopher's system counts as science, and Rancière's revolt turned on the same point from inside the school.

The subsequent history of the claim is a history of retreats, several led by its author. The self criticism of the 1970s demoted the science ideology contrast from a wall to a frontier under permanent dispute, and the tradition that inherited the essay largely followed: concepts such as interpellation and the apparatuses were retained as instruments while the doctrine of an ideology free standpoint was quietly dropped, and ideology came to be treated, even by Althusserians, as a dimension of all practice including theoretical practice, knowable comparatively and partially rather than from above. The retreat costs the essay its tribunal but not its tools. A reader need not believe in a science outside ideology to use the walkthrough table, to study a school's rituals, or to ask who is hailed by a broadcast and in what identity. The questions survive the metaphysics that first framed them, as questions in the history of science often do, and the essay's durability owes less to its author's theory of knowledge than to the quality of the curiosities it taught its readers to have. That verdict, deflationary as it sounds, is the one the reception supports: the concepts are everywhere in working research, the doctrine of science almost nowhere, and the essay is read, profitably, as an investigation whose instruments outlived its philosophy.

## Reading the Essay Well

A text this compressed, this translated and this excerpted punishes casual reading and rewards a few disciplines, which are worth stating as plainly as the theory itself. The first discipline is order: read the theses in the sequence Althusser wrote them, reproduction, apparatuses, ideology in general, material existence, interpellation, and resist the anthology habit of jumping to the street scene. The scene is the conclusion of an argument, and detached from the argument it becomes a parlour trick, a clever way of saying that authority names us, which is not the claim. The claim is that the naming works because institutions have already formed the one who turns, and that claim can only be assessed with the machinery in view. The second discipline is philological: check which text is being quoted. The formulations of the 1970 extract and the arguments of the posthumous manuscript are related but not interchangeable, and a citation that borrows the extract's slogans while claiming the manuscript's authority commits a quiet fraud that examiners and reviewers are increasingly trained to catch. The third discipline is symmetry: state the theory's strongest version before its weakest point, which is the practice this profile has followed by giving the functionalism objection its own section at full strength rather than scattering reservations through the exposition like a nervous tic.

A fourth discipline concerns proportion. The essay is short, and its brevity tempts commentators to supply the missing bulk from their own systems, so that accounts of Althusser on ideology quietly become accounts of what the commentator wishes he had argued. The guard against this is to keep a running tally, while reading any exposition, of the difference between what the text says, what the longer manuscript adds, and what the tradition has inferred, three categories that summaries habitually merge. The text says that ideology interpellates individuals as subjects; the manuscript surrounds that claim with an analysis of law and struggle that the extract omits; the tradition inferred a complete philosophy of the subject that neither text contains. Holding the tally is laborious, and it is meant to be: most of the errors catalogued in this profile, the state run apparatuses, the persuasion model of interpellation, the essay mistaken for a finished system, are tally errors, cases where an inference was promoted to a quotation or a fragment was promoted to a whole. A reader who keeps the three columns separate will disagree with Althusser, where disagreement is due, about what he actually wrote, which is the only disagreement worth having.

Used with those disciplines, the essay remains an instrument of unusual power for a defined set of questions. It is the strongest available account of why institutions that nobody designs as a system nevertheless mesh into one, why the occupants of a social order experience their places as identities rather than as assignments, and why refuting an ideology's propositions leaves the ideology standing. Its concepts have entered working research across film studies, education, media analysis and political theory, often detached from the system that minted them, and the detachment is itself evidence about the concepts: interpellation, the materiality of ideology and the dominant apparatus are tools that function in other hands, on other problems, which is the practical test a theoretical concept can pass. The same reader should keep the theory's boundaries in view with equal clarity. It does not explain the origin of subjectivity, whatever the eternity thesis gestures toward. It does not explain historical rupture from inside its own resources. It does not tell us why this hail, on this corner, at this moment in a movement's growth, suddenly fails for thousands at once, and the history of the century in which it was written is full of such moments. A theory of reproduction was never going to be a theory of revolution, and the essay's tragedy, if it has one, is that its author was read as offering the second when he had written, and marked as provisional, the first.

For organised study of the essay, the most reliable method is the slow one: a notebook in which each thesis is restated in the student's own sentences, tested against one institution the student knows from inside, and checked against the objection that thesis attracts. Working notes of that kind are exactly what [VaultBook study notes for Marxism](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) are built to hold, thesis by thesis and objection by objection, so that the walkthrough table in this article and the reader's own examples accumulate in one place rather than scattering across margins. The habit matters more than the tool. Althusser's prose seduces readers into fluency, the feeling of understanding that comes from recognising the slogans, and only the discipline of restatement exposes the difference between recognising a hail and examining one. That difference, between the subject who turns and the reader who studies the turning, is the space in which the essay can still be read as its author asked: as notes towards an investigation, not as its conclusion.

## For students and exam candidates

The predictable examination question asks for an exposition of the ISAs followed by an evaluation, and scripts fail it in a predictable place: they reproduce the list of apparatuses, retell the policeman anecdote at generous length, and arrive at evaluation with nothing sharper than the observation that people can resist. Build the answer in the essay's own order instead. Open with reproduction, since that is the question the apparatuses answer, and the marker learns in the first paragraph that the script understands what the machinery is for. Then separate the repressive apparatus from the ideological ones by their main instrument, violence against ideology, remembering the secondary admixture in each direction, and state the private domain point before giving examples, because it converts a list into an argument. Give interpellation its mechanism, recognition in the turn, the mirror structure, the guarantee, rather than its illustration alone. For evaluation, the two objections that earn their place are the functionalism charge and the always already circle, each stated strongly and each answered with the struggle passages and the manuscript before the script concedes what remains unanswered. A script that concedes precisely, at named points, outscores a script that defends everything, because it shows the examiner a reader rather than a reciter.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The teaching problem with this text is that its illustration is more memorable than its argument, so the seminar leaves with a policeman and no reproduction. Counter the gravity of the anecdote by withholding it: spend the first session on the reproduction question and the two apparatuses, with students mapping an institution they know onto the primarily and secondarily grid, and admit the street scene only when the machinery it illustrates has been built. A productive exercise assigns each group one step of the walkthrough table and asks for the strongest objection at that step and the best reply, which teaches the text as an argument under pressure rather than a catechism. The extract problem makes an excellent closing topic for advanced groups: distribute a passage from the posthumous manuscript beside the corresponding extract passage and let students discover, in the prose itself, how much of the famous system was an artefact of the cutting. Assessment should reward the student who can say what the theory cannot do, since that discrimination, and not the list, is the transferable skill the text exists to teach.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Cite the essay as what it is: a 1970 extract from a book length manuscript published in French in the mid 1990s and in English as On the Reproduction of Capitalism in the mid 2010s, and consult the fuller text before attributing to Althusser any claim about the absence of class struggle from his account, since the manuscript is where that material lives. Researchers using interpellation empirically should specify the mechanism's observable traces, the rituals, forms of address and recognition practices under study, because the concept degenerates into a synonym for influence when the practices are left unspecified. Historians will find the theory most useful as a generator of questions about institutional routines and least useful as a narrative of events, and should keep Thompson's polemic in the same bibliography as a standing control on functionalist drift. Journalists reaching for the ISAs to describe a school dispute or a broadcasting controversy should remember that the term marks a reproductive function, not a conspiracy, and that the theory's whole point is that no puppeteer is required. The phrase ideological state apparatus earns its place in a report only when the practice doing the work can be named alongside it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is an ideological state apparatus?

An ideological state apparatus is an institution that secures the reproduction of the relations of production primarily by ideology rather than by force. Althusser's examples include the school, the family, the church, the legal system, political parties, trade unions, the press and broadcasting, and the institutions of culture and sport. Most of them are private rather than state owned, which surprises readers who take the word state literally. In the essay the word marks a function: these apparatuses form the skills and the dispositions that labour power must carry into production, and they do so through rituals and practices, examinations, services, schedules and ceremonies, rather than through commands backed by violence.

### Q: What is the difference between repressive and ideological state apparatuses?

The difference lies in the main instrument and in the form of organisation. The repressive state apparatus is a single unified body, comprising the government, administration, army, police, courts and prisons, and it functions primarily by violence, with ideology in a supporting role. The ideological state apparatuses are plural, dispersed and relatively autonomous, and they function primarily by ideology, with repression in reserve: schools expel, churches exclude, families punish. Althusser insists the contrast is one of degree and dominance rather than of kind, since no apparatus works by one instrument alone. The distinction also redistributes analytical attention, from the coercive core of the state toward the institutions of everyday life where consent is manufactured.

### Q: What does interpellation mean?

Interpellation is Althusser's name for the operation by which ideology constitutes individuals as subjects. Ideology addresses the individual, hails him by name or by role, and the individual who recognises the address as meant for him takes up the identity it assigns. The famous illustration is a police officer calling out in the street and the passer by turning around: in that turn, the individual accepts being the one addressed and becomes a subject of the order that hailed him. The mechanism is not persuasion, since nothing is argued, and not deception, since no false proposition is implanted. It is recognition, repeated across a lifetime of small addresses in families, schools, workplaces and media, each confirming the subject in an assigned place.

### Q: What did Althusser mean by saying ideology has no history?

He meant that ideology in general, as distinct from particular ideologies, has no history of its own because its structure is eternal. Particular ideologies, religious, moral, legal, political, are born in definite class conditions and change with them, and Althusser assigns them a history like any other social formation. What does not change is the general form: individuals constituted as subjects, living an imaginary relation to their real conditions of existence. He drew the analogy from Freud, who treated the unconscious as eternal in the sense of standing outside temporal succession. The claim is about form rather than content, so it does not deny that beliefs change; it denies that the subject constituting structure within which beliefs operate is itself a historical invention.

### Q: Why does Althusser say ideology has a material existence?

Because, on his account, ideology does not exist primarily as ideas inside heads but as acts inserted into practices governed by rituals within apparatuses. Following Pascal's advice that kneeling and taking the holy water will produce the belief that argument cannot, Althusser argues that conviction follows practice rather than preceding it. A believer's faith is renewed by attendance, gesture, confession and observance; a student's sense of merit is renewed by examinations and gradings; a citizen's loyalty is renewed by ceremonies and forms. Remove the apparatus and its rituals, and the corresponding belief loses its support and withers or changes shape. The thesis redirects research from doctrines to practices: to study an ideology, study its institutions, its timetables and its repeated ceremonies first.

### Q: Which is the dominant ideological state apparatus?

In mature capitalist formations, Althusser's answer is the educational apparatus, the school. Earlier formations were dominated by the church, which fused religious, educational and cultural functions, but the bourgeois order displaced it, and the school inherited the position. Dominance here is measured by compulsory reach rather than by noise: no other apparatus takes the entire child population, holds it for years of daily attendance during its most formable period, and enjoys the delegated authority of every family at once. Working through the school family couple, the educational apparatus delivers both the know how the division of labour requires and the submission to its rules, which is why Althusser treats it as the load bearing institution of reproduction.

### Q: Is the ISA essay part of a longer work?

Yes. The essay published in 1970, and widely reprinted in Lenin and Philosophy and Other Essays, is an extract from a book length manuscript on reproduction that Althusser did not publish in his lifetime. The manuscript appeared in French in the mid 1990s as Sur la reproduction, and in English in the mid 2010s as On the Reproduction of Capitalism. The fuller text treats the state, law and class struggle at much greater length and gives resistance inside the apparatuses a larger place than the extract does. Anyone citing the essay should therefore cite it as an extract of 1970, positioned within a larger project, rather than as a complete and final statement of Althusser's theory of ideology.

### Q: What is the imaginary relationship to real conditions?

It is the relation that ideology represents, in Althusser's central definition. Ideology does not picture the real conditions of existence directly; it pictures the way individuals stand toward those conditions, their place, their prospects, their obligations, and it pictures that standing in an imaginary register. Imaginary does not mean deliberately invented or simply false. Borrowed from Lacan, the term names the register of images and identifications in which the self experiences its own unity and obviousness. A worker's sense of being a free contractor of his own labour, or a student's sense that examination results measure inner worth, are imaginary relations in this sense: lived as self evident, they misrecognise the conditions that produced them, and they are none the less real in their effects.

### Q: What are the main criticisms of Althusser on ideology?

Three criticisms recur. The functionalism charge, pressed by historians such as E. P. Thompson in The Poverty of Theory, holds that the essay explains institutions by the reproductive function they serve, leaves no mechanism connecting need to existence, and absorbs every counterexample as contradiction or struggle, making the theory unfalsifiable. The circularity charge holds that interpellation presupposes the subject it claims to constitute, since recognising a hail requires a recogniser, and that the always already answer concedes the circle instead of escaping it. The agency charge holds that subjects who work by themselves, as the essay memorably puts it, leave no room for the failure, refusal and invention that the historical record contains. Defenders reply with the struggle passages and the longer manuscript, while conceding that the extract invites all three readings.

### Q: Does Althusser leave any room for resistance?

Formally, yes, though critics dispute how much the room contains. The essay closes by insisting that the ideological state apparatuses are not only the stake of class struggle but also its site, and that the resistance of the exploited classes finds means and occasions to express itself within them, in unions, parties, movements and dissenting practice inside schools and churches. Bad subjects, in his phrase, refuse the places assigned and provoke repression instead of working by themselves. The longer manuscript develops this strand far beyond the extract's brief treatment. The reservation critics keep is structural: resistance appears in the theory as an interruption of a machinery whose default is success, so the essay explains persistence better than it explains the ruptures resistance sometimes achieves.

### Q: How does the mirror example show interpellation at work?

Althusser models interpellation on religious ideology, where a unique central Subject, God, addresses individuals and constitutes them as subjects in a doubled mirror relation. The individual recognises himself in the Subject's address, as Moses is named and answers; the Subject recognises and guarantees the subject in return; fellow subjects recognise one another as members of the same order; and the structure carries a promise that everything will be well for those who keep their place. The mirror image shows why recognition is also misrecognition: the subject sees his identity reflected and confirmed by the centre, while the mechanism assigning the identity stays invisible. Secular apparatuses, on the theory, run the same structure with different centres, merit, the nation, the profession, in place of God.

### Q: What should a student separate in Althusser's claim that ideology is eternal?

Three things. First, the level: the claim concerns ideology in general, the subject constituting structure, not any particular ideology, all of which have histories tied to class conditions. Second, the sense of eternal: borrowed from Freud on the unconscious, it means standing outside historical succession as a form, not being frozen in content, since representations change constantly. Third, the scope: eternity holds, for Althusser, throughout the history of class societies and arguably beyond them, since he expected ideology in some form to persist even in a classless society. A student who keeps level, sense and scope apart can state the thesis accurately and then evaluate it, instead of dismissing a caricature in which beliefs are said never to change.

### Q: Why does Althusser call the school the dominant ideological apparatus?

Because of its unmatched combination of reach, duration and delegated authority. The school takes the whole child population, not a self selecting fraction, and holds it for the longest compulsory period of any apparatus, day after day across the years in which habits and expectations form. Families hand their authority to it voluntarily each morning, which no army or ministry could compel so cheaply. Through that position it performs the central reproductive task: distributing know how in the quantities the division of labour requires while teaching submission to that division as the natural order of merit. Other apparatuses may be louder, but none disposes of so much of a generation's time, and dominance in the essay is measured by exactly that disposal.

### Q: How do repressive and ideological apparatuses differ in their main instrument?

The repressive apparatus works primarily by violence and secondarily by ideology: the army and police rely on force and its threat, though they also cultivate doctrine, ceremony and esprit de corps to hold their own ranks together. The ideological apparatuses work primarily by ideology and secondarily by repression: the school persuades, sorts and certifies, though it also excludes and punishes; the church preaches, though it can excommunicate; the family forms through affection and routine, though it keeps sanctions in reserve. Althusser's adverbs, primarily and secondarily, carry the argument. The distinction is a spectrum of dominance between two instruments, not a sorting of institutions into pure types, and single institutions can shift their balance along it over time.

### Q: What problem does the always-already claim create for explaining change?

If individuals are always already subjects, constituted before birth by the familial ideology that awaits them, then the theory has described a closed loop with no point at which a differently constituted subject could arise. Change becomes hard to locate: every apparent rupture can be redescribed as reinterpellation under a new centre, the convert, the militant and the dropout all occupying recognisable positions the structure provides. The claim therefore protects the theory at the cost of its explanatory reach over origins and transformations. Critics argue that a theory of subjectivity unable to say how subjects could become otherwise has surrendered the question politics most needs answered, while defenders reply that the loop describes reproduction honestly and that change must be theorised from the struggle between apparatuses rather than from within interpellation alone.
