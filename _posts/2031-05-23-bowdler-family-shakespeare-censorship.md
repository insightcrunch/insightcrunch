Bowdler's Family Shakespeare is the most successful censorship in the history of Romeo and Juliet, and its success is the problem this article examines. Between 1807 and 1818 a brother and sister from Bath produced an edition of the plays from which, in the title page's own words, "those words and expressions are omitted which cannot with propriety be read aloud in a family". The edition conquered the nineteenth-century parlour, ran through printings for six decades, provoked a public quarrel between the age's leading journals, drew a famous defence from Swinburne and a famous prosecution from Richard Whiteing, founded an industry of rival expurgations, and left the language a verb, to bowdlerize, that remains the common noun's sharpest instrument against protective editing. The tragedy stood near the centre of the enterprise's difficulty. Romeo and Juliet is a play whose comedy is bawdy in its grain, whose heroine speaks her desire in the period's boldest soliloquy, and whose plot is a clandestine marriage; cleaning it was the method's hardest test, and the record of the cleaning, recoverable line by line from the Family text itself, is the evidence on which a verdict can finally rest.

![Bowdler's Family Shakespeare and the clean play of Romeo and Juliet - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-110.webp)

The verdict, stated at the outset: the expurgated Romeo and Juliet fails as an edition and succeeds as an event, and the two judgements must be kept separate because the critics have spent two centuries spending each on the other. As an edition it fails by its own doctrine's standard, since the matter removed was not the local decoration the prefaces promised to trim but load-bearing language: the first scene's analysis of the feud, the heroine's desire, the Nurse's worldview, Mercutio's wit. As an event it succeeds beyond any expurgator's deserving, since the clean play carried the tragedy into the households that made Shakespeare the national author, made first readers by the hundred thousand, and fixed the received image of the lovers, refined, suffering, and strangely chaste, that fuller texts have been correcting ever since. Two corrections to the common story govern everything that follows, and both are matters of record rather than interpretation. The tragedy does not appear in the famous 1807 edition at all; its expurgation belongs to Thomas Bowdler's completion of 1818, not to his sister Henrietta's founding volumes. And the cuts, when the Family text of 1825 is collated against the Folger text, fall with a consistency the mockery misses: on diction, register, and the plain naming of bodies, while the tragedy's deeds, its poison, its suicides, its tomb, pass almost untouched. The knife was precise. Precision is what makes its work worth auditing.

The method of the article matches the method its subject demands. Every cut claimed in the sections that follow is claimed from a collation: the Family Shakespeare text, in the fourth edition of 1825 that reprints Thomas Bowdler's 1818 Romeo and Juliet in its tenth volume, set against the Folger text of the play, with act and scene cited for each passage. Where a cut rests on the documented collations gathered in the standard accounts rather than on direct inspection of the 1825 printing, the article says so; where the record is contested, as the attribution of the 1807 edition remains contested in its finer grain, the contest is graded rather than concealed. The critics are heard in three ages, the parlour's, the prosecution's, and the scholars', and their disagreement is adjudicated on separate questions, what the edition did to the text, what it did for the readership, and why the age required it. Protection and gutting, the complication the brief assigns, are both given their full weight before the verdict divides them.

## Two Editions, Two Hands: How the Family Shakespeare Was Made

The first fact in the Bowdler record corrects the shape of the story as the story is usually told. *The Family Shakespeare* began in 1807 as a small, anonymous venture: four duodecimo volumes, printed at Bath and published in London by Hatchard, holding twenty plays. The twenty were *The Tempest*, *A Midsummer Night's Dream*, *Much Ado About Nothing*, *As You Like It*, *The Merchant of Venice*, *Twelfth Night*, *The Winter's Tale*, *King John*, the two parts of *Henry IV* with *Richard II* and *Henry V*, *Richard III*, *Henry VIII*, *Julius Caesar*, *Macbeth*, *Cymbeline*, *King Lear*, *Hamlet*, and *Othello*. *Romeo and Juliet* is not on the list. The tragedy of the Veronese lovers reached the family circle only in 1818, when a second edition in ten volumes from Longman and its partners offered all thirty-six plays then counted as the canon, *Pericles* alone excluded. The expurgated *Romeo and Juliet* that polite households read across the nineteenth century, the text reprinted in edition after edition down to the 1880s and beyond, is therefore a document of 1818 and after, and the hand that prepared it matters to every claim made about its cuts.

That hand was not the hand of 1807, and the difference is the second fact. The 1807 volumes appeared without a name because the editor's name could not decently appear: the work was principally that of Henrietta Maria Bowdler (1750 to 1830), published anonymously and only much later credited, in the phrase of her Oxford Dictionary of National Biography entry, as writer and literary editor. Henrietta, called Harriet in the family, was an author in her own right before the edition, a writer of sermons and devotional verse, and a reader of formidable precision. The paradox at the centre of her edition has often been stated: a woman of her moment could not publicly admit to knowing which passages in Shakespeare were indelicate, since the knowledge itself was the indelicacy. Anonymity was the price of the enterprise. Her brother Thomas Bowdler (1754 to 1825), a retired physician of Bath, a prison reformer and a chess writer, negotiated the larger second edition, placed his name alone on its title pages, and took public ownership of the family project. Every later printing carried his name. The verb that English built from the enterprise was built from his name too, so that the sister who devised the method has been remembered, when remembered at all, as a footnote to her brother's notoriety.

The division of labour between the editions is documented rather than conjectured. Henrietta selected twenty plays and edited them against a double test that the next section examines: offence against religion and offence against decency. She also, by the admission embedded in her brother's later practice, cut for a third and quieter reason, removing scenes she judged trivial or uninteresting, matter whose absence no doctrine required. Thomas, taking over in 1818, did three things at once. He expurgated the sixteen plays his sister had not touched, *Romeo and Juliet* among them. He re-edited her twenty, generally continuing her omissions of sexual and profane matter. And he restored passages she had removed on grounds of dullness rather than delicacy, since his brief was propriety and not taste. The 1818 *Romeo and Juliet* is thus a single editor's work in a way the 1807 volumes are not: Thomas Bowdler's hand, Thomas Bowdler's doctrine, Thomas Bowdler's name. When this article speaks of what Bowdler cut from the tragedy, the reference is to that 1818 text and its reprints, collated in the fourth edition of 1825, where the play stands in the tenth volume beside *Hamlet* and *Othello*.

One further boundary keeps the article honest about scale. The Bowdlers were not the first hands laid on Shakespeare in the name of fitness. A Second Folio preserved in the Folger collection carries the ink of Guillermo Sanchez, a censor for the Spanish Holy Office, blacking out passages in the 1630s and striking *Measure for Measure* entire. Nahum Tate rewrote *King Lear* in 1681 with a happy ending and held the stage with it for a century and a half. Charles and Mary Lamb published their *Tales from Shakespeare* in the same year as Henrietta's volumes, 1807, retelling twenty plays in prose for children while seldom quoting the verse. What separates the Bowdler enterprise from every predecessor is method: nothing added, nothing rewritten, the original words left standing wherever the original words could be borne, and the offence removed by subtraction alone. That restraint, announced on the title page, is why the edition could claim to be Shakespeare still, and why the claim deserves the close audit the following sections give it. The modern history of challenge and removal in schools and libraries, a separate record of committees and boards, belongs to [Banned and Challenged: The Play in Schools](/2029/09/28/banned-challenged-in-schools/), and stands at the far end of a line that begins in the Bath parlour examined here.

## The Preface Doctrine: What the Bowdler Censorship Promised

Every censorship announces a theory of reading, and the Bowdler theory is stated with unusual completeness in the two prefaces, Henrietta's of 1807 and Thomas's of 1818. The 1807 preface sets the aim in a single sentence: the edition endeavours "to remove every thing that could give just offence to the religious or virtuous mind". A second phrase names the enemy within the text, the many speeches in which Shakespeare stands accused of having been tempted "to purchase laughter at the price of decency". The promised destination is a book that can "be placed in the hands of young persons of both sexes". Three features of this doctrine repay attention. The offence is defined by the hearer rather than the word: a passage fails not because it is false or vicious in itself but because it gives offence to a mind described as religious or virtuous, a standard that makes the most delicate reader in the room the measure of the text. The theatre of danger is the voice: the subtitle's test, repeated across editions, is what can "with propriety be read aloud in a family", since a printed page can be skipped by a silent eye while a spoken line lands on every ear in the parlour at once. And the doctrine is defensive rather than reforming: the edition does not claim to improve Shakespeare, to correct his morals, or to supply a better play. The claim is narrower and, on its face, humbler. A father reading aloud should not be ambushed by his author.

Thomas's 1818 preface grounds that doctrine in a family memory that functions as the enterprise's founding parable. Their father, he recalls, read Shakespeare to the household with a skill few readers matched: "such was his good taste, his delicacy, and his prompt discretion, that his family listened with delight to Lear, Hamlet, and Othello, without knowing that those matchless tragedies contained words and expressions improper to be pronounced; and without reason to suspect that any parts of the plays had been omitted by the circumspect and judicious reader." The sentence deserves slow reading. The father's cuts were invisible, and their invisibility was the delight's condition: the children enjoyed the tragedies precisely because the editing left no scar. Thomas presents the printed edition as that father's discretion made permanent for households whose reader lacked it. The title page of 1818 turns the parable into a contract: *The Family Shakspeare*, "in which nothing is added to the original text; but those words and expressions are omitted which cannot with propriety be read aloud in a family". The spelling of the name, Shakspeare in Thomas's edition where Henrietta's had used Shakespeare, settled only in printings from 1847 onward, is a reminder that even the author's name had no fixed and protected form in the period. The text underneath the name was to be guarded by a stricter rule than the name itself enjoyed.

### What offence was the Family Shakespeare built to prevent?

The offence was embarrassment at the moment of reading aloud: a father voicing an indelicate line before his wife and daughters, a young person meeting a sexual pun in a book handed over as safe. The edition was engineered against the blush, the pause, and the skipped page in company.

The doctrine had a sterner edge that the parable's warmth can hide. In the 1818 preface Thomas calls the author he is rescuing "our immoral Bard", a phrase that assigns the fault and fixes the editor's office in two words. Shakespeare is the patient; the Bowdlers are the physicians, and Thomas was in fact a physician, trained to remove diseased tissue while preserving the body. The medical metaphor runs quietly through the whole enterprise and explains its confidence: an editor who believes the offence is local, seated in identifiable words and expressions, can promise that excision leaves the organism whole. The sections that follow test that promise against the play where it faced its hardest case, a tragedy whose plot is desire: its courtship conducted in religious metaphor, its consummation anticipated in the heroine's most famous soliloquy, its comedy carried by two speakers, Mercutio and the Nurse, whose wit is bawdy in its grain. If the doctrine of local offence fails anywhere, it fails in Verona, where the indelicacy is not decoration on the action but one of the action's engines.

The prefaces also disclose, in passing, the doctrine's uneven application, and honesty requires the disclosure before the cuts are examined. For *Measure for Measure*, new in 1818, Thomas printed not his own expurgation but John Philip Kemble's amended acting text from Covent Garden, confessing the play too difficult to clean by subtraction; his own version followed in 1820. For *Othello* he wrote a preface conceding that the tragedy's subject is "little suited to family reading", that its arguments for adultery are woven too deep to remove without "destroying the tragedy", and advising any family still uneasy to transfer the volume "from the parlour to the cabinet". No preface stands before *Romeo and Juliet* at all. The silence can be read two ways, and the reading matters: either the tragedy seemed to its editor a straightforward case, its offences seated in detachable innuendo, or the case was so pervasive that comment was pointless. The collation that follows supports the first reading as the editor's intention and the second as the text's reality.

## The Opening Brawl Without the Bawdy: Act 1 in the Family Shakespeare

The collation begins where the play begins, and the first scene of the tragedy shows the method at its most surgical. In the Folger text the servants' quarrel builds through a ladder of sexual threats. Sampson boasts that a dog of the house of Montague moves him to stand, explains that the weakest goes to the wall, and then states the rule of his world: "'Tis true, and therefore women, being the weaker vessels, are ever thrust to the wall. Therefore I will push Montague's men from the wall and thrust his maids to the wall" (1.1, Folger). Gregory observes that the quarrel is between their masters and their men. Sampson answers, "'Tis all one. I will show myself a tyrant. When I have fought with the men, I will be civil with the maids. I will cut off their heads", and the exchange descends through Gregory's question to the pun that gives the joke its point, the heads of the maids "or their maidenheads" (1.1, Folger). Rape, named as a perquisite of street victory, is the first scene's bass note: the feud's violence and its sexual violence are a single gesture, and the tragedy opens by letting two servants say so in the idiom of the gutter.

In the Family Shakespeare text of 1825, collated line by line against that passage, the ladder is dismantled at exactly the rungs where the talk turns sexual. Sampson's weakest-to-the-wall speech stands. Gregory's line about masters and men stands. Sampson's "'Tis all one, I will show myself a tyrant" stands. Then the text moves directly to Gregory's "Draw thy tool; here comes of the house of the Montagues", and the fight begins. Everything between the tyrant boast and the drawn swords is gone: the weaker-vessels rule, the thrusting of maids to the wall, the civility with the maids after the fighting, the heads, and the maidenheads pun that crowns the sequence. The cut is clean enough that a parlour listener hears a complete exchange, servants boasting and brawling in the ordinary idiom of stage quarrels, and never suspects an amputation. The father's invisible editing of the preface parable has been industrialised: the scar does not show.

### How much of the opening scene's talk survives in the Family text?

Most of its length and almost none of its meaning about women. The boasts, the thumb-biting, the Prince's rebuke, and the exposition all stand, while the sequence that links the feud's violence to sexual violence, the thrusting to the wall and the maidenheads pun, is removed entire.

What the cut costs the play can be stated precisely, because the removed lines do structural work. The first scene teaches an audience how to read Verona: a city where honour is measured in domination, where women's bodies are the small change of male quarrels, and where the state's answer, the Prince's death sentence on the next brawl, is already a confession that words have failed. Juliet's story is legible as tragedy partly because that grammar has been established before she appears; the daughter handed to Paris in a negotiated transfer is the domestic form of the maids thrust to the wall in the street. Remove the servants' sexual threats and the feud becomes a generic rivalry, noisy but unexamined, and the marriage market of the Capulet household loses its street-level gloss. The expurgation also flatters the audience it protects. A parlour family could deplore the brawl as vulgar spirits deploring a riot, without hearing the riot's own account of what riots are for. Bowdler's doctrine held that offence is local, seated in expressions. The first scene shows the limit of the doctrine within four pages: the expressions carried an analysis, and removing the expressions removed the analysis with them. The play that remains still opens with a fight. The fight has been stripped of its explanation.

## Saint-Seducing Gold: The Sentence Bowdler Deleted Whole

Subtraction in the Family text works at three scales, and the second scale, the whole sentence removed, is best seen in Romeo's first speech about Rosaline. Benvolio presses him for the cause of his sadness, and Romeo answers in the Petrarchan idiom of the siege: the beloved "will not stay the siege of loving terms, / Nor bide th' encounter of assailing eyes, / Nor ope her lap to saint-seducing gold" (1.1, Folger). The sentence closes a triad of refusals, and its final image condenses the speech's whole economy into one shocking figure: chastity figured as a lap kept shut against gold personified as a seducer of saints, desire and money and blasphemy folded into a single phrase. Documented collations of the Family text record the treatment in one word: omitted. The sentence does not appear in Bowdler's Romeo and Juliet. The siege terms and the assailing eyes around it could stand, since military metaphor for courtship was the common coin of love poetry; the lap and the gold could not, since the figure names the body and prices it in the same breath.

The deletion is small, one sentence in a long play, and the argument of this article is that small deletions of this kind are where the expurgation did its deepest work. A scene removed entire, like the maidenheads exchange, at least announces a gap to any reader who compares texts; a sentence removed from the middle of a speech leaves the speech standing, its rhythm barely disturbed, its argument quietly reweighted. Romeo's Rosaline rhetoric in the full text is a performance the play itself diagnoses: antithetical, self-delighting, in love with its own figures, and the saint-seducing gold is the figure that most openly betrays the performance's nature, since it prices the unattainable woman in the currency of the brothel while dressing the price as devotion. Bowdler's Romeo keeps the sighs and loses the tell. A parlour reader met a melancholy young man of tasteful sorrows, "in sadness, cousin, I do love a woman", and the play's early irony, the sense that Romeo's first love is a style in search of an object, thins to sentiment. The cut also performs a small act of protection for the hero: the full text lets Romeo speak a line of genuine coarseness in his first scene, and the expurgated text keeps his record clean for the balcony. Censorship of this kind is always partly character management, and the Family Shakespeare manages Romeo's character from his first entrance.

Set beside the opening scene, the sentence shows the doctrine's hierarchy of offences with unusual clarity. Violence could stay: the brawl, the Prince's threats, the drawn swords. Threatened sexual violence in the mouths of servants could not stay, and sexual metaphor in the mouth of the hero could not stay, even when the metaphor's target is refusal rather than indulgence. What the doctrine could not process was not obscenity as a dictionary category but the body's presence in language at all, in whatever register, from whatever speaker, to whatever moral end. The standard accounts of the expurgation, from the contemporary reviews to Noel Perrin's history, agree on the scale of the enterprise in this play: the tragedy received more than a hundred alterations in the Family text, a figure the EBSCO research summary repeats, and the great majority were of this sentence-and-phrase kind rather than scene removals. A hundred small cuts re-tune a play more thoroughly than five large ones, because the reader never meets the join. The next two sections follow the knife into the two voices where the re-tuning mattered most, the wittiest man in Verona and the woman who raised its heroine.

## Mercutio Under the Knife: The Dial, the Queen, and the Purse

Mercutio is the expurgation's hardest case in this play, harder than the Nurse, harder than the servants, because his bawdy is not an accent on his wit but the wit itself. The full anatomy of that wordplay, pun by pun and register by register, stands at [Mercutio's Bawdy: Sex, Wit, and Wordplay](/2022/09/16/mercutio-bawdy-wit-wordplay/), and the present section asks the narrower editorial question: what could a subtraction-only editor do with a speaker whose meaning lives in double meanings? A joke with two senses offers the censor a cruel choice. Print the line and the indelicate sense is in the parlour, available to any hearer quick enough to catch it, which in a family circle means the sense arrives precisely where it was meant to be kept from arriving. Cut the line and the delicate sense dies with its twin, since the sentence was built to carry both. The Family text, faced with that choice across Mercutio's part, answers it three ways, and the three answers map the whole range of the enterprise: substitution, excision, and surrender.

Substitution appears in the most quoted single alteration in the play's expurgated history. Mercutio, teasing Romeo for vanishing from the street scene, cries in the Folger text: "Now is he for the numbers that Petrarch flowed in. Laura to his lady was but a kitchen wench", and the speech runs on to the dial joke as the Nurse and Peter approach: "the bawdy hand of the dial is now upon the prick of noon" (2.4, Folger). The line is a clock, a joke about time, and an obscenity in a single mechanism: the hand of the dial is a clock's pointer, a bawdy hand is a lecherous one, and the final noun names the hour with a slang term for the male body. The Family text prints the line as "the hand of the dial is now upon the point of noon". Two words are changed and one deleted. "Point" replaces the slang noun with the clockmaker's term, "bawdy" falls away, and the sentence that remains is a true statement about a clock and nothing else. The substitution is the enterprise in miniature, and it deserves a fair statement before judgement: the metre survives, the speech survives, Romeo's friends still mock his absence, and a father reading aloud reaches noon without incident. What dies is the characterisation. Mercutio's line in the full text performs his whole function in the play, the conversion of everything, time, poetry, friendship, into sexual energy; the cleaned line performs a time check. Multiply the operation across the part and the result is a Mercutio who is merely brisk, a wit with the voltage lowered at every terminal.

### Why was Mercutio the hardest speaker in the play to clean?

Because his indelicacy is structural rather than decorative. The Nurse's jokes can be lifted out as episodes, and the servants' threats form a detachable sequence, but Mercutio's puns carry his meaning inside single words, so that removing the offence from his lines removes the sense from them.

Excision handles the Queen Mab speech. The speech in the Folger text builds from fairy miniatures to a darker register: Mab is "the fairies' midwife", she gallops through lovers' brains and courtiers' knees, and at the speech's turn she becomes "the hag, when maids lie on their backs, / That presses them and learns them first to bear, / Making them women of good carriage" (1.4, Folger). The passage names the nightmare as a sexual initiation, the incubus weight that teaches girls the bearing of men, and it is the speech's destination: Mercutio's fairy lore darkens into an account of how desire enters sleeping minds. Collation of the Family text finds the destination gone. The phrase about maids on their backs and the bearing lesson do not appear in the 1825 printing; the speech's earlier miniatures, the chariot of a hazelnut and the coachmakers among the spiders, could stand as nursery grotesque, while the hag passage, the speech's payload, was lifted out. A parlour Mab is a sprite of dreams in general. The full text's Mab is a theory of where dreams come from, and the theory is carnal. The cut repeats, inside one speech, the doctrine's central mistake: the assumption that the indelicate passage is a growth on the meaning rather than the meaning's root.

Surrender, the third answer, is visible in what the Family text does to Mercutio's prose duels, the exchange with the Nurse in 2.4 and the flyting that fills the street scenes. Where a speech's whole fabric was pun and innuendo, subtraction had nothing to grip: the editor could keep the passage entire and betray the doctrine, or remove the passage entire and betray the play. The collations show the enterprise choosing, speech by speech, between those betrayals, and the pattern of its choices is itself evidence about the doctrine. Passages whose offence could be quarantined in a sentence were cut by the sentence. Passages saturated through their length were shortened hard or dropped. The result for Mercutio is the steepest character loss in the expurgated tragedy: the play's most electric speaker becomes its most edited one, and the balance of the tragedy shifts. In the full text Mercutio's death is the hinge because his energy has defined the play's alternative to the lovers, appetite without illusion, and his curse on both houses lands with the weight of the world's wit condemning the world's feud. A cleaned Mercutio dies as a pleasant companion cut down in a street affray, and the curse survives, since curses on houses were printable, with a fraction of the authority the uncut performance had earned for it.

## The Nurse Silenced in Part: Bawdy, Age, and the Fall Backward

The Nurse's case differs from Mercutio's in one respect that the criticism of expurgation has not always marked: her bawdy is retrospective and maternal, the frankness of a woman who has buried a husband and a daughter and speaks of bodies from the far side of experience. The play gives her the tragedy's most quoted piece of domestic comedy in 1.3, where she measures Juliet's age against the Lammas-tide deadline and wanders into the memory of the child's weaning and fall. Her husband, "God be with his soul, he was a merry man", picked up the toddler who had fallen forward and cut her brow, and made the joke the Nurse cannot stop repeating: "Thou wilt fall backward when thou hast more wit, / Wilt thou not, Jule?" (1.3, Folger). The child said "Ay", the household laughed, and the Nurse has told the story, by her own cheerful admission, a hundred times. The joke is the whole of her worldview in one line: the girl who fell on her face as an infant will fall on her back as a woman, childhood and sexuality joined by a proverb, and the Nurse's tenderness and her earthiness are the same quality.

Collation finds the joke absent from the Family text. The phrase about falling backward does not appear in the 1825 printing, and the scene that remains introduces a Nurse of reduced dimensions: garrulous, affectionate, comically precise about dates, and stripped of the prophetic bawdy that makes her the play's chorus of the body. The cut is a specimen of the doctrine working at the level of the individual word-group, and its cost is worth tracing because the Nurse's later scenes depend on the authority 1.3 establishes. When the same woman returns from Romeo in 2.5 and teases Juliet with delay, when she delivers the marriage arrangements with the ladder speech, "I am the drudge and toil in your delight, / But you shall bear the burden soon at night" (2.5, Folger), the full text's audience hears a woman whose whole idiom treats sex as the household's open secret, funny and ordinary and survivable. When she defects in 3.5 and advises the bigamous marriage to Paris, the fall is measured against that idiom: the counsellor of the body has become the counsellor of convenience. An expurgated Nurse, cleaned of the fall-backward joke and the burden she promises Juliet will bear, defects from a smaller height. Her treachery reads as mere officiousness, one servant's pragmatism, where the full text stages the collapse of the play's earthiest wisdom into its most cynical advice.

The Nurse also exposes a pattern in the expurgation that the Mercutio cuts only suggest: the knife fell unevenly across the social scale. Servants and nurses speak the body's language as their native register, and their speeches are thick with the removable matter; the gentry lovers speak desire in metaphor, sonnet, and religious figure, registers that a word-level censor could often pass. The result, compounded across the play, is a quiet redistribution of dignity. The cleaned text leaves the aristocratic lovers comparatively intact in their lyricism while thinning the speech of every character who talks about sex in the plain style, so that the expurgated tragedy not only censors desire but reclasses it: refined longing survives in the great houses, and the body's comedy is struck from the mouths of the poor. No preface announces that consequence, and no evidence suggests either Bowdler intended it. Consequences of censorship are rarely the ones the censor would choose, which is an argument for reading editions like this one closely rather than laughing at them from a distance. The Family Shakespeare was read by more households across the nineteenth century than any scholarly text of the play, and the Romeo and Juliet formed in those households, refined in its lovers and muted in its servants, is a historical fact about the play's life that no account of the reception can skip.

What the Nurse keeps is also instructive. Her grief survives the knife entire: the lament for Susan, her dead daughter of Juliet's age, the "well, Susan is with God; / She was too good for me" (1.3, Folger) that roots her attachment to Juliet in bereavement, stands in the Family text, since sorrow gave no offence to the religious or virtuous mind. Death, in the doctrine's scale of dangers, ranked far below sex. A nurse could bury a child in print before a family; she could not joke about the act that makes children. The hierarchy sounds absurd stated flatly, and the next sections complicate it, because the same hierarchy let the tragedy's murders, poison, and suicides pass almost untouched while its jokes were hunted line by line. The expurgated play is not a gentler play. It is a play in which the only remaining dangers are fatal ones, and the comedy that in the full text teaches the audience to survive desire has been replaced by a silence that teaches nothing.

## Juliet's Night Speech: Desire Edited at Its Source

Every censorship reveals its centre of gravity at the point where the cutting hurts most, and in this play that point is the opening of Act 3 Scene 2, Juliet alone, waiting for the wedding night. The speech in the Folger text begins with an invocation that has no parallel in the period for a heroine's open desire: "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds, / Towards Phoebus' lodging" (3.2, Folger). Juliet summons the horses of the sun to hurry the day out of the sky so that night can bring her Romeo, and the speech continues into its famous fifth line, "Spread thy close curtain, love-performing night" (3.2, Folger), before asking that Romeo be cut out "in little stars" when she dies. The speech is the tragedy's boldest writing: a bride of not quite fourteen calling down the dark, in language that joins cosmic machinery to the marriage bed without embarrassment and without a chaperone. Documented collations of the Family text show the fifth line surviving only in truncated form, its close curtain and its love-performing epithet removed, the reading reduced toward the later and safer "come, civil night". Collation of the 1825 printing confirms the larger fact directly: the words "Gallop apace" do not appear in the volume. The invocation that opens the speech in every modern edition is absent from the Family text.

### What remains of Juliet's night speech once its opening is removed?

The speech's later and more general beauties remain, the civil night, the stars, the plea for Romeo's coming, while its engine is gone: a bride commanding the sun's horses to hurry so that the wedding night can begin. The Family Juliet waits with dignity. The uncut Juliet desires with authority.

The significance of the cut is sharpened by its direction. The doctrine's stated enemy was laughter purchased at the price of decency, the bawdy joke in the mouths of clowns and servants. Juliet's speech contains no joke. Its offence is sincerity: a young wife's desire spoken in the first person, at length, in verse of the highest finish, by the character the family audience was meant to admire. The knife that fell on the speech was not protecting the parlour from coarseness. The knife was protecting the parlour from the recognition that the tragedy's heroine wants what the tragedy is about, and wants it in words no governess could gloss. Editors of the period understood the problem exactly, which is why the speech's opening, rather than any servant's pun, became one of the set-piece examples in accounts of the expurgation. A cleaned Mercutio is a loss of wit. A cleaned Juliet at the threshold of her wedding night is a change in the tragedy's subject, from a play about desire and its world to a play about affection and its obstacles. Generations of family readers met the second play believing it was the first, and the critical commonplace of the swooning, passive Juliet, a heroine of sentiment rather than appetite, has one of its material sources in the text those readers were given. The full record of the speech, its horses and curtain and stars, had to wait for scholarly editions to restore it to common reading, and the restoration changed the heroine's reputation with it.

There is a further asymmetry that the table in the next section but one makes visible at a glance. Romeo's desire is edited chiefly where it turns coarse in metaphor, the saint-seducing gold; Juliet's desire is edited at its most elevated, in the speech where metaphor becomes invocation. The doctrine could tolerate a hero's sighs because sighs were the period's licensed idiom for male longing, and it could tolerate the heroine's devotion because devotion was the licensed idiom for female feeling. What neither licence covered was the heroine's appetite, stated as appetite, in the imperative mood, addressed to the cosmos. The expurgation thus preserves the period's double standard inside the text itself, keeping the play's men desiring in code and its heroine desiring not at all, and it does so while claiming to remove nothing but words. The claim and the result stand side by side in the collation, and the distance between them is the measure of the enterprise.

## What Bowdler Kept: The Apothecary, the Tomb, and the Happy Dagger

An expurgation is defined as sharply by its survivals as by its cuts, and the survivals in this play overturn the lazy version of the Bowdler story. The assumption travels that the Family Shakespeare, frightened of the tragedy's darkness, softened its ending: that the poison purchase was veiled, the suicides discreetly handled, the tomb scene trimmed toward decency. Collation of the 1825 text against the Folger text shows the assumption to be false in every particular, and the falsity is among the most instructive facts in the whole record. Act 5 in the Family Shakespeare is the tragedy at nearly full strength, and an editor frightened of darkness would have begun his work there. Thomas Bowdler barely touched it.

The apothecary scene stands essentially complete. Romeo enters Mantua with his resolve formed, describes the shop and its starved keeper, and makes the offer in the full text's terms: "Hold, there is forty ducats. Let me have / A dram of poison, such soon-speeding gear / As will disperse itself through all the veins" (5.1, Folger). The Family text prints the forty ducats, the dram, the veins, and the cannon's womb simile that follows. The apothecary's resistance stands: "Such mortal drugs I have, but Mantua's law / Is death to any he that utters them" (5.1, Folger), and so does the moral fencing that resolves it, "My poverty, but not my will, consents" answered by "I pay thy poverty and not thy will" (5.1, Folger). Above all, the scene's great judgement stands uncut: "There is thy gold, worse poison to men's souls, / Doing more murders in this loathsome world / Than these poor compounds that thou mayst not sell. / I sell thee poison; thou hast sold me none" (5.1, Folger). A father could read the whole transaction aloud in the Family text, the negotiation of a suicide's instrument priced and completed, without meeting a word the doctrine excluded. The poison was printable. The pun on the clock was not.

### Why did the poison purchase pass the censor when the wordplay failed?

Because the doctrine policed diction rather than deeds. The apothecary scene contains no indelicate word, no sexual figure, and no profanity; its action is terrible and its language is clean, and a test built on expressions had no instrument for measuring the action at all.

The tomb scene confirms the pattern at the tragedy's climax. Romeo's address to the monument, "Thou detestable maw, thou womb of death" (5.3, Folger), stands in the Family text. His farewell to Juliet's body stands at length, including the conceit that death keeps her "in dark to be his paramour" (5.3, Folger), a figure more morbidly sexual than half the matter cut from Act 1, preserved because its register is funereal rather than comic. "Here will I set up my everlasting rest" (5.3, Folger) stands. The draught is drunk on the page with its benediction, "O true apothecary! / Thy drugs are quick. Thus with a kiss I die" (5.3, Folger). Juliet wakes, finds the cup, and the Family text gives her the line that titles a sibling study in this series: "O happy dagger, / This is thy sheath. There rust, and let me die" (5.3, Folger). Suicide, twice accomplished and once attempted by the Friar's near escape, passes the expurgator entire; the word "whoreson", spoken by Capulet to a servant in the bustle of 4.4 and softened in the Family text to "fellow" by the documented collations, does not. The hierarchy is consistent enough to be stated as a rule: the Bowdler test asked what a passage says, word by word, and never what a passage does. Deeds belonged to the tragedy's moral architecture, which the prefaces praised; words belonged to the parlour, which the prefaces served. An editor armed with that distinction could keep a double suicide and cut a joke about noon, and could experience the two decisions as the same decision.

The rule also explains the expurgation's strangest survivals in the earlier acts. The balcony scene passes with its argument intact, since its desire travels in the licensed idioms of pilgrimage and astronomy. The potion soliloquy passes, terror being clean. The Queen Mab speech loses its hag and keeps its fairy machinery. Even the opening Chorus stands complete, its "fatal loins" and its star-crossed lovers undisturbed, because a sonnet's abstractions give the word-level test nothing to seize. What the family reader lost, across more than a hundred alterations, was therefore a specific stratum of the play: the stratum in which bodies are named in the plain style, in which jokes admit what speeches conceal, and in which the heroine's appetite speaks in imperatives. The stratum is not the whole play, and the Family text is not a travesty; whole acts of it are the tragedy itself. The stratum is, however, the play's connective tissue between desire and laughter, and a Romeo and Juliet without that tissue is a measurably different work, as the verdict section will argue once the critics have been heard.

## The Parlour as Institution: Who the Clean Play Was For

Behind the doctrine stood a room, and the room explains the doctrine better than any theory of prudery. The family circle of the early nineteenth century was an institution with a membership and a discipline: parents and children of both sexes, daughters in particular, gathered for the evening's reading, with servants passing, visitors received, and the reader, ordinarily the father, performing the text aloud as a social act. Belinda Jack's study of the woman reader, cited in the standard accounts for the journal quarrel of 1821 and 1822, places the expurgation inside that room's economy: the book was designed so that the performance could proceed without the reader's improvising the father's "prompt discretion" of the preface parable, skipping on the fly, stumbling into a line that set the room alight. The Edinburgh Review's praise named the room's currency exactly, "awkwardness" and "distress", social emotions rather than theological ones. The offence the edition prevented was not damnation. The offence was a silence at the table, a daughter's question, a guest's raised eyebrow, the small social death of a respectable household caught reading filth in chorus.

That framing revises the enterprise's chronology in a way the record supports. The Family Shakespeare is conventionally filed as a Victorian artefact, and the filing is anachronistic by three decades: Henrietta's volumes appeared in 1807, Thomas's completion in 1818, in the Regency of the Prince Regent, thirty years before Victoria's reign began, in a print culture still arguing over the novel as a danger to young women and over the propriety of the theatre itself. The Bowdlers were not expressing a settled Victorian consensus. They were building, early, one of the instruments by which the coming century's respectability would be manufactured, and the speed with which the market followed them, rivals within a generation and an American branch by 1849, shows the instrument answering a formation already under way: a middle-class readership expanding faster than its confidence, buying the national author as furniture for a household whose standards the author himself would have failed at the door. The clean play was a credential as much as a text. To read Shakespeare in the family was to display cultivation; to read him unexpurgated was to risk the display's collapse. The edition sold insurance against that collapse, priced in missing lines.

The room also decides the question of who was being protected from whom, and the answer is less simple than the caricature of daughters shielded from knowledge. The prefaces address fathers as readers and young persons of both sexes as hearers; the sister who devised the text had herself read everything she cut, in an age when a woman's possessing such knowledge was the scandal the anonymity existed to hide. Protection in the parlour ran in several directions at once: of daughters from lines, of fathers from embarrassment, of the household's reputation from its own library, and, in the founding case, of Henrietta Bowdler from the consequences of her own competence. Censorship is usually described as the strong hand over the weak reader. The Bowdler record shows a more tangled machine, in which the censor is herself among the censored, the protected include the protector's good name, and the text is altered to preserve not innocence, which the editor did not possess and could not restore, but the appearance under which a household could keep reading. That tangle is why the enterprise rewards study rather than mockery. The parlour is gone, and its exact pressures are difficult to feel from a distance of two centuries; the edition remains as the pressure's fossil, legible cut by cut.

## The Critics on Bowdler: Prudery, Service, and the Shears

The reception of the Family Shakespeare divides into three ages, and each age's verdict says as much about the age as about the edition. The first age, the edition's own, argued the question in earnest and largely in the Bowdlers' favour. Henrietta's anonymous volumes of 1807 drew three reviews and little noise: one praised the tastefulness of a version a reviewer could call castrated without intending murder, one judged the edits unnecessary, and a third proposed that the only satisfactory edition of Shakespeare would be a folio of blank pages, a joke that concedes the debate's terms even while mocking them. Thomas's complete edition of 1818 at first travelled the same quiet road. Fame arrived through combat. Between 1821 and 1822 *Blackwood's Magazine* and the *Edinburgh Review*, the period's heavyweight journals, fought over the book in public: *Blackwood's* dismissed the enterprise as "prudery in pasteboard", while the *Edinburgh* commended the expurgations for sparing readers "awkwardness" and "distress". The reading public sided with the *Edinburgh*, sales climbed steeply on the controversy, and new editions followed at intervals of a few years through the 1880s. The episode is the reception historian's foundation document, since it shows the expurgation succeeding not as a conspiracy against Shakespeare but as a marketed answer to a discomfort its buyers genuinely felt: the discomfort of the mixed family circle, the daughter at the table, the servant within earshot, the guest whose delicacy set the room's standard.

The second age turned the Bowdlers into a byword, and its documents are acts of repudiation. The turn is conventionally dated to 1916, when Richard Whiteing published "Bowdler Bowdlerised" in *The English Review* and subjected the edition to the treatment its makers had applied to Shakespeare. Whiteing's charges were specific, and their specificity still stings: the expurgation was inconsistent, cutting in one place what it tolerated in another; the prefaces to the difficult plays were "mealy-mouthed attempts to right himself"; the editorial tactlessness resembled "a baby playing with a pair of shears". His conclusion reversed the doctrine's founding premise, arguing that children required not protection from the facts of the text but instruction in meeting them, and warning that zeal of the Bowdler kind could build an index of forbidden books to rival the Catholic Church's own. *The Nation*, answering in a cooler register, declined the prosecution's rhetoric while accepting its verdict in substance: the Family Shakespeare was a relic of a pre-Victorian standard of propriety, and the verb formed from the family name had suffered degradation as public taste broadened. By 1925 the edition was, in the standard phrase of the censorship encyclopedias, all but obsolete. The word survived the book, carrying the second age's judgement inside itself like a fly in amber.

Between prosecution and defence stands the figure neither age could assimilate, Algernon Charles Swinburne, who in 1894, at the height of the edition's commercial afterlife and two decades before Whiteing's attack, declared the anti-Bowdler fashion itself a kind of cant: "More nauseous and more foolish cant was never chattered than that which would deride the memory or depreciate the merits of Bowdler. No man ever did better service to Shakespeare than the man who made it possible to put him into the hands of intelligent and imaginative children." Swinburne was no prude and no friend of censors; his own poems had been withdrawn by a nervous publisher. His defence therefore isolates the one argument the prosecution never answered on its merits: the access argument. The Family Shakespeare placed a readable, performable, nearly complete Shakespeare into households and childhoods that would otherwise have met the plays late, in excerpts, or not at all, and it did so during the very decades when Shakespeare's position as the national author was being consolidated. A generation's first Romeo and Juliet was, for a great many readers, the expurgated one, and the love formed there sent readers onward to fuller texts when taste and libraries allowed. The prosecution's answer, that a mutilated first meeting is worse than a deferred one, is a judgement about how reading works, and the nineteenth century's reading history, full of adults who graduated from the family edition to the complete works, does not obviously support it.

The third age is the scholarly one, and its representative figure is Noel Perrin, whose *Dr Bowdler's Legacy: A History of Expurgated Books in England and America* (1969) remains the standard history of the practice. Perrin's contribution was to replace temperament with evidence: he read the expurgated books, compared texts, traced the American branch of the trade, and graded the Bowdlers as serious, competent, and sincere editors working a method their market demanded, neither the villains of Whiteing's melodrama nor the saints of Swinburne's. His explanation of the practice's collapse is sociological rather than moral: expurgation fell, in his account, under pressure from Freudian psychology, which made the sexual content of literature a subject rather than a scandal; from feminism, which contested the doctrine that women readers required a protected text; and from mass media, which destroyed the parlour's monopoly on a family's imaginative life. Later scholarship has kept Perrin's frame while sharpening its parts. Michael Dobson's work on the making of the national poet places the Family Shakespeare inside the long domestication of Shakespeare, the process by which a theatre author became a household possession, read at the fireside and taught as national scripture; on that account the Bowdlers are not an embarrassment at the margin of Shakespeare's rise but one of its mechanisms. Alexa Huang's survey of bowdlerized Shakespeare in the nineteenth century, written for *Index on Censorship* in 2016, completes the triangle from the censorship-studies side: the expurgated texts are evidence in the long history of who may read what, and the Bowdler case is exemplary precisely because its motives were protective rather than punitive, its method subtraction rather than suppression, and its target the most canonical author in the language.

The disagreement among these critics can be adjudicated, and the adjudication is this article's position. Whiteing wins on the text: the expurgation is demonstrably inconsistent by its own stated standard, since the paramour conceit at the tomb and the gold that murders more than poison both survive a test that killed a clock pun, and an editor whose rule cannot explain his own retentions has a practice rather than a principle. Swinburne wins on the history: the edition demonstrably widened Shakespeare's readership in the decades of its dominance, and the readers it made were not ruined for the full text, as the same century's thriving scholarly editions, Johnson's and Steevens's successors through the Cambridge Shakespeare of 1863 to 1866, attest in parallel. Perrin wins on the explanation: the practice rose and fell with the institution that housed it, the family reading circle, and neither the prudery theory nor the liberation theory accounts for the timing as cleanly as his sociology does. The three verdicts are compatible because they answer different questions, and a criticism that keeps the questions separate, what the edition did to the text, what the edition did for the readership, and why the age wanted it done, can honour all three without contradiction. Confusion enters only when a verdict earned on one question is spent on another, as when the text's mutilation is offered as proof that the readers were dupes, or the readers' gratitude as proof that the text was whole.

## Two Expurgations, One Name: Henrietta's Method Against Thomas's

The attribution question matters to this play for a reason beyond justice to a forgotten editor: the two Bowdlers edited differently, and Romeo and Juliet bears the second editor's character, not the first's. Henrietta's twenty plays were chosen as much as edited. Her list leans toward comedy, history, and the tragedies whose fame in family reading was already established, *Lear*, *Hamlet*, *Othello*, and it stops where it stops: no *Romeo and Juliet*, no *Antony and Cleopatra*, no *Troilus and Cressida*, no *Measure for Measure*. Selection was her first instrument of censorship, and by the evidence of the list she wielded it deliberately, since a tragedy whose entire action is a clandestine marriage consummated and then destroyed presented difficulties of a different order from a bawdy joke in *Much Ado*. Within her chosen plays her knife worked on three classes of matter: the sexual, the profane and Roman Catholic in reference, since the preface's religious offence was a live category and not a formula, and the merely dull, scenes she considered trivial or uninteresting, removed in a spirit closer to abridgement than to doctrine. Her total excisions ran to roughly a tenth of the text, the figure the standard accounts repeat. The woman who devised the enterprise was, on the evidence, the more interventionist editor of the two, and the more willing to let taste sit beside propriety on the bench.

Thomas's character as an editor is written across the 1818 volumes in decisions that pull against his sister's habits. He completed the canon rather than selecting from it, taking all thirty-six plays including the ones Henrietta had left alone, and accepting the consequence that some, *Othello* by his own preface's admission, resisted the method almost to breaking. He restored matter she had cut as dull, holding the line at propriety and surrendering the claim to improve the plays' pacing or interest. He wrote prefaces where the difficulties were greatest and fell silent where, as with Romeo and Juliet, he seems to have judged the case routine. He also, and the point is easy to miss, moderated the enterprise's public theology: the second edition's selling line is the parlour test, read aloud in a family, a social standard, where Henrietta's preface had led with offence to "the religious or virtuous mind", a spiritual one. Neither editor was a fool, and both were working a coherent brief; but the Romeo and Juliet of the Family Shakespeare is the brother's artefact, produced by completion rather than selection, by retention wherever diction allowed, and by silence in place of a preface. Readings of the expurgated tragedy that blame it on a spinster's prudery, a caricature the nineteenth century itself began and lazy summary has kept, misassign the hand. The cuts examined in this article, the dial, the curtain, the fall backward, the maidenheads, were made or ratified by a fifty-something retired physician who signed his name to them, defended the method in print, and called the author he was cleaning "our immoral Bard".

The contest over the attribution in modern scholarship deserves a graded statement rather than a slogan. That Henrietta principally edited the 1807 edition is supported by the strongest available authorities: her Oxford Dictionary of National Biography entry credits her as the edition's editor, the publishing history shows the 1807 volumes anonymous and the takeover documented, and Thomas's own 1818 posture, presenting the completed work as a family inheritance from his father's reading chair, is consistent with a project begun by another hand in the household. What remains genuinely uncertain is the texture of collaboration across the whole run of editions: whether Thomas assisted in 1807, how far Henrietta's counsel reached into the 1818 revisions of her own twenty plays, and whether the sister read the brother's new volumes, Romeo and Juliet among them, before her death in 1830. The evidence does not settle those questions, and this article leaves them open deliberately. What the evidence does settle is sufficient for the argument: the method was devised in the 1807 volumes, the tragedy was expurgated in the 1818 completion, and the name on the completion, the name the language kept, is the brother's. Scholarship's recovery of Henrietta, a labour of the later twentieth century carried in reference works and reception studies, is itself a small parable of the series' theme: texts are not fixed, and neither are the credits attached to them.

## Two Technologies of 1807: The Lambs' Tales Against the Bowdlers' Text

The year 1807 produced two incompatible answers to the same problem, and the comparison fixes the Bowdler enterprise's distinctiveness more sharply than any definition. Charles and Mary Lamb published their Tales from Shakespeare in 1807, the year of Henrietta's volumes: twenty plays retold in prose for children, with Mary Lamb writing the comedies and Charles the tragedies, Romeo and Juliet among them. The Tales seldom quote the original verse. Their technology is substitution at the level of the whole work: Shakespeare's language is replaced by a lucid narrative digest in which the story survives and the diction, bawdy included, evaporates because the medium cannot hold it. No reader of the Tales mistakes the digest for the play, and no scandal attaches to the method, then or since, because the Tales announce themselves as a doorway rather than a destination. Generations of children entered Shakespeare through them and graduated, exactly as Swinburne would later argue for the family edition, to the texts themselves.

The Bowdler technology is the mirror image: the destination claimed, the language kept, the offence removed from inside the verse. Where the Lambs surrender the words to save the story, the Bowdlers surrender parts of the story's language to save the words. Each technology has its characteristic dishonesty, and honesty requires both to be named. The Tales dishonesty is one of scale: a child who knows the plot of Romeo and Juliet from Mary Lamb's brother's prose knows an outline that feels like knowledge, and the feeling can delay the meeting with the verse for years. The Family Shakespeare's dishonesty is one of surface: a family that reads the expurgated tragedy believes it has read the play, and the belief is false in the specific stratum the profile catalogues. Judged as instruments of access, the two are near equals, and the nineteenth century used both in parallel without sensing a contradiction. Judged as editions, they differ in kind, because only one of them competes with the scholarly text on the same shelf and under the same title. The Bowdlers' offence against the play was never the protection of children, a purpose the Lambs shared and escaped censure for. The offence was the claim, printed on the title page and believed in the parlour, that the protected text was the text, that what could not be read aloud in a family had never needed to exist in Shakespeare at all.

## The Clean Play in the Market: Editions, Rivals, and Imitators

The Family Shakespeare's commercial life is the strongest external evidence for the access argument, and its outline can be told in editions. After the Blackwood's and Edinburgh quarrel of 1821 and 1822 made the book famous, printings followed at intervals of a few years for six decades, the text settling into the form in which the 1825 fourth edition presents it, ten volumes with Romeo and Juliet closing the tenth beside Hamlet and Othello. Rival houses entered the market the Bowdlers had proved: by 1850 there were seven competing expurgated Shakespeares in print, and by 1900 almost fifty, a small industry of family editions, school editions, and girls' editions, each promising the national author without the national author's offences. The first American expurgation appeared in 1849, and the trade crossed the Atlantic as readily as the uncut text had. Imitation on that scale is a market's verdict: the demand the Bowdlers had identified was not a Bath eccentricity but a structural feature of the century's reading, the gap between Shakespeare's canonical altitude and the parlour's standard of speech, and dozens of editors earned livings inside the gap.

Two features of that market deserve emphasis before the verdict, because both complicate the simple story of a prudish century. The first is that the expurgated and the complete Shakespeare grew together. The decades of the Family Shakespeare's dominance were also the decades of the great scholarly editions and of cheap complete texts for working readers; the same household might own the family edition for the circle and a full text for the study, and the same reader might pass from one to the other across a lifetime, as Swinburne's defence assumes. Expurgation was a stage in a reading life as often as it was a substitute for one. The second feature is the edition's long classroom shadow. The family circle and the schoolroom shared a standard, the read-aloud test, and expurgated texts prepared for the one migrated to the other, where they met the distinctively modern machinery of adoption, challenge, and removal; the bridge from Bath to the school board is walked in detail at [Teaching the Bawdy Lines Honestly](/2030/02/08/teaching-the-bawdy-lines/), which takes up the passage-level problem in the classroom where this article leaves it. The point for the market history is narrower: the Bowdler text of Romeo and Juliet was not merely bought. It was assigned, memorised, recited, and examined on, and its version of the tragedy, refined lovers, muted servants, a heroine whose desire begins after the opening of her great speech has been removed, entered the culture's bloodstream at the age when texts are absorbed without suspicion. The clean play was, for thousands of nineteenth-century readers, simply the play.

## The Knife at Work: Subtraction as a Technology

The father's parable in the 1818 preface sets the enterprise's technical standard, and the standard is invisibility: the family listened "without reason to suspect that any parts of the plays had been omitted". A printed edition pursuing invisibility faces constraints that shaped every cut examined in this article, and the constraints explain choices that otherwise look arbitrary. A cut at a speech boundary hides itself, since speeches begin and end cleanly; hence the maidenheads exchange, a self-contained rung in the servants' ladder, could vanish entire. A cut inside a speech must respect the verse, since a limping line advertises the wound; hence the preference for whole sentences, like the saint-seducing gold, whose removal leaves the surrounding lines metrically and grammatically intact. Where neither boundary nor sentence offered a clean edge, substitution did the work of excision while wearing its clothes: "point" for the slang noun in Mercutio's dial line preserves the syllable count, the stress pattern, and the clock, changing nothing a listener could hear except the meaning. The technology's sophistication should be acknowledged before it is condemned. These are not the cuts of a vandal. They are the cuts of editors who understood metre, idiom, and the acoustics of the parlour, and who applied that understanding, line by line across thirty-six plays, in the service of a standard the parlour set.

The technology also had a characteristic failure mode, and the failure mode is the deep reason the enterprise collapsed under Whiteing's ridicule. Invisibility is a standard that punishes honesty: an edition that marked its omissions, as a scholarly text marks variants, would confess on every page that the family was reading a reduced Shakespeare, and the confession was exactly what the product existed to prevent. The Bowdler text therefore cannot signal where it has been, and a reader who suspects a cut has no instrument short of a second edition and an afternoon. Worse, invisibility forbids the editor from explaining a retention. Where the doctrine's letter and the play's needs conflicted, as at the tomb, the text simply keeps the passage and keeps silent, producing the inconsistencies Whiteing catalogued: paramour yes, prick of noon no; poison yes, maidenheads no. A marked edition could have defended those calls in notes, and some later expurgators did annotate. The Family Shakespeare's founding promise, nothing added, barred the defence along with the offence. The technology that made the edition trustworthy to its buyers, the seamless surface, is the same technology that made it indefensible to its critics, and the tension is not a personal failure of either Bowdler. It is built into the idea of an expurgation that will not show its seams.

## Saints, Shrivers, and the Friar: The Religious Test in Verona

Henrietta's preface names two offences, the religious and the indelicate, and the history of the enterprise in other plays shows the religious test cutting deep: in Hamlet the Family text's excisions fall heavily on blasphemy, turning "For God's love, let me hear" into "For Heaven's love, let me hear" and stripping oaths and curses of their sacred vocabulary. Romeo and Juliet presented the religious test with a stranger problem. The tragedy's machinery is saturated with the old religion: a Franciscan friar who hears confessions, shrives the heroine, and plots in a cell; lovers who conduct their first exchange as pilgrim and saint, "For saints have hands that pilgrims' hands do touch" (1.5, Folger); a heroine who calls her lover "the god of my idolatry" (2.2, Folger). A rigorous application of the religious offence, of the kind that struck Roman Catholic references from Henrietta's twenty plays, would have had to dismantle the plot itself, since the tragedy cannot run without its friar, its shriving, and its saint-play. The Family text does nothing of the kind. The pilgrim sonnet stands, the Friar's cell stands, the confession scenes stand, and the religious test, so busy in Hamlet, idles in Verona.

The idling is evidence about the doctrine's real priorities, and it corrects a possible misreading of the enterprise as primarily theological. In the play where sex and religion are woven into the same speeches, the knife followed the sex and spared the religion every time the two could be separated, and spared both where they could not. The saint-seducing gold is the limiting case and repays a second look in this light: the sentence is at once the play's most sexual metaphor in Act 1 and a small blasphemy, gold enthroned as a seducer of saints. Its removal can be filed under either offence, and the filing hardly matters; what matters is that the pilgrim sonnet, equally a fusion of devotion and desire, survives entire a few scenes later because its fusion is beautiful, formal, and spoken by the lovers in the licensed posture of courtship. The doctrine, applied by human editors across a long text, resolved into a hierarchy that the prefaces never state: form could redeem content, elevation could license appetite, and the plain style, in servants, nurses, and metaphors that named the body in the vernacular, carried the whole weight of both offences. The religious test in Verona thus ends as a footnote to the sexual one, and the footnote matters for the verdict, since it shows the expurgation grading the play's language by register and class even where its stated categories pointed elsewhere.

## Reading the Clean Play as Evidence

The expurgated Romeo and Juliet has outlived its purpose and acquired a second one, and the second purpose governs how the text is handled in scholarship and teaching. As a reading text the Family Shakespeare is finished; no syllabus assigns it, no family circle gathers round it, and its reprints are bought, in the standard account's phrase, to observe what exactly Bowdler removed and why. As an evidence text it is inexhaustible. The Folger's education programme treats censored Shakespeare as a teaching subject in its own right, and the classroom exercise the profile formalises, the two texts open side by side, has become a standard way of showing students that a classic is not a monument but a negotiation, re-fought by every age that inherits it. The cliché of reception imagines a fixed text travelling untouched through the centuries while opinions change around it. The Bowdler volumes refute the picture in the most literal way available: for the century of the play's widest domestic readership, the text itself was different, shorter by a hundred cuts, cleaner in its servants and its heroine, and the opinions formed in those parlours were formed on the altered object.

That is the series thesis this article was commissioned to carry, and the evidence has carried it further than the thesis required. The cleaning of Romeo and Juliet was not an episode at the margin of the play's life. It was, for several generations, the play's principal mode of existence in the home, the form in which the tragedy was memorised, quoted, and loved, and the standard against which fuller texts later struck readers as surprisingly coarse, a shock recorded in reverse whenever a reader raised on expurgation met the uncut Mercutio or the uncut opening of Juliet's night speech and felt the canonical author misbehaving. Reception history written without the Family Shakespeare mistakes that shock for a modern discovery. The bawdy was never lost and found; it was printed, cut, restored, and argued over in an unbroken sequence, and the Bowdler volumes are the sequence's best-documented link, precisely because their makers were systematic, prolific, and proud enough of the method to explain it in prefaces that survive.

## Bowdlerize: The Verb That Outlived the Edition

The strangest fact in the whole record is lexical. Thomas Bowdler died on 24 February 1825, and by the middle of that decade, on the account given in the Encyclopedia of Censorship and repeated in the standard histories, his surname had already become a verb, to bowdlerize, meaning to expurgate a text for family or youthful reading. The verb's first appearance in the dictionaries' citation files is dated a little later: the Oxford tradition and Merriam-Webster both give 1836 as the first recorded use, and the etymological dictionaries follow them. The gap between circulation and citation is ordinary in word history and worth stating plainly, since the article's standard is evidence graded by kind: the word was in the air by the later 1820s, in print by 1836, and pejorative in tendency from early in its life, since a verb coined from a living editor's name, for an act half the literary world considered vandalism, could hardly circulate as a compliment. What the word named, at first, was a genre as much as a crime: the family edition, the school edition, the girls' edition, the whole shelf of protected texts whose production the Bowdlers had pioneered and whose numbers, seven rivals by 1850 and almost fifty by 1900, measure the genre's commercial health.

The verb's second life is the one dictionaries record with a widened definition. Merriam-Webster's entry carries two senses: to expurgate by omitting or modifying parts considered vulgar, and, beyond books, to modify by abridging, simplifying, or distorting in style or content. The second sense detaches the word from sex and from Shakespeare alike and makes it available for any act of protective alteration, a syllabus trimmed of difficulty, a translation softened for its market, a classic retold until its teeth are drawn. That widening is the precise measure of the Bowdlers' cultural victory and defeat at once. The victory: their surname entered the small company of eponyms, beside boycott and sandwich in the EBSCO summary's grouping, that name a permanent possibility of the language. The defeat: the word's settled connotation is contempt. To call an edition bowdlerized in a review is to end the argument, not to open one, and the contempt attaches to the intention as much as the act, presuming that the expurgator acted from fear and in bad faith. The historical Bowdlers, on the evidence of their prefaces and their sales, acted from a doctrine they stated openly and a demand they did not create. The verb has no room for either fact. It is the second age's verdict, Whiteing's age, preserved as vocabulary, and like all preserved verdicts it should be read as evidence about its preservers.

The folklore that gathers around a famous word supplies a final caution, and the caution is documented rather than decorative. The most repeated single charge against Bowdler in popular writing is that he changed Lady Macbeth's "Out, damned spot" into "Out, crimson spot", a substitution quoted against him by prominent modern journalists and usage writers. The charge is false. The Family Shakespeare does not contain the crimson reading; the substitution belongs to Thomas and Stephen Bulfinch's edition of 1865, as Ross Davies demonstrated in the Green Bag's patient unravelling of the error. The myth matters here for two reasons. It shows the verb's gravity at work, pulling every act of Victorian expurgation into orbit around the one famous name, until the Bowdlers stand accused of cuts they never made by writers who never opened their volumes. And it states, by example, the standard this article has tried to keep: a cut is claimed only where the two texts, Folger and Family, can be set side by side, and an editor is blamed only for the work of the editor's own hand. The Bowdlers' actual cuts, catalogued in the profile, are quite sufficient for judgement without borrowing the Bulfinches'.

The name's afterlife has one more chamber. On 11 July, Thomas Bowdler's birthday, librarians and literature readers mark Bowdler's Day, an ironic commemoration recorded in the legal and library press, in which the editor's meddlings are celebrated in the spirit of a cautionary tale. The observance is good-humoured and its moral is earnest: the day exists to remind the professions that guard texts how easily protection becomes erasure. Set beside the verb and the myth, the day completes the pattern of the afterlife. The edition is obsolete, the method is a warning, the name is a common noun's raw material, and the book itself survives in scholarly hands chiefly as an instrument for studying what its makers removed and why. Few editors in any century have been so thoroughly answered by the language they worked in.

## The Verdict on Bowdlerizing the Play

The brief for this article demands a verdict on bowdlerizing Romeo and Juliet, and the evidence assembled supports a verdict with two clauses that must not be separated. As an edition of the play, the Family Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet fails, and fails in a way its own doctrine cannot excuse. The expurgation's founding claim was anatomical: offence is local, seated in words and expressions, and removable without injury to the organism. The collation refutes the claim on the doctrine's own ground. The words removed from this play were load-bearing. The servants' sexual threats carry the first scene's analysis of the feud. The saint-seducing gold betrays the performed character of Romeo's first love. The hag in Queen Mab is the speech's theory of desire. The fall backward is the Nurse's worldview and the measure of her later fall. The opening of Juliet's night speech is the heroine's desire stated as the tragedy's subject. Remove the stratum and the organism does not merely blush less; it means less, and means differently, redistributing dignity upward from servants to masters and converting a tragedy of appetite into a tragedy of affection. A censorship that changes what a work means while promising to change only what a work says has failed by its own standard first, and by any later standard second. Whiteing's charge of inconsistency lands for the same reason: a test that kills the clock pun and keeps the paramour, that softens "whoreson" and prints the poison bargain entire, is not a principle imperfectly applied but a sensibility armed with a knife, and sensibility is not transferable, defensible, or reviewable. The edition's Romeo and Juliet should not be read as the play, taught as the play, or forgiven as the play, and on the textual question the prosecution's case is closed.

As an event in the play's history, the same edition succeeds, and the success is not a consolation prize but a fact of the same magnitude. The Family Shakespeare carried Romeo and Juliet, at nearly full architectural strength and in verse a parlour could perform, into the households that built Shakespeare's nineteenth-century supremacy. It made first readers by the hundred thousand, it taught the culture which play this was, a love tragedy of refined and suffering young people, a formation of the play's meaning that later and fuller reading has had to argue with ever since, and it modelled, for good and ill, the protective editing that every school text and family edition since has practised in its shadow. Swinburne's defence and Perrin's sociology, adjudicated in the critics section, hold: access widened, readers graduated, and the institution that demanded the edition, the family circle reading aloud, was a real institution with real claims, not a conspiracy of the timid. The two clauses reconcile in a single sentence. Bowdlerizing the play was a good service performed by a bad instrument: the service was distribution, the instrument was subtraction, and the tragedy's afterlife is richer for the distribution and poorer for every line the subtraction took. The mature response to the Family Shakespeare is neither the parlour's gratitude nor Whiteing's contempt but the collator's patience: two texts, side by side, and the cuts read as the most honest criticism the early nineteenth century ever wrote about what Romeo and Juliet actually contains. A censor's index, kept carefully, is a map of the work's power drawn by its enemies, and no subsequent age has drawn the map of this play's energies, its sex, its laughter, its heroine's will, more precisely than the brother and sister from Bath who set out to hide them.

The complication the brief assigns, protecting readers against gutting the play, deserves its own paragraph before the record closes, because the verdict's two clauses can be mistaken for a compromise and are instead a distinction. Protection is a legitimate office. Every age performs it, by selection, by retelling, by the school edition and the family reading list, and the Lambs' Tales prove that the office can be discharged in 1807 itself without a single falsified line: a digest announces its own scale and deceives nobody. Gutting begins where protection claims the original's name, where the altered object circulates as the thing itself, so that a reader's gratitude, a student's examination answer, and a century's image of a heroine are all formed on evidence that has been tampered with in secret. The Bowdlers stand on both sides of that line at once, which is what makes their case the permanent test of it. Their intention, stated in prefaces of unusual candour, was protection in its most defensible form, for named readers in a named room against a named embarrassment. Their instrument, subtraction under a promise of invisibility, crossed into gutting at exactly the points where the removed language carried meaning, and the collation, not the intention, is where the crossing can be seen. Later protective editing has faced the same choice and mostly chosen better instruments: annotation, selection honestly labelled, retelling honestly scaled. The Family Shakespeare remains the monument of the worse instrument in the service of the better motive, and Romeo and Juliet, the play it altered most intimately among the tragedies it reached late, remains the clearest place to watch the choice being made.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why does Romeo and Juliet not appear in the 1807 first edition of the Family Shakespeare?

The tragedy was not among Henrietta Bowdler's twenty. Her 1807 volumes, published anonymously in four small volumes, held a selection weighted toward comedy and history, closing with King Lear, Hamlet, and Othello. Selection was her first instrument: plays whose plots resisted the subtraction method could simply be left for another day. Romeo and Juliet, whose action turns on a clandestine marriage and a heroine's desire, waited until Thomas Bowdler completed the canon in 1818, when all thirty-six plays then recognised appeared in ten volumes and the Veronese tragedy took its place in the tenth, beside Hamlet and Othello.

### Q: What does the Family Shakespeare title page promise, word for word?

The 1818 title page promises an edition "in which nothing is added to the original text; but those words and expressions are omitted which cannot with propriety be read aloud in a family". The promise is the enterprise's contract and its boast in a single sentence. Nothing added distinguishes the Bowdlers from rewriters such as Nahum Tate, who gave King Lear a happy ending; the read-aloud test names the standard every cut was meant to serve, the mixed family circle; and the restriction to words and expressions states the doctrine that offence is local and verbal, the claim the collation of the play tests and finds wanting.

### Q: Which words replace the slang in Mercutio's dial line, and what happens to the joke?

In the Folger text Mercutio says "the bawdy hand of the dial is now upon the prick of noon" (2.4). The Family text prints "the hand of the dial is now upon the point of noon". "Point", the clockmaker's term, replaces the slang noun, and "bawdy" is deleted, so the metre and the time of day survive while the joke's mechanism dies. The original line fuses a clock's pointer, a lecherous hand, and an obscenity into one figure for Mercutio's conversion of everything into sexual energy. The cleaned line reports that it is midday. Documented collations make the substitution the most quoted single alteration in the play's expurgated history.

### Q: Why did Bowdler's knife fall on single words and sentences more often than on whole scenes in this play?

Because invisibility was the technical standard. The 1818 preface praises the editors' father for omitting passages so discreetly that his family never suspected an omission, and a printed text pursuing that standard cuts where the join will not show: at speech boundaries, in whole sentences whose removal leaves metre and grammar intact, or by substitutions that preserve the syllable count. Whole scenes advertise their absence; a sentence lifted from a speech does not. The result in this play was more than a hundred alterations, most of them small, which is why the expurgated tragedy reads smoothly and why its losses are invisible to any reader without a second edition open.

### Q: What survives of the apothecary scene in Mantua in the Family Shakespeare text?

Nearly everything. Collation of the 1825 printing against the Folger text finds the forty ducats offered and accepted, the dram of poison described in its speed and violence, the apothecary's protest that Mantua's law is death to the seller, the exchange "My poverty, but not my will, consents" and "I pay thy poverty and not thy will", and Romeo's judgement entire: "There is thy gold, worse poison to men's souls, / Doing more murders in this loathsome world / Than these poor compounds that thou mayst not sell." The scene's diction is clean even though its deed is a suicide's purchase, and the Bowdler test measured diction.

### Q: How did Thomas Bowdler change his sister's twenty plays when he took the edition over in 1818?

He re-edited all twenty, generally continuing Henrietta's omissions of sexual and profane matter, while restoring scenes she had removed for a different reason, because she judged them trivial or uninteresting. The distinction marks the editors' different briefs. Henrietta's standard joined propriety to taste, and she cut dullness as readily as decency required; Thomas held the line at propriety alone, surrendering the claim to improve the plays' interest. He also added the sixteen plays she had not touched, wrote prefaces for the hardest cases such as Othello and Measure for Measure, and placed his name alone on the title pages, where hers had been hidden by anonymity in 1807.

### Q: Why is there no editor's preface before Romeo and Juliet in the Family Shakespeare?

Because Thomas Bowdler seems to have judged the case routine. He wrote prefaces where the method met its limits: for Othello, conceding that the subject is little suited to family reading and advising transfer from the parlour to the cabinet; for Measure for Measure, explaining the borrowed Kemble text. Before Romeo and Juliet there is silence. The silence can be read as confidence that the tragedy's offences sat in detachable innuendo, removable by the usual surgery. The collation supports the confidence as intention and refutes it as fact, since the removed matter includes the heroine's desire and the first scene's analysis of the feud, neither of them detachable.

### Q: What did Blackwood's Magazine and the Edinburgh Review each say about the enterprise in 1821 and 1822?

The two leading journals fought over the completed edition in public and made its fortune. Blackwood's dismissed the Family Shakespeare as "prudery in pasteboard", pasteboard being the cheap binding of a shoddy article. The Edinburgh Review commended the expurgations for sparing family readers "awkwardness" and "distress", naming the social emotions of the reading circle rather than any theological danger. The public sided with the Edinburgh. Sales rose sharply on the free publicity of the quarrel, and new editions followed at intervals of a few years through the 1880s, a publishing history that shows the expurgation answering a discomfort its buyers recognised as their own.

### Q: Why did Swinburne defend Bowdler in 1894 against the mockers?

Algernon Charles Swinburne defended the service rather than the surgery. Writing in 1894, he declared that "more nauseous and more foolish cant was never chattered than that which would deride the memory or depreciate the merits of Bowdler", and that no man did better service to Shakespeare than the man who put the plays "into the hands of intelligent and imaginative children". Swinburne was no prude; a publisher had withdrawn his own poems for their content. His defence isolates the access argument: the family edition made first readers in households that would otherwise have met Shakespeare late or in fragments, and first readers graduated to fuller texts.

### Q: Which famous Macbeth alteration is blamed on Bowdler but belongs to another edition?

The crimson spot. Lady Macbeth's "Out, damned spot" is often said, by prominent modern journalists and usage writers among others, to have been softened by Bowdler into "Out, crimson spot". The Family Shakespeare contains no such reading. The substitution belongs to the edition of Thomas and Stephen Bulfinch published in 1865, as Ross Davies demonstrated in the Green Bag's examination of the error's spread. The myth is instructive: the verb bowdlerize exerts a gravity that pulls every Victorian expurgation into orbit around the famous name, until the Bowdlers stand accused of cuts their volumes never made, by writers who never opened them.

### Q: What happened to "Gallop apace" at the head of Juliet's night speech?

It was removed. Juliet's soliloquy at the opening of Act 3 Scene 2 begins in the Folger text with the invocation "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds, / Towards Phoebus' lodging", a bride commanding the sun's horses to hurry the day away so that night can bring her husband. Collation of the 1825 Family printing finds the words "Gallop apace" absent from the volume, and documented collations show the speech's fifth line, "Spread thy close curtain, love-performing night", surviving only in reduced form toward "come, civil night". The speech's later beauties remain; its engine, a young wife's desire spoken in imperatives to the cosmos, is gone.

### Q: How does the expurgation treat Juliet's desire compared with the servants' bawdy?

The servants lose episodes; Juliet loses her register. Sampson and Gregory's sexual threats form detachable sequences that vanish entire, and the Nurse's jokes lift out as anecdotes. Juliet's desire is woven into the tragedy's central lyricism, so the knife works differently: it removes the opening of her night speech, where desire speaks in its own voice, while leaving her devotion, her terror at the potion, and her grief untouched. The pattern preserves the period's double standard inside the text. Male longing keeps its licensed codes of sigh and sonnet; the heroine's appetite, stated as appetite, is the one element the family text cannot carry.

### Q: What did Richard Whiteing mean by calling his 1916 attack "Bowdler Bowdlerised"?

The title announces the method: Whiteing proposed to do to Bowdler what Bowdler did to Shakespeare, subjecting the editor to unsparing treatment in The English Review. His charges were specific. The expurgation was inconsistent, cutting in one place what it tolerated in another; the prefaces to the difficult plays were "mealy-mouthed attempts to right himself"; the editorial touch resembled "a baby playing with a pair of shears". He argued that children required instruction in meeting the text's facts rather than protection from them, and warned that protective zeal could build an index of forbidden books rivalling the Catholic Church's. The piece is conventionally dated as the turn of informed opinion against the enterprise.

### Q: Why does the Bowdler text of the tomb scene keep Romeo's paramour speech?

Because its register is funereal rather than comic. Romeo, over Juliet's body, imagines death as a rival who keeps her "in dark to be his paramour" (5.3, Folger), a conceit more morbidly sexual than much of the matter cut from Act 1. The Family text retains it, along with "my everlasting rest" and the draught drunk with "O true apothecary! / Thy drugs are quick." The doctrine policed diction in its social setting: a joke about the body in a street scene threatened the parlour's composure, while a death-haunted figure in a tomb, spoken in high tragic verse at the catastrophe, presented no phrase a father could stumble over aloud.

### Q: How many rival expurgated Shakespeares stood in print by 1850 and by 1900?

Seven by 1850 and almost fifty by 1900, on the figures carried in the standard censorship references. The numbers measure the market the Bowdlers created and proved: family editions, school editions, and girls' editions, each promising the national author without the offences, multiplied across the century, and the trade crossed the Atlantic, with the first American expurgation appearing in 1849. Imitation on that scale is evidence in the access debate. The demand was structural, a widening middle-class readership buying a canonical author whose speech its parlours could not perform, and dozens of editors earned their livings inside the gap the Bowdlers had opened.

### Q: What does the InsightCrunch Bowdler profile record, row by row?

The profile sets the Folger text of the tragedy beside the Family Shakespeare text of 1818, reprinted in 1825, for thirteen passages, recording the treatment and the ground of the cut in each case. Its rows show the servants' thrusting sequence and maidenheads pun omitted, the saint-seducing gold sentence removed whole, the Queen Mab hag passage lifted out, the Nurse's fall-backward joke absent, Mercutio's dial line substituted word for word, the opening of Juliet's night speech removed, "whoreson" softened to "fellow", and, in the retained column, the apothecary bargain, the tomb speeches, and the happy dagger standing entire. The pattern across the rows is a censorship of diction laid over an untouched architecture of action.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Bowdler profile to argue that the expurgation censored diction rather than deeds?

By arguing from the retained column. An essay can concede the cuts in the profile's left-hand rows, the puns, jokes, and metaphors that name the body, and then set against them the survivals: a poison bought with forty ducats and praised as quick, a double suicide performed in full, a heroine's dagger welcomed as happy. If the edition feared the tragedy's darkness, Act 5 would show the deepest cuts; Act 5 shows almost none. The variable that predicts a passage's fate is not its moral weight but its vocabulary and register, comic naming against funereal figure. The profile's two columns, quoted in pairs, make that argument without a single appeal to the editors' temperaments.

### Q: Why did the expurgated Romeo and Juliet keep the suicides that end the play?

Because the doctrine's test was verbal and social rather than moral. The suicides are deeds, carried in diction of prayer, farewell, and benediction: Romeo drinks with a lover's toast, Juliet dies with a welcome for the dagger. No indelicate word, no sexual figure in the comic register, and no profanity attends either death, so a test built on expressions had no instrument for them. The prefaces, meanwhile, praised the tragedies' moral architecture, and the deaths are the architecture's keystone, the price the feud exacts and the city reads. Cutting them would have destroyed the very instruction the edition claimed to deliver. The jokes, by that logic, were expendable; the catastrophe was the lesson.

### Q: What separated Bowdler's method from Nahum Tate's rewriting of King Lear?

Restraint of hand. Tate, in 1681, rewrote: he supplied King Lear with a happy ending, married Cordelia to Edgar, and held the stage with the invention for a century and a half, adding freely in the confidence that the text was raw material. The Bowdlers added nothing. Their title page makes the abstinence a boast, and their practice honours it with rare exceptions of single-word substitution, "point" for the slang noun, "fellow" for "whoreson". The difference decides how each artefact should be judged. Tate's Lear is a new play wearing an old name; the Family Shakespeare is the old play with pieces missing, which is why collation can audit it line by line.

### Q: Does the Family Shakespeare text survive inside modern editions of the play?

No. Modern editions of Romeo and Juliet descend from the early printed texts, the second quarto of 1599 and the First Folio, by way of the scholarly tradition that the Cambridge editors and their successors built; no reading in a standard modern text derives from Bowdler. The Family text survives on its own track, reprinted as a curiosity and studied as evidence: scholars open it to learn what the nineteenth century could not say aloud, teachers set it beside the Folger text to show a class what censorship looks like at the level of the line, and its readings persist in the culture chiefly as the contents of the verb it fathered.
