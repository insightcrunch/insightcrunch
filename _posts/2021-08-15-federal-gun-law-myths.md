## Federal Gun Law Myths and the Texts They Are Tested Against

Among federal gun law myths, one misunderstanding carries consequences that reach far beyond a single transaction. A widely held belief holds that every approved background check creates a lasting federal record that links a named buyer to an identified firearm, so that approval after approval builds a government registry of gun owners. The belief matters because a registry changes the meaning of the check itself. A check understood as a screening step before a transfer raises one set of questions. A check understood as an act of registration raises a different set, about custody, retention, retrieval, and the future use of a record assembled person by person. Debate about expanding checks, narrowing checks, or relying on checks as an enforcement tool takes a different shape depending on which system a participant believes Congress built. A reader who begins with the wrong system will misread every later provision that touches records, because retention, destruction, and custody are the provisions that decide what the check leaves behind.

Belief in a federal registry spread through a set of facts that are individually real and collectively easy to assemble into the wrong picture. A transfer through a licensee does produce a detailed transaction record. A check does transmit identifying information about a buyer to a federal system before a transfer proceeds. Records connected with denied transactions are retained on a stated term rather than destroyed on the schedule that governs approvals. Government record systems in other fields ordinarily keep identifying files for long periods and make those files retrievable by name. Each fact invites an inference that federal firearms records follow the same pattern, with the same custody and the same retrievability. The inference feels natural because the pieces sit close together in a single purchase. A form at a counter, a call or an electronic submission, an answer returned to a licensee, and a firearm handed over can look like one continuous federal file, even when statute and regulation assign the pieces to different custodians, different retention periods, and different legal fates. Visibility does much of the work. The buyer sees the form and the check. The buyer does not see a destruction deadline applied inside a federal system, a prohibition aimed at centralization, or an audit log reduced to a number and a date.

![Federal gun law myths tested against the statutory text of the federal firearms statutes - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-64.webp)

That single misunderstanding illustrates a wider pattern across the field. Beliefs about federal firearms law diverge from the statutory text in both directions, and the direction of error changes from claim to claim. Some claims invent restrictions that the text does not contain, assigning Congress a mandate that no cited section imposes or treating a regulated channel as a closed one. Other claims erase restrictions that the text states expressly, describing a transfer as unchecked even though a licensee, a record, and a check stand in the middle of the lawful route. A third group erases preservations that a text states expressly, reading a decision or a statute as though qualifications written into the governing document had never been placed there. No single suspicion corrects every error. A reader primed to expect hidden control will miss provisions that genuinely restrict a transaction. A reader primed to expect evasion will miss provisions that forbid the construction of a feared system or preserve categories of regulation in express terms. Careful reading has to begin from neutrality about direction and proceed claim by claim, text by text.

Compression explains much of the divergence. Federal firearms law reaches a transaction through layers that a short statement tends to flatten. One layer identifies the sovereign, because Congress and a state legislature can impose different duties on the same transfer and a duty belonging to one body is often attributed to the other. Another layer identifies the seller, because duties that attach to a licensed importer, manufacturer, or dealer do not automatically attach to a private person, and the seller category often drops out of a repeated claim. Another layer identifies the instrument, because a handgun, a long gun, a machine gun, and a silencer can sit under different provisions inside the same purchase setting. Another layer identifies the moment, because a duty at transfer differs from a duty during possession, and a prohibition in force for a stated term differs from a prohibition without a term. A claim that loses one layer during repetition gains simplicity and loses accuracy at the same rate. A ten year term becomes a repeal decision, and a device handed over at a counter becomes a household mandate.

Medium and setting supply another source of error. An internet storefront looks like a new channel of commerce, and a crowded show floor looks like a legal setting apart from an ordinary store. Federal law, however, attaches transfer duties mainly to licensed status, to the crossing of a state line, and to the category of the firearm, rather than to the advertising medium or the room in which parties meet. Technology changed ordering. A venue gathers buyers and sellers. Neither fact rewrites the sections governing shipment, delivery, acquisition, or transfer. Error grows when a visible feature of a transaction receives credit for legal consequences produced by a less visible feature, such as the presence or absence of a license, the residence of the parties, or a cutoff date fixed decades earlier in a provision that later summaries omit.

Severity of penalty supplies a further source of confusion. A felony penalty attached to an unregistered transfer can make the underlying article sound prohibited altogether, even when the penalty defines the edge of a lawful registered channel. A dismissal bar in civil litigation can make an industry sound immune altogether, even when the definition of the barred action contains exclusions that leave channels of suit open. Figures detached from method produce a similar effect in measurement disputes, as a percentage drawn from one survey question hardens during repetition into a direct count of a different quantity. Each problem has the same remedy. The penalty has to be read with the channel the penalty polices, the bar with the definition that bounds the bar, the figure with the chain and explanations that produced the figure, and the percentage with the question and population behind the percentage.

The method applied to every claim in the examination that follows is deliberately plain. Each claim appears first in the strongest form that holders of the claim would recognize, stated fairly and without softening language that would make the later answer easier. The claim then meets the material that decides the claim, namely the statutory text, the implementing regulation, the judicial decision, or the named dataset, quoted or described at the precision the source carries. Dates and sources travel with counts that depend on dates and sources. A figure from a report appears with the explanations the report supplied for the figure. A count from an organization appears with the name of the organization and the period the count describes. Where the record contains a genuine measurement disagreement rather than a resolvable textual error, the examination labels the disagreement as contested and explains why two measurements can differ without either measurement refuting the other. Verdicts draw on a short vocabulary and attach to a named deciding authority, so that a reader can locate the provision, passage, or report that carries the outcome and test the reasoning against that source.

Paired treatment reinforces the discipline. Claims that overstate federal control sit near claims that understate federal control, so that a reader cannot settle into one corrective habit and apply that habit mechanically. A provision that protects an approved buyer against one form of recordkeeping sits in the same federal scheme as a provision that routes an online order through a counter and a check. A device supplied at a transfer sits near a debate about transfers that carry no federal check. A prohibition with a prewritten ending sits near a civil liability bar with written exclusions. Juxtaposition keeps attention on text rather than temperament. The question throughout is whether a claim matches the words, dates, categories, and records that govern the subject.

A careful reader will also notice how often precision turns on a short passage that summaries skip. A deadline can sit in an appropriations provision rather than in the code section that states a destruction rule. A sunset can be drafted in the language of repeal and operate without a later vote. A court can preserve categories in the same passage that announces a right. Such details decide outcomes, and the details resist slogans because each detail attaches a claim to a sovereign, a seller, a category, a date, or a clause. The examination returns repeatedly to those attachments, because federal gun law myths lose force at exactly the point where a missing attachment is restored to the sentence that carries the claim.

## What the Check Leaves Behind and What the Counter Requires

### Federal background checks build a registry of gun owners

Among federal gun law myths, the registry assertion describes a system that federal law describes in order to forbid. In the strongest form its holders would recognize, the assertion holds that each background check sends a buyer's identity into a federal file, that the file keeps the identity linked to the firearm acquired, and that accumulation across transactions gives the federal government a registry of gun owners, searchable by name. A registry in that sense gathers records under one control, keeps identifying records durably, and links a person to a firearm retrievably. The check system fails each element by enacted design, and an account of [how the NICS background check law works](/2021/06/15/nics-background-check-law/) shows the design operating from the call that opens a check to the purge that closes the federal file.

### What remains in federal hands after an approved check clears?

After an approved check, the federal system retains the transaction number and the date assigned to that number. Identifying information tied to the approval faces destruction within the governing deadline, and any remaining identifying residue faces purging within ninety days under the governing regulation.

Section 922(t)(2) of title 18 governs the moment a check returns an approval. On an approval, the system assigns a unique identification number, and the provision directs destruction of all records of the system respecting the call other than the identifying number and the date the number was assigned. The provision orders destruction and excepts two items, rather than authorizing retention subject to policy. A federal file built on that command holds an identifier for a transaction and a calendar fact about the identifier. The number establishes that a transaction received an approval on a date. The number alone does not reconstruct the person behind the transaction, because the material that linked the number to a person is the material the command reaches.

Precision about the deadline requires a second source, and attribution of the deadline to section 922(t)(2) misstates the law. The 24-hour deadline does not sit in section 922(t)(2) itself. Section 617 of Public Law 108-199, the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2004, signed on January 23, 2004, requires destruction of identifying information on an approved transaction not more than 24 hours after the licensee receives notification of the approval. The requirement has been carried in appropriations acts since that enactment, and 28 C.F.R. 25.9(b) implements the requirement. The code provision states the destruction rule and names the excepted items. The appropriations provision sets the clock. Merging the two provisions into one citation loses the legislative sequence behind the clock, a sequence addressed in the oversight passage below.

The audit log gives the destruction rule a concrete residue. For an approved transaction, the residue consists of the NICS Transaction Number and the date, and remaining identifying residue faces purging within 90 days under 28 C.F.R. 25.9(b). An audit log of that form supports a count of transactions and defeats retrieval by person, because retrieval by person requires identifying material and the identifying material is the material ordered destroyed and purged. Delayed and open transactions follow a parallel path. For a transaction that remains delayed or open, identifying information faces destruction at 90 days, and the date and the number face retention. Approval and delay therefore converge on the same minimal federal residue, a number and a date, across the outcomes in which a transfer may proceed.

Denial stands on a different footing, and the difference confirms the analysis. Records of a denied transaction face retention in the audit log for 10 years, then pass to an electronic database maintained by the Federal Bureau of Investigation under 28 C.F.R. 25.9(b). Retention serves the appeal and correction functions available to a denied buyer. The registry assertion, however, concerns persons whose transfers proceeded. A file of denied transactions, held for a stated term, does not place an approved buyer in a federal file. Collapsing the denial file and the approval residue into one description produces the myth, because the collapse treats a prohibited-person record with a ten-year term as evidence of an owner registry with different content and different custody.

Two statutory bars stand behind the destruction rule, and each bar closes a different construction route. Section 926(a) of title 18 forbids any rule or regulation requiring records of licensees to be centralized or transferred to a facility owned, managed, or controlled by the United States or a state, and forbids any rule or regulation establishing any system of registration of firearms, owners, or transactions. Section 103(i) of the Brady Act, Public Law 103-159, bars use of the check system to establish any system of registration, except with respect to persons prohibited from receiving a firearm. The first bar reaches the regulatory route from licensee records. The second bar reaches the check system itself, the very instrument the assertion names. Destruction then removes the raw material for approved transactions, so each prohibition operates on a minimal residue. The exception for prohibited persons in section 103(i) matches the ten-year denial retention and extends no further.

The detailed record of a transfer exists, and the custody of the record answers the assertion as directly as the destruction rule does. The Firearms Transaction Record, ATF Form 4473, holds the detailed account, and the licensee retains the form for not less than 20 years under 27 C.F.R. 478.129(b). The form reaches the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives only if the licensee discontinues business, under section 923(g)(4) of title 18. The twenty-year term describes a decentralized archive distributed across licensed premises rather than a federal registry assembled in one facility. Each licensee holds forms for transfers that licensee completed. No provision gathers the forms during active business or indexes the forms in a federal system searchable by buyer. Transfer of custody follows the end of a business rather than completion of a transaction. The assertion mistakes a durable record for a registry and ignores the custody rule that separates the two concepts.

Content supplies a further separation. A check transmits the buyer's identifying information and descriptors and the category of firearm, stated as handgun, long gun, or other. The check does not transmit the make, the model, or the serial number. The make, the model, and the serial number appear only on the form the licensee retains. A registry in the strong sense asserted requires a retrievable federal link between a named person and an identified firearm. The federal transmission holds identity and category rather than identity and instrument, and the federal residue holds a number and a date rather than identity at all. At no point in the sequence does a federal file hold the paired record that the word registry, used with precision, requires.

One consent-based exception tests the rule and confirms the rule. The Voluntary Appeal File allows a buyer who faces repeated delay or erroneous denial to consent to retention of identifying information by the Federal Bureau of Investigation and to receive a Unique Personal Identification Number. Entry rests on consent, serves a buyer who seeks retention, and concerns repeated delay or wrongful denial rather than an ordinary approval. Retention required a separate consent mechanism precisely because the default rule ordered destruction. A narrow exception built on individual consent does not convert a destruction regime into a registry regime.

The destruction rule carries a cost, and a complete treatment states the cost at full strength alongside the protection. The same rule that prevents assembly of a registry from approved checks makes retrospective analysis of an approved check impossible from the federal system's own file, because destruction removes the record that retrospective analysis would examine. After destruction, an auditor who seeks to sample approved transactions finds the population available for sampling destroyed on the enacted schedule, and audit sampling faces a structural limit. A later inquiry into a specific approved check, whether the inquiry concerns a mistaken approval, a procedural failure, or the accuracy of information supplied during the call, finds a number and a date where a fuller record existed for hours. Appropriations riders in the late 1990s and the early 2000s first required retention for audit for periods of months, on the stated basis that auditors needed surviving records in order to sample transactions, and the fiscal 2004 act reversed that model by imposing destruction within 24 hours. The earlier model preserved oversight capacity at the price of a surviving federal record. The enacted model protects the approved buyer at the price of oversight capacity. Both consequences follow from the same rule, and neither consequence alters the registry analysis, because a record too thin for retrospective audit is also too thin for a registry.

Measured against the governing text, the assertion fails at the definition, the deadline, the residue, the bars, and the custody of the detailed record. Destruction operates within 24 hours under section 617 of Public Law 108-199. The surviving federal residue is a transaction number and a date. Sections 926(a) and 103(i) forbid construction of a registration system from licensee records and from the check system alike. The detailed record rests with the licensee for 20 years and moves to federal custody only at the close of a business. Federal gun law myths of the registry form persist in part because a durable record somewhere invites description as a registry everywhere, and the text answers with destruction, prohibition, and dispersal.

The verdict is False.

### A firearm bought online arrives with no check at all

The online assertion reverses the direction of the registry assertion while sharing an error about where federal duties attach. In the strongest form its holders would recognize, the assertion holds that a buyer selects a firearm on a website, pays through the website, and receives the firearm by delivery at home, with no licensee involved at transfer, no transaction record completed, and no background check run at any stage. The picture treats the internet as a separate legal channel that bypasses the counter, and the picture borrows force from ordinary commerce, in which an online order for most goods ends in a parcel at a door. Federal firearms law draws the controlling line between ordering and transfer. A website functions as an ordering medium. A transfer to a nonlicensee buyer completes in person through a licensee, with the record and the check required at licensed premises. The ordering medium changed with the internet. The transfer channel did not change with the medium.

The lawful route, stated in sequence, dissolves the picture of doorstep delivery. A firearm sold online by a licensee may not travel directly from the selling licensee to a nonlicensee buyer across a state line. The selling licensee ships the firearm to a licensee in the buyer's state, and the buyer completes the transfer in person at the receiving licensee's premises. Completion in person means the transaction record is completed at that premises and the background check is run at that premises, exactly as for an in-store sale. The internet portion of the transaction ends at selection and order. The regulated portion proceeds through shipment between licensees and ends at the counter of the licensee in the buyer's home state. Distance changes the shipping leg and leaves the transfer leg untouched. A buyer near a state line and a buyer far from the selling licensee face the same counter in the home state, because the receiving licensee must sit in the buyer's state outside the narrow exception addressed in the statutory passage that follows.

Sections 922(a)(2) and 922(b)(3) of title 18 supply the basis for the channel. A licensee may not sell or deliver a firearm to a nonresident nonlicensee, subject to a defined exception, and licensee-to-licensee shipment supplies the lawful interstate movement of the firearm before transfer. The defined exception concerns a long gun sale completed face to face, where the sale complies with the law of both states. The exception requires presence, requires a long gun rather than a handgun, and requires compliance with the law of the seller's state and the law of the buyer's state together. The handgun route admits no parallel exception and always runs through a licensee in the buyer's home state. Even the long gun exception completes face to face through a licensee, with the record and the check that attach to a licensee transfer. No provision authorizes a licensee to deliver a firearm to a nonlicensee buyer in another state by carrier for completion at home. Delivery to the home-state licensee and delivery to the home address are different legal events, and the statute authorizes the first while withholding authorization for the second.

Two further provisions close routes around the licensed channel, and each bar carries narrow exceptions that confirm the breadth of the bar. Section 922(a)(3) of title 18 bars a nonlicensee from acquiring a firearm in another state and transporting or receiving the firearm at home outside the licensee channel, with exceptions for acquisition by bequest or by intestate succession. Inheritance stands outside ordinary commerce, and the exception reaches an heir rather than a shopper. A purchase arranged online and collected in another state for carriage home falls inside the bar, because purchase is not bequest and travel for purchase is not succession. Section 922(a)(5) of title 18 bars a nonlicensee from transferring a firearm to a nonlicensee whom the transferor knows or has reasonable cause to believe resides in another state, with exceptions for a temporary loan or rental for lawful sporting purposes and for bequest or intestate succession. A sporting loan serves use rather than ownership and contemplates return of the firearm. A bequest follows death rather than bargain. Neither exception describes an online sale between persons in different states. The two provisions govern opposite sides of an interstate transaction between nonlicensees, acquisition under one provision and transfer under the other, so the imagined bypass fails from either direction of approach.

History explains why the channel stood closed before online retail existed. The Gun Control Act of 1968 ended the mail-order channel to nonlicensees. Remote ordering before that enactment could end in direct delivery to a nonlicensee buyer, and the 1968 act terminated that structure by confining interstate movement to the licensed channel and by barring the acquisition and transfer routes described in the preceding passages. An internet storefront is a new ordering medium inside the same channel rules, not a new legal channel. A new medium changes how a buyer finds a firearm, compares offerings, and places an order. A new channel would change how a firearm lawfully moves from seller to buyer and where transfer duties attach. No enactment created a new channel for internet orders, and the 1968 rules govern an order however the order arrives, by catalogue and post in the earlier period or by website in the later period. The assertion treats a change in the medium as a change in the channel. Federal law treats the categories separately, and the separation decides the claim.

The check sits at the center of the receiving licensee's duties, and the assertion denies the check specifically. Completion at the home-state licensee includes the transaction record and the background check in the same form as a purchase from that licensee's own stock. The receiving licensee does not act as a parcel recipient or as a storage point for a completed sale. The receiving licensee acts as transferor at the moment of completion, running the check before transfer and completing the record that documents the transfer. A failed check stops the transfer at that counter as a failed check stops an ordinary counter sale. An approval allows the transfer to proceed from that counter and from no earlier point. Travel between licensees confers no right of possession on the buyer during transit. Possession passes at completion, in person, after the record and after the check. Federal duties attach to licensed status and to transfer rather than to the advertising medium, and duties that attach to transfer travel with the firearm to the counter where transfer occurs.

Geography tests the assertion through variations in ordering and travel and produces the same result at each variation. A buyer orders from a licensee in a distant state, and shipment runs licensee to licensee with completion through the home-state counter. A buyer orders from a licensee across a nearby state line and proposes collection at the seller's premises, and the proposal succeeds for a long gun only inside the face-to-face exception where the law of both states is satisfied, with the record and the check completed at that transfer, while the proposal fails for a handgun because the handgun route runs through a home-state licensee. A buyer proposes direct shipment to a home address from an out-of-state licensee, and the proposal finds no authorizing provision. A buyer proposes purchase from a private seller in another state arranged through a listing, and the proposal meets the acquisition bar and the transfer bar governing nonlicensees across state lines. Each variation changes distance, convenience, or ordering method. No variation changes the transfer rule, because the transfer rule follows the state line and the licensed status of the parties.

The sole remaining space, a transfer arranged between two private persons who reside in the same state, belongs to the next claim in the sequence and is taken up there.

Measured against the governing text, the assertion fails at ordering, at shipment, at delivery, and at completion. Ordering online creates no transfer. Shipment of a licensee-sold firearm across a state line runs licensee to licensee under sections 922(a)(2) and 922(b)(3) of title 18. Delivery to a nonlicensee buyer completes in person at the home-state licensee, with the transaction record completed and the background check run at that counter. Acquisition and transfer between nonlicensees across a state line face bars at sections 922(a)(3) and 922(a)(5), subject only to exceptions for succession and for temporary sporting loan or rental that describe no retail purchase. The 1968 act closed the mail-order channel to nonlicensees before online retail existed, and the internet added a storefront without adding a channel. Federal gun law myths of the online form persist in part because the visible portion of the transaction, selection and payment on a screen, resembles parcel commerce in other goods, while the regulated portion, shipment between licensees and completion at a counter, culminates only when the buyer stands at the counter in person. Visibility explains the myth without sustaining the myth against the text.

The verdict is False.

## The Device at the Counter and the Floor of the Show

### Federal law requires gun owners to store firearms safely at home

The claim in its strongest form holds that federal law reaches into the home and obliges a gun owner to keep firearms stored safely there. Stated that way, the assertion assigns the federal government a continuing supervisory role over a private household decision, namely the manner in which a firearm is kept after a lawful acquisition has ended. A federal storage mandate would be a distinctive legal instrument. Such a mandate would identify the person under the duty, define the condition required in the home, and attach a consequence to a failure to maintain that condition. The federal provision that gives the claim its footing does none of those things in that form. A real federal requirement exists in this area, and the distance between that requirement and the claim measures the verdict.

The provision at the center of the claim is section 922(z)(1) of title 18. Section 5 of Public Law 109-92 added that provision, in a part of the statute cited as the Child Safety Lock Act of 2005, and the provision took effect on April 24, 2006. The text makes it unlawful for a licensed importer, a licensed manufacturer, or a licensed dealer to sell, deliver, or transfer a handgun to a person other than a licensee unless the transferee is provided with a secure gun storage or safety device for that handgun. The statutory term for the device supplied at the transfer is defined at section 921(a)(34) of title 18. Each element of that sentence carries weight, and each element also marks a boundary that the claim in its strong form crosses without support from the text.

The first boundary is the identity of the person regulated. Section 922(z)(1) speaks to a licensed importer, a licensed manufacturer, and a licensed dealer. The duty belongs to the licensee engaged in the transfer. The buyer, described in the provision as a transferee other than a licensee, is the person to whom the device must be provided. The provision does not address the buyer as the bearer of a storage obligation. That allocation is not a technicality detached from the substance of the claim. A storage mandate for the home would place the duty on the person who keeps the firearm in the home. Section 922(z)(1) places its duty on the commercial or licensed actor who hands the handgun over, and that actor's duty is complete or breached at the transfer itself.

The second boundary is the object covered. The provision concerns a handgun transferred to a nonlicensee. The claim speaks of firearms generally and of gun owners generally, a broader class of weapons and a broader class of persons than the provision names. Precision about the covered instrument matters in a myths examination because federal gun law myths often grow by expansion, taking a rule written for one category and restating it as a rule for a larger category. Here the expansion runs from a handgun transferred by a licensee to firearms held by owners in private homes. The statutory sentence supports the first description and not the second. The term for the required device is defined at section 921(a)(34), and the requirement is that the transferee be provided with such a device for that handgun, tied to the particular handgun being transferred.

The third boundary is the moment at which the duty operates. The requirement attaches to the licensee at the moment of transfer. The covered acts are selling, delivering, and transferring, and the condition is that the transferee be provided with the device in connection with that transfer. Nothing in the cited provision extends the licensee's act into a continuing federal inspection of the home, and nothing in the provision creates a federal standard against which the condition of a firearm in a home is measured on any later day. The duty is transactional. It is triggered by a transfer, it is discharged by providing the device, and its legal life is located at the counter rather than in the room where an owner keeps a firearm after the transaction has closed.

### Does federal law require a buyer to use the device provided at transfer?

No. Section 922(z)(1) requires a licensed importer, manufacturer, or dealer to provide a secure gun storage or safety device when transferring a handgun to a nonlicensee. The provision imposes no duty on the transferee to use the device, keep it attached, or store the handgun in a particular manner afterward at home.

That direct answer follows from the placement of the duty in the text. A requirement to provide an object differs in kind from a requirement to use an object, and the difference is the heart of this claim. Providing a device makes an instrument available to the transferee. Requiring storage would govern conduct after availability, specifying how a firearm must be kept and exposing an owner to a penalty for a different manner of keeping. Section 922(z)(1) contains the providing language and does not contain the governing language. The provision does not require the transferee to use the device, and no provision of federal law requires a firearm owner to store a firearm locked, unloaded, or in any particular manner in the home.

The name of the 2005 act does not rescue the claim either. The Child Safety Lock Act of 2005 supplied the statutory addition, and its title signals a legislative concern with safety devices for handguns. A title, however, is not a storage standard. The operative sentence is section 922(z)(1), and that sentence regulates the transferor's conduct at a sale, a delivery, or a transfer. Reading a household storage duty into that sentence would add a regulated person, a regulated place, and a regulated course of conduct that the text does not name.

The exceptions at section 922(z)(2) confirm the transactional character of the provision by carving out categories of transfer from the licensee's duty. The exceptions cover transfers to or for the United States, states, and their agencies and subdivisions. The exceptions also cover law enforcement officers for law enforcement purposes and rail police officers. A further exception covers handguns listed as curios or relics. A final exception addresses temporary unavailability of a device, with delivery to the transferee within ten calendar days. Each exception is framed as a circumstance in which the providing duty at transfer is lifted or adjusted. None of the exceptions is framed as an exemption from a household storage rule, because no household storage rule sits behind them waiting to be exempted from.

The penalty provision points in the same direction. Section 924(p) of title 18 supplies the penalty for a violation of the device requirement, and that penalty runs against the licensee. Enforcement follows the duty. If the federal obligation were a homeowner's storage obligation, the penalty structure would be expected to reach the homeowner whose storage fell short of a federal standard. The cited penalty instead reaches the licensed actor who failed to provide the device at the covered transfer. The alignment between the duty holder in section 922(z)(1) and the penalty target in section 924(p) leaves little room for the claim's relocation of the obligation into the home.

The wider statute reaches the identical result from the opposite direction. No provision of chapter 44 of title 18 sets a federal storage standard for a firearm held in a home. Chapter 44 contains the device provision, the exceptions, and the penalty against the licensee, and chapter 44 contains no companion provision instructing an owner how a firearm must be stored in a residence. The presence of the first statement explains why the claim sounds plausible to a reader who has encountered a device handed over at a purchase. The absence of the second explains why the claim, stated as a federal storage mandate, cannot stand in full.

Storage mandates belong to a different body of law. Where storage duties exist, those duties are state law. Several states impose storage duties, typically keyed to access by minors, and that generic description is the accurate level of statement for this examination. A state storage duty and the federal device provision can coexist, and a resident of a state with such a duty may face a genuine legal obligation about storage at home. That obligation derives from the state enactment and not from section 922(z)(1). Attributing a state duty to federal law misidentifies the legislature that acted and the text that supplies the standard, the exceptions, and the penalty.

The claim therefore divides into a supported part and an unsupported part, and the division is clean. The supported part is that federal law, through section 922(z)(1), added by section 5 of Public Law 109-92 and effective April 24, 2006, required a licensed importer, manufacturer, or dealer to provide a secure gun storage or safety device, as defined at section 921(a)(34), when selling, delivering, or transferring a handgun to a nonlicensee, subject to the exceptions at section 922(z)(2) and a penalty at section 924(p) running against the licensee. The unsupported part is that federal law requires safe storage of firearms in the home, requires a buyer to use the device supplied, or sets any federal manner of storage for a firearm held in a residence. Because a real federal requirement stands near the claim and the claim overstates that requirement into a different kind of mandate, a verdict of False would erase the device provision and a verdict of True would invent the storage mandate. The verdict is Partly true.


### Federal law lets unlicensed sellers skip checks at gun shows

The claim in its strongest form holds that a gun show is a place apart in federal law, a venue where the ordinary rules fall away and where unlicensed sellers may transfer firearms without the background check that a sale elsewhere would require. Holders of the claim state the point in a customary short form: federal law has a gun show loophole. That short form packs two assertions into a single phrase. The first assertion is descriptive, pointing to transfers that occur without a federal check and that a buyer and a seller can arrange at a show. The second assertion is legal, attributing that result to a show-specific feature of federal law. The examination separates those two assertions, because the record supports a version of the first and contradicts the second.

The legal assertion fails at the level of the statute's structure. No provision of federal law treats a gun show differently from any other setting. Federal transfer duties do not switch on or off according to venue, and the statute contains no show rule that suspends a duty inside an exhibition hall and restores that duty outside the hall. The absence is complete rather than partial. A reader searching chapter 44 of title 18 for a special gun show exemption finds no such provision, and a reader searching for a special gun show burden finds none either. The show enters federal law through the rules governing where a licensee may conduct business, and those rules treat the show as an extension of the licensed premises rather than as an escape from the licensed premises.

Section 923(j) of title 18 and section 478.100 of title 27 of the Code of Federal Regulations supply that treatment. A licensee may conduct business temporarily at a gun show or event in the state specified on the license. The show location is considered part of the licensed premises. That classification carries the full set of obligations that attach at the licensee's regular place of business. The licensee must post the license at the show. The licensee must keep the same records required at the licensed premises, under section 478.100(c) of title 27. The licensee must also run the same background check required by section 478.102 of title 27 before transferring a firearm to a nonlicensee. A licensee at a show therefore operates under identical federal duties as a licensee at a store, and the claim's picture of a licensed counter at a show operating free of the check has no statutory basis.

The identity of the seller, rather than the character of the venue, decides whether a federal check duty attaches. Where the seller is a licensee, the check duty applies, and the show setting changes nothing. Where both parties are nonlicensees residing in the same state, federal law imposes no license requirement, no record requirement, and no check requirement on the transfer. That space exists at a show floor, and that space also exists in a home, at a flea market, and in a transfer arranged through an online listing between two private persons in the same state. The accurate description of the phenomenon is therefore the private transfer space, a space defined by licensed status and by the state line. Venue does no legal work in the definition, and an account built around venue misdescribes the boundary that federal law actually draws.

That boundary is a consequence of the design of the federal scheme, under which transfer duties attach to licensees and to interstate movement. A transfer between two nonlicensees in the same state sits outside both triggers. Such a transfer can occur without a federal check at a gun show for the same reason that the same transfer can occur without a federal check in a private residence. The setting supplies an occasion for buyers and sellers to meet, and a show plainly gathers many potential parties in one place. An occasion, however, is not an exemption. Federal law did not carve the show out of a general check duty that otherwise covered private transfers, because federal law imposed no general check duty on private transfers between residents of the same state in any setting.

One federal transfer rule reaches every transferor, licensed or unlicensed, and that rule qualifies any description of the private transfer space as unregulated in federal law. Section 922(d) of title 18 provides that no person may sell or otherwise dispose of a firearm to a person the transferor knows or has reasonable cause to believe falls within a prohibited category. That prohibition applies to a private seller at a show, to a private seller in a home, and to a licensee at a licensed premises alike. The rule is substantive rather than procedural. Federal law forbids the transfer to a person known or reasonably believed to be prohibited, and federal law gives a private transferor no instrument for verifying the recipient's status. The check system is open to licensees for licensee transfers. A private seller therefore carries a federal prohibition without a federal verification mechanism, a combination that distinguishes the private transfer space from a zone of federal indifference.

State law overlays that space, and the overlay is substantial enough that a federal description alone leaves the legal picture incomplete. By the counts delivered in the sibling treatment of the check system, twenty states and the District of Columbia required checks for transfers of at least some firearms between private parties, by the count of the Giffords Law Center. A second measure reached a higher figure. Twenty-two states and the District of Columbia fell within the February 2021 measure of the Center for American Progress, a measure that includes permit-to-purchase systems reaching private sales. The two counts differ because the methodologies differ, and neither count is adopted here as the vocabulary of the examination. Stated together, the counts show that a private transfer at a show may be unchecked under federal law and checked under the law of the state where the transfer occurs.

The divergence between the two counts also illustrates why the venue framing distorts policy description. A state check requirement for private transfers applies because the state legislature regulated private transfers, and such a requirement reaches a show transfer for the same reason that the requirement reaches a transfer in a driveway or a kitchen. Closing or narrowing the private transfer space is a legislative act directed at transfers between nonlicensees. Describing that act as a show measure would misstate its reach, just as describing federal law through the show misstates federal reach. The legal unit in both bodies of law is the transfer and the status of the parties. The floor of the show is a location where transfers occur, not the legal unit that any of the cited provisions regulate.

Attribution confirms the origin of the customary word while also marking that word as a holder's term rather than a statutory term. Handgun Control, Inc. and the Brady Campaign to Prevent Gun Violence described the private transfer space with the word "loophole." That attributed use is recorded in the framework treatment of the federal scheme. The organizations named there chose a word that implies a defect in drafting, a gap between a law's evident design and a law's operative text. The implication is contestable on the record assembled here, because the private transfer space follows from where the federal scheme placed its duties in the first place, on licensees and on interstate movement. Whether that placement was wise is a legislative question. Whether that placement exists is a textual question, and the text answers the textual question without reference to a show.

The strongest residue of the claim survives that correction. A person attending a gun show can acquire a firearm from a private seller residing in the same state without a federal background check, in a state that has not imposed a check requirement on that private transfer. That transaction is real, and a reader who denied that transaction could occur would understate the reach of private transfers as much as a holder of the claim overstates the role of the venue. The residue also extends beyond the show. The same acquisition can occur away from any show, between the same categories of parties, under the same federal rule. Federal gun law myths in this area propagate by taking a genuine transaction, locating that transaction in its most visible setting, and then treating the setting as the cause. The correction relocates the cause to the status of the seller and the residence of the parties.

The claim therefore divides along the same line as the storage claim, with the direction of error reversed. The storage claim overstated a federal duty by converting a device provided at a transfer into a household mandate. The show claim understates a federal duty in one respect, by suggesting that licensees at a show operate without the check and records duties that sections 923(j), 478.100, and 478.102 of the cited authorities impose on licensees at a show exactly as at licensed premises. The show claim also overstates a federal gap in another respect, by presenting a general private transfer space as a show-specific defect. Both errors sit inside a claim that points to a genuine phenomenon, a private in-state transfer without a federal check. A verdict of False would deny that phenomenon. A verdict of True would endorse the venue theory that the statute refutes. The verdict is Partly true.

## The Two Taxed Instruments

### Suppressors are illegal to own

The assertion in its strongest form holds that a suppressor sits outside lawful private ownership altogether, that federal law treats the device as contraband in private hands, and that no application, payment, or registration can cure the defect because the underlying possession is itself prohibited. On that reading, the device belongs in the same legal category as an article that Congress removed from private commerce entirely, and a person who acquired one through any seller, however careful the paperwork, would hold an object that the law forbids private persons to hold at all. The statement gains force from the genuine severity of the penalties that surround the device, from the unfamiliar federal procedure that governs a lawful transfer, and from the fact that a minority of states do prohibit private ownership within their own borders. Taken together, those elements make a prohibition sound plausible to a reader who encounters the penalties and the procedure without encountering the channel that the procedure was built to serve.

The statutory text answers the assertion at the level of definition before any question of prohibition arises. Federal law does not use the popular name for the device as its governing term. The statute's term is firearm silencer or firearm muffler, defined at 26 U.S.C. 5845(a)(7) in the National Firearms Act and at 18 U.S.C. 921(a)(24) in the Gun Control Act, and a silencer is also a firearm for chapter 44 purposes under 18 U.S.C. 921(a)(3). That double placement matters because it locates the device inside two overlapping regulatory structures rather than outside lawful commerce. A prohibited article would not require an elaborate transfer definition, a registration entry, and a tax provision calibrated to a lawful change of ownership. Congress instead wrote the silencer into the definitional sections that support registration and taxed transfer, and the structure of the provisions presupposes that ownership changes hands through a recorded federal process.

Suppressors have sat inside the National Firearms Act registration and tax regime since 1934, and the regime that governs a transfer has four elements that recur in every lawful transaction. The first element is registration in the National Firearms Registration and Transfer Record, the federal registry in which each covered device is recorded by its identifying particulars and its registered owner. The second element is the transfer tax of $200 imposed by 26 U.S.C. 5811, a fixed sum attached to the transfer itself rather than to the price of the device. The third element is advance approval under 26 U.S.C. 5812, under which the transfer application must be approved before the transfer occurs, so that the sequence runs from application to approval to delivery rather than from purchase to later notification. The fourth element is the penalty structure at 26 U.S.C. 5861, under which possession of a covered device that is not registered to the possessor in the federal record is a felony. Each element points in the same direction. Registration supplies the record, the tax supplies the fiscal marker of a lawful transfer, advance approval supplies the federal decision point, and the felony provision supplies the sanction for stepping outside the recorded channel rather than for ownership as such.

### Can a private buyer lawfully acquire a suppressor through the federal channel?

A private buyer can lawfully acquire a suppressor where state law permits private ownership, by purchasing through the federal registration channel, submitting the transfer application, paying the statutory tax, and receiving approval before taking possession, with the completed transfer recorded in the federal registry maintained under the National Firearms Act.

The distinction between a burdened channel and a closed channel decides the claim. A closed channel admits no lawful private transaction at any price and under any procedure, and federal law contains examples of closure elsewhere in the same statutory neighborhood, where a cutoff date freezes the stock that may circulate among private persons. The suppressor channel bears no such cutoff. New suppressors may be manufactured and registered, so the supply of devices available for lawful private transfer is not limited to a fixed set recorded before a date in the past. A manufacturer may produce a new device, the device may enter the federal registry, and a qualified buyer in a state that permits private ownership may acquire that device through the application, tax, and approval sequence. The passage of time therefore does not shrink the lawful stock by operation of the federal statute, and the federal scheme contemplates continuing manufacture for the private market alongside continuing registration of each new unit.

State law forms the second layer of the answer, and the state count must be stated with its source and its date because state permissions changed across the decades after 1934 and a count without a date describes no verifiable moment. The American Suppressor Association's count published in January 2017 recorded suppressors as lawful for private ownership in 42 states and lawful for use in hunting in 40 states. Those two figures carry different meanings and the difference reinforces the central point. Ownership in 42 states establishes that private possession under the federal channel extended across the great majority of the country at the date of the count. Lawful hunting use in 40 states establishes a slightly narrower permission, because a state may allow a resident to own a registered device while withholding permission to carry that device in the field for hunting. The gap between the two figures is a gap between ownership and a particular use, not a gap between legality and illegality in ownership itself. In the remaining minority of states, state prohibition rather than federal prohibition supplies the bar, and that bar operates state by state rather than as a national rule.

The penalty provision deserves separate attention because the severity of 26 U.S.C. 5861 is often treated as evidence that the underlying object is banned. The inference reverses the function of the provision. A felony penalty for unregistered possession presupposes a registered form of possession that the law recognizes and protects, because the offense is defined by the absence of registration rather than by possession alone. The registered owner who acquired a device through an approved transfer, paid the tax imposed by 5811, and held the device within the terms of the registration commits no offense under that provision by holding the device. The unregistered possessor faces felony liability precisely because a lawful route existed and was bypassed. Penalty severity therefore measures the importance Congress attached to the registry, and a severe penalty attached to an unrecorded transfer cannot be converted into evidence that every transfer is forbidden. The same logic governs other taxed and registered articles under the same chapter, where the sanction falls on the failure to register, to pay, or to obtain approval rather than on the category of article in the abstract.

The procedure also explains why the assertion persists in public discussion despite the openness of the channel in law. A suppressor purchase does not resemble an ordinary retail purchase of an unregulated accessory, because the device is a firearm under chapter 44 and a National Firearms Act article under chapter 53 of title 26. The buyer encounters federal forms, a tax payment, a waiting period while the application is considered, and a delivery that follows approval rather than preceding it. A transaction structured in that sequence can feel, to a participant or an observer, like an exceptional indulgence granted against a background of prohibition. The feeling misreads the sequence. Advance approval is a condition of lawful transfer within a standing system that processes such transfers as a routine category, and the registry exists to record the resulting ownership on a continuing basis. Burden, delay, and expense are real features of the channel, and none of those features converts a regulated permission into a prohibition without a statutory provision that actually closes the channel to private persons.

Measured against the text, the assertion fails on each of its necessary elements. Federal law defines the device under a statutory name, places that device inside a registration and tax regime dating to 1934, provides a transfer tax and an advance approval mechanism that together constitute a working channel for private acquisition, and punishes unregistered possession rather than possession under registration. State law at the American Suppressor Association's January 2017 count permitted private ownership in 42 states and hunting use in 40 states, leaving prohibition as a minority state position rather than a federal rule. New manufacture for registration keeps the channel supplied, which separates the suppressor from articles whose private stock a later statute froze. The accurate description is a heavily conditioned permission administered through registration, taxation, and prior approval, and a heavily conditioned permission is the opposite of illegality for the registered owner who satisfies the conditions.

The verdict is False.

### Machine guns can be bought like any other firearm

The assertion in its strongest form holds that a machine gun is available through ordinary commerce on ordinary terms, that a buyer with the purchase price may select a newly made example from ordinary stock, complete the transaction in the manner of any other firearm purchase, and take possession without confronting a distinct federal regime or a closed supply. On that reading, the special statutory history surrounding the weapon amounts to background detail rather than operative law, and differences between a machine gun transaction and any other firearm transaction are differences of price or scarcity alone. The statement draws strength from a genuine underlying fact, namely that a class of machine guns does circulate lawfully among private buyers and changes hands for consideration under federal approval. From that observed circulation, the assertion leaps to freedom of purchase, and the distance between lawful circulation within a fixed stock and purchase on ordinary terms is exactly the distance the statutory text measures.

Machine guns sit inside the National Firearms Act of 1934 regime, and the first layer of that regime resembles the layer governing suppressors while the second layer departs from it decisively. The definition appears at 26 U.S.C. 5845(b). A covered weapon is registered in the National Firearms Registration and Transfer Record, a transfer bears the $200 tax imposed by 26 U.S.C. 5811, and approval must precede transfer under 26 U.S.C. 5812. A buyer therefore never faced, even before 1986, a transaction identical to the purchase of an ordinary firearm from a licensee, because registration, taxation, and advance federal approval stood between payment and possession in every lawful case. The pre-1986 system was burdensome in the same structural sense as the suppressor system, with the registry as its center and felony penalties at its edge. What changed in 1986 was not the existence of the regime but the supply of weapons that the regime would allow into private hands, and that change converted a burdened channel into a channel fed by a fixed and diminishing stock.

The operative provision is 18 U.S.C. 922(o), added by the Hughes Amendment to the Firearm Owners' Protection Act of 1986, Public Law 99-308, signed May 19, 1986. The provision makes transfer or possession of a machine gun unlawful except for two classes. The first class covers transfers to or by, or possession by or under the authority of, the United States or a state or their agencies and subdivisions. The second class covers a machine gun lawfully possessed before May 19, 1986. No third class exists for a private buyer seeking a weapon manufactured after the cutoff, and no provision in 922(o) authorizes a licensee, a manufacturer, or a federal official to enlarge the private stock by registering a newly made weapon for private transfer. The exception language is therefore exhaustive in effect. Government possession proceeds under government authority, private possession proceeds only where lawful possession predated the signing date, and every private transaction after that date must locate its object inside the set that the second exception preserves.

The consequence follows step by step when the provision is applied to a sequence of manufacture and sale. A manufacturer may produce machine guns after May 19, 1986 for government use, because the government class in 922(o) accommodates transfers to and possession by public agencies. Such a weapon, however, cannot pass from government channels into private ownership through an ordinary sale, because the private exception reaches only a weapon lawfully possessed before the cutoff and a post-cutoff weapon fails that test by definition. A private buyer who orders a newly manufactured example therefore orders an object that federal law will not register for private transfer, regardless of willingness to pay the tax or submit the application. The transferable stock is the fixed set registered and lawfully possessed before the cutoff. The set can shrink through destruction, seizure, or deterioration, because each loss removes a unit that no new manufacture may replace for private purposes. The set cannot be enlarged by new manufacture for private transfer. Scarcity in this market is therefore a statutory artifact with a date and a mechanism, not a product of fashion, production cost, or collector preference alone.

The size of the preserved set is a matter of federal record, and the count must carry its attribution and its caveat because the registry is an administrative record assembled over decades rather than a census taken at a single moment. In a February 24, 2016 letter responding to a records request, the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives reported that the registry held 175,977 transferable pre-1986 machine guns, alongside 297,667 restricted machine guns in the post-1986 government and dealer categories and 17,020 sales samples. The Bureau noted that the aggregate figures carried an inherent margin of error from the query method. The three categories together demonstrate the structure that 922(o) created. The restricted category, larger than the transferable category in the 2016 count, consists of weapons that exist in the country and in the registry without being available to private buyers at all. Physical existence therefore proves nothing about private availability, and the only figure that describes the private market is the 175,977 transferable weapons counted in that letter, subject to the margin of error the Bureau attached to the aggregate figures.

What a lawful purchase actually requires begins with the search rather than with the payment. A buyer must find an item from the fixed stock, meaning a specific registered weapon lawfully possessed before May 19, 1986 and eligible for transfer to a private person. The buyer must then file the transfer application with fingerprints and photographs, pay the $200 tax, and wait for approval before the transfer may occur. Delivery follows the federal decision rather than the agreement of the parties, and a seller and buyer who complete payment and handover without the required approval have not completed a lawful purchase however complete their private bargain appears. Unregistered possession is a federal felony under 26 U.S.C. 5861(d), and possession outside the exceptions in 18 U.S.C. 922(o) carries its own unlawfulness under that provision. Every stage of the lawful route contradicts purchase on ordinary terms. The stock is closed, the application is personal and documented, the tax is statutory, the approval is antecedent, and the sanction for bypassing the sequence is felony liability rather than a civil defect in the sale.

Comparison with the suppressor channel sharpens the distinction that the pairing of the two taxed instruments is built to expose. Both articles entered the National Firearms Act regime in 1934, both bear the same $200 transfer tax, both move only after advance approval, and both expose an unregistered possessor to felony penalties under 26 U.S.C. 5861. Federal treatment diverges at the point of supply. A suppressor manufactured in a later year may be registered and transferred to a private buyer through the standing channel, while a machine gun manufactured after May 19, 1986 may not enter private hands under any application. One market is burdened and open, fed by continuing manufacture. The other market is burdened and closed, confined to a registry count that the Bureau's February 24, 2016 letter placed at 175,977 transferable weapons with an acknowledged margin of error. A description that treats the second market as ordinary commerce erases the cutoff, the exceptions that define it, and the fixed stock that the cutoff produced, and each of those elements is a matter of enacted text rather than interpretation.

The government exception also fails to soften the provision for a private buyer, because the exception runs to institutions rather than to persons acting in a private capacity. A transfer to the United States or to a state, or to an agency or subdivision of either, serves a public armory, and possession by or under the authority of such a body remains public possession throughout the transaction. A private collector, a private dealer acting for a private customer, and a private seller each stand outside that class, and none of those parties can borrow government authority to validate a transfer that the second exception does not reach. For private commerce the statute therefore leaves a single door, and the door opens only onto a weapon whose lawful possession began before the signing date of May 19, 1986. A transaction that cannot identify such a weapon at its outset has no path to approval, however complete the fingerprints, photographs, tax payment, and application papers may be, because the defect lies in the object offered for transfer rather than in the documentation assembled around that object.

The fixed character of the stock also shapes every later transaction in a way that ordinary firearm commerce never reproduces. Each lawful transfer moves a unit within the preserved set from one registered owner to another, and the registry entry changes while the size of the set remains untouched by the movement. A generation of transfers can therefore pass over the same unit without adding a single weapon to private availability, and a buyer entering the market competes for units recorded decades earlier under a statute that froze supply at the 1986 line. Loss operates in one direction only. A weapon destroyed in a fire, surrendered after a seizure, or degraded beyond repair leaves the transferable category permanently, and the Bureau's February 24, 2016 count of 175,977 transferable weapons, itself carrying the margin of error the Bureau acknowledged, describes a ceiling that later lawful activity could maintain or reduce but could never raise through new private manufacture.

The verdict is False.

## What Congress Ended and What Congress Preserved

### Congress repealed the assault weapons ban

The strongest form of the assertion is direct and familiar. Congress enacted a federal ban on specified semiautomatic weapons and on magazines of larger capacity, the ban governed manufacture, transfer, and possession for a decade, and then Congress repealed the ban in 2004, restoring the legal position that had existed before the ban took effect. In that form, the ending reads as a second legislative decision that reversed the first, at the moment the prohibitions ceased. The form gives the ending an author, a date, and an act, and the word repeal does appear in the governing statute itself. A reader who encountered that language without its timing could assume that a repeal provision was a repeal vote, and a reader who knew only that the prohibitions ended in September 2004 could assume that some action in 2004 had ended them. Each assumption fails against the enacted text and the legislative record of the year in which the prohibitions ended.

The ban was the Public Safety and Recreational Firearms Use Protection Act, enacted as Subtitle A of Title XI of the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994, Public Law 103-322, signed on September 13, 1994. The subtitle created the prohibitions that later sections of title 18 carried, including the definitions at former sections 921(a)(30) and 921(a)(31) of title 18 and the operative prohibitions at former sections 922(v) and 922(w) of title 18. Those provisions restricted specified semiautomatic assault weapons and large capacity ammunition feeding devices, subject to the exceptions and grandfathering terms that the subtitle itself supplied. The measure that reached the President in 1994 was therefore not an open ended prohibition awaiting a later decision about duration. From the first day of its existence, the subtitle carried its own ending inside its own text, and any account of the ending that begins in 2004 has started the story a decade late.

Section 110105(2) of Public Law 103-322 provided that the subtitle and the amendments made by the subtitle were repealed effective ten years after the effective date of the act. The effective date was September 13, 1994, and the arithmetic of the provision placed the termination on September 13, 2004. The termination was self-executing, drafted in the language of repeal. That pairing deserves separate emphasis because each half corrects a different misunderstanding. The termination was drafted in the language of repeal, so the word repeal in descriptions of the ending has a genuine textual source. The termination was also self-executing, so that word in the 1994 text required no further vote, no further signature, and no further instrument to carry it into effect a decade later. The repeal was legislated in 1994, to operate in 2004, and nothing in the operative provision left the question open for a future Congress.

### Did any chamber vote to end the ban in the year it ended?

No chamber voted to end the ban in 2004, and no chamber voted to extend it into law. A Senate amendment favoring extension passed one vote and then failed with the underlying bill. The ending occurred because the 1994 statute contained its own termination date and executed that instruction without further action.

The legislative record of 2004 confirms that reading at every point where a contrary reading would need support. Congress never voted to repeal the ban. No bill repealing the subtitle passed either chamber in that year, no repeal measure reached the President, and no enrolled text altered the date that section 110105(2) had fixed ten years earlier. Congress also never enacted an extension. The distinction between voting on a proposal and enacting a law controls the entire sequence, because the one chamber action toward extension that did occur illustrates exactly how far an adopted amendment can fall short of legal effect when the vehicle carrying the amendment fails. An account that treats any expression of preference by a chamber as a decision of Congress collapses that distinction, and the collapse is where the repeal assertion gathers the plausibility that the record denies to the assertion in full form.

On March 2, 2004, the Senate took up S. 1805, a bill concerning protection of lawful commerce in arms, and adopted an amendment offered by Senator Feinstein and co-sponsored by Senator Warner that would have extended the ban. The amendment was adopted by a vote of 52 to 47. The Senate then rejected the amended bill itself. Because the underlying bill failed, the amendment adopted into that bill had no legal effect. No statute extended the subtitle, no new termination date replaced the date in section 110105(2), and the legal position on the morning after the vote was identical to the legal position on the morning before the vote. The sequence is instructive precisely because a majority of senators voting on the amendment favored extension, and extension nevertheless did not occur. A vote for an amendment to a bill that a chamber then rejects is a recorded preference inside a failed vehicle, not an enactment, and federal law changes through enactment alone.

The House of Representatives held no extension vote before the date arrived. No House action supplied the missing half of a bicameral passage, and no conference process produced an enrolled extension bill. President George W. Bush had stated a willingness to sign an extension if Congress passed one, and that statement changed nothing without a passed bill, because legal effect attaches to a bill that both chambers have passed and that the President has signed. No bill reached the President. When September 13, 2004 arrived, the prohibitions at former sections 921(a)(30), 921(a)(31), 922(v), and 922(w) of title 18 ended by operation of the prewritten term, because the statute enacted in 1994 instructed that result and no later enactment instructed any other result. The fuller account in [the profile of the 1994 federal assault weapons ban](/2021/05/01/federal-assault-weapons-ban-1994/) traces the subtitle from enactment through that termination date.

Precision about mechanism matters because the compressed version misassigns responsibility and misdescribes how federal statutes end. Describing the ending as a congressional repeal compresses two events into a decision no chamber took in 2004. The first event was a legislative choice made in 1994, when Congress enacted a prohibition with a built in expiration expressed as a deferred repeal. The second event was the arrival of a calendar date in 2004, when a provision already on the books executed an instruction already given. Between those events lay a decade in which Congress could have extended the prohibitions by ordinary legislation and did not enact an extension, and could have accelerated the ending by ordinary legislation and did not enact a repeal. Inaction across that decade had legal consequences because the default written into the statute favored termination, yet inaction is not a vote, and a consequence produced by a default rule is not a decision taken at the moment the consequence matures.

The counter-reading carries real weight, and the weight belongs in the account rather than in a concession appended to the account. The prohibitions did end in 2004. Manufacture, transfer, and possession that the subtitle had restricted ceased to be restricted by those provisions on September 13, 2004, and the practical difference between a ban repealed by a fresh vote on that date and a ban terminated by a prewritten provision on that date was nil for a person whose conduct the subtitle had governed. A manufacturer, a seller, or an owner experienced the ending as an ending, whatever instrument produced the result. The error in the assertion is in the mechanism, not in the fact of the ending. A corrected account therefore concedes the outcome in full and disputes only the attribution, holding that Congress in 1994 chose a temporary prohibition and that Congress in 2004 neither renewed that prohibition nor voted to end the prohibition early.

That distinction also explains why the assertion has persisted in public discussion of federal gun law myths. Sunset provisions are comparatively rare in major criminal statutes, and public vocabulary for statutory endings is dominated by repeal, a word that ordinarily signals a fresh legislative act. Where a statute carries an ordinary repeal, a later Congress has weighed the earlier policy and displaced the earlier policy, and the political meaning lies in that later weighing. Where a statute carries a self-executing termination, the political meaning lies earlier, in the compromise that made a temporary measure passable when a permanent measure might not have passed at all. The ten year term was part of the price of enactment in 1994, and the events of 2004 tested whether a different coalition could assemble an extension under ordinary rules. The Senate sequence showed a majority for an amendment and no enacted law, the House showed no vote, and the presidency showed a stated willingness without a bill. The prewritten term governed the outcome, and the term governed.

A careful statement of the ending therefore requires three clauses held together. The ban ended on September 13, 2004. The ending occurred because section 110105(2) of Public Law 103-322 had provided in 1994 that the subtitle and its amendments stood repealed effective ten years after enactment. No vote in 2004 repealed the ban, and no enactment in 2004 extended the ban. Any shorter statement that assigns the ending to a congressional repeal in 2004 sacrifices the second and third clauses to the first, and the sacrifice converts a story about legislative drafting into a story about a legislative decision that the journals of both chambers fail to record. The verdict is False.

### The firearms industry cannot be sued

The strongest form of the second assertion is equally direct. After Congress enacted federal protection for lawful commerce in arms, manufacturers and sellers of firearms stood beyond the reach of civil lawsuits, so that a person harmed through the criminal misuse of a firearm had no civil remedy against the enterprise that made or sold the weapon, whatever the conduct of that enterprise had been. In that form, the protection reads as blanket immunity, closing the courthouse to an entire class of plaintiffs against an entire industry. The form draws support from a real statute with a real dismissal command and from the dismissal of cases pending when the statute took effect. Each element of support is genuine, and the conclusion exceeds the text that supplies the elements, because the statute defines the barred category by exclusion as carefully as by inclusion.

The statute is the Protection of Lawful Commerce in Arms Act, Public Law 109-92, signed on October 26, 2005, and codified at sections 7901 to 7903 of title 15. Section 7902(a) provides that a qualified civil liability action may not be brought in any federal or state court, and section 7902(b) directed immediate dismissal of actions within the category pending at enactment. The bar is mandatory in form and broad in forum, covering state courts as fully as federal courts and pending actions as fully as actions filed afterward. Any accurate account grants that force in full. The disputed question is the boundary of the category, and Congress located the boundary inside the statutory definition.

Section 7903(5)(A) of title 15 defines a qualified civil liability action as a civil action or proceeding, or an administrative proceeding, brought by any person, including a governmental entity, against a manufacturer or seller of a qualified product, or against a trade association, for damages, punitive damages, injunctive relief, declaratory relief, abatement, restitution, fines, penalties, or other relief, resulting from the criminal or unlawful misuse of the qualified product by the person or by a third party. Every element narrows the bar. An action that fails any element sits outside the definition before any exclusion is reached, and an action that satisfies every element may nevertheless sit outside the definition through the exclusions that the same paragraph supplies. Those exclusions are the portion of the statute that the blanket immunity assertion omits entirely.

Congress wrote six exclusions into the definition, and an action inside an exclusion is not a qualified civil liability action at all. The first exclusion, at clause (i) of section 7903(5)(A), covers an action against a transferor convicted under section 924(h) of title 18, which addresses knowing transfer to a person prohibited from possessing a firearm, or convicted under a comparable or identical state felony law, brought by a party directly harmed by the conduct underlying the conviction. The exclusion is narrow, tied to a completed conviction. The second exclusion, at clause (ii), covers an action against a seller for negligent entrustment or negligence per se. Section 7903(5)(B) defines negligent entrustment as supplying a qualified product when the seller knows, or reasonably should know, the recipient is likely to, and does, use the product in a manner involving unreasonable risk of physical injury. Seller conduct at the point of supply is the center of that exclusion.

The third exclusion, at clause (iii), is the predicate exception. Clause (iii) covers an action in which a manufacturer or seller knowingly violated a state or federal statute applicable to the sale or marketing of the product, and the violation was a proximate cause of the harm for which relief is sought. Clause (iii)(I) reaches false entries or statements in required records, or a false statement in a license application or another required submission. Clause (iii)(II) reaches a manufacturer or seller that aids, abets, or conspires to sell or deliver a product to a person known, or reasonably believed, to be prohibited from receiving or possessing a firearm under section 922(g) or section 922(n) of title 18. The prohibited person concept lives in clause (iii)(II), not in clause (i). Clause (i) turns on a transferor convicted under section 924(h), and clause (iii)(II) turns on aiding, abetting, or conspiring in a sale to a prohibited person, without any requirement of a prior conviction. Naming that conflation matters because the conflation is the most common error in descriptions of the statute, and the error reverses the practical content of both clauses.

The fourth exclusion, at clause (iv), covers an action for breach of contract or warranty in connection with the purchase of the product. Commercial obligations assumed by agreement therefore survived the act intact. The fifth exclusion, at clause (v), covers an action for death, physical injuries, or property damage resulting directly from a defect in design or manufacture of the product, when the product was used as intended or in a reasonably foreseeable manner, with the qualification that a volitional criminal act causing the discharge is treated as the sole proximate cause for that category. Defect litigation grounded in design or manufacture, and in a use that was intended or reasonably foreseeable, therefore sits outside the barred category. The sixth exclusion, at clause (vi), covers an action or proceeding commenced by the Attorney General to enforce chapter 44 of title 18 or chapter 53 of title 26.

Section 7903(5)(C) states the relationship between the exclusions and the rest of the law. The exclusions preserve claims supplied by other law, and the act creates no cause of action of its own. A plaintiff inside an exclusion must therefore ground the claim in a source outside the act, in a common law duty, a contract, a warranty, or a statute that the predicate exception makes actionable. A statute that defines a barred category and then preserves six channels of other law has regulated which claims proceed, under which theories, against which defendants, and the regulation is substantial without being total. The fuller treatment in [the guide to the Protection of Lawful Commerce in Arms Act](/2021/05/15/plcaa-2005-guide/) works through each exclusion against the statutory text in sequence.

Documented operation confirms that the seller conduct exclusions functioned as written rather than as decoration around a total bar. In the Badger Guns litigation, a Milwaukee County jury on October 13, 2015 returned a verdict for the plaintiffs of just over five million dollars in compensatory damages and punitive damages against a licensed dealer, on negligent entrustment and statutory violation theories arising from a straw sale. Described by posture only, the litigation presented a dealer defendant, theories that tracked clause (ii) and clause (iii), a trial, and a plaintiff verdict. A single verdict establishes no general rate of success for plaintiffs proceeding under the exclusions. The verdict establishes the narrower point that decides the assertion under examination, which is that an action inside the exclusions could be filed, tried, and succeed against a licensed dealer. Blanket immunity admits no such case, and the record contains such a case.

The counter-reading carries real weight, stated plainly. The bar is real and broad within its category. A plaintiff whose harm resulted from the criminal or unlawful misuse of a qualified product, whose theory fits no exclusion among the six, and whose defendant is a manufacturer, a seller, or a trade association has no route under the statute, in state court or in federal court, and section 7902(b) demonstrated the seriousness of that closure by directing dismissal of pending actions inside the category. Theories built on general negligence, public nuisance, or marketing practices unconnected to a statute applicable to sale or marketing faced the knowing violation and proximate cause requirements that clause (iii) imposes. That breadth is the accurate core that the assertion exaggerates into totality. Suits were genuinely hard to bring past the bar, and entire categories of theory were closed.

Exaggeration into totality nevertheless changes the litigation strategy and the public understanding that follow from the statute. A dealer that supplies a firearm to a buyer the dealer knows, or reasonably should know, is likely to use the firearm in a manner involving unreasonable risk faces the negligent entrustment exclusion. A seller that knowingly violates a statute applicable to the sale or marketing of the product, where the violation proximately causes harm, faces the predicate exception. A manufacturer that ships a product with a defect in design or manufacture faces the defect exclusion where the product was used as intended or in a reasonably foreseeable manner. A buyer faces no bar on contract or warranty claims at all. Each exposure turns on conduct or on product condition rather than on the criminal act of a third party alone. The statute withdrew civil liability where responsibility rested solely on intervening criminal misuse by another person, and the assertion under examination erases that line.

A careful statement of the protection holds the bar and the exclusions together. Section 7902(a) of title 15 bars a qualified civil liability action in any federal or state court. Section 7903(5)(A) defines that category and excludes six classes of action, covering a convicted transferor, negligent entrustment and negligence per se, a knowing violation of an applicable statute with proximate cause, breach of contract or warranty, defect in design or manufacture under intended or reasonably foreseeable use, and enforcement by the Attorney General. Section 7903(5)(C) preserves the claims that other law supplies inside those exclusions and creates none. A statement that the industry cannot be sued discards the definition, the exclusions, and the distinction between a barred category and a barred defendant, and the discarded material determines whether any given action proceeds. The verdict is False.

## What the Court Preserved and What the System Pursues

### The Supreme Court struck down gun regulation

The assertion in the strongest form holds that the Supreme Court, in the leading Second Amendment decision, swept firearms regulation aside as a general matter. On that reading, the decision did far more than resolve a dispute over two District of Columbia provisions. The assertion treats the judgment as a broad act of judicial deregulation, one that removed regulation across the field outright. The question under examination concerns the distance between that holding and the general invalidation the assertion describes. Measured against the text of the opinion, that distance is wide, and the opinion itself supplies the language that closes most of the distance in the opposite direction. The holding and the qualifications sit in the same document, and a faithful account carries both parts together rather than separating the result from the limits the Court placed around the result.

District of Columbia v. Heller, 554 U.S. 570 (2008), was decided by a divided Court, five to four, in an opinion by Justice Scalia. The Court held that the Second Amendment protects an individual right to possess a firearm unconnected with militia service, for traditionally lawful purposes, including self-defense in the home. That holding answered a threshold question about the character of the right. The operative judgment then addressed the District provisions before the Court. The District handgun prohibition fell, and the District trigger-lock requirement fell as applied in the posture presented. Those two invalidations gave the decision a concrete deregulatory effect within a defined field of action. Precision about the provisions that fell matters because the assertion converts a judgment against identified District law into a judgment against regulation in general, and the conversion adds a scope that the judgment itself never carried on the terms the Court stated.

The same opinion states, in terms that leave little room for a general reading, that the right is "not unlimited" (554 U.S. at 626). Placement carries weight in the assessment. The qualification does not appear in a later gloss, a separate writing, or a subsequent retreat from a broader holding. The qualification appears in Heller itself, as part of the majority exposition of the right the Court recognized. A reader who accepts the individual-right holding on the authority of the opinion encounters the limit on the same authority and in the same reasoning. The opinion therefore performs two moves at once. The first move identifies a protected individual right detached from militia service. The second move denies that recognition of the right settles every regulatory question in favor of invalidation. The assertion under examination captures the first move and discards the second, even though the Court joined the two moves together.

The passage at pages 626 to 627 of the opinion gives the qualification concrete content. The Court stated that nothing in the opinion should be taken to cast doubt on longstanding prohibitions on the possession of firearms by felons and the mentally ill. The Court stated the same protection for laws forbidding the carrying of firearms in sensitive places such as schools and government buildings, and for laws imposing conditions and qualifications on the commercial sale of arms. Each category corresponds to a large and familiar body of regulation. Possession prohibitions tied to felony status and mental illness reach the eligibility rules at the center of federal firearms law. Sensitive-place prohibitions reach the location rules that govern carrying in civic and educational settings. Conditions and qualifications on commercial sale reach the licensed trade through which most lawful acquisitions pass. An opinion that preserves those categories in express terms has not invalidated firearms regulation generally, whatever the force of the opinion on the distinct provisions that the opinion struck down.

Placement of Heller within the line of Second Amendment decisions sharpens the same point, and [the account of Second Amendment cases before the Supreme Court](/2021/07/01/second-amendment-supreme-court-cases/) situates the 2008 decision among the rulings that extended and bounded the right in defined steps. McDonald v. City of Chicago, 561 U.S. 742 (2010), applied the right against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. That step concerned the governments bound by the right, extending the Heller rule from a federal district to state and local governments, without enlarging the catalogue of invalidated measures beyond the incorporation question presented. Caetano v. Massachusetts, 577 U.S. 411 (2016), a per curiam decision, confirmed that the right is not limited to arms in existence at the founding, rejecting a founding-era freeze on protected arms. Each ruling in the sequence resolved a bounded question, and neither ruling converted Heller into a general invalidation of regulation.

### What did the Heller opinion expressly preserve?

The opinion preserved the categories named as presumptively lawful in the passage at pages 626 to 627. The list covered longstanding prohibitions on possession by felons and the mentally ill, laws forbidding carrying in sensitive places such as schools and government buildings, and laws imposing conditions and qualifications on the commercial sale of arms.

Examination of the preserved categories repays close attention because the opinion attached a specific label to the measures named. The Court called the listed measures presumptively lawful. Presumption language performs distinct work in a constitutional opinion. A presumptively lawful measure enters later litigation with the opinion itself supplying a reason to sustain the measure, rather than a reason to suspect the measure. The label also signals that the Court regarded the listed categories as compatible with the right, grounded in a longstanding regulatory tradition that the right never displaced. The Court added a second signal by stating that the list does not purport to be exhaustive. A nonexhaustive list leaves room for other measures to claim a comparable footing through comparable reasoning about tradition and duration, and the room left open runs in the direction of preservation rather than invalidation. The opinion therefore declined to treat the categories named as the outer boundary of regulation compatible with the right.

The opinion also recorded support in the historical tradition for prohibiting the carrying of dangerous and unusual weapons. That passage matters because the right recognized in Heller was defined through historical inquiry, and the tradition the Court consulted supplied limits as well as protection. A tradition that supports prohibition of a class of weapons defined by danger and unusual character gives later courts a historically grounded basis for sustaining at least some weapons restrictions under the right itself, without treating each restriction as an external exception to an otherwise boundless guarantee. Taken together, the preserved categories, the presumption attached to the categories, the refusal to close the list, and the dangerous and unusual weapons tradition form a coherent internal structure describing a right with edges supplied by longstanding practice. The assertion under examination describes a right without those edges, and the description fails against the document invoked as authority for the assertion.

The decade of lower-court litigation that followed Heller supplies an empirical check on the textual reading, and the check points the same way. Federal courts of appeals sustained the great majority of the regulations challenged under Heller during that decade, applying the two-step framework every circuit adopted. The pattern of outcomes is difficult to reconcile with a decision that invalidated regulation generally. A general invalidation, carried into the lower courts as the governing rule, would have produced widespread invalidation across categories and jurisdictions. The record instead shows sustained regulation as the dominant outcome under a shared framework, with courts working through challenges measure by measure. The two-step framework operated as the operative description of lower-court practice at the vantage of August 2021, and within that practice Heller functioned as a source of specific protection and specific preservation at the same time.

Consideration of each preserved category in turn shows how much regulatory ground a general invalidation would have had to clear, and how directly the opinion refused to clear that ground. Possession prohibitions for felons and for the mentally ill embody a judgment that some persons stand outside lawful possession by reason of status, a judgment embedded in federal law long before 2008 and restated by the Court without doubt cast upon the judgment. Sensitive-place prohibitions embody a judgment that some locations call for exclusion of carried firearms even for persons otherwise entitled to possess arms, and the examples the Court gave, schools and government buildings, cover institutions at the center of public life. Conditions and qualifications on commercial sale embody a judgment that the market in arms may be channeled through regulated transactions, with eligibility and process requirements attached to lawful commerce. A decision preserving status-based possession bars, place-based carrying bars, and transaction-based sale conditions has preserved the architecture through which firearms regulation operates in ordinary practice.

The distinction between recognizing a right and invalidating a body of law resolves the remaining force of the assertion. Recognition of an individual right changes the constitutional posture of regulation by making regulation answerable to the right, and Heller plainly accomplished that change for the District provisions and for comparable prohibitions on protected possession in the home. Answerability, however, is not invalidation. A measure answerable to a right may satisfy the right, fall within a category the defining opinion called presumptively lawful, or rest on a tradition the defining opinion treated as supportive. Heller supplied each of those routes in the text of the decision itself, and the lower-court record in the decade after 2008 shows courts taking those routes in the great majority of challenges. What the decision invalidated was the District of Columbia provisions before the Court, the handgun prohibition and the trigger-lock requirement as applied. Generalization from those provisions to regulation as a whole adds a proposition the opinion contradicted in express language at pages 626 to 627. The verdict is False.

### Nobody is prosecuted after a failed background check

The assertion in the strongest form holds that a person denied by a background check faces no prosecution at all, stated as an absolute. On that reading, a denial ends the matter in every case, the file closes, and the criminal law never follows a failed attempt to acquire a firearm through a licensee. The absolute word carries the whole assertion, because a single documented prosecution defeats the assertion as stated, and the documented record contains prosecutions at the federal level and convictions at the state level. At the same time, an honest assessment cannot stop at the defeat of the absolute. The assertion points toward a documented narrowing between denials and prosecutions so steep that the underlying observation about enforcement volume deserves direct treatment. Both halves belong in the assessment. Prosecutions occur, and prosecutions remain rare in relation to denials for reasons that the documenting report stated in the same passages as the figures. Exclusion of either half distorts the record, the first half by converting rarity into nonexistence, the second half by converting documented prosecutions into evidence of routine enforcement that the figures do not support.

Federal law supplies a defined offense for the conduct at the center of a prosecuted denial. A false statement to a licensee in connection with acquiring a firearm violates 18 U.S.C. 922(a)(6), and the penalty through 18 U.S.C. 924(a)(3) was imprisonment for up to ten years at the vantage of August 2021. A person within a prohibited category who attempts a purchase may also violate the possession provisions of 18 U.S.C. 922(g) on receipt, and the prosecuted attempt offense in the documented record is the false statement offense. The structure matters for the assessment because prosecution after a denial is not an improvised response without a statutory home. A buyer who lies on the transaction record about a disqualifying fact commits a defined federal offense at the moment of the statement, before any firearm changes hands, and the denial generated by the check supplies the setting in which the statement comes to official attention. The legal question in the documented pattern is therefore never whether an offense exists on the books. The legal question is how a defined offense, documented in a large pool of denials, moves or fails to move through referral and prosecution decisions in practice.

The Government Accountability Office report GAO-18-440, issued in June 2018 and covering fiscal 2017 federal transactions processed by the FBI only, recorded 8,606,286 federal transactions, 112,090 denials at about 1.3 percent of those transactions, 12,710 referrals by the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives to field divisions, and 12 prosecutions by United States Attorneys offices through June 2018, and the same report documented the explanations that accompany each narrowing stage and give the figures meaning. Prosecutors applied declination guidelines amid competing gun violence cases competing for the same offices and resources. Proof that a buyer knew of the prohibition presented genuine difficulty where convictions were old or status was misunderstood. Field divisions increasingly issued warning notices in place of investigation as referral volume rose 59 percent over the six years the report covered. The denial pool contained erroneous and records-based denials later resolved on challenge, so only a subset of denials could ever support a prosecution. Each figure in the chain has to be read with those four explanations attached, because the explanations identify the mechanisms that produced the narrowing and distinguish a denial that evidenced a knowing false statement from a denial that rested on a record error, an old conviction the buyer may not have understood as disqualifying, or a referral that a field division answered with a warning rather than an investigation.

The chain between a denial and a prosecution runs through discretionary gates, and each gate reflects the documented explanations operating in sequence. A denial establishes that the check returned a prohibiting record or an unresolved record, not that a prosecutor can prove a knowing false statement beyond the required standard. Referral depends on an investigative judgment by the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives about which denials warrant field attention, and warning notices absorbed a rising share of that attention as volume climbed. Prosecution depends on a further judgment by a United States Attorneys office under declination guidelines that rank a false-statement case against gun violence cases demanding the same prosecutorial hours. Knowledge sits at the center of the filtering because the offense turns on a statement made knowingly, and an old conviction, a distant disposition, or a genuinely misunderstood status can leave a denial fully justified as a screening result while leaving the knowledge element exposed to reasonable doubt at trial. Erroneous and records-based denials cut the pool from the other side, since a denial resolved on challenge in favor of the buyer identifies a transaction in which prosecution would have rested on a mistaken premise. The steepness of the narrowing therefore records a series of legal and institutional filters, not a single decision to ignore a defined offense across the board.

The state pattern in the same Government Accountability Office report presents a parallel picture under different law, and the state figures belong in a passage carrying the documented explanations for the federal narrowing as context for comparison. Officials in 10 of the 13 states selected for review said the states did not investigate or prosecute denials, citing resource demands and missing state statutes, while one state reported about 1,900 referrals for prosecution in 2017 and about 470 convictions, figures arising under different law and from a different pool than the federal chain, so neither set of figures is a simple rescaling of the other. The federal explanations documented in the same report frame the comparison without displacing the state reasons stated by state officials: prosecutors applied declination guidelines amid competing gun violence cases, proof that a buyer knew of the prohibition presented genuine difficulty where convictions were old or status was misunderstood, field divisions increasingly issued warning notices in place of investigation as referral volume rose 59 percent over the six years the report covered, and the denial pool contained erroneous and records-based denials later resolved on challenge, so only a subset of denials could ever support a prosecution. Read together, the state responses show enforcement after denial varying sharply by jurisdiction, absent as a practice in most states reviewed, and substantial in the one state that reported a high-volume referral and conviction record under a distinct legal structure.

Scope limits define how far the documented pattern reaches, and observance of those limits keeps the assessment accurate in both directions. The federal chain measures transactions processed by the FBI, and point-of-contact state transactions fall outside the measure, so the federal figures describe the federal segment of the national check system rather than a complete national account. The state responses describe the states selected for review, under state law that differs from the federal offense structure and from the law of other states, and the reporting state drew referrals from a pool shaped by that state law. A careful reader therefore treats the documented pattern as a measured account of the segment examined, strong enough to defeat an absolute assertion about prosecution after denial and to establish a real enforcement gap within the measured segment, without converting the segment into a claim about every jurisdiction or every transaction channel in the country.

The documented explanations deserve separate weight because each explanation identifies a different kind of limit on prosecution, and the limits do not collapse into a single claim about indifference. Declination guidelines amid competing gun violence cases describe a resource and priority limit, in which a false-statement prosecution competes against cases involving completed violence for finite prosecutorial capacity. The knowledge difficulty describes an evidentiary limit, in which the screening result and the trial proof diverge because a database match and a knowing lie are different propositions, separated by the age of a record and the understanding of the buyer. Warning notices describe an institutional substitution, in which a field division answers a rising referral volume with notice to the denied person rather than investigation toward referral for charges, a response that records official contact without producing a prosecution statistic. Erroneous and records-based denials describe a composition limit, in which part of the pool never contained prosecutable conduct at all, so the gap between denials and prosecutions overstates any enforcement shortfall unless the erroneous share is kept in view. Together, the four explanations show a gap produced by interacting legal, evidentiary, and institutional conditions, documented by the Government Accountability Office in the same report as the figures, rather than a gap explained by any single cause.

The assertion therefore fails in the absolute form and succeeds in the directional observation underneath the absolute form. Prosecutions after denial are documented at the federal level in the measured year, and convictions after denial are documented at the state level in the reporting state, so the proposition that prosecution never occurs cannot survive contact with GAO-18-440. The narrowing that the assertion gestures toward is equally documented, as the federal chain moved from a large denial pool through a far smaller referral set to a very small prosecution count, under explanations that the report stated alongside the numbers at every stage, and as most states selected for review reported no investigation or prosecution practice at all. A verdict that called the assertion simply false would erase the documented gap, and a verdict that called the assertion simply true would erase the documented prosecutions and convictions. The record supports the middle judgment, crediting the gap and rejecting the absolute. The verdict is Partly true.

## Two Questions That Decide Most Federal Gun Law Claims

Most federal gun law myths share a shape. A statement may assign a duty to the wrong level of government, or to the wrong kind of seller, or do both at once, combining a state rule with a transaction that federal law reaches through a different channel. The diagnostic developed across the preceding claim examinations has a name, the two questions. The first question asks whether the speaker is describing federal law or a particular state law. The second question asks whether the transaction involves a licensed dealer. An assertion that cannot survive the appropriate question does not need a more elaborate refutation, because the error sits at the level of attribution. An assertion that survives both questions requires evidence of a different kind, and the volume dispute examined below survives both, which explains its different status.

The first question separates two bodies of law that public discussion routinely merges. Federal law, principally chapter 44 of title 18, sets the framework for licensed importers, manufacturers, and dealers, governs movement across state lines, defines categories of persons prohibited from receiving or possessing firearms, and establishes the background check system licensees use before transferring a firearm to a nonlicensee. State law operates alongside that framework and supplies many duties governing conduct inside a home, conditions on private transfers within a state, and permit systems reaching transactions federal law leaves to the states. A duty in a state statute does not become a federal duty through repetition, and absence of a duty from federal law does not establish absence from every state code. If the sovereign behind a rule is a state legislature, the assertion may describe that state accurately but fails as a statement about federal law. If the sovereign is Congress, the federal text must contain the duty in the terms claimed.

The second question separates sellers by licensed status, the organizing distinction of the federal transfer system described in [the complete guide to the Gun Control Act of 1968](/2021/04/01/gun-control-act-1968-complete-guide/). Federal law imposes the transaction record, the background check, and related duties on licensees. A transfer between two nonlicensees residing in the same state carries no federal license, record, or check requirement, wherever the transfer occurs. That private transfer space is the residue of the 1968 design, not an announced exemption for a place or an occasion, and its boundary is seller status and the presence or absence of a state line. One federal rule reaches every transferor. Section 922(d) of title 18 forbids any person to sell or dispose of a firearm to a person the transferor knows or has reasonable cause to believe falls within a prohibited category. A private transferor nevertheless has no federal instrument for verifying status, because the check system is open to licensees for licensee transfers. The second question thus decides which federal machinery applies, while section 922(d) supplies a knowledge based prohibition rather than a check the private transferor cannot run.

### Where does the line between federal law and state law run?

Federal law governs licensees and interstate movement, while state law adds storage duties, private transfer checks, and other rules within a state. A statement fails the first question when it assigns a state duty to Congress, and it fails the second when it assigns a licensee duty to a private seller.

The storage claim fails the first question, and the failure is instructive because a genuine federal provision sits near the assertion. Section 922(z)(1) of title 18, added by section 5 of Public Law 109-92 and cited as the Child Safety Lock Act of 2005, made it unlawful for a licensed importer, manufacturer, or dealer to sell, deliver, or transfer a handgun to a person other than a licensee unless the transferee is provided with a secure gun storage or safety device for that handgun. The requirement attaches to the licensee at the moment of transfer, subject to the exceptions stated in section 922(z)(2). The federal provision does not require the transferee to use the device, and no provision of federal law requires a firearm owner to store a firearm locked, unloaded, or in any particular manner in the home. Storage mandates, where such mandates exist, are state law, typically keyed in state law to access by minors. The assertion therefore takes a federal device at transfer requirement, converts provision of a device into a duty of use, moves that duty from the counter into the home, and attributes the resulting mandate to Congress.

The show claim fails the second question, because federal law contains no provision that treats a gun show differently from any other setting. A licensee may conduct business temporarily at a gun show or event in the state specified on the license, under section 923(j) of title 18 and section 478.100(a)(1) of title 27 of the Code of Federal Regulations, and the show location is considered part of the licensed premises for that purpose. The licensee must post the license, keep the same records required by section 478.100(c), and run the same background check required by section 478.102 before transferring a firearm to a nonlicensee. A licensee at a show therefore operates under federal duties identical to the duties at the licensed premises. The transaction that carries no federal check at a show is a transfer between two nonlicensees residing in the same state, and the identical transfer carries no federal check at a home, at a flea market, or through a listing arranged online. Venue explains nothing in federal law. Seller status and residence explain the outcome.

The online claim fails the second question for the same structural reason, although an internet storefront feels detached from a counter. A firearm sold online by a licensee may not be delivered directly to a nonlicensee buyer across a state line. The lawful route requires the selling licensee to ship the firearm to a licensee in the state of the buyer, after which the transfer is completed in person at the premises of the receiving licensee, with the transaction record completed and the check run, exactly as for an in store sale. Sections 922(a)(2) and 922(b)(3) of title 18 supply the structure for licensee sales and deliveries, section 922(a)(3) bars a nonlicensee from acquiring a firearm in another state and transporting or receiving the firearm at home outside the licensee channel, apart from bequest or intestate succession, and section 922(a)(5) bars a nonlicensee transfer to a nonlicensee the transferor knows or has reasonable cause to believe resides in another state, apart from stated exceptions that include a temporary sporting loan or rental and bequest or intestate succession. The 1968 act ended the mail order channel to nonlicensees, and an internet storefront is a new ordering medium inside the same channel rules, rather than a new legal channel. The sole residue of accuracy is that an online listing can serve as the advertising medium by which two private persons in the same state arrange a transfer that federal law does not check, subject to state law. That residue belongs to the private transfer space, and licensed status, rather than the screen, decides the federal treatment.

The registry claim fails against the federal text itself, a failure that the two questions expose by directing attention to the provisions the assertion must overcome. Section 922(t)(2) of title 18 provides that, on an approval, the system assigns a unique identification number and destroys all records of the system respecting the call other than the identifying number and the date the number was assigned. Section 617 of Public Law 108-199, the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2004, requires destruction of identifying information on an approved transaction not more than 24 hours after the licensee receives notification of the approval, a requirement carried in appropriations acts after that act and implemented at section 25.9(b) of title 28 of the Code of Federal Regulations. Two statutory bars reinforce the destruction rule. Section 926(a) of title 18 forbids a rule or regulation requiring licensee records to be centralized or transferred to a facility owned, managed, or controlled by the United States or a state, and forbids a rule or regulation establishing a system of registration of firearms, owners, or transactions. Section 103(i) of the Brady Act, Public Law 103-159, bars use of the check system to establish a system of registration, except with respect to persons prohibited from receiving a firearm. The durable record sits with the licensee, on ATF Form 4473, retained for not less than 20 years under section 478.129(b) of title 27, and the form reaches the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives if the licensee discontinues business, under section 923(g)(4) of title 18. A check transmits identifying information and descriptors concerning the buyer and the category of firearm, handgun, long gun, or other, rather than the make, model, or serial number, which appear on the form retained by the licensee.

The volume dispute survives both tests, and survival is the reason the dispute receives a different label and a fuller evidentiary treatment below. A person can state the federal position correctly, acknowledging that licensee transfers pass through the check system and that a transfer between two nonlicensees residing in the same state carries no federal check requirement, and can identify state law correctly, acknowledging that some states impose checks on private transfers and other states do not, and can nevertheless dispute how many transfers occur without a check. The first question sorts the governing law, and the second question sorts the channel. Neither question counts transactions inside the channel that federal law leaves unchecked, and no answer to either question supplies a numerator, a denominator, or a method for relating one to the other. The dispute is empirical rather than attributional. The statute defines the space within which unchecked transfers can occur without measuring the traffic through that space, so the two questions also identify assertions that remain open because the legal framework, correctly described, leaves a quantity unmeasured.

### How many transfers escape the check is a genuinely open question

The 1994 National Survey of Private Ownership of Firearms, conducted by Cook and Ludwig, supplied the older figure that shaped discussion of transfer volume. The survey was widely reported as estimating that about 40 percent of firearm transfers occurred outside the licensed channel. The wording of the survey matters as much as the percentage drawn from the survey, because the percentage is a proxy constructed from a different question. Cook and Ludwig asked whether the most recent firearm of the respondent came from a federally licensed dealer. The survey did not ask directly whether a background check occurred during the acquisition, so an answer about source was used to infer the presence or absence of a check. The survey also counted gifts and other non purchase acquisitions alongside sales, so the figure covered a broader class of transfers than purchases from sellers. A gift within a family, counted in the acquisition pool, could contribute to the share described as occurring outside the licensed channel even though the transaction was not a sale, and even though the figure was later quoted in arguments framed entirely around sales. The roughly 40 percent figure therefore states the share suggested by a 1994 source based proxy in which acquisition from a source other than a federally licensed dealer stood in for absence of a check, across a pool that combined sales with gifts and other non purchase acquisitions.

Miller, Hepburn, and Azrael, writing in the Annals of Internal Medicine in 2017, reported a measurement built on a different design. The authors reported a 2015 national survey of 1,613 gun owners that asked directly about background checks. Among gun owners who had acquired a firearm in the previous two years, 22 percent did so without a background check. The direct question removes one inferential step present in the 1994 design, because a respondent reported screening rather than a source from which screening was inferred, and the reference period tied the answer to acquisitions in the two years before the 2015 survey rather than to the most recent firearm whenever that firearm had been acquired. The same study reported a sharp division by state law among private purchases. In states without private sale check laws, 57 percent of private purchases occurred without a check, against 26 percent in states with such laws. That division returns the analysis to the first of the two questions, because state law changes the expected share inside the private channel, and a national figure combines populations governed by different state rules.

The two figures measure different things, and neither figure refutes the other directly. The 1994 figure measures source of acquisition, drawn from the most recent firearm of the respondent, across sales and non purchase acquisitions combined. The 2015 figure measures self reported screening among owners who acquired a firearm in the previous two years, based on a direct question about a background check. A change from about two fifths to about one fifth across those instruments may reflect a difference in method, in the population measured, in the period covered, in the mix of gifts and sales, or in the underlying volume, and the two instruments cannot apportion the gap among those explanations. The honest statement is that the volume of unchecked transfers is contested, with the direct 2015 measurement at about one fifth of acquisitions in the measured period and the older proxy figure at about two fifths of transfers under the broader 1994 construction. The verdict on the volume question is Contested. That label records a genuine disagreement between measurements of different quantities, rather than a failure to locate a governing text, and the label protects both figures from a use each design cannot support.

A structural feature of the federal system prevents an administrative count from settling the dispute, and the feature is the destruction rule that defeated the registry claim. On an approved transaction, identifying information must be destroyed under the timetable supplied by section 617 of Public Law 108-199, and the audit log residue for an approved transaction consists of the NICS Transaction Number and the date, with remaining identifying residue purged within 90 days. After destruction, no approved transaction can be reconstructed from the file of the federal system itself. The system therefore cannot produce a later administrative census of approved checks against which survey estimates of all transfers could be checked, because the file that such a census would count was designed to disappear. The licensee record does not repair the gap, because ATF Form 4473 remains with the licensee rather than in a centralized federal file, and section 926(a) forbids centralization of licensee records through rule or regulation. The destruction rule protects privacy and limits oversight, including audit, error review, and later inquiry into a specific approved check. For the volume question, the consequence is decisive for method. Surveys must estimate a quantity that the federal check system, by statutory design, does not preserve in reconstructable form, and disagreement between survey instruments cannot be resolved by ordering a count of records that federal law required to be destroyed.

### The sourcing errors that carry false claims forward

Certain mechanical errors recur because each error is easy to make in a summary and hard to notice after the summary has detached from the text that would correct the error. The first error describes a state statute as federal law, and the correction is to name the sovereign that enacted the duty, since a state storage or private transfer rule governs within that state and supplies no federal mandate. The second error attributes a licensee duty to every seller, and the correction is to ask whether the seller holds a federal license, since the federal record and check duties attach to licensees while a transfer between same state nonlicensees carries no federal check requirement. The third error quotes the 1994 proxy figure as if the figure counted unchecked sales, and the correction is to state the method, since Cook and Ludwig asked about the source of the most recent firearm, counted gifts alongside sales, and asked no direct question about screening. The fourth error describes a scheduled sunset as a repeal vote, and the correction is to state that the assault weapons ban carried a self executing termination drafted in the language of repeal in 1994, so the prohibitions ended on September 13, 2004 by operation of that term rather than through a repeal decision taken by Congress in 2004. The fifth error describes a defined statutory bar with six exclusions as total immunity, and the correction is to state that the Protection of Lawful Commerce in Arms Act bars a qualified civil liability action as defined in section 7903(5)(A) of title 15, while an action inside any of the six exclusions in that definition is outside the barred category altogether.

Each error propagates through the same mechanism, the loss of a qualifier during repetition. A state duty loses the name of the state and acquires a national scope that the duty never held. A licensee duty loses the word licensee and acquires every seller as its subject. A proxy loses the method that produced the proxy and acquires the appearance of a direct count. A sunset loses the decade between enactment and operation and acquires the appearance of a fresh legislative decision at the date of expiry. A defined bar loses the exclusions written into the definition and acquires a breadth that the enacted definition denied to the bar from the beginning. The two questions reverse the first two losses by restoring the sovereign and the seller to the sentence, attention to method reverses the third loss, attention to dates and enacted terms reverses the fourth loss, and attention to definitions reverses the fifth loss by restoring the exclusions that form part of the statutory category.

## What a Careful Reader Should Believe

A careful reader should believe the record in both directions, without allowing either direction to become a general theory of federal firearms law. The federal scheme proved narrower than a set of familiar claims in several places. An approved check left a transaction number and a date in federal hands rather than an owner registry. A handgun transfer carried a duty to provide a safety device rather than a federal mandate governing storage inside a home. A civil liability bar contained six exclusions rather than blanket protection for an industry. A silencer sat inside a registration and tax channel rather than under a federal prohibition, with lawful private ownership recorded in most states at the dated count. A leading court decision recognized an individual right and preserved named categories of regulation in the passage that limited the right. Each result rests on words placed in a statute, a regulation, or an opinion, and each result would disappear from view under a summary that stopped at a title, a penalty, or a headline.

The federal scheme also proved more demanding than another set of familiar claims in several places. An online order from a licensee ended at a counter in the state of the buyer, with a record and a check, rather than at a doorstep. A licensee at a show carried the same record and check duties that applied at licensed premises. A machine gun moved only inside a fixed registered stock closed by a cutoff date, under application, tax, and advance approval. A ban ended through a term written a decade earlier rather than through a fresh vote in the year of expiry, a distinction that assigns responsibility to the drafting choice that produced the ending. Prosecution after a denial occurred in documented cases, even though the documented chain from denials through referrals to prosecutions narrowed sharply for reasons stated beside the figures. Each result likewise rests on enacted words and dated records, and each result corrects a picture built from a visible setting, a familiar commercial habit, or an absolute word placed over a qualified record.

Between those two directions sits the space that federal law genuinely leaves to other law and to private action. A transfer between two nonlicensees residing in the same state carried no federal check requirement, wherever the transfer occurred, while a federal prohibition reached a transferor who knew or had reasonable cause to believe that a recipient fell within a prohibited category. State law supplied storage duties, private transfer checks, waiting periods, and ownership permissions that varied from state to state. The volume of transfers without a check remained contested, because two named surveys measured different quantities through different questions and the federal record system destroyed the identifying material that an administrative count would require. A careful reader therefore holds three categories apart. Text decides claims that a text governs. A named dataset describes a measured segment, with method and date attached. A contested label marks a quantity that the available measurements frame without settling.

The durable habit is an attachment habit. A claim gains credibility only after the sentence carrying the claim names the sovereign, the seller, the instrument, the moment, and the passage relied upon. A federal claim has to survive a federal text. A licensee duty has to be distinguished from a private transferor position. A handgun rule has to be distinguished from a long gun rule and from a rule for a taxed instrument. A duty at transfer has to be distinguished from a duty during possession. A definition has to be read with exclusions, and a right has to be read with the limits stated by the opinion that recognized the right. Dates have to remain attached to sunsets, counts, surveys, and reports, because a date often supplies the difference between a decision and a default, between a closed stock and an open channel, and between a measurement and a slogan.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Is it a myth that a federal gun law was repealed in 2004?

The description compresses a self-executing sunset into a later repeal vote. The federal assault weapons ban formed Subtitle A of Title XI of Public Law 103-322, signed on September 13, 1994. Section 110105(2) provided that the subtitle and its amendments stood repealed effective ten years after that date, so the prohibitions ended on September 13, 2004 by operation of the term written in 1994. Congress took no repeal vote in 2004 and enacted no extension. The Senate adopted an extension amendment on March 2, 2004 by 52 to 47 and then rejected the amended bill, leaving the amendment without legal effect. The House held no extension vote before the date arrived, and no bill reached President George W. Bush.

### Q: Does federal gun law create a registry?

An approved check does not create a federal registry of gun owners. Section 922(t)(2) of title 18 directs destruction of system records respecting an approved call other than the identifying number and the date assigned. Section 617 of Public Law 108-199 requires destruction of identifying information not more than 24 hours after notice of approval, implemented at 28 C.F.R. 25.9(b), with remaining identifying residue purged within 90 days. Section 926(a) forbids centralization of licensee records and any system of registration, and section 103(i) of the Brady Act bars use of the check system to establish registration except for prohibited persons. The detailed ATF Form 4473 remains with the licensee for at least 20 years and reaches federal custody only when a licensee discontinues business.

### Q: Can you buy a gun online without a check under federal gun law?

A firearm bought online from a licensee does not arrive through a check-free channel. The selling licensee ships the firearm to a licensee in the state of the buyer, and the transfer completes in person at the receiving premises with the transaction record completed and the background check run, exactly as for an in-store sale. Sections 922(a)(2) and 922(b)(3) of title 18 supply that licensed channel, while sections 922(a)(3) and 922(a)(5) bar acquisition and transfer between nonlicensees across a state line outside narrow exceptions for succession and temporary sporting loan or rental. An online listing can advertise a private transfer between residents of the same state that federal law does not check, subject to state law, and that private space differs from an online purchase through a licensee.

### Q: Is there a gun show loophole in federal gun law?

The claim in this question divides into a supported phenomenon and an unsupported legal description. No provision of federal law treats a show differently from any other setting, and the show claim therefore fails as a description of a show-specific rule. A licensee may conduct business temporarily at a show in the state specified on the license under section 923(j) of title 18 and 27 C.F.R. 478.100, and the location counts as part of the licensed premises, with the license posted, the same records kept, and the same check run before transfer to a nonlicensee. The unchecked transaction is a transfer between two nonlicensees residing in the same state, which carries no federal check at a show, at a home, or at a flea market. Section 922(d) separately forbids any transferor from selling to a person known or reasonably believed to be prohibited.

### Q: Are machine guns legal to buy under federal gun law?

A machine gun can change hands lawfully only inside a closed and heavily regulated channel. Machine guns sit under the National Firearms Act regime, with registration in the National Firearms Registration and Transfer Record, a transfer tax of 200 dollars under 26 U.S.C. 5811, and approval before transfer under 26 U.S.C. 5812. Section 922(o) of title 18 makes transfer or possession unlawful except for government use and for a machine gun lawfully possessed before May 19, 1986. A lawful private purchase therefore requires an item from the fixed pre-1986 stock, an application with fingerprints and photographs, payment of the tax, and approval before delivery. A February 24, 2016 letter from the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives counted 175,977 transferable pre-1986 machine guns, with an acknowledged margin of error from the query method.

### Q: Does federal gun law require safe storage?

Federal law requires a device at a covered transfer and does not impose a federal storage mandate in the home. Section 922(z)(1) of title 18, added by section 5 of Public Law 109-92 and effective April 24, 2006, makes it unlawful for a licensed importer, manufacturer, or dealer to sell, deliver, or transfer a handgun to a nonlicensee unless the transferee is provided with a secure gun storage or safety device for that handgun. Exceptions appear at section 922(z)(2), and the penalty at section 924(p) runs against the licensee. The provision does not require the transferee to use the device, and no provision of federal law requires an owner to store a firearm locked, unloaded, or in a particular manner in a residence. Storage mandates, where enacted, are state law.

### Q: Are suppressors illegal under federal gun law?

A suppressor is regulated rather than prohibited under federal law. The statutory term is firearm silencer or firearm muffler, defined at 26 U.S.C. 5845(a)(7), and the device has sat inside the National Firearms Act registration and tax regime since 1934. A lawful transfer requires registration in the National Firearms Registration and Transfer Record, a transfer tax of 200 dollars under 26 U.S.C. 5811, and advance approval under 26 U.S.C. 5812, with felony penalties for unregistered possession under 26 U.S.C. 5861. New suppressors may be manufactured and registered, so the stock is not closed by a cutoff date. The American Suppressor Association count published in January 2017 recorded suppressors as lawful for private ownership in 42 states and lawful for use in hunting in 40 states, leaving prohibition as a minority state position.

### Q: Does federal gun law prosecute failed background checks?

Prosecutions after a denial are documented, and the documented narrowing from denials to prosecutions is steep. Government Accountability Office report GAO-18-440, issued in June 2018 and covering fiscal 2017 federal transactions processed by the Federal Bureau of Investigation, recorded 8,606,286 transactions, 112,090 denials, 12,710 referrals by the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives to field divisions, and 12 prosecutions by United States Attorneys through June 2018. The same report supplied the explanations attached to those figures. Prosecutors applied declination guidelines amid competing gun violence cases. Proof that a buyer knew of a prohibition proved difficult where convictions were old or status was misunderstood. Field divisions increasingly issued warning notices in place of investigation as referral volume rose 59 percent over six years. The denial pool also contained erroneous and records-based denials later resolved on challenge, so only a subset of denials could support a prosecution.

### Q: Did the Supreme Court strike down the federal background check system?

Printz v. United States, 521 U.S. 898 (1997), decided on June 27, 1997 by a five to four vote, did not strike down the federal background check system. The decision invalidated only the Brady Act interim direction commanding chief local law enforcement officers to make a reasonable effort to determine whether a transfer would be unlawful, at 18 U.S.C. 922(s)(2), and the implied duty to accept the licensee statement form, at 922(s)(1)(A)(i)(III) and (IV). The interim waiting period itself survived, and the licensee duties survived. State alternative systems survived as well. The forthcoming federally operated system also survived, and the permanent federal check structure proceeded on the foundation that the Printz decision left intact.

### Q: Does a background check record the make and model of the firearm bought?

A check under section 922(t) of title 18 does not transmit the make, model, or serial number of the firearm bought. The check transmits the identifying information and descriptors of the purchaser and the category of firearm, stated as handgun, long gun, or other. The make, model, and serial number appear on the transaction form completed at the licensed premises. That form, ATF Form 4473, remains in the custody of the licensee rather than passing into the federal check system as part of the call. The distinction between category and particular instrument is central to the record analysis, because a federal transmission containing identity and category differs from a federal record pairing a named person with an identified firearm.

### Q: How many firearm transfers happen without a background check?

The volume of transfers without a background check is Contested, and two named measurements frame the disagreement without settling the quantity. The 1994 National Survey of Private Ownership of Firearms, conducted by Cook and Ludwig, was widely reported as estimating that about 40 percent of firearm transfers occurred outside the licensed channel. That survey asked whether the most recent firearm of a respondent came from a federally licensed dealer, did not ask directly whether a background check occurred, and counted gifts and other non-purchase acquisitions alongside sales. Miller, Hepburn, and Azrael reported a 2015 national survey of 1,613 gun owners in the Annals of Internal Medicine in 2017 and found that 22 percent of owners who had acquired a firearm in the previous two years did so without a background check. Among private purchases, the reported share was 57 percent in states without private-sale check laws against 26 percent in states with such laws.

### Q: Does federal law impose a waiting period on firearm purchases?

Federal law imposed a waiting period only during the interim phase of the Brady Act. The interim provisions took effect on February 28, 1994, ninety days after the November 30, 1993 signing of Public Law 103-159, and the interim waiting period ran up to five business days for handgun transfers by licensees. The interim phase ceased on November 30, 1998, when the Federal Bureau of Investigation activated the national instant criminal background check system. No federal waiting period has operated since the interim phase ended. A waiting period encountered in a particular purchase therefore derives from state law in states that enacted such a period, rather than from a federal provision governing licensee transfers after November 30, 1998.

### Q: Can a buyer purchase a handgun in another state and take it home the same day?

A handgun purchase does not follow that route under federal law. A licensee may not sell or deliver a handgun to a nonresident nonlicensee for direct carriage home, and the handgun route always runs through a licensee in the state of the buyer. The firearm ships from the selling licensee to a receiving licensee in the home state, and the transfer completes in person at the receiving premises with the transaction record completed and the check run. Section 922(a)(3) of title 18 also bars a nonlicensee from acquiring a firearm in another state and transporting or receiving the firearm at home outside the licensee channel, apart from bequest or intestate succession. A narrow face-to-face exception exists for a long gun sale complying with the law of both states, and that exception does not convert a handgun purchase into a same-day interstate purchase.

### Q: Did the Gun Control Act end mail-order firearm sales?

The Gun Control Act of 1968 ended the mail-order channel to nonlicensees. Before that enactment, remote ordering could end in direct delivery to a nonlicensee buyer. The 1968 act confined interstate movement to the licensed channel and barred the acquisition and transfer routes that had allowed a firearm to reach a nonlicensee outside that channel. An internet storefront represents a later ordering medium inside the same channel rules rather than a new legal channel created for electronic commerce. Selection and payment can occur at a distance, while shipment between licensees and completion in person at the receiving licensee carry the transfer. The distinction between medium and channel explains why a website order and a catalogue order receive the same federal treatment at the transfer stage.

### Q: Can an eighteen year old buy a handgun from a licensed dealer?

Section 922(b)(1) of title 18 sets two age floors for sales by a licensee, and the handgun floor answers the question against an eighteen year old purchaser. A licensee may not sell or deliver a firearm other than a shotgun or a rifle, a category that covers a handgun in practice, to a person under twenty-one years of age. A licensee may not sell or deliver a shotgun or a rifle to a person under eighteen years of age. An eighteen year old therefore falls below the federal floor for a handgun purchase from a licensee, while reaching the federal floor for a shotgun or rifle sale, subject to the other requirements governing a licensee transfer. The floors attach to licensee sales and define the federal position for the counter transaction described in the question.

### Q: Why does the destruction rule prevent a later audit of an approved check?

The destruction rule removes the record that a later audit would examine. Identifying information on an approved transaction faces destruction not more than 24 hours after notice of approval under section 617 of Public Law 108-199, implemented at 28 C.F.R. 25.9(b), and remaining identifying residue faces purging within 90 days. The audit log residue for an approved transaction consists of the NICS Transaction Number and the date. After destruction, no approved transaction can be reconstructed from the file of the federal system, so an auditor cannot sample a surviving population of approved transactions in the manner available under a retention regime. Appropriations riders in the late 1990s and early 2000s had required retention for audit for periods of months, on the stated basis that auditors needed surviving records, and the fiscal 2004 act reversed that model. Error review and later inquiry into a specific approved check face the same structural limit.

### Q: Are federal gun laws the same in every state?

The federal text applies as a national framework, and the combined legal position governing a transfer differs from state to state because state law overlays the federal framework. Federal law governs licensees, interstate movement, prohibited categories, and the check that a licensee runs. State law supplies storage duties in several states, waiting periods in some states, and checks for private transfers under counts that depend on method. By the Giffords Law Center count, twenty states and the District of Columbia required checks for transfers of at least some firearms between private parties. By the February 2021 measure of the Center for American Progress, twenty-two states and the District of Columbia fell inside a measure including permit-to-purchase systems reaching private sales. Suppressor permissions also varied, with lawful private ownership recorded in 42 states at the January 2017 American Suppressor Association count. A single transfer therefore sits under one federal text and a state overlay that depends on location.

### Q: What happens to the record when a check is denied?

A denied transaction follows a retention path unlike the destruction path for an approval. Records of a denied transaction are retained in the audit log for 10 years under 28 C.F.R. 25.9(b). After that term, the records pass to an electronic database maintained by the Federal Bureau of Investigation. The Brady Act bar at section 103(i) of Public Law 103-159 contains an exception matching that treatment, because the bar on use of the check system to establish a system of registration excludes persons prohibited from receiving a firearm. Delayed or open transactions follow a different path, with identifying information destroyed at 90 days and the date and transaction number retained. Denial retention therefore concerns a prohibited-person record held for a stated term, rather than an approved buyer record held in a federal owner file.

### Q: What two questions should a reader ask about any federal gun law claim?

The first question asks whether a claim describes federal law or the law of a particular state. A storage duty, a private transfer check, or a waiting period drawn from a state statute may govern inside that state without supplying a federal rule. The second question asks whether a transaction involves a licensed dealer. Federal record, check, and related transfer duties attach to licensees, while a transfer between two nonlicensees residing in the same state carries no federal check requirement wherever the transfer occurs. One federal rule reaches every transferor through section 922(d) of title 18, which forbids a sale to a person known or reasonably believed to be prohibited. A claim that assigns a state duty to Congress fails the first question, and a claim that assigns a licensee duty to every seller fails the second question.

### Q: What should a student remember about federal gun law claims for an exam?

A student should remember a method before a list of outcomes. A strong answer states a claim fairly, locates the passage that decides the claim, and names the sovereign, the seller category, the instrument category, and the date attached to the governing provision. Sunset language requires attention to the enactment that wrote the term and to the date on which the term operated. A statutory definition requires attention to exclusions written inside the definition. A court decision requires attention to provisions invalidated and to categories preserved in the same opinion. A survey figure requires the question asked, the population measured, and the period covered. A report figure requires the explanations supplied beside the figure. Verdict language stays inside a short vocabulary of true, partly true, false, and contested, and each verdict attaches to a statute, regulation, decision, or dataset that a reader can inspect.
