## Is the Casa di Giulietta real? The verdict on Verona's balcony

The Casa di Giulietta at Via Cappello 23 in Verona is a genuine medieval tower house wearing a false name, and its famous balcony is a twentieth-century construction assembled from old tomb marble. That double verdict, half acquittal and half conviction, is the only honest starting point for the most visited literary address in Italy. The building exists, and parts of it stood when Dante was alive. The heroine whose house it claims to be never existed at all. Between those two facts the city of Verona built a shrine, and the shrine, rather than the stones, is the real subject: a fiction so beloved that a municipality gave it a front door, a courtyard, and the single architectural feature the play itself never names.

![The courtyard of the Casa di Giulietta in Verona beneath the added balcony - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-49.webp)

The argument of this article is that the balcony lies, but that the lie is precise, datable, and more instructive than most truths in the heritage business. The word balcony appears in no early text of the play. The family that owned the house for centuries was called Cappello, not Capuleti, and the carved hat that sealed the identification is a pun in stone, not a genealogy. The balcony itself was fixed to the courtyard wall in the restoration campaign of 1939 and 1940, under the direction of Antonio Avena, the city's museum director, and its carved front was assembled from fourteenth-century marble that had spent decades in the stores of the Castelvecchio museum. None of this is rumour. Every element of the fabrication sits in the documented record, and much of it sits in the city's own published descriptions. The myth survives not because the evidence is hidden but because the evidence does not matter to the ritual performed beneath the balcony, and separating those two registers, the archive and the ritual, is the work of the pages that follow.

A verdict is owed, and it can be given at the outset. As archaeology, the Casa di Giulietta fails on every count that matters: no Capulet lived there, no balcony stood there before the age of the motor car, and the story the courtyard tells is false in its family, its furniture, and its centrepiece. As a document of reading, the same courtyard is authentic beyond dispute, because it records, in brick and recycled marble, what four centuries of readers did with a play: they demanded a place, and a city supplied one. The dishonesty that deserves condemnation is narrower than the site itself. It lives in any presentation that lets a visitor leave believing the stones are evidence for the story, when the stones are evidence only for the story's power. Verona built a stage set and called it a house. Judged as a stage set, it is a masterpiece of the genre. Judged as a house, it is a forgery with a medieval skeleton.

One preliminary correction belongs at the gate, because the site's fame is usually stated in numbers the record does not support. Guidebook prose speaks of millions visiting the balcony, and the figure survives because it is true of the wrong unit: the Smithsonian's account counts some two million visitors a year to Verona as a whole, and hundreds of thousands to the house within that traffic, while the municipal museums' report for 2016 counted 329,000 paid admissions through the house's own door. The house is, by any honest measure, one of the most visited literary sites in Europe. It is not visited by millions, and the inflation matters to the argument, because a myth believed to draw millions acquires a false inevitability, as if crowds of that size could only gather for a truth. The documented hundreds of thousands are impressive enough, and they gathered for a fiction, which is the more interesting fact.

## Via Cappello 23: the medieval house behind the myth

What stands at the address is a casatorre, a tower house of the kind that fortified families raised across the cities of medieval Italy, documented from 1351 and carrying fabric that reaches back into the thirteenth century. Its position is prime and unromantic: a short walk from Piazza delle Erbe, the old forum and market square, in the dense commercial heart of the city. For most of its documented life the building had nothing to do with literature. It belonged for centuries to the Dal Cappello family, Veronese property holders whose name the street itself carries, and it earned its keep in the hospitality trade as the inn or hostelry of the Cappello, the stallo del Cappello in the documents, later passing through phases as stables, workshops, and subdivided tenements as its fortunes declined. A pilgrim trade of a literary kind reached it late; the identification of the building as the Capulets' house took shape in the eighteenth century, on the strength of a resemblance and a carving, and hardened into a destination during the nineteenth, when travellers who had already visited the supposed tomb across the city added the supposed house to the itinerary.

The carving is the hinge of the whole identification, and it deserves a close look, because it is the one piece of evidence the site offers that a visitor can still touch. Set into the keystone of the arch that leads into the inner courtyard is the coat of arms of the Cappello family: a hat. Italian canting arms of this kind pun on the family name, cappello being the ordinary word for a hat, and the carving announces, with medieval directness, whose house this was. Antonio Avena's restorers kept it, polished it, and made it the first thing the entering visitor passes beneath. The pun did the rest. Cappello sounds, to an ear that wants the resemblance, close to Capuleti, and Capuleti in the English form, Capulet, is the surname the play gives its heroine. A name that is nearly right, carved over the door of a genuinely old house, on a street in the city where the story is set: the identification needed nothing else, and for two centuries it has needed nothing else, because nothing else exists.

### Does any document connect the Cappello family to the lovers' story before the age of tourism?

No document does. The Cappello appear in the records as property owners and innkeepers, and no chronicle, letter, or legal instrument ties the family to a feud, a secret marriage, or a pair of dead lovers. The archive gives the house owners, tenants, and a trade; the story was supplied by readers.

The connection begins as an inference from a similar name and a carved hat, drawn by antiquarians and travellers in the eighteenth century, and it hardened into fact by repetition in guidebooks, each edition citing the last until the inference wore the uniform of a finding. No competing house ever produced a document either. The claim won by default, in a field where the prize went to the only candidate with a pun over its door.

What the building genuinely offers, once the name is stripped away, is a solid specimen of the domestic architecture of Scaliger Verona, the city of the Della Scala lords under whom the tale's first teller set his fiction. The tower form, the brick facade with its Gothic portal, the courtyard plan, the scale of the rooms: these belong to the documented fabric of the medieval city, and they are the reason the fabrication could work. A purpose-built fake in plaster would have fooled nobody for a season. A real medieval house, refronted and refurnished, could carry the fiction indefinitely, because every visitor intuition that said the place felt old was answering a true signal. The fabric was old. Only the label was new. That division, between authentic fabric and invented identity, runs through every element of the site, and the restoration campaign of the late 1930s understood it perfectly, because the campaign's method was to enrich the true fabric until the invented identity looked inevitable.

The municipality's purchase of the property in 1905 marks the moment the inference became official policy. The city bought the house from the Cappello heirs, an act that belongs to the civic housekeeping of the period, when Italian municipalities were acquiring historic palazzi for museums, and Verona acquired its most peculiar property with open eyes: the official interest was never in the Cappello family, a minor line of no distinction, but in the name over their door. For three decades after the purchase the house waited in municipal hands, used and largely uncelebrated, while the tomb at San Francesco al Corso carried the weight of the city's Shakespeare pilgrimage. The house's turn came when the civic museums acquired a director with a programme. Antonio Avena, who had already re-medievalized the museum at Castelvecchio in the 1920s, believed that Verona's monuments should be composed into legible pictures of the city's past, restored not to a documented moment but to a persuasive one. Under Avena the house on Via Cappello stopped being an old inn with a suggestive name and became, by design, the Casa di Giulietta. Section by section, the making of that transformation is examined below. First, the stranger and more fundamental fact has to be faced: the feature the transformation was built around does not exist in the play.

## The window twice more: height and voice in the text

The play returns its heroine to a high window once more, and the second visit confirms what the first established: the text thinks in voices and sightlines, not in galleries. At dawn in Act 3 Scene 5, the lovers part at her chamber window, and the scene's pain is conducted entirely through what can be seen and mis-seen from a height: "Art thou gone so? love, lord, ay husband, friend!" she cries, watching him descend, and the altitude itself turns prophetic in her mouth: "Methinks I see thee, now thou art below, / As one dead in the bottom of a tomb" (3.5.55-56). The window functions here as it did in the orchard, as a membrane between the house's enclosed female world and the street's male violence, permeable to speech and sight, fatal to cross in the wrong direction. No staging tradition calls this the second balcony scene, because no illustrator fixed it; the play's own architecture gives the two scenes the same furniture, and only the industry of pictures separated them into an icon and an afterthought.

Measure the orchard scene's own language once more and the balcony's absence grows louder. The heroine's first sound in the scene is a sigh overheard, "Ay me!" (2.2.25), and her lover's response defines the scene's true stage picture: "She speaks. / O, speak again, bright angel, for thou art / As glorious to this night, being o'er my head, / As is a winged messenger of heaven" (2.2.25-28). Being o'er my head: the text's own stage direction is a relative altitude, not a structure. When the longing turns tactile it reaches for the smallest possible architecture, a hand and a cheek: "See how she leans her cheek upon her hand. / O that I were a glove upon that hand, / That I might touch that cheek!" (2.2.43-45). A balcony interposes balustrade, platform, and processional space between that hand and the night air; the text interposes nothing. The scene's erotics are the erotics of the unmediated voice at an open window, and the shrine's centrepiece, for all its beauty, monumentalizes a mediation the scene itself refuses. Verona built the illustration. The distinction matters for the verdict: the balcony is not a corruption of the play introduced by tourism, since the stage corrupted it first, but neither is it the play's bequest. It is the bequest of the play's picture-making centuries, and the Casa is their museum as much as it is the heroine's.

## An inn becomes an address: the making of the identification

The identification of the house was a slow accretion, and its chronology matters, because each layer mistook the previous one for evidence. Verona's Shakespeare pilgrimage began at the tomb, not the house. An empty red-marble sarcophagus at the Franciscan convent outside the walls had been shown as the heroine's grave since the seventeenth century, and by the age of the Grand Tour it drew the travellers whose names made the cult respectable: Byron visited Verona in 1816 and treated the tomb as the city's essential sight, and Charles Dickens, describing the same sarcophagus in Pictures from Italy in 1846, gave the pilgrimage its classic English sentence of mingled belief and embarrassment. The tomb had what the house lacked: an object. A traveller could stand before a coffin, however empty and unattributable, and perform the appropriate feelings at a thing. The house, by contrast, offered only an argument, the argument from the name and the hat, and arguments make poor relics. Through the eighteenth century the antiquarian inference circulated in local erudition, the house of the Cappello proposed, on the strength of the keystone and the resemblance, as the probable home of the Cappelletti of the tale. Through the nineteenth it passed into the guidebook literature as the railway age regularized the Italian itinerary and Verona took its place as a scheduled stop between Venice and Milan. Travellers arrived with the scene already pictured, walked from the tomb to the inn on Via Cappello, looked at an old facade with no feature answering to the picture, and left with the name as their souvenir.

The asymmetry between the two sites explains the house's long probation in municipal hands. A sarcophagus needs no improvement; it is complete in its emptiness. A house identified by a pun needs everything: furnishing, dressing, and, at the centre, the feature its visitors demanded. The city's purchase of 1905 should be read in that light. It was not yet the founding of a shrine. Italian municipalities of the period were gathering historic buildings into civic keeping as a matter of patrimony policy, and Verona's acquisition of a documented medieval tower house on a central street was defensible on the fabric alone. But the name over the door was the property's distinction, and everyone in the transaction knew it: the Cappello heirs sold an old inn, and the city bought, at the price of an old inn, the freehold of a legend. For three decades the legend waited in store, like the marble at Castelvecchio, while the tomb carried the pilgrimage and the house subsided through the tenancy and workshop uses of a municipal property with no settled purpose. The wait is the most honest period in the site's history, the decades when the building was simply what it was, an old house with a suggestive name, before anyone undertook to make the suggestion visible.

Two developments of the 1930s ended the wait together. The first was administrative: Avena's direction of the civic museums gave Verona a restorer with a doctrine, a budget line, and a citywide programme of composed monuments. The second was cinematic. The play's prestige screen version of 1936, the MGM production directed by George Cukor, fixed the balcony image for a mass international audience in the same years, dressing its Verona in the Italianate architecture its designers had drawn from Italian originals. The city's campaign and the film's release belong to one cultural moment, in which the balcony ceased to be a stage convention among others and became the story's universal logo. When the Casa opened its composed courtyard at the decade's end, the world arrived pre-instructed: it knew exactly what it was looking at, because it had been shown the picture by every medium except the text. The house's transformation was therefore less an act of invention than of synchronization. Avena built the one object that the tomb's three centuries of pilgrimage had never required and the image's three decades of circulation made indispensable, and the identification, an antiquarian guess of the age of wigs, completed itself in the age of the newsreel.

## The word balcony never appears in the play

The scene that fills the courtyard with cameras is misnamed, and the misnaming begins in the text's absence. In Act 2 Scene 2, the orchard scene that stage tradition rebranded as the balcony scene, the early texts give the heroine a window and a position above the stage, and they give her lover, below in the Capulet orchard, a line that names the window outright: "But soft! what light through yonder window breaks?" (2.2.2). The stage directions of the early editions bring her on above, at a window, and the dialogue keeps the architecture at that level of generality: a window, a height, a light. The word balcony occurs nowhere in the 1597 quarto, nowhere in the fuller 1599 quarto, nowhere in the First Folio of 1623. It is not that the play's first printers carelessly dropped a term the author used. The word itself had only lately entered English from Italian, and the projecting stone gallery it names belonged to Italian building practice, not to the London stage's vocabulary for the scene. The scene has no balcony because the text builds its night encounter out of three elements only: darkness, a window, and a voice.

The balcony entered the story's record through adaptation. When Thomas Otway rebuilt the tale as The History and Fall of Caius Marius in 1680, transplanting the lovers to ancient Rome and renaming the heroine Lavinia, the printed text gave English drama its early direction for a balcony in this story, and the stage picture hardened from there. Eighteenth-century productions regularized the feature; illustrators drew it; the scene acquired its modern title, the balcony scene, a name no early edition knows. By the time guidebooks began sending travellers to Verona, the balcony had become the scene's defining piece of furniture in the minds of readers, more vivid than the window the text actually specifies, because a balcony can be drawn, photographed, and built, and a voice at an unspecified window cannot. A full anatomy of the scene's text and staging is available in [the scene-by-scene account of the balcony scene, Act 2 Scene 2](/2023/07/21/balcony-scene-act-2-scene-2-analysis/), which tracks what the quartos actually direct. The consequence for Verona is direct: when the city came to build its shrine, the feature it had to supply was the one the illustrators and the stage had invented, because that was the feature its visitors were travelling to see.

### Where does the heroine stand in Act 2 Scene 2, if not on a balcony?

She stands at a window, above the stage, in the wording the early texts support. The directions place her above, the dialogue names a window, and her lover below reads the light in that window as the dawn: "It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2.2-3). No platform, railing, or gallery is specified.

The balcony is a contribution of later staging and illustration, naturalized so completely that the scene itself is named after furniture the text never mentions, and the naturalization reached the point where school editions print the scene's title with the balcony in it, teaching the substitution before the text has a chance to correct it.

Consider what the substitution cost the scene, because the courtyard at Via Cappello inherits the cost in stone. The text's arrangement is acoustical before it is visual: a girl thinking aloud at a window, overheard by a man she cannot see, in a garden where discovery means death. The drama lives in the asymmetry, her unselfconscious speech against his concealment, and the window serves that asymmetry perfectly, framing a face and a voice while withholding the body. A balcony dissolves the asymmetry into a picture. It gives the heroine a stage, turns her soliloquy into an aria delivered to an audience below, and converts the orchard into an auditorium with Romeo as its first spectator. Stage practice made the trade centuries before Verona did, and made it for sound theatrical reasons: the picture is beautiful, legible at distance, and endlessly reproducible. Verona's restorers were not corrupting a pure text. They were building, in masonry, the version of the scene that the theatre and the illustrated book had already substituted for the text, and their balcony is best understood as a piece of stage scenery that escaped the theatre and took root in a street.

The play's own language, read closely, even resists the architecture Verona supplied. The lovers' night meeting is saturated with the imagery of height and peril, of walls scaled and watchmen feared: "With love's light wings did I o'erperch these walls" (2.2.66), Romeo tells her, and the orchard walls are the scene's real architecture, high and hard to climb, the boundary between the street's feud and the garden's truce. A balcony projecting from the house into the courtyard air belongs to a different spatial logic, the logic of display rather than trespass. The Verona balcony faces inward, over a courtyard that functions as a pit of spectators, and the visitor who stands beneath it occupies, whether the fact is registered or not, the position of the audience at a performance, not the position of a trespasser in an orchard. The shrine restages the scene as theatre and then invites the crowd to forget that theatre is what it is. That forgetting is the site's central transaction, and the rest of this article keeps returning to it: the Casa works by being honest scenery and dishonest history at the same time.

## Montecchi and Cappelletti: Dante's factions, not Verona's families

The house's false surname rests on a deeper confusion, and clearing it requires going behind the play to the poem that supplied its names. In the sixth canto of the Purgatorio, Dante turns on the absent emperor and summons him to survey an Italy ruined by faction: "Vieni a veder Montecchi e Cappelletti" (Purgatorio 6.106), come and see the Montecchi and the Cappelletti, families already brought low, in the lines that follow, by the feuds of the age. The pairing is the seed of everything that followed. Commentary on the passage, from the earliest glossators onward, identifies the two names as opposites in the great Guelf and Ghibelline division of Italy, and it places them in different cities. The Montecchi were a power in Verona, Ghibelline in allegiance, broken after the death of Ezzelino and exiled from their city in the 1260s. The Cappelletti were a faction of Cremona, Guelf in allegiance, whose fortunes Dante's commentators trace through uprisings, banishment in 1249, and destruction by their Cremonese rivals the Barbarasi. Dante yoked the two names as a rhetorical pair, an emblem of Italian self-slaughter, not as neighbours, and nothing in the poem or its commentary makes them enemies of each other, in Verona or anywhere else.

The scholarship on this point is old, settled, and blunt. Olin H. Moore, whose study of the legend's origins appeared in Speculum in 1930 and grew into the standard monograph on the tale's Italian prehistory, examined the records and concluded that Montecchi and Cappelletti were the nicknames of political parties, not the names of private houses: the families of Montague and Capulet who figure in the play, he wrote, never resided in Verona, and in fact never existed at all. Moore traced the terms as badges. The Cappelletti, on his showing, took their name from the small hats or caps the faction wore as its insignia, cappelletti being the diminutive, little hats, while the Montecchi name survived as a party label in Verona long after the family behind it, real enough in the twelfth century on Luigi Simeoni's evidence, had vanished from the city's politics. Two party badges, from two cities, yoked by a poet's rhetoric: that is the whole historical substance behind the most famous family feud in literature.

### Did two families named Montecchi and Capuleti ever feud in Verona?

No. The Montecchi were a Veronese power of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries and then a Ghibelline party label; the Cappelletti were a Guelf faction of Cremona, ruined by their Cremonese enemies. No record places the two in conflict with each other, in Verona or elsewhere.

Dante names them side by side as twin symptoms of Italy's factional disease, paired for rhetoric rather than geography, and the feud between their supposed houses was invented by the storytellers who borrowed his line and needed the two names in one city, at one another's throats.

The borrowing was the work of Luigi da Porto, the Vicentine soldier and writer whose novella of the two noble lovers, composed in the 1520s and published after his death in 1530 and 1531, gave the tale its durable form. Da Porto set the story in Verona under Bartolomeo della Scala, named his heroine's family Cappelletti and his hero's Montecchi, and presented the whole as recovered history, a true account of events in his adopted city's past. The names came to him ready-made as a pair, stamped with Dante's authority, and he localized them with a storyteller's confidence: since a Montecchi party had genuinely belonged to Verona, the fiction could annex the city for both houses. Matteo Bandello's retelling of 1554 amplified the tale, Pierre Boaistuau carried it into French in 1559, and Arthur Brooke's English poem of 1562 and William Painter's prose version delivered it to the young dramatist in London. Shakespeare's Capulet is thus the end of a chain of transmissions in which an Italian party badge from Cremona became, by stages, a Veronese surname, an English family name, and at last, by a final punning slide, the name of an innkeeping family on the Via Cappello whose carved hat seemed to confirm everything. The layers of the error are worth holding apart, because the house's claim quietly collapses all of them into one: it asks a resemblance between Cappello and Capuleti to certify a descent from a Capulet who never existed, in a family that never existed, in a feud that never happened.

Against this record, the persistence of the opposite claim in official places is itself a document. Verona's own tourism portal, in its account of the city, asserts that two families named Montecchi and Capuleti, or more precisely Cappelletti, did indeed exist in Verona, and points to Dante's mention as corroboration. Moore's refutation of exactly this assertion has been in print since 1930, and the Cremonese evidence behind it is older still. The adjudication between the portal and the scholarship is not close, and it is taken up in full in the critics' section below; here the point is narrower. The house at Via Cappello depends for its identity on a chain in which every link is a documented error, and the chain's final link, the hat in the keystone, is the frailest of all, since the Cappello family's arms certify only the Cappello. There is even a bleak symmetry in the evidence: the Cappelletti of Cremona owed their party name, on Moore's account, to little hats worn as a badge, so the story's supposed Veronese family was named for hats in the history books, and the house that claims the heroine was chosen for a hat carved in stone. Coincidence could hardly have arranged a more elegant trap, and the city walked into it with its eyes open, because the trap was exactly what the pilgrimage economy required. For the setting's own documentary history, the fuller account of [how Verona became the play's city, and what the city gave the tale](/2022/03/04/romeo-and-juliet-setting-verona-explained/) separates the same layers in the play's geography itself.

## Three names and a hat: the onomastic chain

Because the house's claim reduces, at the last analysis, to a sequence of similar sounds, the sequence deserves to be spelled out with the care usually reserved for a stemma of manuscripts. The first name is Cappello, the family on Via Cappello: an ordinary Italian surname of the kind that arises from trades, places, and emblems, borne in Verona by a line of property holders and innkeepers, and written in stone over their courtyard arch in the form of its meaning, a hat. Canting arms of this type, in which the shield pictures the name, were a standard heraldic joke of the Italian communes, and the keystone is a good specimen of the genre: it says Cappello the way a rebus says its word, and it says nothing else. The second name is Cappelletti, the Cremona faction of Dante's line: a party badge whose own etymology, on Moore's showing, also runs through headwear, the little caps worn as the faction's distinguishing sign. The third name is the one Da Porto fastened on his heroine's house, Cappelletti again, borrowed from Dante's pair and reassigned to Verona as a family; Bandello's retelling smoothed the word toward Capelletti, Brooke's English poem naturalized it as Capulet, and the play fixed the anglicized form in the world's ear. Between the first name and the third there is no documentary relation whatsoever. The Cappello of Verona were not the Cappelletti of Cremona; the Cappelletti of Cremona were not a family but a faction; the faction was never in Verona; and the heroine's house, in the novella that invented her address, is a literary property with no more existence than its owner.

The identification at Via Cappello therefore requires its believer to accept three substitutions in a row: that a surname resembling a faction's nickname establishes kinship, that a faction's nickname establishes a family, and that a family invented by a novelist establishes a residence in a house owned by namesakes of the nickname. Each substitution is the kind that flourishes in oral tradition and dies in an archive. What protected the chain from archival scrutiny for two centuries was the hat, because the carved arms gave the first substitution a physical exhibit: an inquirer shown the keystone felt the argument had been answered by the building itself, when the building had answered a different question. The keystone is genuine evidence, and it is evidence for the Cappello. It becomes evidence for the Capulets only after the chain's three substitutions have been granted in advance, which is to say it becomes evidence only for those who need none. Heritage practice has a word for objects of this class, documents whose authenticity is real and whose relevance is manufactured, and the Casa's keystone may stand as the type specimen of the class: a true carving conscripted into a false proof.

The point is not pedantic, because the same homophonic method, applied consistently, would people half the old town with literature. Verona holds streets and houses named for families whose names brush against half the dramatis personae of the Italian novelle, and the city has wisely declined to build shrines at most of them. The Cappello house was chosen because the resemblance served a story the city already owned through Da Porto and Dante, and because the building, an inn on a pilgrim route between the tomb and the market square, stood where the traffic could use it. Resemblance was the warrant; convenience was the reason; the hat was the seal. A colder age might have left the matter there, as a footnote in a guidebook. The age that bought the house and built the balcony had other uses for footnotes, and it is time to watch the uses being made.

## 1939 and 1940: the campaign that built the balcony

Antonio Avena directed Verona's civic museums for the middle decades of the twentieth century, and his restoration philosophy shaped the visible past of the whole city. At Castelvecchio in the 1920s he had already demonstrated the method: a fortress of mixed and damaged fabric was recomposed into a coherent medieval castle, its towers, battlements, and interiors arranged to present a single persuasive period picture. The method was the period's Italian practice, restoration as composition, in which the restorer's duty ran to the monument's legibility and emotional effect rather than to the archaeology of its surviving fabric. Surviving stones could be moved, supplemented, and set in invented contexts, provided the result looked like the past the city wished to remember. When Avena turned to the house on Via Cappello in the campaign the city's own portal dates to 1939 and 1940, he applied the Castelvecchio method to a literary brief, and the brief demanded one feature above all: the scene his visitors had crossed Europe to stand inside required a balcony, and the house had none.

The works recomposed the courtyard facade as a period picture. The brick front was dressed with a Gothic portal, trefoil windows, carved stone dressings drawn from the civic collections, and an external balustrade walkway linking the bodies of the house, each element plausibly medieval and few of them born in those positions. At the centre of the composition, over the courtyard, went the balcony. The city's published account of its construction is specific about the materials: the balcony was assembled from fourteenth-century marble remains that had lain until 1920 in the stores of the Castelvecchio museum, kept as reusable stone in the manner of the time. Other accounts in the reference literature describe the core piece more particularly as the front of an old sarcophagus, a tomb slab of carved marble given a second life as the balcony's face, and the two descriptions converge on the same object seen at different distances: old funerary marble, centuries in store, cut and completed with side pieces and a balustrade for its new role. The design's inspiration, in the municipal descriptions of the work, was the hanging tombs of the Scaliger lords, the arche scaligere whose marble canopies still rise over a nearby churchyard, so that the lovers' balcony was consciously modelled on the architecture of Veronese tombs. No designer could have chosen a more apt source, and none more revealing: the scene of the play's declaration was built, deliberately, in the idiom of the play's ending.

The dating of the balcony carries a small, instructive division in the sources, and honesty requires it to be stated rather than smoothed. The city's tourism portal assigns Avena's restoration, balcony included, to 1939 and 1940. A wide run of reference accounts, including the Smithsonian's folklife magazine in its survey of the Verona cult, dates the balcony's addition to 1936, within a restoration of the later 1930s more loosely bounded. The division is between an official campaign date and a received date, and the archive-minded reader will take the portal's 1939 and 1940 for the campaign's completion while noting that preparation and partial works belonged to the decade's second half. What no source disputes is the century: the balcony is a work of the late 1930s, separated from the play by roughly three and a half centuries and from the house's medieval fabric by six. The received 1936 date has its own poetry, since 1936 was also the year of Hollywood's first prestige film of the play, George Cukor's production with Norma Shearer and Leslie Howard, but poetry is not evidence, and the official dating stands in this article as the safer record.

### Which parts of the famous facade did Avena's campaign compose?

Nearly every feature the courtyard visitor photographs. The Gothic portal, the trefoil windows, the stone dressings, the external walkway, and the balcony itself were placed or recomposed in the 1939 and 1940 campaign from genuine old materials held in the civic stores. The medieval walls behind the composition are the house's own.

Avena's facade is best described as a collage of authentic fragments in invented positions, which is why it photographs as medieval and reads, on inspection, as a montage: the eye accepts each stone, and only the plan betrays the century that arranged the stones.

The composed house was formally presented to the city at the campaign's completion in 1940, in the municipality's own framing as the House of Juliet, and then history intervened in the shrine's biography as it intervened in everything else. The war years emptied the courtyards of Italy, and the Casa waited out the emergency as a set without an audience, its balcony unphotographed, its identity suspended between a regime that had sponsored the city's self-display and a republic that inherited the display as patrimony. The postwar decades supplied the audience at a scale the makers had not imagined: the accounts of the house's management record its steady growth in popularity after the Second World War, as motor tourism, the package itinerary, and the international fame of the filmed versions converted a municipal curiosity into a fixture of the European circuit. By the time the interior was furnished and the museum opened in 1973, the courtyard's ritual was already mature, and the museum formalized a cult that the street had built. The sequence matters for the verdict: the shrine was not imposed on a reluctant public by a propaganda ministry and then abandoned at the regime's fall. It was adopted, across a change of regimes and generations, by the freest jury a cultural object can face, the travelling public of half a century, and adopted in full knowledge, available to any inquirer, of the dates on its stones.

The interior received its furnishing a generation later, and it continued the same method by other hands. The house opened to the public as a museum in 1973, its rooms dressed in the manner of a Renaissance residence, with period furniture, paintings of the lovers by nineteenth-century hands, and ceramics and costume pieces that built the domestic illusion the facade promised. One room took its decoration from Francesco Hayez's celebrated canvas of 1823, The Last Kiss of Romeo and Juliet, so that the chamber giving on the balcony reproduces a Romantic painting's idea of the heroine's room. The star exhibits of the interior are frankly cinematic: the bed displayed as the heroine's comes from Franco Zeffirelli's film of 1968, which used it in the lovers' chamber, and costumes from the same production complete the dressing. A visitor moving from courtyard to chamber thus passes through three nested fictions, the novelist's Verona, the painter's Verona, and the film director's Verona, each presented in the grammar of a historic house. The building holds them all without strain, because a house museum of this kind is not a container of evidence but a sequence of sets, and its visitors are audience and cast by turns.

Two judgements of Avena's work are possible, and the critics' section below weighs them against each other; the factual core admits no dispute. The campaign did not restore a balcony, uncover a balcony, or preserve a balcony. It created one, from tomb marble and store-room fragments, in the image of the Scaliger tombs, as the keystone of a facade composed for a fictional resident. Every guidebook phrase that softens this, speaking of the balcony as reconstructed or rebuilt, misleads: nothing stood in that position to be reconstructed. What the campaign preserved was the medieval carcass of an inn. What it invented was the identity that filled the carcass, and the invention was executed with a craftsman's conscience toward materials and a propagandist's conscience toward facts. The stones are old. Their arrangement is an argument. The argument is that a girl named Capulet once leaned where brides and school parties lean for photographs, and the argument is false.

## Tomb marble: the balcony's material confession

The balcony's fabric deserves a slower reading than its dating, because the materials confess what the presentation conceals. Restoration practice in Avena's Italy treated the civic museum stores as quarries of authenticity: fragments of demolished churches, palaces, and tombs accumulated in the lapidary collections, and a restorer composing a period facade drew on them as a painter draws on a pigment box. The Casa's balcony came out of that system. The city's portal states the provenance with bureaucratic calm: marble remains of the fourteenth century, held at Castelvecchio as reusable stone until 1920, assembled into the feature the courtyard required. The reference literature's more vivid version, in which the balcony's front is the slab of an old sarcophagus, and the municipal-derived descriptions, in which the design follows the hanging tombs of the Scaliger lords, are best read as three descriptions of one object at three levels of candour: stored medieval marble, of funerary origin or funerary styling, cut, completed with new side pieces and a balustrade in matching stone, and mounted as the centrepiece of a love shrine. The accounts divide on the sarcophagus's century, fourteenth in the portal's phrasing for the marble generally, seventeenth in the Smithsonian's sentence for the coffin specifically, and the division is recorded here rather than resolved, because the surviving descriptions do not permit resolution and because the point of substance survives either dating: the balcony is spolia, old stone in a new argument.

Spolia is the exact term, borrowed from the study of late antique building, where columns and carvings from older monuments were set into new walls to borrow their authority. The borrowing at Via Cappello is unusually legible. The marble's age is real, and it radiates the authenticity the composition needs, while its original context, a tomb or a store-room of tomb fragments, is edited out of the presentation. A visitor told that the balcony is made of fourteenth-century marble hears a certificate of age for the balcony as a whole; the certificate covers the atoms and not the molecule. The same technique governs the facade around it, portals and windows of genuine old stone in invented positions, and it explains the site's peculiar power over visitors who pride themselves on detecting fakes. The material tests all pass. The stone is old, the carving is old, the walls are old. Only the arrangement is young, and arrangement, in architecture, is everything: it is the difference between a sentence and a bag of words. Avena's courtyard is a sentence composed in a vocabulary of genuine fragments, and its statement, that a heroine stood here, is the one thing its materials cannot certify.

Seen at the courtyard's distance, the construction resolves into its parts. The platform rides on stone supports that spring from the facade, corbels of the kind Veronese builders had used for centuries, and the door from the first-floor chamber gives onto it directly, so that the feature functions as architecture and not as appliqué: a person can stand on it, lean on its balustrade, and be photographed in the heroine's coordinates. The front that carries the composition is the old slab, its carved face worked with a rank of small Gothic arches in the manner of the city's tomb chests, flanked and completed by the plainer new stone of the side pieces. The ensemble's craftsmanship is the source of its long success, since a flimsier invention would have weathered into obviousness within a generation. Instead the balcony aged with the wall that carries it, its borrowed marble darkening in step with the genuine fabric, until the distinction between the house's own centuries and its added one required an archive to detect. Time, the restorer's accomplice in every composed monument, has been working for the invention since 1940, and works for it still.

The funerary character of the centrepiece completes the confession. The Scaliger arche, the canopied tombs that rise over the churchyard of Santa Maria Antica a few streets away, are Verona's signature monuments of lordly death, marble beds on columns under Gothic canopies, and a balcony designed in their idiom carries their grammar into the courtyard: elevation, display, and commemoration of the dead. Whether Avena's workshop chose the tomb idiom by deliberation, as the municipal descriptions suggest, or absorbed it from the store-room's contents, the result stands as the site's one involuntary truth. The play's lovers are united, in the fiction, only in a tomb, and the Prince's closing vow fills Verona with their statues; the shrine, reaching for the scene of their courtship, built it out of the furniture of their burial. Guidebook language calls the balcony the place where love was declared. Its marble says, to anyone who reads materials, that the declaration and the burial were always the same story, and that the city's craftsmen, assembling a love scene from a coffin, understood the play's structure better than the shrine's marketing ever admitted.

## Restoration on trial: Boito, Avena, and Brandi

Avena's campaign has a defence available that the debunkers rarely grant it, and the defence is chronological. Italian restoration theory had condemned his method in advance, and then condemned it again afterward, but during his working lifetime the method was the establishment's own. Camillo Boito, the architect and theorist whose principles of 1883 became the first Italian charter of restoration, had argued that a monument is a document: every stratum of its fabric is evidence, additions must declare themselves as additions, and the restorer who completes a building in a style it never finished commits a forgery against the archive. Judged by Boito's charter, the courtyard facade of 1939 and 1940 is an offence in every particular, old stones conscripted into a composition they never formed, a feature invented and then aged by its materials. But Boito's charter was a programme before it was a practice, and the Italy of the ventennio preferred the opposite doctrine, in which the monument served the nation's image of itself and the restorer acted as a composer of patriotic scenery. Avena worked inside that licence, for a regime and a municipality that measured restoration by the legibility and splendour of the result. His Verona of composed castles and heroines' houses is not a private eccentricity. It is the period's official aesthetics applied with unusual talent, and the balcony is its most exported product.

The reckoning arrived with Cesare Brandi, whose Teoria del restauro of 1963 refounded Italian practice on the principle that restoration may address only the work's material, never invent its image, and must respect the historical instance of the object as strictly as its aesthetic one. Under Brandi's theory, and under the Venice Charter of 1964 that carried the same principles into international doctrine, the Casa campaign stands as a textbook falsification: a conjectural feature, added for narrative effect, executed in genuinely old material precisely so that the conjecture would pass as survival. The Italian state that adopted those doctrines inherited the balcony as a protected monument, and has kept it, cleaned it, ticketed it, and defended it ever since, which is the final irony of the theory's triumph. The falsification proved too beloved to correct and too honest, in its way, to hide: it had become, by the time the charters condemned its method, a historical stratum in its own right, a document of 1939 that a Boitan restorer of the present day would be obliged to preserve as evidence. The charters thus acquit the balcony in the act of condemning its makers, since the same doctrine that forbids inventing a past forbids erasing one, and the invention is part of the record.

This double judgement is the precise legal and theoretical form of the verdict this article reached by the literary road. The campaign was illegitimate as restoration and is untouchable as history. Avena forged a document, and the forgery has aged into a document of forgery, complete with its maker's signature in every composed stone. The visitor who learns to read the courtyard in both registers at once, as a medieval carcass and a twentieth-century argument, possesses the whole truth of the site, and the tragedy of the site's presentation is that its guardians still encourage the single-register reading, the one in which the argument disappears into the carcass and the balcony stands as a survivor instead of what it is, a survivor's costume sewn from a tomb.

## The courtyard cult: statue, notes, and the traffic of a shrine

A shrine is known by its practices, and the courtyard at Via Cappello developed a complete liturgy within a generation of the balcony's construction. Its elements are fixed and repeatable: entry through the passage beneath the Cappello hat, a first look upward at the balcony, a photograph taken from the heroine's supposed viewpoint or beneath it, contact with the bronze figure in the courtyard, and a written deposit left on the passage walls. The bronze is the courtyard's second great anachronism. Nereo Costantini's statue of the heroine was placed in the courtyard in 1972, a gift to the city associated with the Lions Club, and it presents the girl as the twentieth century imagined her, in flowing dress, one hand raised to her breast. Decades of ritual touching wore and finally damaged the figure: in 2014 the original was withdrawn into museum keeping and a replica took its place under the balcony, so that the object pilgrims touch is a copy of a 1972 imagining of a girl who never lived, a triple remove that disturbs the ritual not at all. The statue's own strange career, and the luck custom attached to it, belong to a separate examination in this series; here it matters as the courtyard's altar piece, the point where the architecture's fiction condenses into a body that can be touched.

The written deposits are the courtyard's most revealing feature, because they show the fiction being used rather than merely viewed. The entrance passage and the courtyard walls accumulated, across decades, a sediment of names, dates, declarations, and pleas, scrawled in marker, scratched into plaster, and fixed with chewing gum in the form of folded notes wedged between bricks. The municipality has alternated between tolerating the sediment as part of the attraction and moving against it as damage: removable panels were provided to receive the writing, cleanings periodically stripped the walls back to brick, and the city set a fine of five hundred euros for defacing the monument, a figure reported in the press coverage of the clean-up campaigns. The cycle of inscription and erasure repeats without resolution, because the writing is not vandalism in the writers' own understanding but correspondence. A large share of the deposits are messages to the heroine herself, left at her address by visitors who also send her letters through the post, a correspondence answered for decades by the volunteer secretaries of [the Juliet Club, whose work with the letters to Juliet](/2029/06/29/juliet-club-letters-to-juliet/) is the institutional half of the courtyard's paper cult. The wall notes are the same impulse at shorter range: mail delivered by hand to a house that exists because mail kept arriving for its resident.

### Do the visitors who crowd the courtyard believe the house is genuine?

The evidence of their behaviour says the question misses the point. Visitors photograph a balcony the official descriptions openly date to 1939 and 1940, touch a statue whose replacement by a replica was public knowledge, and write to a heroine no record supports. The behaviour is consistent, informed, and entirely willing.

The literary-tourism scholarship reads this not as gullibility but as a practised double awareness, in which the site is used as a stage for the visitor's own story. Belief, in the courtyard, is a tool taken up for the duration of a ritual and set down again at the gate, and the tool works precisely because it is never quite mistaken for a fact.

The courtyard also functions as the anchor of a wider civic geography that the municipality assembled around the tale. A few streets away stands the house assigned to Romeo, a medieval palazzo in private hands, viewable only from the street and never developed as a museum, an asymmetry that [the mapping of Romeo's house and Verona's tourist geography](/2029/07/20/romeos-house-verona-tourism/) examines in full: the heroine received a shrine because her scene supplied the icon, while the hero, who owns no comparable architectural moment in the popular image, received a plaque's worth of attention. Across the city, the tomb at San Francesco al Corso completes the itinerary, and between house and tomb the visitor walks a route that the nineteenth-century pilgrims established and the twentieth-century city paved, signed, and staffed. Weddings entered the liturgy as its most official rite: the house's Capulet Hall hosts civil ceremonies, and couples marry under the auspices of a marriage that the play itself shows ending in a vault within four days. The irony is not hidden and not felt as irony by the participants, because the site's Verona is the city of the balcony scene only, a single act lifted from the tragedy and given an address, while the tomb across town keeps the ending in a separate building, as if the two halves of the story could be visited on different tickets and never reconciled.

The shrine's success eventually forced its guardians to ration it. For most of the museum's life the courtyard stood open from the street, free to any passer-by, and the crush at peak hours reduced the ritual to a shuffle past the statue under the balcony's sightline. In December 2025 the municipality ended the free regime and made courtyard entry a paid, pre-booked ticket, a decision the city framed as a defence of the monument against its own popularity; from April 2026 the entrance was routed through the Teatro Nuovo on Piazzetta Navona, with the old archway on Via Cappello serving as the exit, so that the pilgrimage acquired a turnstile and a one-way flow. The house that a pun created processes its devotees with the crowd engineering of a stadium, and the change belongs in the building's biography as the final stage of the identification: first an inn, then an argument, then a set, then a shrine, and at last a managed attraction whose guardians meter the devotion they spent a century cultivating.

Attendance figures belong to the economy of the site, which is a separate article's ground, but one municipal number belongs in the record of the house itself, because it measures the shrine's draw in the city's own accounting. The Verona museums' annual report for 2016 counted 329,000 paid admissions to the house, a figure cited in Francesco Marcorin's study of the city's crafted identity, and the same reporting placed the house second only to the Roman Arena among the city's museums by share of museum visitors. Paid admissions understate the courtyard's traffic in the years when entry to the courtyard stood free, and the Smithsonian's account speaks of hundreds of thousands visiting the house each year within some two million visitors to the city. The orders of magnitude agree across sources: a minor medieval inn, acquired for its name, draws crowds that major monuments in larger cities would envy, and it draws them to see a balcony built within living memory of the motor age.

## The courtyard as auditorium: reading the composed facade

Stand in the courtyard with the record in mind and the composition resolves into stagecraft. The entrance passage, the androne under the Cappello arch, is a compressed threshold of the kind theatres use to separate street from auditorium: a low, dark, note-papered corridor that strips the entrant of the city's noise and delivers a single framed view at its end. The courtyard itself is small, enclosed, and vertical, a pit perhaps fifteen paces across, and every position in it commands the same sightline, upward to the balcony at first-floor level on the inner facade. No competing feature draws the eye. The medieval fabric of the side walls serves as wings; the composed front, with its Gothic portal, its trefoil windows, its external balustrade walkway stitching the upper rooms together, reads as a backdrop flat executed in masonry; and the balcony projects from the backdrop at the exact height a stage manager would choose, close enough for faces, high enough for the geometry of longing. The room that serves the balcony from inside, dressed after Hayez's painting, is the tiring-house of the set, where the visitor cast as the heroine waits for an entrance. A working theatre could not improve the arrangement, because a working theatre designed it, across the long collaboration between stage practice and Avena's workshop.

The street facade tells the complementary truth by saying almost nothing. From Via Cappello the house presents the doorway, the passage, and upper windows of a prosperous medieval front, legible as old and mute as to identity. The composition faces inward because the audience it serves is captive: the performance is for those who have entered, and the courtyard's enclosure guarantees that every spectator sees the set from the lover's position, in the round of a shared ritual, rather than from the multiple viewpoints a public square would allow. Even the note-covered walls find their place in the reading, as the graffiti of a house that the audience has been invited to treat as its own prompt book, the margins filled by generations of understudies. None of this spatial intelligence required the identity to be true. It required only that the building be old, the courtyard be small, and the balcony be built, and the intelligence is why the site outdraws genuine monuments of greater age and consequence across Italy: it is not a house that visitors inspect but a scene they enter, and entry, not inspection, is what the pilgrimage sells.

## The interior as anthology: rooms that quote other fictions

The museum inside the house completes the site's method, and a walk through its rooms is a walk through the tale's afterlife arranged as furniture. The bed displayed as the heroine's is a prop from Zeffirelli's film of 1968, built for a camera and a pair of teenage actors in a studio-built chamber, and its presence in a medieval tower house is defended by no claim stronger than association: the most famous screen Juliet slept in it, in fiction, and the fiction is the house's whole charter. The chamber that serves the balcony takes its painted decoration from Hayez's canvas of 1823, so a visitor posing for the ritual photograph is framed, whether the fact is known or not, inside a Romantic painter's composition of the lovers' last kiss. The walls elsewhere carry nineteenth-century paintings of the story, including work by Cosroe Dusi, and the costume pieces and ceramics fill the remaining space with a generalized Renaissance that belongs to no century and every schoolroom. Room by room, the interior quotes: a film quotes a play, a painting quotes a novella, a furnishing scheme quotes the idea of the past itself, and the house, the only genuinely old thing in the sequence, lends its walls to the anthology as a lending library lends its rooms to other men's books.

Anthology is the right word, and it distinguishes the Casa from a simple fake. A forgery claims to be the original; the interior claims only to gather the versions, and its curatorial logic, accumulation by celebrity, is the logic of the play's whole cultural afterlife compressed into a floor plan. The failure is one of labelling, again: an anthology presented as a residence, versions shelved as relics. A visitor told plainly that the bed is Zeffirelli's, the room Hayez's, the balcony Avena's, and the walls the Cappellos' would lose nothing of the visit's pleasure and gain the site's actual subject, which is the four-century collaboration that built the heroine a home. The information exists in the official descriptions, scattered and unemphasized, like a confession filed in a drawer. The house museum that confessed on its walls would be the first of its kind, and the most honest literary shrine in Europe.

## A visitor's audit: five stops, five different centuries

The separation the table performs on paper can be performed on foot, because the courtyard arranges its evidence in a sequence, and each object in the sequence belongs to a different century and a different order of truth. The first stop is the keystone in the entrance arch: fourteenth-century carving, the Cappello hat, genuine in material and date, and genuine also in its testimony, which concerns innkeepers. Nothing at this stop is false, and nothing here concerns the play. The second stop is the courtyard sightline itself, the composed inner facade: medieval walls carrying a 1939 arrangement, the first object in the audit whose truth is double, old in substance and modern in every decision that gives the substance meaning. The third stop is the balcony, examined at the distance the courtyard allows: the audit's centre, fourteenth-century marble in a twentieth-century argument, tomb stone in a love scene, the single object on the site whose fame is inverse to its authenticity. The fourth stop is the statue's plinth: a 1972 bronze replaced by its own replica in 2014, the only object in the courtyard whose original and copy have exchanged places within living memory, and therefore the audit's clearest lesson in how little the ritual depends on the relic. The fifth stop is the passage wall on the way out, papered with the handwriting of the visitors themselves: the one stratum of the site that is exactly what it appears to be, an unmediated archive of the cult, added daily, periodically destroyed by the cult's own guardians.

Walked in that order, the audit teaches the visitor to sort the site's claims by touch. The medieval stops testify about Verona. The twentieth-century stops testify about the play's audience. The wall testifies about the visitor personally, since a share of its handwriting is fresh enough to include a name the visitor might add. No other literary shrine in Europe compresses its own historiography into so small a space or arranges the strata in so legible an order, which is the strongest argument for the site's preservation exactly as it stands, false labels and all: the courtyard is a syllabus in the archaeology of a myth, and its errors, dated and documented, are the syllabus's most instructive entries. The tragedy, in the Prince's closing summary, left Verona with "a glooming peace" (5.3) and a pair of promised statues. The peace the city made with the story was of another kind: it built the statues, staffed the shrine, and left the evidence of the building lying in plain sight for any visitor willing to read the stops in order.

## Fraud, brand, or pilgrimage: the critics weighed

Four accounts of the Casa di Giulietta compete in the literature, and they do not agree about what kind of object stands at Via Cappello 23. The oldest is the debunker's. Olin H. Moore's archival work of 1930 established that the feud's families were party badges rather than Veronese houses, and the debunking tradition that descends from Moore treats the Casa as the monumental form of a scholarly error: a false genealogy, carved over a door, drawing crowds to a mistake. On this account the site's offence is epistemic. It teaches a falsehood in stone, and the falsehood is not trivial, because it converts a poem's machinery into counterfeit history and sends visitors home with the counterfeit in their photograph albums. The debunker's evidence is unanswerable on its own ground. No link in the chain from Dante's line to the Cappello keystone survives inspection, and the city portal's continued assertion that the two families existed in Verona shows the counterfeit still circulating through official channels a lifetime after its refutation.

The second account is the historian's, and its sharpest modern statement is Francesco Marcorin's, in his study of Verona's crafted identities for the Italian Academy at Columbia University. Marcorin reads the Casa as an instrument of civic self-fashioning, the most successful exhibit in a long municipal practice of composing the city's past into a marketable image. His sentence on the balcony deserves to stand as the epigraph of the whole question: the legendary balcony, he writes, did not exist until 1940, when the entire museum was created to launch a new brand in the world, Verona, the city of lovers. On this account the relevant agent is not the gullible tourist but the calculating city, and the relevant date is not 1595 or 1351 but 1940, the year a brand acquired its logo in marble. Marcorin's statistics give the brand its measure: the 329,000 paid admissions of the 2016 municipal report, the house's second place behind the Arena in the city's museum traffic. The historian's account corrects the debunker's aim, since a brand is not exactly a lie told to the deceived but an image offered to the willing, yet it leaves the offering itself unjudged, as if the city's intention settled the matter.

The third account is the cultural critic's, stated for the Verona case by Lara Stich in her 2025 study of the tragedy and its tourism in the Shakespeare Seminar. Stich traces the heroine's conversion into what she calls a symbolic persona and, in the city's branding, an ambassador: by the late 1980s, on the evidence she draws from Tessari's work on the city's promotion, the fictional girl had been installed as Verona's emblem and the city of love formula as its strategy, so that the play circulates in the tourist economy less as a drama than as a reservoir of images and emotions, endlessly repackaged. The commodification account sees the courtyard's liturgy with a colder eye than Marcorin's: the notes, the photographs, the wedding hall, and the balcony itself are SKUs in a catalogue of purchasable feelings, and the reservoir metaphor explains why the tragedy keeps being drained of its ending. A reservoir of love imagery cannot stock a double suicide, so the ending is warehoused across the city in the tomb, and the house sells the second act alone. The critic's account is the strongest of the three on the site's grammar, and the weakest on its visitors, who appear in it as consumers of feelings rather than as readers performing a rite.

The fourth account is the literary tourist's defence, and its author is Nicola Watson, whose study of literary tourism and whose essay on the heroine's tomb in the Bigliazzi and Calvi volume on the play and civic life give the Verona sites their most sympathetic scholarly reading. Watson calls Shakespeare's Verona a product of wishful thinking, and treats the wishfulness not as a defect to be exposed but as the phenomenon itself: literary pilgrimage, in her account, is a mode of reading, in which the traveller materializes a relationship with a text by standing where its persons stood, or where they would have stood. The pilgrim at a fictional shrine is not a failed historian but a successful reader, and the site's falsity to the archive is beside the point, because its truth is to the reading experience that built it. Watson's Verona, in which the itinerary runs from tomb to house and the fiction is knowingly inhabited, accommodates what the other accounts cannot: the plain fact that a great many visitors arrive already aware that the heroine is fictional, announce the awareness cheerfully, and weep at the balcony anyway.

The adjudication must be by parts, because the four accounts answer four different questions, and the Casa is guilty or innocent count by count. On the facts, Moore wins outright, and every later account quietly concedes his verdict while changing the subject. On the making of the site, Marcorin wins: the documentary record of purchase, campaign, and brand fits his frame at every point, and Avena's composition method is civic image-crafting by definition. On the site's economy of feeling, Stich wins the grammar and loses the congregation: the reservoir account correctly predicts which half of the play the house sells, but it cannot explain why the purchasers so often behave as congregants rather than customers, leaving notes that are prayers and letters that are confessions. On the visitors, Watson wins, and her victory carries the verdict's centre: the courtyard's rituals are a form of reading, public, embodied, and social, and they no more require the house to be genuine than a performance requires the stage to be Verona. The site's real offence survives all four defences, and it is the debunker's point narrowed to its defensible core. The offence is not that the city built the shrine, nor that readers use it. The offence is the residue of official suggestion, in portal prose and guidebook echo, that the stones certify the story: the families existed, the house was hers, the balcony is where it happened. A shrine to reading needs no such certification, and a city confident in its brand could print the dates 1939 and 1940 on the courtyard wall in letters as large as the heroine's name. That it does not is the measure of the gap between the Casa as the critics explain it and the Casa as it is sold.

## The InsightCrunch balcony myth-versus-history

The separation this article has argued in prose is set out in one place in the InsightCrunch balcony myth-versus-history, which pairs each claim the courtyard makes, or lets its visitors make, with the documented record that answers it. The table is designed for the use the site's own scholarship recommends: a reader preparing a visit, a lesson, or a presentation does better with the two columns kept apart than with either the guidebook's romance or the debunker's scorn, and assembling the separated claims into slides for that purpose is a natural study task, for which the [ReportMedic PPT viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) offers a clean way to display the comparison. Read down the pairs and the pattern of the whole site emerges: the left-hand column is never a medieval survival and rarely a deliberate falsehood told in words; it is an arrangement of true old stones and true strong feelings into a configuration that asserts what no document asserts. The right-hand column is, in every row, the more interesting story.

| The claim the courtyard makes | The documented record |
| --- | --- |
| Juliet lived in this house | The heroine is a fictional character, formed by Da Porto, Bandello, and Brooke before Shakespeare; no person of her name and story appears in any record |
| The Capulet family owned the house | The Dal Cappello family held it for centuries and ran an inn there; their arms, a hat, are carved on the courtyard keystone |

| The courtyard statue is an old image of Juliet | Nereo Costantini made the bronze in the twentieth century, placed in 1972; the original was retired in 2014 and a replica stands in its place |
| The bedroom shows the heroine's room | The interior was furnished as a museum for the 1973 opening; its famous bed is a prop from Zeffirelli's film of 1968 |
| The house has always drawn pilgrims | It worked as an inn, stables, and tenements for centuries; the identification dates to the eighteenth century and the museum to the twentieth |

Three features of the table repay attention. The first is the distribution of dates on the right: the record's anchors are 1351, the 1520s, 1905, 1939, 1972, and 1973, a chronology in which the twentieth century does nearly all the work and the medieval period supplies only raw material. The second is the direction of borrowing in the balcony row: the shrine's centrepiece is funerary marble, tomb material promoted to love's architecture, which is less an embarrassment than an accidental honesty, since the play's own balcony scene is shadowed from its first performances by the tomb waiting in Act 5, and the play ends with the families promising statues of gold, "For I will raise her statue in pure gold" (5.3) among them, a vow Capulet answers with a matching figure for Romeo. Verona, which cast the heroine in bronze and housed her in a tomb-fronted balcony, has been fulfilling the play's closing stage directions for a century without noticing. The third feature is what the table cannot hold: the left column's claims are believed by almost no one in their literal form and acted on by nearly everyone in their ritual form, and that gap, between assent and practice, is where the Casa actually lives.

## The plaque the courtyard lacks: what an honest presentation would say

Nothing in the record assembled here is secret, which raises the practical question the critics leave behind: what would the Casa look like if its presentation caught up with its archive? The experiment is worth conducting in words, because the site is one act of labelling away from innocence. The keystone would keep its place over the arch, captioned as what it is, the arms of the Cappello innkeepers, with the pun and its consequences stated in a sentence. The balcony would carry its dates, 1939 and 1940, and its materials, tomb marble from the civic stores, in letters no smaller than the heroine's name on the tickets. The bedroom would label its bed as Zeffirelli's and its painted walls as Hayez's, claims of association honestly framed, since association is the house's real stock in trade. And the city's portal would retire the sentence asserting that the heroine's family existed in Verona, replacing a ninety-year-refuted claim with the documented one: that Dante named two factions, that a novelist made them a family, and that Verona, in love with the novel, gave the family a house.

The cost of the honest presentation is the objection usually raised against it, and the objection deserves its answer. Candour, the guardians fear, would break the spell, cheapen the ritual, and tax the devotion the site exists to serve. The site's own evidence refutes the fear. The official descriptions already date the balcony; the visitors already know the heroine is fictional; the statue's replacement was conducted in public and altered no behaviour in the courtyard. Devotion at Via Cappello has survived every disclosure the twentieth century published, because the devotion was never mortgaged to the facts in the first place. What candour would break is not the spell but the alibi, the residual suggestion that the stones vouch for the story, and the alibi is the only part of the enterprise that the scholarship, the charters, and the record combine to condemn. A city that printed the truth on the courtyard wall would lose nothing but a falsehood it no longer needs, and would gain the distinction its brand has always claimed and never earned: the one literary shrine in Europe that trusts its visitors with its own biography.

## Why the myth needed a balcony, and not a window

The question the Casa finally poses is not why Verona lied, but why the lie took this shape. The city owned a genuinely medieval house, a suggestive name, and a carved hat, and it waited three decades after the purchase before acting. The trigger, when it came, was the gap between the house as found and the scene as imagined. Generations of readers had formed the scene from illustrated editions, stage productions, paintings like Hayez's canvas of the last kiss, and, by the 1930s, film, and in every one of those media the encounter had acquired the same furniture: a raised stone gallery from which the heroine leans into the night. A visitor shown a blank courtyard wall, however old, would have experienced the site as a broken promise. The pilgrimage economy runs on recognition, and recognition requires the icon. Avena's campaign is best understood as an act of iconographic compliance: the city rebuilt its property until the property matched the picture its visitors carried in their heads, and the balcony was the picture's centre, because the balcony is the one element of the scene that architecture, unlike poetry, can hand over intact.

A window could never have done the work, for reasons that are practical before they are symbolic. A window is an opening in someone else's wall: it can be looked at, but it cannot be occupied by the visitor, photographed from within, married beneath with any comfort, or distinguished from a dozen other windows on the same facade. A balcony is a small public stage with a built-in auditorium at its feet. It holds one person in the heroine's place and arranges everyone else in the lover's place below, which is to say it casts the crowd, distributing the two roles of the scene to whoever enters the courtyard, without instruction, in seconds. The ritual photographs that pour out of Via Cappello are not records of looking; they are records of casting, couples and school parties dividing themselves between the balcony and the yard to re-perform the geometry of the scene. Avena, a museum man of the theatre's century, may or may not have reasoned the matter through in those terms; the result reasons it for him. The balcony turned spectators into participants, and participation is the one souvenir no shop can sell and no visitor can be refused.

There is a further, quieter reason the balcony and not some other invention became the shrine's heart, and it lies in the play's own economy of images. The tragedy is poor in portable objects: a ring, a dagger, a vial, a rope ladder, none of which anchors a building. Its wealth is vertical, a drama of heights and depths, orchard walls climbed, a girl above and a boy below, a vault entered by torchlight, statues promised at a height above the stage. Of the play's vertical images, only the heroine's elevation is both joyful and architectural, and a city building a shrine must build the joyful image, since nobody erects a monument to a vial. The balcony is the tragedy with the tragedy removed, Act 2 preserved in stone and Acts 3 to 5 left to the tomb across town, and the surgical neatness of that division is the surest sign of design. Verona did not misunderstand the play when it built the shrine. It edited the play, as every age edits it, and built the edit.

The invention also succeeded because it was typologically honest. Projecting stone balconies carried on corbels belonged to the building practice of late medieval and Renaissance Italy; the palazzi of Verona and Florence carry genuine examples, and a balcony on a fourteenth-century Veronese facade offends no law of period architecture. Avena's feature is false for this house and true for its century and city, a counterfeit struck in the correct metal, and the distinction explains its durability under expert scrutiny: architects who would dismiss a plaster fake acknowledge the courtyard's centrepiece as a competent piece of period work, because as period work, in every respect except its own biography, it is one. The play's misfortune, or its luck, was to be illustrated by a culture whose architecture supplied the prop ready-made. An English manor could not have grown this shrine. The balcony had to be invented where balconies were native, and Verona, which had exported the word itself into English in the centuries after the play, received its own export back as a building order: construct the thing the word names, at the address the poem's namesakes suggest, and the readers of the world will do the rest.

Photography sealed the arrangement. Views of the courtyard from the decades before the campaign show an inner front of respectable plainness, a wall with windows, awaiting its meaning; the postcard industry of the years after 1940 made the composed facade, balcony centred, into one of the most reproduced images in Italian tourism, and each reproduction recruited the next season's pilgrims. The feedback loop is the site's true foundation deed: the balcony was built to match a picture, and the picture then multiplied until the built thing and its image became indistinguishable in the public mind, so that visitors arrive less to see the courtyard than to verify a photograph already in their possession and to insert themselves into its frame. A window generates no such loop, since a window cannot anchor a composition. The balcony, symmetrical, elevated, and empty until a visitor mounts it, is a photograph waiting for its subject, and the subject, in millions of holiday albums, is the visitor. Verona's restorers, working before the age of universal cameras, built the most photogenic literary monument in Europe by instinct, and the instinct has outearned every calculation the city's economists later made about it.

## The verdict on the Casa di Giulietta

The counts can be summed. The Casa di Giulietta is a real thirteenth and fourteenth-century tower house, documented from 1351, worked for centuries as an inn by the Cappello family, bought by the city in 1905 for the sake of a pun, and recomposed in 1939 and 1940 by Antonio Avena into the residence of a girl who never existed, around a balcony that the play never mentions, assembled from tomb marble kept in a museum store. Its statue is a 1972 bronze replaced by a replica, its bedroom is furnished with a film prop, its walls are papered with mail for the dead, and its official guardians have at times asserted, against ninety years of settled scholarship, that the heroine's family was real. Every part of that sentence is documented, and the sentence, read whole, describes not a scandal but the most complete physical record in existence of what a play can do to a city.

The verdict follows from the record, and it is a split verdict with a clear centre. As a historic house, the Casa fails, and no honest account should soften the failure: the building certifies nothing about the story, the family, or the scene, and presentations that imply otherwise, whether by portal prose, guidebook echo, or signage that dates the stones and not the story, trade in a falsehood that scholars refuted before the balcony was built. As stage architecture, the Casa succeeds completely: Avena composed, from authentic fragments, the most durable set the play has ever had, a set that has held its production, six days a week, for the better part of a century, with the audience rotating through the leading roles. And as evidence, which is the register that finally matters for a cultural afterlife, the Casa is invaluable and irreplaceable, because it documents its makers more honestly than its makers intended. The courtyard preserves, in legible layers, the antiquarian guess of the eighteenth century, the civic ambition of 1905, the restoration philosophy of 1939, the film culture of 1968 and 1973, and the reading rituals of millions who wrote on its walls. Stratford's custodians rebuilt a birthplace in the nineteenth century with the same composed confidence, and Verona's offence, if offence is the word, is the common one of a literary nation tidying its relics. The balcony lies about its past. About everything else, the desire of readers, the craft of cities, the long half-life of a fiction, it tells the plain truth in stone, and that is why it deserves study rather than either worship or scorn.

The cliché that sent the crowds to Via Cappello treats the balcony as the play's surviving furniture, the one piece of the set that history kept. The restored record shows the transaction running the other way: the play kept nothing, and the audience built everything, supplying the heroine with the house her author never gave her, the family her sources never had, and the balcony her text never named, then queuing for a century to visit its own supplies. A shrine of that kind is not an embarrassment to be exposed and abandoned. It is the largest single document of the play's afterlife in the physical world, more eloquent about the force of reading than any edition, and its correct catalogue entry is the one this article has argued from the first page: not the house of Juliet, who had none, but the house that Juliet's readers built for her in Verona, dated, signed, and open to inspection by any reader who cares to check.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Where exactly is the Casa di Giulietta, and what kind of building is it?

The Casa di Giulietta stands at Via Cappello 23 in Verona, a short walk from Piazza delle Erbe in the historic centre. It is a medieval tower house, a casatorre of the kind fortified families raised in the Italian communes, with a brick facade, a Gothic portal, and a small enclosed courtyard. Its fabric reaches back into the thirteenth century, and the building is documented from 1351. Nothing in its construction connects it to the play: the resemblance between its address, the street of the Cappello family, and the heroine's surname is the entire foundation of its fame, built upward by the restorations and rituals examined in this article.

### Q: When is the house first documented, and in whose hands?

The house is first documented in 1351, and it stood in the hands of the Dal Cappello family, whose heirs held it for centuries. The family's carved arms, a hat, survive on the keystone of the arch into the courtyard. In the documents the property works for its living: it served as the inn or hostelry of the Cappello, the stallo del Cappello, where travellers lodged in a city on the routes between Venice, Milan, and the Brenner passes. Later centuries saw the building pass through stables, workshops, and subdivided tenements as its fortunes declined, before the municipality bought it from the Cappello heirs in 1905.

### Q: What does the hat carved on the courtyard arch actually signify?

The hat signifies the Cappello family and nothing more. It is a canting coat of arms, a heraldic pun of a standard medieval type, in which the shield pictures the family name: cappello is the Italian word for a hat. Carved into the keystone of the arch leading to the inner courtyard, the arms announced the owner's name to anyone entering, in an age when a picture served readers and non-readers alike. The carving is genuine and medieval. Its later career as evidence for the Capulets rests on a slide between two similar sounds, Cappello and Capuleti, and the stone itself certifies only the family that commissioned it.

### Q: When did Verona buy the house, and from whom?

The Municipality of Verona bought the house in 1905, from the heirs of the Cappello family who had held it for centuries. The purchase belonged to the period's civic housekeeping, when Italian cities were acquiring historic palazzi for public keeping, and the fabric alone, a documented medieval tower house on a central street, justified the acquisition. The name over the door was nonetheless the property's distinction: the city acquired, at the price of an old inn, the freehold of a legend that antiquarians had attached to the building in the eighteenth century. Three decades passed before the municipality developed the site in earnest.

### Q: Who was Antonio Avena, and what was his role in the house's transformation?

Antonio Avena (1882 to 1967) was the director of Verona's civic museums and the most influential restorer of the city's monuments in the twentieth century. At Castelvecchio in the 1920s he had already recomposed a damaged fortress into a coherent medieval castle, practising restoration as composition: surviving fabric arranged, supplemented, and completed until the monument presented a single persuasive period picture. At the Casa di Giulietta he applied the same method to a literary brief. Under his direction the courtyard facade was dressed with a Gothic portal, trefoil windows, and stone ornaments from the civic stores, and the balcony, the feature the pilgrimage required, was created outright.

### Q: In which years was the balcony added to the Casa di Giulietta?

The balcony was added in the restoration campaign of 1939 and 1940, the dates given in the city of Verona's own published account of the house. A division in the sources deserves recording: a wide run of reference accounts dates the addition to 1936, within a looser restoration of the later 1930s. The official dating is followed in this article for the campaign's completion, with the received 1936 date noted as the alternative the literature repeats. On either dating the fact is identical and undisputed: the balcony is a twentieth-century work, separated from the play by roughly three and a half centuries.

### Q: From what materials was the balcony assembled?

The balcony was assembled from fourteenth-century marble remains that had lain in the stores of the Castelvecchio museum, kept as reusable stone until 1920, in the city portal's account. Reference accounts describe the carved front more particularly as the slab of an old sarcophagus, and municipal descriptions add that the design followed the hanging tombs of the Scaliger lords; the side pieces and balustrade were new work cut to complete the composition. The accounts divide on details and on the sarcophagus's century, and this article records the division rather than resolving it. The substance is agreed: old funerary marble, newly arranged.

### Q: What did the courtyard look like before the balcony was added?

Before the campaign of 1939 and 1940, the courtyard was the working yard of a declined inn: an enclosed space behind a medieval street facade, without any projecting gallery on its inner front. The inner facade that carries the balcony, with its Gothic portal, trefoil windows, stone dressings, and external balustrade walkway, is substantially the composition Avena's restorers made, set against the house's genuine medieval walls. Earlier visitors therefore saw an old house with a suggestive name and no feature answering to the scene in their heads, which is precisely the deficiency the restoration was commissioned to repair.

### Q: Why did the identification fasten on this house rather than another Veronese palazzo?

The identification fastened on this house because three conveniences converged in one doorway. The family's name, Cappello, sounded close enough to Capuleti to carry a pun; the family's arms carved the pun in stone over the courtyard arch, giving the guess a physical exhibit; and the building, a working inn on a central street between the market square and the pilgrims' route to the tomb, stood where travellers already passed. No other Veronese house offered name, emblem, and position together. The choice was overdetermined by everything except evidence, since no document ties the Cappello to the tale at any point.

### Q: How did the house serve travellers before it became a shrine?

The house served travellers as an inn. Documented as the stallo del Cappello, the hostelry of the Cappello family, it lodged guests in a city positioned on the routes between Venice, the Lombard plain, and the Alpine passes, taking its income from beds, stabling, and the traffic of a market district near Piazza delle Erbe. The trade was ordinary and profitable enough to sustain the property for centuries. The irony is structural: a building in the hospitality business for half a millennium eventually changed its clientele from travellers who needed lodging to travellers who needed a heroine, and prospered far more from the second trade than the first.

### Q: What is inside the house museum?

The interior is a house museum dressed as a Renaissance residence. Its rooms carry period furniture, ceramics, paintings of the lovers by nineteenth-century hands, and costume pieces, arranged to extend the courtyard's illusion into domestic space. The chamber serving the balcony takes its decoration from Francesco Hayez's canvas of 1823, The Last Kiss of Romeo and Juliet, so that the heroine's room reproduces a Romantic painting's version of her. The star exhibits are cinematic: the bed displayed in the chamber comes from Franco Zeffirelli's film of 1968, with costumes from the same production, layers of later fiction presented in the grammar of a historic interior.

### Q: When did the house open to the public as a museum?

The house opened to the public as a museum in 1973, with its interior furnished for display in the early 1970s. The opening completed a sequence that had run for most of the century: municipal purchase in 1905, the composed facade and created balcony of 1939 and 1940, and at last a furnished interior and a ticketing public. The statue of the heroine, Nereo Costantini's bronze, had been placed in the courtyard in 1972, in time to receive the museum's first visitors. Before 1973 the site was a municipal property with a famous courtyard; after it, the shrine had its full liturgy of rooms, relics, and ritual.

### Q: What does Dante actually say about Montecchi and Cappelletti?

Dante names the two in the sixth canto of the Purgatorio, summoning the absent emperor to survey a faction-ruined Italy: "Vieni a veder Montecchi e Cappelletti" (Purgatorio 6.106), come and see the Montecchi and the Cappelletti. The commentary tradition identifies them as opposing powers in the Guelf and Ghibelline division, placed in different cities: the Montecchi a Veronese power, the Cappelletti a faction of Cremona. Dante pairs the names as twin symptoms of Italy's self-slaughter, in parallel with the Monaldi and Filippeschi of Orvieto named beside them. The poem gives the two houses no quarrel with each other and no lovers.

### Q: Which writer gave the Verona story the names Montecchi and Cappelletti?

Luigi da Porto gave the story those names. The Vicentine soldier and writer composed his novella of the two noble lovers in the 1520s; it was published after his death, in 1530 and 1531, as the tale of Giulietta and Romeo set in Verona under Bartolomeo della Scala. Da Porto took the paired names from Dante's line, converted the party badges into feuding families, fixed the action in Verona, and presented the fiction as recovered history. Bandello's retelling of 1554 and the French and English versions that followed carried his construction to Shakespeare, whose Capulet descends from Da Porto's Cappelletti by transmission, not from any Veronese family.

### Q: What does the city portal claim about the two families, and what does scholarship say instead?

Verona's tourism portal asserts that two families named Montecchi and Capuleti, or more precisely Cappelletti, did indeed exist in Verona, and cites Dante's mention as support. The scholarship says the opposite. Olin H. Moore's archival study of 1930 concluded that the terms were nicknames of political parties rather than names of private families, that the Montecchi belonged to Verona only as a party after their family's extinction, that the Cappelletti were Cremona's, and that the play's families never resided in Verona and in fact never existed at all. The scholarly verdict has stood unrefuted for ninety years; the portal's sentence repeats the civic tradition regardless.

### Q: What does the word cappelletti mean, and why does the meaning matter for the house's claim?

Cappelletti means little hats. On Olin H. Moore's showing, the Cremona faction took its name from the small caps its members wore as a distinguishing badge, the diminutive of cappello, a hat. The meaning matters because it exposes the machinery of the whole identification: the story's supposed family was named, in the historical record, for hats, and the house that claims the heroine was selected for a hat carved over its door. Hat answers to little hats across three substitutions, faction to family, family to heroine, heroine to innkeepers, and at no point does a document enter the chain.

### Q: Can weddings be held at the Casa di Giulietta?

Weddings can be held there. The house's Capulet Hall hosts civil ceremonies, and the municipality offers the site to couples who marry in the heroine's supposed home, beneath the balcony built in 1939 and 1940. The rite is the shrine's most official sacrament and its most concentrated irony, since the fiction being honoured joins its couple in a secret ceremony that leads within four days to a double grave. The participants, on the evidence of the practice's popularity, read the site selectively, as the city presents it: the second act with an address, the ending kept across town in the tomb.

### Q: What rules govern the notes visitors leave in the entrance passage?

The writing of notes on the monument itself is prohibited and fined. Across decades of tolerated inscription, in marker, in scratches, and in folded notes fixed with chewing gum between the bricks, the municipality has alternated tolerance with enforcement: removable panels were installed to receive the messages, cleanings periodically stripped the passage walls back to brick, and the city set a fine of five hundred euros for defacing the monument, reported in the press coverage of the clean-up campaigns. The cycle persists because the writers understand the notes as correspondence with the heroine rather than as damage, and the house exists because such correspondence kept arriving.

The InsightCrunch balcony myth-versus-history is the table in this article that pairs each claim the courtyard makes with the documented record answering it. It separates eight claims from their evidence: the heroine's residence, the Capulet ownership, the Verona feud, the balcony's medieval date, the play's supposed balcony, the statue's age, the bedroom's authenticity, and the shrine's antiquity. ### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch balcony myth-versus-history to argue that the shrine documents reading rather than residence?

An essay should build the argument row by row. The residence column fails on every documentary test the table applies: the family, the feud, the balcony, and the bedroom each dissolve into a named maker and a date between 1905 and 1973. The reading column succeeds on the same evidence, because every maker and date in the table records an act of reception, antiquarians inferring, a city purchasing, a restorer composing, a film furnishing, and millions writing on walls. The essay's conclusion follows the table's pattern: the Casa is a primary source for the history of the play's audience, and a forgery only when miscatalogued as a source for the play.
