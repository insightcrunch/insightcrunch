The founding of the Indian National Congress in 1885 is usually argued as a contest of intentions. In one telling, the retired civil servant Allan Octavian Hume created the Congress as a safety valve through which Indian discontent could escape before it exploded, and the Viceroy, Lord Dufferin, blessed the device. In the rival telling, Indian political leaders built the Congress as their own instrument of national assertion and Hume merely assisted at a birth he did not conceive. Both tellings ask who meant what in December 1885, and both leave the larger question untouched: what, institutionally, did the founding build? This article defends a prior claim. The seventy-two delegates who met at Bombay between 28 and 31 December 1885 established a petition-politics architecture, a machine with four load-bearing choices: annual sessions as the body's primary activity, a modest formal membership, an elite professional composition, and constitutional methods of resolution and petition. Those choices, rather than any founder's intention, set the parameters within which the Moderate phase operated for twenty-two years, and they explain both what the Moderates achieved and why the architecture failed its first great test at Surat in 1907.

## The Preconditions: A Decade of Association Before the Founding

The Congress was not conjured from nothing in December 1885, and the architectural reading begins with the stock of habits its founders brought into the hall. The colonial state itself had supplied the first training ground. Lord Ripon's resolution on local self-government of May 1882 planted elected municipal and district boards across British India, and the men who fought the board elections, chaired the municipalities and drafted the boards' memorials learned the committee craft the Congress would nationalise. The Ilbert Bill agitation of 1883 and 1884 supplied the second lesson, a harsher one. When the European community of India organised a furious campaign against a modest bill allowing Indian judges to try European offenders, Indian associations in every presidency watched an interest with newspapers, funds and London access defeat a viceroy's measure, and drew the conclusion Banerjee's Indian Association acted upon: Indian opinion required organisation of the same density, or it would keep losing to opponents who had it.

Hume's own road to the founding runs through the same years and shows the initiative and the Indian groundwork intertwining from the start. On 1 March 1883 Hume published an open letter to the graduates of the University of Calcutta, the university whose convocation he had addressed in his official years, appealing for fifty young men of character, intelligence and selflessness to form the nucleus of a national movement for India's moral, social and political regeneration. The letter drew responses from political workers across the presidencies and gave Hume a correspondence network that no Indian leader of the day could match, because it crossed the provincial boundaries that confined every association's list. Through 1884 Hume organised his Indian National Union as the intended vehicle, enrolling sympathisers and sounding the provincial leaders on a conference. The Indian side of the prehistory has its own documentary spine. At Madras, in the last days of December 1884, a group of seventeen political workers met privately at the house of Dewan Bahadur Raghunath Rao, in the margin of the Theosophical Society's annual convention at Adyar, and resolved to work for a national political conference the following year. S. R. Mehrotra's reconstruction of these months, The Emergence of the Indian National Congress of 1971, established that the movement toward an all-India body was running on several tracks at once: Hume's union, the Madras meeting's resolution, Banerjee's National Conference preparing its second session, and the provincial associations' growing habit of corresponding on common questions.

One further precondition sits inside Hume's biography, and it shaped the founding's tone. Hume had entered the Indian Civil Service in 1849 and served as collector of Etawah through the revolt of 1857, an experience that left him with a district officer's knowledge of rural distress and a permanent suspicion of the secretariat's distance from it. His later career in the Government of India's Department of Revenue, Agriculture and Commerce ended in demotion in 1879, after friction with Lord Lytton's administration, and in retirement in 1882. The Congress's founder was thus not the empire's confident insider of the safety-valve telling but a cashiered reformer with twenty years of Indian service, an ornithologist of distinction, a Theosophist by sympathy in his Madras years, and a correspondent of Indian political workers in every presidency. The Indian leaders who accepted his convenorship in 1885 knew this record. They were not deceived about the man, and they were not his instruments; they were employers of his unmatched assets, his all-India correspondence, his freedom from official constraint as a pensioner, and his credibility in the London world the petition architecture was built to address.

The founding at Bombay closed these tracks into one, and the merger explains the architecture the founders chose. A body assembled from associations does not begin by dissolving them. It begins by giving them a common platform, a common week and a common form of words, and it leaves the components intact beneath the platform because the platform's authority derives from theirs. The four choices of 1885, the annual session, the loose membership, the elite composition and the constitutional method, are what a federation of petitioning associations produces when it is asked to design a national body in a fortnight. The safety-valve and autonomy theses both begin their stories too late, at the moment of the meeting. The architectural reading begins with the decade of association that made the meeting's design decisions nearly automatic, and it is to the meeting itself that the account turns.

## The 1885 Founding at Bombay: Venue, Delegates and Resolutions

The founding session very nearly met somewhere else, and the accident of its venue is the first evidence that the Congress began as an act of organisation rather than an act of state. Hume had spent 1884 assembling an Indian National Union, a preparatory body drawn from the provincial associations, and its conference was announced for Poona in the last week of December 1885. Poona offered the Sarvajanik Sabha's infrastructure and the Deccan's dense network of political workers. A cholera outbreak in the city forced the transfer to Bombay, where the Bombay Presidency Association's leaders, Pherozeshah Mehta, Kashinath Trimbak Telang and Badruddin Tyabji among them, could host at short notice. The delegates gathered instead at the Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College in Bombay on 28 December 1885, and the session sat for four days, closing on 31 December. The Congress that Indian political memory treats as immemorial began as a contingency plan executed in a borrowed hall.

### Why was the first Congress session moved from Poona to Bombay?

The organisers had announced the conference for Poona, headquarters of the Poona Sarvajanik Sabha and the Deccan's most experienced political association. A cholera outbreak in Poona in December 1885 made the venue untenable. Hume and the Bombay Presidency Association's leaders transferred the session to Bombay, where it opened at the Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College on 28 December.

Seventy-two delegates attended. The number is small enough to invite condescension and large enough to matter, because the seventy-two were not a crowd but a cross-section of the provincial political class that had been organising itself for a decade. Womesh Chunder Bonnerjee, a Bengali barrister of the Calcutta bar, took the presidential chair. Around him sat Dadabhai Naoroji, the Parsi merchant and economic writer whose statistical indictments of British rule were already circulating in London; Pherozeshah Mehta and Dinshaw Wacha of Bombay; K. T. Telang, jurist and scholar; S. Subramania Iyer and P. Ananda Charlu from Madras; G. Subramania Iyer, editor of The Hindu; Rahmatullah M. Sayani; and Gopal Ganesh Agarkar of Poona. Hume sat among them in the office he would hold for two decades, general secretary, the body's organiser, correspondent and memory. The composition was deliberate in one respect above all: every presidency and most provinces had a man in the room, so that the resolutions could be carried back as the product of India speaking in one hall rather than of one city speaking for itself.

One absence deserves record, because it corrects a durable misremembering and because it carries the article's argument in miniature. Surendranath Banerjee, founder of the Indian Association of Calcutta, was not at Bombay. His Indian National Conference, the parallel all-India body he had convened first in 1883, held its second session at Calcutta in the same final week of December 1885, and Banerjee presided over his own creation rather than travel to Hume's. The standard delegate lists that place him in the Gokuldas Tejpal hall conflate the two organisations' overlapping memberships. The point is not biographical pedantry. The Congress of 1885 was assembled from pre-existing provincial and national associations whose leaders negotiated their relationship to the new body, and the architecture adopted at Bombay had to accommodate organisations that already existed. The Indian National Conference merged itself into the Congress at the Calcutta session of 1886, and Banerjee became a Congress leader of the first rank, presiding at Poona in 1895 and at Ahmedabad in 1902. The founding architecture was from its first week a federation of prior institutions wearing a single name.

Bonnerjee's presidential address, delivered on 28 December 1885 and preserved in the published proceedings of the first session, set out the objects the founders claimed for the body: the promotion of personal intimacy and friendship among political workers from every part of India, the consolidation of national unity, the formulation of popular demands, and the education of public opinion. Read as rhetoric, the address is unremarkable. Read as architecture, it is exact. Every object Bonnerjee named is an activity of a deliberative body that meets, resolves and disperses. None of them requires a permanent mass organisation, a treasury, a cadre or a chain of command. The founders were not failing to build a modern party. They were declining to build one, because the instrument they wanted was a parliament of opinion that would convene, speak with a single voice for four days, and then work upon the colonial state and British opinion through the year by the accumulated authority of its resolutions.

The session's procedural choreography, visible in the same proceedings, was as considered as its resolutions. The movers and seconders of the nine resolutions were distributed among the presidencies, so that Bengal moved where Bombay seconded and Madras spoke where the Punjab had been heard, and no province could be said to have authored the programme alone. The presidential chair went to a Calcutta barrister in a Bombay hall organised by Bombay men, with a Madras-heavy platform of supporting speakers: the founding's allocations of honour were the federation's treaty in miniature. The session closed by resolving to meet again at Calcutta in the December following and by reposing the interim work in Hume's secretaryship, which is to say the annual cycle and the secretary's machine, the phase's two continuous institutions, were both invented in the founding week by men resolving business in a borrowed hall. Later Congresses would add the reception committee's formal election of the president, the subjects committee's settled authority and the exhibition and conference ecology described in this article, but the template of December 1885, four days, a chair, a secretary, nine resolutions and a city named for the next meeting, proved sufficient to run a national movement for a generation.

The nine resolutions the session adopted, recorded in the proceedings preserved in the All India Congress Committee archives, show the same design at the level of content. The delegates demanded a royal commission of inquiry into the working of the Indian administration. They demanded the abolition of the Council of India in London. They demanded the reform and expansion of the legislative councils, with a substantial elected element and the right to interrogate the executive. They demanded that examinations for the Indian Civil Service be held simultaneously in England and in India, with the maximum age for candidates raised. They demanded a reduction of military expenditure. They protested against the annexation of Upper Burma, which Lord Dufferin's government had proclaimed weeks earlier and would formalise on 1 January 1886. They demanded the separation of judicial from executive functions, the extension of trial by jury, and the repeal of the Arms Act of 1878. Each demand was framed as a resolution addressed upward to authority, drafted to be printed, transmitted and argued over in London and in the press. The founding's substantive legacy lies less in this list than in the form the list took: a body whose characteristic act was the formal resolution, adopted by assembled delegates, aimed at persuading a state that claimed to rule by reason. That form, repeated annually for twenty-two years, is the petition-politics architecture, and its four design choices deserve examination one by one.

Two details of the founding scene complete its portrait and both are architectural in character. The first is the calendar. December was chosen with a professional man's precision: the law courts rose for the Christmas vacation, the colleges closed, the cold season made railway travel across India tolerable, and British officialdom in London approached the parliamentary year's turn, when Indian questions could be planted for the spring. A body of barristers, teachers and journalists could meet only in the interstices of the colonial working year, and the founding took the interstice the calendar offered, which is one more sense in which the architecture was designed by its intended members' circumstances rather than by any theorist's plan. The second detail is the professions of loyalty with which the session clothed itself. The delegates sang God Save the Queen, addressed the Viceroy in the language of devoted attachment, and took care that the proceedings should everywhere advertise constitutional intent. Later generations read these professions as servility or as camouflage, and they were partly both; architecturally, they were the price of the hall. A body that the state might at any moment have dispersed chose to make its dispersal politically expensive by conducting itself as the most loyal gathering in India, and the tactic worked for twenty-two years, which is the only test its designers set it.

## Choice One: The Annual Session as the Body's Primary Activity

The first architectural choice was to make the annual session the Congress, and everything else scaffolding. The body convened for three or four days each December, in a different city each year, heard the presidential address, debated the resolutions prepared by a subjects committee, adopted them in open session, and dissolved until the following December. Between sessions, the work devolved upon the general secretary, upon the provincial associations that had hosted and would host, and after 1886 upon provincial Congress committees whose creation the Calcutta session had resolved. There was no permanent central office of consequence, no salaried national staff beyond a handful of clerks, and no executive empowered to commit the body between its meetings. Within [the four-phase organisational framework](/2019/10/27/indian-national-congress-long-arc-1885-2020/) that traces the Congress across its whole career, this seasonal design marks Phase One precisely: later phases would build permanent machinery, mass enrolment and a working committee that governed continuously, and each of those builds was a repudiation of some element of the 1885 design.

Rotation of the venue was not pageantry. It was load-bearing. A Congress that sat each year in a different presidency town converted its host association into a local organising committee, compelled the host city to raise funds, enrol delegates and advertise the cause, and carried the spectacle of all-India deliberation physically across the country. The session map of the Moderate phase reads as a tour of the provincial political class: Bombay in 1885, Calcutta in 1886, Madras in 1887, Allahabad in 1888, Bombay again in 1889, then Calcutta, Nagpur, Allahabad, Lahore, Madras, Poona, Calcutta, Amraoti, Madras, Lucknow, Calcutta, Ahmedabad, Lahore, Benares and Calcutta again by 1906. Each session left behind a reception committee's lists, a corps of local workers who had organised a national event, and newspaper coverage in the vernacular and English-language press of the region. The architecture reproduced itself geographically at almost no central cost, which is why a body with negligible funds could plausibly claim an all-India character within five years of its birth.

The attendance figures measure the design's reach and its limit in the same numbers. Seventy-two delegates sat at Bombay in 1885. The Calcutta session of December 1886 drew 434 delegates, swollen by the absorption of the Indian National Conference. Madras in 1887 drew 607. Allahabad in 1888 drew 1,248, a figure that rattled the provincial administration, whose head, Lieutenant Governor Auckland Colvin, had tried to obstruct the session's arrangements and whose government watched the proceedings with open suspicion. Growth on that curve looks like the start of a mass movement, and the founders' critics later charged the Moderates with failing to continue it. The charge misreads the architecture. Delegate numbers plateaued in the hundreds and low thousands through the 1890s because the design had no use for larger numbers. Delegates were chosen by public meetings and by the affiliated associations, they paid their own way, and their function was deliberative: to hear evidence, amend resolutions and lend their names and local standing to the result. Ten times the delegates would have produced a rally, not a better resolution, and the founding design wanted resolutions.

### Who was the first Muslim president of the Congress?

Badruddin Tyabji of Bombay presided over the third session at Madras in December 1887, the first Muslim to hold the Congress presidency. A judge of the Bombay High Court and a founder of the Bombay Presidency Association, Tyabji used his presidential address to answer the charge, pressed by Sir Syed Ahmad Khan, that the Congress spoke for Hindus alone.

The seasonal design also dictated the body's relationship to time, and thereby to power. A body that exists for four days a year cannot negotiate, because negotiation requires a counterparty who can bind his side on any day of the year. It cannot enforce, because enforcement requires machinery that persists after the hall empties. It cannot respond to events at the speed events occur: when the colonial state acted in March, the Congress's answer arrived in December, in the form of a resolution recording its disapproval. What the design could do superbly well was deliberate in public, accumulate a record, and educate. The printed proceedings of each session, circulated to members of Parliament, to the India Office and to newspaper offices in both countries, constituted a growing archive of Indian grievance stated in the language of British constitutionalism. The Moderates understood the trade they had made. Gopal Krishna Gokhale, who joined the Congress in 1889 and became the phase's most disciplined intelligence, described the early Congress's task as the slow creation of a public, a body of Indians trained to think politically about all-India questions. The annual session was the school, and its curriculum was the resolution book.

The session's internal mechanics deserve a closer look, because they reveal how much government the founders packed into four days. The subjects committee, composed of leading delegates from each province and sitting late into the session's nights, settled the resolutions' final wording before the open session voted, which meant the public proceedings could display unanimity because the arguments had already happened in committee. The presidential address functioned as the body's annual state paper, an hour or more of argument that the newspapers printed entire: Naoroji's addresses surveyed the economic condition of India with statistical tables, Gokhale's at Benares in 1905 anatomised the partition of Bengal, and the presidents in rotation, Tyabji at Madras in 1887, George Yule at Allahabad in 1888, William Wedderburn at Bombay in 1889, Alfred Webb at Madras in 1894, Banerjee at Poona in 1895, Rahimtullah Sayani at Calcutta in 1896, Romesh Chunder Dutt at Lucknow in 1899, used the chair to address the questions the year had set. The session also generated the phase's rituals of nationhood almost in passing. At Calcutta in 1896 Rabindranath Tagore sang Bankim Chandra Chatterjee's Vande Mataram before the assembled delegates, the song's first performance on a Congress platform. At Calcutta in 1901 a young South African Indian lawyer named Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi attended as a delegate for Natal, moved a resolution on the treatment of Indians in South Africa, and was put to work drafting for the leaders, his first enlistment in the body he would one day rebuild from its foundations.

The printed proceedings bound the cycle together. Each session's report, with the presidential address, the resolutions and the debate summaries, was published and sold, lodged in the libraries of the associations, cited in the press campaigns of the following year and laid before the British Committee in London as the mandate for its lobbying. The report was the Congress's true continuous existence: the session dissolved in December, but the volume persisted, and a political worker in Madurai or Lahore who had never travelled to a session could hold the year's Congress in his hands for the price of the book. Hume understood the volume's function and supervised the early reports with a secretary's care. An architecture is a set of decisions about where an institution lives between its public appearances, and the 1885 design answered that the Congress lived in print, in the associations' committee rooms, and in one man's correspondence. The answer worked for twenty-two years, which is longer than most institutional designs of the nineteenth century survived their founders.

The session week also grew an ecology around itself, and the ecology multiplied the architecture's effects without adding a rupee of machinery. From 1887, when the Madras session met, the Indian National Social Conference, founded by Ranade and his fellow reformers, held its sittings in the same city in the same week, borrowing the Congress's pandal and its assembled audience to debate social reform questions the Congress itself, by a self-denying ordinance of the founding years, kept off its political agenda. Other conferences and exhibitions clustered in the penumbra in later years, and the host city's December became a fair of national causes: a delegate travelled once and could attend the Congress, the Social Conference, the industrial and temperance meetings and the reunions of a dozen associations in a single week. The arrangement suited the founding design perfectly. The Congress kept its agenda narrow, constitutional and defensible, while the wider movement's energies, social, economic and cultural, organised themselves alongside it under separate banners with overlapping personnel. Critics then and since have read the separation as timidity, and the Social Conference's exclusion from the Congress pandal after the controversies of the mid-1890s, when Tilak's Poona following objected to the reformers' use of the shared hall, showed the arrangement's tensions. Read architecturally, the ecology was load-bearing: it let a deliberately narrow political machine anchor a broad national movement without assuming responsibility for the movement's every cause, and it helps explain how a body of a few thousand members could plausibly stand, by 1905, at the head of a politics far larger than itself.

## Choice Two: Modest Formal Membership and the Missing Constitution

The second architectural choice was to keep formal membership small, loose and legally almost undefined. For its first twenty-three years the Congress had no written constitution at all. It had customs: a president elected by the host reception committee's recommendation and acclaimed by the session, a general secretary who served indefinitely and managed correspondence, subjects committees that settled the resolutions before the open session ratified them, and delegates credentialed by the associations and public meetings that sent them. It had no membership register, no subscription that conferred rights, no provincial units chartered by the centre and no procedure for expelling anyone. A man was of the Congress if the Congress world treated him as of it, and the Congress world's test was participation in the annual cycle of meetings, committees and sessions.

### When did the Congress adopt a written constitution?

The Congress worked under unwritten custom from 1885 until the split at Surat in December 1907 exposed the cost of having no rules for membership or credentials. The Moderate convention committee at Allahabad in April 1908 framed a constitution, and the Madras session of December 1908 adopted it, defining the aim as self-government within the Empire by constitutional means.

The absence of a membership architecture was partly poverty and partly principle. The principle deserves emphasis, because later Congress generations regarded the loose founding structure as a defect to be corrected and therefore understated how functional it was for the founders' purposes. A petitioning body gains authority from the standing of its signatories, not from their number. When the Congress resolved that the Indian Civil Service examinations should be held simultaneously in India, the colonial state did not count heads; it weighed the fact that the demand came from a hall containing high court judges, newspaper editors, college principals, merchants and barristers from every province, speaking after deliberation in a form the state itself recognised as legitimate. Enrolling a hundred thousand members of no public standing would have added expense and risk without adding an ounce of that authority. Hume, who had spent a career inside the colonial bureaucracy, understood the audience perfectly. The architecture addressed the state in the state's own grammar, and its membership stayed modest because its influence was designed to run through reputation rather than numbers.

The costs of the design were deferred, which is why they are easy to miss in the founding decade and impossible to miss at its end. A body without a membership register cannot settle a contested succession, because there is no roll against which to test a claim to speak for it. A body without chartered units cannot discipline a provincial faction, because the faction's credentials derive from the same public meetings as everyone else's. A body whose officers serve by acclamation cannot remove them. Through the 1890s, with the founding generation's prestige intact and the colonial state the obvious antagonist, none of this mattered. When the challenge came from inside, from Bal Gangadhar Tilak's Deccan following and the younger Extremist cohort who disputed the Moderates' control of the machine, the Congress discovered that it possessed no constitutional means of deciding who it was. The Surat session of December 1907 collapsed into disorder partly over the presidency and partly over the resolutions, and it could not be repaired by any procedure, because no procedure existed. The 1908 constitution was the Moderates' answer, written after the rupture it might have prevented. The architectural reading insists on the sequence: the missing constitution was not an oversight in an otherwise complete design but a founding choice whose benefits were consumed in the first two decades and whose bill arrived in the third.

The mechanics of becoming a delegate show the design's economics at work. A delegate was produced in one of three ways: a provincial or district association named him, a public meeting in a district town elected him, or a body such as a municipal board, a bar association or a university constituency returned him under the conventions that grew up in the first decade. No central office vetted the returns, and no fee bought a seat, but the journey to the session city, the week's lodging and the loss of professional earnings were the delegate's own charge, and that single economic fact filtered the body's composition more efficiently than any rule could have done. The host city bore the session's costs through its reception committee, which raised a local Congress fund by subscription among the city's merchants and professional men; the presidency towns competed informally for the honour and the expense together. Funds so raised were spent on the hall, the printing and the delegates' entertainment, and disputes over money, when they came, ran along the same provincial lines as disputes over precedence. The architecture's finances were the architecture in miniature: local, reputational, raised for the occasion and dissolved with it, sufficient for deliberation and useless for the maintenance of a permanent machine. When later Congress generations wanted a machine, the first thing they had to invent was a way to pay for it that did not depend on a host city's December generosity, and the four-anna membership of a later reconstruction was precisely that invention, converting the body's finance from subscription by the eminent to enrolment of the many.

The presidency's mode of choice shows the customary constitution under strain. The host reception committee proposed the president, the session acclaimed him, and no ballot existed by which a rival claim could be tested or a defeated faction bound. While the founding generation's prestige ordered the succession, the custom produced the stately rotation the record shows: Bonnerjee twice, Naoroji three times across twenty years, and a chair occupied in turn by judges, editors, merchants and former officials of four provinces and three religions. As the body's factions hardened after 1900, the same custom converted each December's choice into a preliminary battle for the Congress itself, fought in the reception committees and the press because it could be fought nowhere else, and the Surat dispute of 1907 opened, precisely, on the presidency: the Extremist demand for Lajpat Rai, the Moderate insistence on Rash Behari Ghosh, and no rule by which the question could be put. Institutions reveal their missing parts at the moment of contest, and the missing part in 1907 was not courage or programme. It was a procedure.

## The Secretary's Machine: The One Continuous Institution

Between the sessions, the Congress existed in exactly one place, and a section on the founding's architecture must name it: the general secretary's desk. Hume conducted the body's correspondence from wherever he sat, first in India and after 1894 from his house at Dulwich in London, and the correspondence was the institution. Letters went out to the provincial associations soliciting delegates, resolutions and subscriptions; letters came back carrying the provinces' grievances, which the secretary's office sorted into the agenda of the next session; the annual report of proceedings was compiled under the secretary's eye; the British Committee in London took its brief from the same desk. Dinshaw Wacha's labour in Bombay complemented Hume's at a distance of continents: Wacha, a mill manager's son who became the Congress's indispensable man of business, handled the Indian secretariat's daily work for the better part of two decades, served the body in secretarial office year after year, and presided at Calcutta in 1901 as the organisation's own candidate, the secretary elevated to the chair. The arrangement amounted to a two-man civil service for a body that refused to build one, and it worked because the men were indefatigable and because the architecture asked so little of continuity: keep the lists, print the record, convene the December meeting, transmit the resolutions.

The machine's frailties were the design's frailties wearing office dress. A correspondence web centred on persons cannot bequeath itself: when Hume's energy failed and his generation thinned, the web thinned with it, and no office, archive protocol or trained cadre stood behind the names. The provincial Congress committees resolved upon in 1886 existed unevenly across the phase, vigorous where a strong association underlay them, as in Bombay and Bengal, and nominal in provinces where the Congress was a December visit rather than a standing presence; the secretary's letters could exhort them but could not staff them. Funds told the same story. The London committee and its journal were maintained substantially by Indian subscription, money raised through the same reputational channels as the sessions' costs, and the body's chronic impecunity was a standing item of internal complaint: the most ambitious political organisation in India ran for twenty-two years on sums a single Bombay merchant prince could have supplied from a year's surplus, and chose, in effect, to run on reputation instead. None of this was hidden from the participants. The founders had weighed a staffed, funded, enrolled organisation in 1885 and declined it, partly from poverty of means and partly from their theory of influence, and the secretary's machine is what the declining produced: the minimum continuous institution compatible with the annual deliberative design, and, at the end, its most obvious point of failure when the deliberative design itself came under attack.

## Choice Three: An Elite Professional Composition

The third architectural choice fixed who the Congress was made of. The founding generation was drawn, overwhelmingly, from the English-educated professional classes of the presidency towns: barristers and pleaders first of all, then journalists and newspaper proprietors, school and college teachers, doctors, merchants and a sprinkling of retired officials and substantial landholders. The legal profession's dominance was structural, not accidental. Lawyers possessed the English language at forensic standard, the habit of framing grievances as cases, the income to travel to a December session at their own expense, and a grievance of their own, since the colonial legal order employed their skills and capped their advancement. When the Congress resolved upon simultaneous civil service examinations or the separation of the judiciary from the executive, it spoke in the idiom its members had been trained in, and the idiom was the point: the body's composition guaranteed that its demands would arrive before the state already translated into the state's preferred language of precedent, procedure and statute.

Beneath the Congress stood the provincial associations, and the brief's evidence file rightly treats their archives as the under-cited primary source for the founding. The Poona Sarvajanik Sabha, founded in 1870 by Mahadev Govind Ranade, Ganesh Vasudeo Joshi and their associates, had spent fifteen years petitioning on revenue settlements, forest rules and municipal questions before it helped host the transferred conference of 1885. The Indian Association, founded at Calcutta on 26 July 1876 by Surendranath Banerjee and Ananda Mohan Bose, had campaigned across northern India on the civil service age limit and the Ilbert Bill. The Madras Mahajana Sabha, founded in May 1884, sent P. Ananda Charlu and its circle north to Bombay. The Bombay Presidency Association, founded in January 1885 with Mehta, Telang and Tyabji at its head, was eleven months old when it organised the founding session's local arrangements. The papers of these bodies, preserved in regional archives and mined far less than the Congress's own printed proceedings, document the founding's true preconditions: the delegate lists, the fund-raising, the correspondence about venues and presidents. The Congress architecture of 1885 was a federation of these associations' habits, and its elite composition was theirs before it was its own.

### Who was the first English president of the Congress?

George Yule, a Scottish merchant of Calcutta, presided over the Allahabad session in December 1888, the first Englishman to hold the Congress presidency. The choice was deliberate stagecraft: a British president advertised the body's loyalty and respectability to its London audience. William Wedderburn followed at Bombay in 1889, and the Irish MP Alfred Webb presided at Madras in 1894.

The composition carried exclusions that the founders accepted as the price of the design and that their critics catalogued as its fraudulence. The peasantry, the overwhelming majority of the population, had no delegates and no channel through which a delegate might be produced; the associations that credentialed delegates met in towns and conducted their business in English. Industrial labour, still small in 1885, was absent entirely. Women were absent from the founding hall; the first woman to attend as a delegate, Kadambini Ganguly, one of the first two women graduates of Calcutta University, came to the Bombay session of 1889, and she addressed the Calcutta session of 1890, the first woman to speak from a Congress platform. Muslim participation at Bombay was token in scale: Rahmatullah M. Sayani, who would preside at Calcutta in 1896, was among the few Muslim delegates present, and Sir Syed Ahmad Khan's campaign against the Congress, organised from 1888 through the United Indian Patriotic Association, worked precisely on the gap between the Congress's all-India claim and its narrow social base. Tyabji's Madras presidency in 1887 and Sayani's in 1896 were the architecture's answers, demonstrations mounted from the presidential chair because the delegate body itself could not yet supply the demonstration.

The architectural reading neither excuses the narrowness nor treats it as the founding's essence. It asks what the narrowness did. It bought the Congress two decades of physical safety and procedural courtesy: the colonial state harassed the Congress, obstructed its venues at Allahabad in 1888 and sneered at its pretensions, but it did not ban a body composed of judges, editors and merchants conducting itself by the rules of debate. It bought fluency: no political organisation in the colonised world of the 1880s could out-draft the Congress's subjects committees. And it deferred, at compound interest, the question of the base. Every later phase of the Congress can be described as an attempt to widen this founding composition without losing the fluency it conferred, and the Extremist challenge of 1905 to 1907 was the first attempt, made by men who had concluded that the price of the narrow base had begun to exceed its yield.

Two features of the composition repay separate notice, because both were engineered rather than inherited. The first is the Parsi prominence at the body's head. Naoroji, Mehta and Wacha, three of the founding generation's half-dozen most consequential figures, came from a community numbered in the tens of thousands, concentrated in Bombay, educated early in English schools, enriched by trade and unencumbered by the landed and caste hierarchies that slowed political organisation elsewhere. Their presence at the Congress's centre was the predictable yield of the architecture: a body whose currency was English forensic argument and London correspondence rewarded exactly the assets Bombay's Parsi professional class had accumulated, and the community's distance from the Hindu-Muslim contest for the Congress's identity made its members acceptable brokers in the body's internal disputes. The second feature is the landed interest's marginality. The great landholders' associations, the British Indian Association of Calcutta above all, kept their distance from the founding, preferring their own channels to the state and fearing the professional men's reformism on tenancy and revenue questions. The Congress's programme, with its demands on land revenue and its sympathy for the raiyat in the tenancy debates of the 1880s, was written by men who did not own the land they taxed in argument, and the absence of the landed magnates from the delegate body is the clearest single marker that the Congress of 1885 was a professionals' institution and not a durbar of notables. Regional balance, the third engineered feature, was maintained by the presidency's rotation and the session's migration, and its maintenance cost the leadership constant negotiation between the Bombay and Bengal organisations, the body's two heaviest components, each of which suspected the other of capturing the machine. The suspicion was itself an architectural product: in a body without rules, every allocation of honour is a precedent and every precedent a grievance, and the founding generation managed the rivalry by the same currency of prestige and turn-taking that Surat would one day find exhausted.

The composition also reproduced itself, and the reproduction system explains the phase's durability better than any list of its leaders. The nurseries were educational and journalistic. At Poona, the Deccan Education Society, founded in 1884 by Tilak, Gopal Ganesh Agarkar and Vishnushastri Chiplunkar, opened Fergusson College in 1885, the founding year itself, and the college's staff room became the Deccan's political seminary: Gokhale taught mathematics and history there from 1886, and the society's life members formed a cadre of graduates in public service that the Congress could draw upon for secretaries, organisers and speakers. Ranade's circle ran a parallel apprenticeship through the Sarvajanik Sabha's committees and the quarterly journal he edited, in which young men learned to draft memorials on revenue and judicial questions to a exacting standard. The newspaper offices performed the same office in the other presidencies: The Hindu's Madras rooms trained speakers in the assembly of statistics into argument, and the Amrita Bazar Patrika's office was a Bengal political education in itself. A composition drawn from these nurseries renewed itself without any formal recruitment, because the nurseries were institutions with their own lives, and it aged in place for the same reason: the pipeline produced more forensic draftsmen of the founders' stamp long after the political demand had shifted toward organisers of crowds, and the phase's benches in 1905 were filled by the ablest products of a system designed for 1885. The Extremist challenge was, among other things, a rival pipeline announcing itself, Tilak's Kesari office and the Deccan's festival organisations training a different kind of political worker for a different architecture.

## Choice Four: Constitutional Methods of Resolution and Petition

The fourth choice governed what the Congress did, and it followed from the other three as a keystone follows an arch. The methods were four: the resolution adopted in open session after committee deliberation; the memorial and petition, addressed to the Viceroy, to the Secretary of State and to Parliament; the deputation, carrying the resolutions in person to officials in India and to opinion in England; and the press campaign, conducted through newspapers the Congress's own members edited and through sympathetic journals in both countries. Absent from the repertoire, by deliberate design, were the boycott, the strike, the mass meeting addressed to the unlettered, and any form of resistance to a law in force. The Moderates' India was to be persuaded, and its rulers were to be persuaded, in that order and by argument.

The London end of the machine shows the methods at their most developed, and it qualifies any picture of the Moderates as mere petitioners waiting at a durbar. The British Committee of the Indian National Congress was founded in London in July 1889, with William Wedderburn as chairman and the journalist William Digby, author of a statistical anatomy of Indian poverty, as its working secretary. From 1890 the committee published the journal India, which carried the Congress's resolutions, Digby's economic documentation and parliamentary intelligence to members of Parliament, journalists and the India Office. Congress deputations crossed to England in election seasons to address meetings and brief candidates, and Indian questions were planted in the House of Commons through friendly members. When the founders' architecture is described whole, it resembles less a durbar petition than a two-capital lobbying system, Delhi-facing and London-facing at once, built by men who understood that the Indian administration answered in part to British opinion and meant to organise that opinion.

The press was the machine's third limb, and the cheapest. G. Subramania Iyer's The Hindu, founded at Madras in 1878, and the Amrita Bazar Patrika of Calcutta, which the Ghosh brothers had converted from Bengali to English in a single night in 1878 to escape the Vernacular Press Act, carried session reports and Congress argument to reading publics the sessions themselves could never seat. Surendranath Banerjee's Bengalee, the Indian Mirror, Tilak's Kesari and Mahratta from Poona, and Naoroji's Voice of India from Bombay formed a web in which a resolution adopted at Allahabad could be editorialised in four provinces within the week. The architecture's enemies understood its dependence on print: the colonial state's press laws of the period, and the sedition prosecutions that would reach Tilak in 1897, were aimed at the limb through which a body of a few thousand members addressed a subcontinent.

The memorial, second of the four methods, deserves its mechanics on the record, because it was the point where the petition architecture touched ordinary political workers. A resolution adopted at the session was engrossed as a memorial to the Viceroy or to Parliament, and the provincial associations then carried the text through the district towns, holding the public meetings at which it was read, debated and signed. The signature sheets came back to the secretary's office as the body's census of the convinced, and the deputation that presented the memorial in London or Simla could speak of names gathered town by town rather than of a platform's opinion alone. The campaigns on the councils question and the civil service examinations in the late 1880s, run in this fashion year after year, were the closest the founding architecture came to a membership drive, and their limits were the design's limits in miniature: the meetings were held, the sheets were signed, the memorial was presented, and the signatories went home enrolled in nothing, members of no continuing body, awaiting the next December's summons. Petition-politics could mobilise signatures. It had made no provision to keep the signers.

The four choices together constitute the findable artifact of this article, the four-architectural-choice map set out in Table 1, which the surrounding sections cite as the pillar's reference for the Moderate phase. Students and researchers who assemble the evidence for the map from the printed session proceedings, the Hume correspondence and the provincial association papers will find the proceedings increasingly consulted in scanned and tabulated form; readers who gather such sets into presentation order for teaching or citation can open and arrange the files through the [PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) in the browser. The map is offered as the article's citable core: each choice is stated with the form it took in 1885, the capacity it created, and the limit that the years to 1907 exposed.

**Table 1: The Four Architectural Choices of 1885 and Their Consequences to 1907**

| Architectural choice | Form adopted in 1885 | Capacity it created | Limit exposed by 1907 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual sessions as primary activity | A three to four day session each December, rotating among presidency and provincial cities, with deliberation by subjects committee and ratification in open session | A printed all-India record of grievance and demand, renewed yearly, carried home by delegates to every province | No machinery to negotiate, enforce or respond between sessions; answers to state action arrived a season late |
| Modest formal membership | No written constitution, no membership register, no subscription rights; delegates credentialed by associations and public meetings | Authority drawn from the public standing of participants rather than their number, at negligible central cost | No roll or procedure by which a contested claim to speak for the body could be settled, as Surat in 1907 proved |
| Elite professional composition | Barristers, journalists, teachers, doctors, merchants and association leaders of the presidency towns, presided over in rotation by figures of public eminence | Fluency in the colonial state's constitutional grammar, safety from suppression, command of the English-language press | A narrow social base that excluded the peasantry, labour and, at first, women, and fed the charge of a microscopic minority |
| Constitutional methods | Resolutions, memorials, petitions, deputations to India and London, a British Committee from 1889 and a sustained press campaign | Concessions extracted at the margin, notably the Indian Councils Act of 1892, and Indian questions planted in Parliament | Petition could persuade but not compel; the partition of Bengal in 1905 was imposed over the architecture's full opposition |

The methods' self-limitation was visible to the founding generation's own children before it was visible to history. Between August 1893 and March 1894 the Bombay journal Indu Prakash carried an unsigned series, New Lamps for Old, later attributed to the young Aurobindo Ghose, which arraigned the Congress for precisely the architecture this article describes: its appeal to British audiences over Indian ones, its substitution of eloquence for organisation, its fear of the people it claimed to represent. The series is the Extremist critique in draft, written fourteen years before Surat, and it matters to the adjudication that follows because it shows the architectural limits were not discovered by later historians. They were felt, from inside the elite world the founders had built, by the generation that would be asked to inherit a machine designed for persuasion at the moment persuasion stopped working.

The methods' parliamentary yield can be tracked bill by bill, and the tracking shows a lobbying system of real sophistication rather than a ritual of supplication. The radical member of Parliament Charles Bradlaugh attended the Bombay session of 1889 as the Congress's guest, addressed the delegates, and carried their councils demand back to Westminster, introducing a bill for the reform of the Indian councils in the House of Commons in 1890. The government's own measure, piloted by the Secretary of State, Lord Cross, moved in the same session under the pressure the agitation had built, and though the parliamentary session consumed both efforts, the Indian Councils Act that emerged in 1892 bore the Congress campaign's fingerprints in its elective principle by recommendation. The royal commission route ran in parallel. The Congress's witnesses and members used the Royal Commission on Indian Expenditure, appointed in 1895 under Lord Welby, as a second front: Naoroji sat as a member of the commission itself, the first Indian appointed to such a body, and Gokhale crossed to London in 1897 to give evidence whose mastery of the expenditure figures the commission's proceedings preserved. When the commission reported in 1900, Naoroji, Wedderburn and William Caine signed a dissenting report carrying the Congress's case into the official record itself. Petition, deputation, parliamentary bill, commission evidence: the repertoire was constitutional, but it was worked with an energy and a tactical range that the word mendicancy, later thrown at the Moderates by their Extremist heirs, does not describe.

The architecture also generated its own evidence culture, a feature the standard accounts underrate. The associations behind the Congress had spent the 1880s learning to fight the state with documentation: the Poona Sarvajanik Sabha's memorials rested on revenue settlement reports its workers had mastered, Ranade's essays on Indian economics supplied a standing brief against the official political economy, and the Congress sessions institutionalised the habit by debating resolutions framed as findings. Romesh Chunder Dutt carried the culture to its height in the famine years, lecturing and writing on famines and land assessments with the settlement records open before him, and the Congress's demand for a royal commission of inquiry into the administration, carried in the founding session's first resolutions, was the evidence culture's characteristic ask: not concession in the corridor but investigation on the record. When the Welby Commission took evidence in 1897, the Congress movement fielded witnesses who could meet the India Office on its own statistical ground, and the commission's dissenting report showed the encounter's result. A petition backed by superior documentation is a different instrument from a petition backed by sentiment, and the difference is one of the founding generation's durable bequests to the movement that displaced them: every later Congress campaign of consequence, down to the economic resolutions of the independence era, ran on paper the Moderates' methods had taught Indian politics to prepare.

## The Safety-Valve Thesis and Its Limits

The safety-valve thesis entered the record as a polemic and hardened into a commonplace. Its canonical statement is Lala Lajpat Rai's, in Young India, published in 1916, in which the Extremist leader, writing in exile, held that the Congress had been started more with the object of saving the British Empire from danger than of winning political liberty for India, and that it functioned as a safety valve for the escape of forces gathering dangerously among the educated. Lajpat Rai's authority for the founding story was Hume's own circle: William Wedderburn's biography, Allan Octavian Hume, Father of the Indian National Congress, published in 1913, had retailed Hume's account of his alarm in the late 1870s, when, on Hume's telling, he was shown seven volumes of secret reports documenting seething discontent among the poor and concluded that a political body was needed through which discontent could find constitutional expression. The thesis thus rests on the founder's own retrospective self-presentation, mediated by his disciple's biography and weaponised by his critic. That provenance does not make it false. It makes it evidence about Hume's self-understanding, which is a narrower thing than evidence about the Congress.

The documentary support for the strong version of the thesis is thin where it needs to be thick. Hume did correspond with the Viceroy in 1885, and the Hume-Dufferin correspondence preserved in the Dufferin Papers at the British Library shows a viceroy willing to be informed and a founder anxious to present his project as loyal and useful. It does not show Dufferin commissioning the Congress, drafting its programme or controlling its proceedings. Dufferin's private letters of the period treat the new body with the wary condescension of a proconsul humouring a novelty he expects to manage. Within three years the management had curdled into contempt, and the Viceroy said so in public.

### What did Viceroy Lord Dufferin call the Congress in 1888?

Speaking at the St Andrew's Day dinner at Calcutta on 30 November 1888, Lord Dufferin dismissed the Congress as representing a microscopic minority of India's population. The nationalist press repeated the phrase for decades. It matters evidentially: a viceroy does not publicly belittle his own safety valve before the audience the valve was built to impress.

Dufferin's own paper trail, read closely, refuses to play the part the thesis assigns him. Hume, by his circle's account, laid the project before the Viceroy at Simla in May 1885 and came away believing he had a guarded blessing, a belief Wedderburn's biography repeated and Lajpat Rai's polemic converted into a commission. Dufferin's private record shows a viceroy who saw no present harm in a loyal debating body and took care to commit himself to nothing: no minute of his authorised the Congress, directed its programme or promised it protection, and his correspondence treats the founders' professions of loyalty as a convenience he was content to accept at face value. As the body's demands hardened on the councils and the services, the viceregal tone cooled in exact proportion, through the official discourtesies of 1887 to the St Andrew's dinner contempt of November 1888 and the restrictions his administration encouraged. A safety valve installed by the state is maintained by the state; this one was tolerated in its weak year, obstructed as it grew, and disowned by its supposed installer in public. The thesis survives the archive only in the diminished form the mature scholarship gives it, as a statement about what Hume hoped and what Dufferin briefly found convenient, and in that form it has no quarrel with the architectural reading, which accommodates both men's intentions inside a design neither of them controlled for long.

The mature scholarly treatment of the thesis, Bipan Chandra's in India's Struggle for Independence 1857-1947 of 1988, delivers the qualified verdict that the evidence sustains. Chandra and his co-authors accept that Hume, and the liberal British circle around him, hoped the Congress would work as a safety valve, and they quote Gopal Krishna Gokhale's cooler metaphor in reply: Hume had served, Gokhale argued, as a lightning conductor, drawing into a visible and constitutional channel forces that might otherwise have struck blindly, and no Indian leader of 1885 could have convened an all-India body without the authorities moving to crush it at birth. On Chandra's reading the safety valve describes the founder's hope and the empire's toleration, not the institution's behaviour. The Congress's resolutions, its London lobbying, its economic critique and its steady radicalisation of demand across the Moderate decades are not the output of a device for releasing pressure harmlessly. They are the output of Indian politicians using an available shelter to build positions the shelter's designer had not scripted. Sumit Sarkar's Modern India 1885-1947 of 1983 reaches a compatible destination from the other side, treating the safety-valve story as one strand in a founding whose Indian participants had purposes, associations and programmes of their own that predated Hume's initiative.

The architectural reading absorbs what is true in the thesis and discards the rest. It is true, and important, that the founding's design choices made the Congress tolerable to the colonial state in 1885, and that Hume's presence and intentions were part of that tolerability. An architecture that another power finds harmless, however, is not thereby that power's architecture. The four choices were made by the men who had to live inside them, Indian association leaders in the majority, for reasons that their own prior decade of provincial petitioning fully explains. The safety-valve thesis mistakes the conditions of the founding's permission for the causes of its design, and its long reign in the textbooks owes more to its narrative economy, one Englishman, one viceroy, one motive, than to the archive it claims to rest on.

The seven volumes at the thesis's heart repay the scepticism the archives impose. Hume, in retirement, told Wedderburn that around 1878, when he was a secretary to the Government of India with access to official reporting, he was quietly shown seven volumes of secret reports gathered from across the country, recording the talk of the poor and the disaffection spreading beneath the surface of order, and that he carried the memory of those volumes as the warrant for his organising. No such volumes have been identified in the record, no second official corroborated the story, and the tale's shape, a lone Englishman reading the subcontinent's mind in secret files, is the shape of a founding myth told by its hero. Historians divide over how much of Hume's wider claim to trust: that discontent existed in 1878 is beyond argument, given the Deccan riots of 1875 and the famine of 1876 to 1878; that it required a safety valve designed in the secretariat is the inference the evidence will not carry. The thesis also acquired a harder variant in the twentieth century, when R. Palme Dutt's India Today of 1940 recast the founding as a deliberate imperialist conspiracy to head off revolution, a reading that dispenses with the Indian founders' agency altogether and that the documentary record of their prior organisation refutes in the act of being described. The architectural reading files the whole family of intention-theories under a single verdict: they answer the question of who wanted the Congress, and the interesting question, the one the subsequent twenty-two years pose, is what the Congress, once wanted, was built to do.

## The Autonomy Thesis and Its Limits

The autonomy thesis was the founding's official memory of itself, codified in Pattabhi Sitaramayya's History of the Indian National Congress, published in 1935 with the movement's blessing, and absorbed into the post-1947 nationalist historiography that Bipan Chandra's generation both inherited and revised. On this reading, the Congress was Indian political assertion from its first hour. Indian leaders had been building toward an all-India body through the provincial associations and through Banerjee's Indian National Conference of 1883; Hume enters the story as facilitator, correspondent and convenor, a useful Englishman whose official past opened doors; the initiative, the programme and the personnel were Indian. The thesis performed necessary work against the safety-valve slur, and S. R. Mehrotra's documentary study, The Emergence of the Indian National Congress of 1971, gave its core a solid archival footing by reconstructing the Indian organisational efforts of 1883 to 1885 that preceded and accompanied Hume's.

Its weakness is the mirror of its rival's. It treats Hume as staff when the record shows him as a designer. The 1883 open letter in which Hume, signing himself in hope, called upon the graduates of Calcutta University to supply fifty young men of character for a national organisation, was an Indian programme in an Englishman's hand two years before the founding. The logistics of December 1885, the circulars, the invitations, the management of the transfer from Poona, ran through Hume's secretariat. The selection of Bonnerjee, a respected Calcutta barrister of moderate temper and no association machine of his own, to preside over a body whose strongest components were Bombay and Bengal organisations, bears the mark of Hume's balancing hand. Above all, the secretaryship was not a clerkship. Hume held it from 1885 into the new century, working after his return to England in 1894 through Dinshaw Wacha's labour in India, and the correspondence that held the provincial associations in a single web was his. An account in which the man who drafted the invitations, chaired the correspondence for twenty years and shaped the presidency's selection is a facilitator has defined facilitation out of existence.

The selection of the first president illustrates the autonomy thesis's difficulty in miniature. Bengal supplied the Congress's senior political organisation in Banerjee's Indian Association, and Bombay supplied its host machinery; a Bengali barrister of distinction who belonged to neither city's machine, Bonnerjee, whose presidency Hume promoted, allowed both components to join a body neither had captured. The pattern recurred in the secretaryship's division of labour: Hume's name headed the correspondence, Wacha's labour sustained it from Bombay, and the body's Indian leaders accepted the arrangement because the Englishman's pen carried their resolutions into rooms their own pens could not enter. B. R. Nanda's biographical work on the Moderate generation, including his Gokhale of 1977, documented from the inside how the arrangement felt to its Indian participants: not subordination but deployment, the deliberate use of a founder whose biography was itself an instrument. The autonomy thesis requires its readers to treat all of this as scaffolding around an Indian building. The record reads more naturally the other way: the scaffolding was part of the design, and the designer of the scaffolding was Hume.

Anil Seal's The Emergence of Indian Nationalism, published in 1968, applied a third pressure that neither thesis survives intact. Seal read the early Congress through the competition of educated elites for place, patronage and precedence within the colonial order, and found its politics explicable as the manoeuvres of provincial factions whose all-India rhetoric outran their all-India organisation. Seal's reduction has its own excesses, and the economic and ideological content of the Moderate programme, Naoroji's drain argument above all, cannot be dissolved into place-seeking without remainder. But Seal's institutional scepticism usefully disciplines both inherited tellings. Against the safety valve, he shows founders with local interests too various to be an empire's instrument. Against autonomy, he shows an assertion too entangled with competition for the state's favours to be the nation's undivided voice. What survives Seal, and what this article defends, is the institution itself as the object of study: whatever the founders intended, competed for or feared, they built a machine with four identifiable design choices, and the machine's subsequent behaviour, its capacities and its characteristic failures, followed from the design more faithfully than from any of the intentions.

## The Moderate Phase in Operation: Concessions, Critique and the Bengal Test

An architecture is known by its outputs, and the Moderate phase's twenty-two years produced a record substantial enough to embarrass any account of the period as wasted time. The first output was absorption. The Calcutta session of December 1886, presided over by Naoroji with 434 delegates in attendance, folded Banerjee's Indian National Conference into the Congress and resolved to plant provincial Congress committees across the country, giving the seasonal body a thin but real year-round skeleton. The second output was concession. The Indian Councils Act, which received royal assent on 20 June 1892 after years of Congress memorials and London lobbying through the British Committee, enlarged the legislative councils, armed their members with the right to discuss the annual budget and to put questions on notice, and arranged nomination so that municipalities, district boards, universities and chambers of commerce recommended the men the government then appointed. Election was conceded in practice and denied in name, which is the precise signature of petition politics succeeding: the substance moved a measured distance in the direction of the demand, and the state kept the credit for the movement.

The third output was intellectual, and it outlasted the phase. Naoroji's election to the House of Commons for Finsbury Central in July 1892, won for the Liberal Party by a margin of five votes after recounts, made him the first Indian to sit in the imperial Parliament, and his evidence before royal commissions and his parliamentary questions fed the drain argument that his Poverty and Un-British Rule in India, published in 1901, assembled into a single indictment: India's wealth was transferred to Britain through home charges, pensions and the structure of trade, and the transfer, not Indian incapacity, explained Indian poverty. Romesh Chunder Dutt, who presided at Lucknow in 1899 and demanded the permanent settlement of the land revenue, gave the critique its economic history in the two volumes of his Economic History of India of 1902 and 1904. Gokhale, elected to the Imperial Legislative Council in 1902, turned the annual budget debate into a seminar on the drain's arithmetic, and in June 1905 at Poona he founded the Servants of India Society to train full-time political workers, an implicit admission that the seasonal architecture needed supplementing by men who gave politics their whole lives. The founding generation's habits of constitutional argument ran directly into the deliberative culture that [the founding-period long-arc context](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) traces forward into the republic's constitution-making; the comparison with the later founders is instructive, since the men of 1947 worked as a triangulated leadership of a kind the scholarship calls [the founding triangulation as later comparator](/2018/12/17/patel-nehru-ambedkar-founding-triangulation/), whereas the men of 1885 worked as a federation of provincial associations held together by a secretary's correspondence.

The councils in operation supplied the phase's most instructive single datum, and it cuts both ways in the adjudication. On 2 June 1893 the House of Commons itself, after years of British Committee briefing and Congress memorials, carried a resolution in favour of holding the Indian Civil Service examinations simultaneously in England and India, the founding session's own demand. The India Office declined to give the resolution effect, and the demand remained unmet for a generation. Petition-politics had won the argument in the sovereign legislature and lost the policy in the department, a result that taught two lessons at once: the method's reach was real, since it could carry a Commons division against the department's wishes, and its ceiling was structural, since a department responsible to no Indian constituency could simply decline. The men who filled the enlarged councils under the 1892 Act's recommendation system, Mehta in Bombay among them, used the budget discussions and the question hour to prosecute the same case inside the constitution, and their performances built the parliamentary tradition the later Congress inherited. But the 1893 lesson lodged in the movement's memory, and when the Extremists of the next decade argued that resolutions had reached their limit, they cited the simultaneous-examinations vote as exhibit one: the empire had been persuaded, in form, and had proceeded exactly as before.

### When did the partition of Bengal take effect?

Lord Curzon's partition of Bengal, announced in July 1905, took effect on 16 October 1905, dividing the province into a reduced Bengal and a new province of Eastern Bengal and Assam. Calcutta observed the day in mourning, and Rabindranath Tagore's rakhi-tying across the new boundary turned a bureaucratic act into the swadeshi movement's founding symbol.

Two further outputs of the operation belong in the record before the terminal crisis, because both show the architecture extending itself in directions its founders had not scripted. At Calcutta in 1901, under Wacha's presidency, the Congress attached an industrial exhibition to its session for the first time, displaying Indian manufactures alongside the deliberations, and the exhibition became a fixture of Congress week. The device translated the drain argument into objects a delegate could handle: the economic critique, which had lived in Naoroji's statistics and Dutt's histories, acquired a showroom, and swadeshi acquired a Congress pedigree four years before the Bengal agitation made it a battle cry. At Bombay in 1904 the presidency went to Henry Cotton, a retired Bengal civilian who had written sympathetically of Indian aspirations in his New India of 1885 and who used his address to bless the Congress's programme from inside the services' own fellowship. A former chief commissioner in the chair was the petition architecture's ideal of itself made visible: the case for India argued so reasonably, by such men, that the empire's own servants supplied the chairman. Within three years the same architecture would be denounced by its heirs as a drawing room, which is less a refutation of the Cotton moment than a measure of how completely the political weather changed between 1904 and Surat.

The compromise of 1906 deserves its own paragraph, because its drafting history contains Surat in embryo. The four Calcutta resolutions were negotiated in the subjects committee between the Moderate leadership, determined to keep the body's professions within the constitution, and the Extremist cohort, determined to commit the Congress to boycott as a political weapon of general application. The wording that carried the session confined the boycott's endorsement in terms the Moderates could afterwards read as Bengal-specific and extraordinary, a legitimate weapon in the province's exceptional circumstances, and Tilak's camp could read as the Congress's adoption of the new politics entire. Naoroji's presidential authority held the formula together for a year. What no one at Calcutta possessed was a procedure for authoritatively interpreting a resolution once adopted, since the body that might have interpreted it, a permanent executive armed with a constitution, was one of the things the founding had declined to build. The rival readings travelled back to the provinces as rival mandates, the Maharashtra and Bengal organisations drilled their delegates for the next session on incompatible instructions, and the Surat meeting of December 1907 opened with the presidency itself, Rash Behari Ghosh's election, already contested in the corridors. A body designed to display unanimity had generated two texts of its own most important resolution, and the architecture's founding choices, no constitution, no executive, no roll, left the dispute to be settled by voice vote in a hall that ended by settling it with a thrown shoe and a police-cleared pandal.

Bengal's answer, it should be recorded, was led in its first hours by the founding generation itself, which complicates every simple story of Moderate irrelevance in 1905. On 16 October 1905, the day the partition took effect, Ananda Mohan Bose, co-founder of the Indian Association in 1876 and a president of the Congress at Madras in 1898, laid the foundation stone of the Federation Hall in Calcutta before an immense gathering, consecrating a building as the monument of Bengal's indivisible unity, and the aged leader, already ill, was carried to the ceremony as the movement's patriarch. Banerjee, in his late sixties, threw the Bengalee and his own formidable oratory into the boycott campaign and walked the procession routes of the capital. The founding generation could still fill a street when the cause was Bengal's unity. What it could not do was convert the street into the Congress, because the Congress it had built had no door through which a street could enter: no membership to offer the crowds, no cadre to marshal them, no authority between sessions to direct them. The swadeshi movement therefore grew its own organisations beside the Congress, in the samitis and the national schools, and the Congress of 1906 and 1907 faced a movement that owed it reverence and did not owe it obedience. The interface failure was not that the Moderates opposed the new politics. It was that their architecture gave the new politics no constitutional home inside the body that claimed to lead it.

The Bengal partition was the phase's terminal examination, and the architecture sat it badly. The Moderates opposed the partition with every instrument the 1885 design supplied: resolutions at the Benares session of December 1905 over which Gokhale presided, memorials, deputations, a press campaign of great brilliance in which Banerjee's Bengalee and the Amrita Bazar Patrika led. The state proceeded regardless, because the design's instruments all terminated in persuasion, and Curzon's government had decided not to be persuaded. The response that worked came from outside the repertoire. At a meeting in Calcutta Town Hall on 7 August 1905, before the partition took effect, the boycott of British goods was proclaimed as Bengal's answer, and swadeshi, the use of indigenous manufactures, moved from economic doctrine to mass practice. The Moderates adopted swadeshi and blessed the boycott for Bengal while resisting its extension into a general political weapon, a distinction that satisfied neither the Bengal street nor the Extremist leadership gathering around Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal and Aurobindo. At Calcutta in December 1906 the octogenarian Naoroji, recalled to the presidency to hold the body together, named the goal in his address as self-government, swaraj, and the session carried resolutions on swaraj, swadeshi, boycott and national education in compromise wording. The architecture had absorbed the new demand's vocabulary without absorbing its method. Twelve months later, at Surat in December 1907, the dispute over whether those resolutions bound the Congress to the new politics wrecked the session, the presidency and the united body in an afternoon. The split that followed, and the rival claims to the Congress name that it generated, belong to the pillar's next inquiry; for the founding's story, Surat is the datum that matters, the moment the 1885 machine met a challenge it had no procedure to process.

The phase's second decade tested the architecture against calamity and a hardening state, and the record of those years fills out the operation's portrait. The famines of 1896 to 1897 and 1899 to 1900 killed in the millions across the Deccan, Gujarat and central India, and the Congress sessions of those years, Amraoti in 1897 under Sankaran Nair and Lucknow in 1899 under Dutt, resolved upon the famine administration's failures, the land revenue's rigidity and the poverty the drain argument named as the calamities' underlying cause. The state answered the criticism with the instruments described earlier: a government circular of 1890 had already barred government servants from attending Congress sessions even as spectators, and in 1897 the sedition law reached the Congress's own extended family when Tilak was convicted for his Kesari writings after the Poona plague administration's brutalities and the assassination of the plague commissioner Rand. Tilak was no Moderate, and his method of confrontation lay outside the 1885 repertoire, which is the analytical point: by the later 1890s the architecture faced, inside the nationalist camp, an alternative method gathering adherents precisely where petition had been tried against famine, plague and revenue demand and found unable to move the state. Curzon's viceroyalty after 1899 sharpened the test. His Indian Universities Act of 1904, carried against the opposition Gokhale voiced in the Imperial Legislative Council, tightened official control of the universities that supplied the Congress its members; his partition of Bengal followed in 1905. The Moderates' leader in the council chamber fought the measures with the architecture's full armament of statistics, precedent and reasoned amendment, and lost on the division every time. The losses, recorded and argued, educated the audience that mattered, the Indian political public, in the limits of the method, and an educated audience does not unlearn the lesson.

## Effectiveness Within the Architecture, Failure at Its Interface

The strongest counter to the architectural reading must be faced in its strongest form. The Moderates' record, runs the counter, refutes the language of limits. A body that extracted the Indian Councils Act of 1892 from a reluctant empire, that built the provincial networks and the press system on which all later nationalism ran, that formulated the economic critique the whole freedom movement inherited, and that mobilised the agitation under which Bengal's partition was eventually annulled in December 1911, was not a machine failing at its purpose. It was a machine succeeding at it, and the transition after 1907 reflects the Extremists' impatience and the state's repression more than any defect of design. There is force in every clause of that. The architectural reading survives it by insisting on a distinction the counter ignores: effectiveness within an architecture is a different property from effectiveness at the architecture's interface with a challenge the architecture was not built to meet.

Within its own terms, the petition-politics design performed superbly for two decades, and the achievements catalogued above are its genuine outputs, not consolations. The interface failures are equally genuine and they cluster with diagnostic precision. The design could not compel a state that declined persuasion, as 1905 showed. It could not process an internal challenger armed with a rival method and a rival constituency, as 1907 showed. It could not widen its own base at the speed events demanded, because widening required the enrolment machinery the founders had chosen not to build. Each failure is traceable to one of the four founding choices, and none is traceable to the founders' intentions, which is the test that separates the architectural reading from the two framings it displaces. Hume's hopes and Banerjee's ambitions alike are consistent with the record; the design predicts it. The generation that inherited the machine after 1907, and still more the generation that rebuilt it after 1919, understood the diagnosis in practice: every great Congress reconstruction since has been a revision of one of the four choices of 1885, in membership, in machinery, in composition or in method.

The afterlife of the four choices confirms the diagnosis by reversal. When the Congress was rebuilt as a mass organisation at Nagpur in 1920, the reconstruction addressed the founding choices one by one, as if the builders had the 1885 map open on the table. The seasonal session acquired a permanent Working Committee and a year-round executive, answering the first choice. A written constitution with a four-anna membership and a register of members answered the second, giving the body rolls against which disputes could be tested. Linguistic provincial committees and a deliberate recruitment beyond the presidency towns answered the third, carrying the organisation into districts and languages the founding's composition had never reached. Non-cooperation and civil resistance answered the fourth, retiring petition from the head of the repertoire. That the rebuilders were the founding generation's own heirs, Gandhi with Gokhale for his acknowledged guru, and that they rebuilt by negating the 1885 design rather than by extending it, is the architectural reading's final piece of evidence. Institutions are understood by their successors' corrections, and the corrections of 1920 map, point for point, onto the choices of 1885. What Surat began as rupture, Nagpur completed as redesign, and the redesign's targets were never the founders' intentions. They were the founders' architecture.

The distinction also resolves the scholarly quarrel the phase leaves behind. Sumit Sarkar's Modern India, the brief's canonical secondary source, credits the Moderates with the freedom movement's foundational work: the economic critique that taught India to read its poverty as a product of rule, the all-India political forum in which a national leadership first recognised itself, and the liberal idiom in which the national claim was first stated to the world. Sarkar is equally plain that the same men's social position bounded their politics, that their fear of the masses they invoked was real, and that the bound was structural rather than a failure of nerve. The architectural reading translates Sarkar's double judgment into institutional terms and thereby reconciles it with the Extremists' contemporary verdict, which Sarkar also records: the Moderates built the machine their class position designed, the machine performed as designed for twenty years, and the rebellion against it was a rebellion against its design specifications, prosecuted by men the machine's own nurseries had trained. Effectiveness within, failure at the interface: the formula is this article's, but the judgement it formalises is the one the best scholarship of the phase has been approaching from both directions.

One inheritance deserves separate statement, because it is the least visible and the most pervasive. The subjects committee, settling the text of resolutions in camera before the open session ratified them, was the founding's characteristic invention, and Indian deliberative politics never gave it up. The drafted resolution, negotiated among the stakeholders before the public vote and carried by a managed unanimity, became the Congress's permanent grammar, visible in the working committee resolutions of the mass era and in the committee system through which the Constituent Assembly of 1946 to 1949 processed the republic's founding text. The Moderates' critics derided the managed session as theatre, and the Surat generation demanded open contest instead, but the institution they built in its place kept the committee at its heart. What the founders of 1885 truly bequeathed was less a set of demands, most of which were conceded or superseded, than a way of conducting collective political business: deliberate in committee, document the finding, publish the record, and let the printed word accumulate authority. The petition was the era's weapon. The procedure was its legacy.

The founding at Bombay therefore deserves a colder and more respectful reading than either of its inherited legends allows. The seventy-two delegates were not the empire's plumbers, and they were not yet the nation's tribunes. They were institution-builders working with the materials a colonised elite possessed in 1885: provincial associations, forensic English, the printing press and a secretary of genius for organisation. What they built, a petition-politics architecture of annual sessions, modest membership, elite composition and constitutional methods, governed Indian nationalism for twenty-two years, educated the political class that would supersede it, and left its imprint on every successor design, including the deliberative habits that survived into the republic whose birth [the post-1947 partition context](/2019/01/08/partition-constitutional-consequences-india/) records from the other side. The 1885 founding established the petition-politics architecture; the architectural reading captures what the safety-valve and autonomy framings miss.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did the post-1969 Congress split reshape the party?

The split of November 1969, in which Indira Gandhi broke with the party's established organisational leadership, the group journalists called the Syndicate, divided the Congress into her Congress (R) and the old guard's Congress (O) and reset the party's internal architecture. The pre-1969 Congress had been a federation in the founding tradition, in which state leaders controlled the organisation and the parliamentary leadership negotiated with them. After the split, authority flowed from the leader downward: organisational elections withered, state units were staffed by nomination, and the party's federal bargaining structure gave way to a leader-anchored machine. The reshaping proved durable, and later Congress generations inherited the post-1969 architecture rather than the federal design the nineteenth-century founders and their successors had operated.

### Q: Who founded the Indian National Congress?

The Indian National Congress was founded at Bombay in December 1885 through the joint work of the retired Indian Civil Service officer Allan Octavian Hume and the leaders of India's provincial political associations. Hume convened the founding conference, served as general secretary and supplied the organisational labour of circulars, invitations and correspondence. The Indian founders, among them W. C. Bonnerjee, Pherozeshah Mehta, Dadabhai Naoroji, K. T. Telang and Dinshaw Wacha, supplied the personnel, the programme and the provincial networks on which the body stood. Historians dispute the balance between Hume's initiative and Indian assertion, a dispute this article adjudicates in favour of an institutional reading: the founding's significance lies in the petition-politics architecture the founders together established, whoever first proposed the meeting.

### Q: What is the Moderate phase of the INC?

The Moderate phase is the first period of the Indian National Congress, running from the founding at Bombay in December 1885 to the split at Surat in December 1907. Its leaders, the Moderates, included Dadabhai Naoroji, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Pherozeshah Mehta, Dinshaw Wacha, Surendranath Banerjee and W. C. Bonnerjee. The phase is defined by its methods and its social character: an annual deliberative session, a small membership drawn from the English-educated professional classes, and a programme pursued by resolutions, petitions, deputations and press argument rather than by mass agitation. Its substantive agenda joined constitutional reform, expansion of the legislative councils and Indianisation of the services to the economic critique of colonial rule associated with Naoroji's drain argument and Romesh Chunder Dutt's economic histories.

### Q: Was the Congress a British safety-valve?

The safety-valve thesis holds that A. O. Hume founded the Congress, with Viceroy Lord Dufferin's knowledge, to give Indian discontent a harmless constitutional outlet. Lala Lajpat Rai gave the thesis its canonical statement in Young India in 1916, drawing on William Wedderburn's 1913 biography of Hume and its story of seven volumes of secret reports on popular discontent. The evidence supports a narrower claim. Hume hoped the body would channel discontent constitutionally, and Gokhale replied that Hume had acted as a lightning conductor, but the Dufferin correspondence shows no viceregal commissioning of the Congress, Dufferin himself publicly dismissed the body as a microscopic minority in 1888, and the Congress's programme and London lobbying exceeded anything a safety valve required. Bipan Chandra's treatment of 1988 gives the settled scholarly verdict: safety valve describes the founder's hope, not the institution's behaviour.

### Q: Who was the first Congress President?

Womesh Chunder Bonnerjee, a barrister of the Calcutta bar, presided over the founding session of the Indian National Congress at Bombay from 28 to 31 December 1885. His presidential address of 28 December 1885 set out the objects the founders claimed for the body: friendship and cooperation among political workers from every part of India, the consolidation of national unity, the formulation of popular demands, and the training of public opinion. Bonnerjee remained a central Moderate figure, presiding a second time at Allahabad in 1892, the year the Indian Councils Act conceded part of the Congress programme. The choice of a respected Bengali lawyer of moderate temper, heading a body whose organisational weight lay in Bombay and the provincial associations, balanced the founding's regional components from its first day.

### Q: What were the early Congress demands?

The nine resolutions adopted at Bombay in December 1885 define the early Congress programme. The delegates demanded a royal commission of inquiry into the Indian administration; the abolition of the Council of India in London; the reform and expansion of the legislative councils with a substantial elected element and the right to question the executive; simultaneous examinations for the Indian Civil Service in England and India with a raised age limit; a reduction of military expenditure; the separation of judicial from executive functions; the extension of trial by jury; the repeal of the Arms Act of 1878; and they protested against the annexation of Upper Burma. Later sessions added the Indianisation of the services, the reduction of the land revenue burden, and the economic demands arising from Naoroji's drain argument, but the founding list set the programme's constitutional and administrative character.

### Q: What is petition-politics?

Petition-politics is the method of political action that defined the Moderate phase of the Congress: the pursuit of reform through formal resolutions adopted in deliberative session, memorials and petitions addressed to the Viceroy, the Secretary of State and Parliament, deputations carrying those demands in person, and sustained argument in the press. Its premise was that the colonial state claimed to rule by reason and could be moved by demonstrated grievance stated in constitutional language. The method deliberately excluded boycott, mass mobilisation and resistance to laws in force. Petition-politics extracted real concessions, notably elements of the Indian Councils Act of 1892, and built the printed record on which later nationalism drew, but it could persuade only a state willing to be persuaded, and its limits were exposed by the partition of Bengal in 1905 and the Surat split of 1907.

### Q: How was the first Congress session organised?

The founding session was organised as a conference of delegates rather than a meeting of enrolled members, because the Congress of 1885 had no membership register and no constitution. Hume's circulars invited the provincial political associations and public meetings across India to send representatives to a conference announced for Poona and transferred, after a cholera outbreak, to the Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College at Bombay. Seventy-two delegates attended between 28 and 31 December 1885. A subjects committee settled the resolutions before the open session debated and adopted them, Bonnerjee presided, and Hume managed proceedings as general secretary. Delegates bore their own costs. The pattern set that week, local selection, committee drafting, presidential address, open ratification, persisted as the Congress's annual ritual throughout the Moderate phase.

### Q: What was A.O. Hume's role in the Congress after its founding?

Hume served as general secretary of the Congress from 1885 into the first years of the twentieth century, and the office in his hands was the body's central machinery. He conducted the correspondence that bound the provincial associations into a single network, drafted circulars and reports, supervised the arrangements that preceded each annual session, and spoke for the Congress to British officials and sympathisers. After his return to England in 1894 he continued the work from London in harness with the British Committee, while Dinshaw Wacha carried much of the Indian labour. Hume's authority rested on labour rather than office-holding charisma, and it faded as the founding generation thinned. He died in London on 31 July 1912, having watched the body he convened abandon, at Surat and after, the decorum he had designed it to keep.

### Q: What was the Indian National Conference, and when did it merge with the Congress?

The Indian National Conference was the all-India political body founded by Surendranath Banerjee and the Indian Association of Calcutta, holding its first session at Calcutta in December 1883 and its second in the last week of December 1885. Its programme, representative institutions, civil service reform and national unity, overlapped the Congress's almost completely, and its membership overlapped heavily, with many political workers attached to both ventures. The simultaneity of the two bodies' December 1885 sessions kept Banerjee in Calcutta and out of the founding hall at Bombay. Duplication made little sense to either side, and at the Calcutta session of the Congress in December 1886 the National Conference merged itself into the Congress, giving the younger body Bengal's strongest organisation and Banerjee's services as one of its principal leaders.

### Q: What was Dadabhai Naoroji's drain of wealth argument?

Naoroji's drain argument, developed in papers from the 1870s and assembled in his Poverty and Un-British Rule in India of 1901, held that India's poverty resulted from a continuous transfer of wealth to Britain built into the structure of colonial rule. The transfer ran through home charges levied on Indian revenues, the pensions and salaries of British officials remitted abroad, the costs of the India Office and of wars beyond India's frontiers charged to India, and a trade pattern that exported India's surplus without equivalent return. Naoroji supported the argument statistically, estimating Indian national income and per-capita income to show a population living at subsistence while its revenues financed another country. The drain argument gave the Moderate phase its economic doctrine: constitutional demands rested on a demonstrated material injury, not on sentiment.

### Q: Who was Gopal Krishna Gokhale, and what was his place among the Moderates?

Gopal Krishna Gokhale of Poona, a professor of history and economics at Fergusson College, joined the Congress in 1889 and rose to become the Moderate phase's most disciplined parliamentary intelligence. Elected to the Imperial Legislative Council in 1902, he turned the annual budget debate into an audit of the drain argument's arithmetic, questioning expenditure head by head with a command of the figures officials respected and feared. He presided over the Benares session of December 1905, which condemned the partition of Bengal and endorsed swadeshi for the province. In June 1905 he founded the Servants of India Society at Poona, a cadre of full-time political workers pledged to national service, which amounted to a Moderate admission that the seasonal Congress needed supplementing by men who gave politics their entire lives. Gandhi counted him a political guru.

### Q: What was the British Committee of the Indian National Congress?

The British Committee was the Congress's London organisation, founded in July 1889 to conduct the body's case before British opinion and Parliament. William Wedderburn, the former Bombay judge who presided over the Congress at Bombay in 1889 and at Allahabad in 1910, chaired it, and the journalist William Digby served as its working secretary. From 1890 the committee published the journal India, which printed Congress resolutions, Digby's economic documentation and parliamentary intelligence for members of Parliament, editors and officials. The committee briefed friendly members, arranged the deputations that carried Congress argument into British constituencies during election seasons, and supplied the evidence Indian witnesses laid before royal commissions. It was the London limb of the petition-politics architecture, and its existence qualifies any picture of the Moderates as petitioners waiting passively on decisions taken elsewhere.

### Q: Who was the first Indian elected to the British Parliament?

Dadabhai Naoroji was the first Indian elected to the House of Commons, winning the London constituency of Finsbury Central for the Liberal Party at the general election of July 1892 by a margin of five votes after recounts. His candidature had been preceded by defeat at Holborn in 1886 and by years of work in the East India Association and the British Committee's circles. In Parliament he pressed the Indian case on the costs of the Indian administration, the simultaneous civil service examinations and the drain of wealth, sat on the Royal Commission on Indian Expenditure appointed in 1895, and used the Commons' procedures to place Indian grievances on the imperial record. His single term ended in 1895, but the election itself was a Moderate demonstration: the petition-politics architecture could seat its author inside the legislature it petitioned.

### Q: What part did newspapers play in Moderate politics?

Newspapers were the petition-politics architecture's cheapest and widest limb, the means by which a body of a few thousand members addressed a subcontinent. Congress leaders edited the instruments themselves: G. Subramania Iyer's The Hindu at Madras, founded in 1878; the Amrita Bazar Patrika at Calcutta, which the Ghosh brothers converted from Bengali to English overnight in 1878 to escape the Vernacular Press Act; Surendranath Banerjee's Bengalee; the Indian Mirror; Tilak's Kesari and Mahratta at Poona; and Naoroji's Voice of India at Bombay. Session proceedings, resolutions and presidential addresses were printed in full, editorialised across provinces within days, and reprinted in the British Committee's journal India for the London audience. The colonial state understood the dependence and answered it with press legislation and, in time, sedition prosecutions, of which Tilak's conviction of 1897 was the phase's warning.

### Q: How did the colonial government treat the early Congress?

Official treatment moved through three registers across the Moderate phase: tolerance, snub and obstruction. In 1885 Lord Dufferin's government let the founding proceed and received its loyal addresses without commitment. The turn came as the delegate body grew: at Allahabad in 1888 the Lieutenant Governor, Auckland Colvin, attempted to obstruct the session's site arrangements, officials were discouraged from association with the Congress, and Dufferin, at the St Andrew's dinner of 30 November 1888, dismissed the body as a microscopic minority. Through the 1890s the register hardened into surveillance, official circulars against government servants' participation, and press and sedition law aimed at the newspapers that carried Congress argument. The trajectory matters to the founding debate: an empire does not obstruct, surveil and prosecute the instruments of its own safety valve with steadily increasing energy for two decades.

### Q: How large did the annual Congress sessions grow in the founding years?

The delegate body grew quickly from its small founding base and then settled at the scale the architecture required. Seventy-two delegates attended at Bombay in December 1885. The Calcutta session of 1886, reinforced by the merger of the Indian National Conference, drew 434. Madras in 1887 drew 607, and Allahabad in 1888 drew 1,248, a figure large enough to alarm the provincial administration and to feature in official complaint. Thereafter attendance through the 1890s and into the new century fluctuated in the hundreds and low thousands according to the host city's accessibility and the temperature of politics, without any organisational drive to maximise it. Delegates were selected locally, paid their own way and came to deliberate, not to demonstrate; the founders measured a session by the weight of its resolutions and the standing of its platform, not by the size of its crowd.

### Q: What is Anil Seal's reading of the early Congress?

Anil Seal's The Emergence of Indian Nationalism, published in 1968, read the early Congress through the competition of educated Indian elites for employment, patronage and precedence within the colonial order. On Seal's account, the all-India rhetoric of the founding generation covered provincial and local rivalries: factions of lawyers, journalists and association men used the Congress platform to press claims their provinces and professions generated, and the body's politics followed the contours of that competition. The reading disciplines both inherited legends of the founding, since founders busy competing for place are neither an empire's simple instrument nor a nation's undivided voice. Critics answer that the drain argument and the constitutional programme cannot be dissolved into place-seeking without remainder. This article treats Seal as a necessary solvent and locates the founding's durable significance in the institution the competitors jointly built.

### Q: Why did the Congress work without a written constitution for over two decades?

The Congress worked without a written constitution from 1885 to 1908 because its founding design had no use for one and could not have operated the machinery a constitution implies. A body that exists to deliberate and petition needs custom, a president acclaimed on the host committee's recommendation, a general secretary serving by consent, and resolutions settled in committee; it does not need a membership roll, chartered units or disciplinary procedure, and the founders built none. Custom sufficed while the founding generation's prestige held the federation of associations together and the colonial state served as the common antagonist. The lack became a liability only when the challenge turned internal after 1905. The Surat collapse of December 1907, which no procedure existed to arbitrate, forced the question, and the Moderate convention committee at Allahabad in April 1908 framed the constitution the Madras session adopted that December.

### Q: Which provincial associations prepared the ground for the Congress?

Four associations supplied the founding's organisational preconditions and much of its personnel. The Poona Sarvajanik Sabha, founded in 1870 by Mahadev Govind Ranade, Ganesh Vasudeo Joshi and their associates, had petitioned on revenue, forest and municipal questions for fifteen years and anchored the Deccan's political work. The Indian Association, founded at Calcutta on 26 July 1876 by Surendranath Banerjee and Ananda Mohan Bose, campaigned across northern India on the civil service age limit and the Ilbert Bill. The Madras Mahajana Sabha, founded in May 1884, organised the Madras presidency's political class around P. Ananda Charlu and his circle. The Bombay Presidency Association, founded in January 1885 with Pherozeshah Mehta, K. T. Telang and Badruddin Tyabji at its head, ran the founding session's local arrangements. Their papers, preserved in regional archives, are the founding's under-cited archive.
