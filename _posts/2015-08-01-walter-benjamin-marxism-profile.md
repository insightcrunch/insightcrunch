Walter Benjamin is probably the most quoted Marxist critic of the twentieth century and one of the least carefully read. His sentences travel everywhere: into film studies syllabi, museum catalogues, design manifestos and the footnotes of disciplines he never heard of. Yet the text on which the reputation rests, the essay on the work of art and its reproduction, reaches most of its readers in a form its author did not finally approve, under a title that mistranslates its central term, attributed to a school he never joined. A profile that repeats the standard summary, aura, mechanical reproduction, optimism about the masses, would pass on all three errors at once. This profile is built to correct them, because the corrections are not pedantic footnotes to the life. They change what the work is understood to claim.

![Walter Benjamin and mass culture, aura, the artwork essay and the Arcades - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The canonical statement is quickly made. Walter Benjamin was a German critic and essayist, born in Berlin in 1892, associated with the Frankfurt School but never a member of it, funded by its institute and published in its journal while remaining an outsider to its programme. His writing joined three elements that his contemporaries usually kept apart: a Marxist analysis of culture under capitalism, a set of categories drawn from Jewish religious thought, and a literary critic's attention to the single charged detail. His most influential essay analysed how the technological reproducibility of artworks transforms their status, dissolving what he called their aura and shifting their social function from cult to exhibition. He died at the Spanish border in September 1940, a refugee from occupied France. Everything else in this profile is an elaboration of that paragraph, and much of it consists of showing where the paragraph's familiar short forms mislead.

Three corrections organise the argument. The first concerns the text itself. The artwork essay exists in three versions composed between 1935 and 1939, and the version most widely read in English, the third, is not the version scholars treat as standard, which is the second. The second concerns the title. The familiar English rendering speaks of mechanical reproduction, but the German term names something different: technische Reproduzierbarkeit, technological reproducibility, a capacity the artwork possesses under modern technical conditions rather than a mechanical process inflicted on it from outside. That distinction, which this article calls the reproducibility correction, decides whether the essay is read as a piece of technological determinism or as an analysis of a changed relation between works and publics. The third correction concerns the institution. Benjamin was paid by the Institute for Social Research and argued with its leading members for a decade, but he held no post there, attended none of its internal deliberations as a member, and was treated by its director in New York as a talented and difficult contractor rather than a colleague. The school's complete institutional history is told in the companion [Frankfurt School complete guide](/2015/06/01/frankfurt-school-complete-guide/); here the relationship matters because it shaped what he wrote, what he was asked to revise, and what he refused to change.

Around those corrections the life arranges itself with unusual neatness, because Benjamin's biography is largely the biography of his texts and their circumstances. A wealthy childhood in Wilhelmine Berlin produced a collector and a connoisseur of the nineteenth century city. A failed academic career produced an independent critic who lived by reviews, radio talks and translation. A friendship with a Latvian theatre director in Capri and then with Bertolt Brecht turned a metaphysical critic of language into a materialist. Thirteen years of work on the Paris arcades produced an unfinished archive that became, after his death, one of the most influential ruins in modern scholarship. And the decade of exile produced the two short works on which his fame now rests, the artwork essay and the theses on the concept of history, written in the shadow of a catastrophe he analysed more lucidly than he escaped.

## A Berlin childhood and a scholar without a chair

He was born on 15 July 1892 into the prosperous stratum of Berlin's assimilated Jewish bourgeoisie. His father Emil was a banker turned dealer in art and antiques, and the household combined commercial wealth with a cultivated taste for old objects, fine editions and the rituals of collection. The son inherited the taste and inverted its purpose. Where the father accumulated, the son studied accumulation itself: the child's rooms, the collector's cabinet and the antique shop became his first laboratories for a lifelong question about how objects carry meaning and how that meaning decays or survives. His memoir of a Berlin childhood, composed in exile in the nineteen thirties, returns to those interiors with a precision that is also a theory, treating the bourgeois apartment as a shell in which a class had tried to make itself at home in a century that was already leaving it behind.

His schooling passed through the Kaiser Friedrich School in Charlottenburg and then, after illness, through a boarding school in Thuringia run on reform principles, where he fell under the influence of Gustav Wyneken, a charismatic educational reformer whose youth movement promised the regeneration of culture through the autonomy of the young. Benjamin became a contributor to the movement's journal and one of its more articulate ideologues before he was twenty. The First World War broke the allegiance. Wyneken welcomed the war as a moral tonic, and Benjamin, who had been manoeuvring to avoid conscription and who watched school friends volunteer and die, repudiated his mentor in a famous letter of farewell. The episode matters for the later work in two ways. It gave him an early education in the seductions of aestheticised politics, the conversion of violence into spectacle and meaning, which the artwork essay would name at its close. And it taught him the intellectual's loneliness of refusing a collective enthusiasm at the moment of its greatest warmth.

At university in Freiburg, Munich and Bern he studied philosophy, German literature and art history, moving through the neo-Kantian and phenomenological climates of the period while developing the idiosyncratic concerns that would define him: the philosophy of language, the nature of allegory, the structure of experience in the modern city. His doctoral dissertation, completed at Bern in 1919 and published the following year, examined the concept of criticism in German Romanticism and argued, against the grain of the scholarship, that the Romantics had understood the artwork as a fragment of an infinite medium of reflection which criticism completes rather than judges. The argument already contains the germ of his method: the single work read as a constellation, the critic as the one who finishes what the work began. A companion essay on Goethe's novel Elective Affinities, written in the early nineteen twenties, extended the method into a distinction between commentary, which attends to material content, and criticism, which attends to truth content, a pair he would use all his life.

The path of a German professor then required a second, larger qualification, the Habilitation, and it was here that the career closed. Benjamin offered the University of Frankfurt a study of the Baroque German mourning play, the Trauerspiel, arguing that these neglected seventeenth century dramas embodied an allegorical vision of history as ruin and decay which the classicist tradition, with its cult of the symbol, had rendered invisible. The manuscript ranged across theology, emblem books and melancholy treatises with an erudition its examiners found bewildering and a method they could not place in any discipline they recognised. Advised that the thesis would be rejected, he withdrew it in 1925 rather than suffer a formal refusal, and with the withdrawal the possibility of a chair ended permanently. The book appeared in 1928 as The Origin of German Tragic Drama and was received coldly; decades later it would be recognised as one of the century's most original works of literary scholarship. The pattern of the life is already visible in the episode: a work too early for its institution, a refusal to trim it into acceptability, and a reputation deferred to posterity by exactly the qualities that blocked advancement in the present.

### Why did the examiners turn the Baroque thesis away?

The Frankfurt examiners faced a manuscript that satisfied none of their expectations: it treated forgotten Baroque dramatists as seriously as Goethe, read emblems and stage machinery as philosophy, and argued that allegory, long dismissed as crude decoration, was the period's deepest form. Benjamin withdrew the thesis rather than accept a formal rejection, ending his prospect of a university chair.

Freed from the academy by failure, he became one of the most productive freelance critics of the Weimar Republic. He wrote for the great liberal newspapers and for the literary journals, reviewing books, films, toys, children's literature, radio plays and the new illustrated press. He broadcast talks for children on Berlin and Frankfurt radio, explaining stamp collecting, Pompeii and the art of telling a story with the same gravity he brought to Goethe. He translated Baudelaire's Tableaux parisiens, an early labour that funded and formed his lifelong study of Paris, and worked at Proust in collaboration with Franz Hessel. In 1928 he published One-Way Street, a book of aphorisms, dream records and urban miniatures composed in a montage form he was beginning to theorise: philosophy conducted through the arrangement of fragments rather than through the construction of a system. A journey to Moscow in the winter of 1926 and 1927, undertaken partly for a newspaper commission and partly to visit Asja Lacis, the Latvian director who had introduced him to Brecht's theatre and to communism as a lived practice, produced a diary of extraordinary observational sharpness and a set of articles coolly sceptical about the Soviet cultural settlement. He did not join the Communist Party, then or ever. He joined its century as an analyst.

The private arrangements matched the professional precarity. He had married Dora Pollak in 1917; they had a son, Stefan, and divorced in 1930, in proceedings embittered by money. His income depended on commissions, his library was periodically pawned or sold in part, and his dependence on friends, editors and eventually on the Institute's stipend structured his choices in exile. None of this should be sentimentalised. The point for the reader of the work is methodological. A critic without an institution developed the portable instruments of the essay, the review, the radio talk and the montage of quotations, and when the great subject arrived, the Paris of the nineteenth century, he approached it with those instruments rather than with the monograph his training had envisaged. The failure of 1925 is therefore not a sad prelude to the achievement. It is one of its conditions.

## The young philosopher: Hölderlin and the programme for a coming philosophy

Two apprentice works fix the coordinates of everything later, and a profile claiming completeness should name them. The essay on two poems by Hölderlin, written in the winter of 1914 and 1915, when its author was twenty two, already practises the immanent criticism he would theorise in the Goethe essay: the poem is read as a structure of relations, its truth located neither in the poet's biography nor in the reader's impression but in the functional nexus the verses build, and the poetised, his coinage for the poem's inner world, is treated as a category of its own, between the life and the work. The second text, the programme for a coming philosophy, drafted in 1917 and 1918, takes aim at Kant. The Kantian concept of experience, Benjamin argues, is calibrated to the experience of the natural sciences and is therefore blind: a philosophy adequate to the full range of experience, religious and historical experience included, must construct a concept in which knowledge does not degrade its object into a mechanism, and it must ground that concept in language, in the sphere where spirit communicates itself without an instrument. The programme was never executed as a system, and its Kantian scaffolding was soon abandoned, but its demand survived intact through every later idiom: for a concept of experience richer than the one modernity supplies, and for language as the medium in which that experience becomes knowledge. The artwork essay's Erfahrung, the theology's name, the translator's afterlife of the work, are each the programme's heirs in a different costume, and the continuity licenses the profile's organising decision, to read the famous exile from the apprentice texts forward rather than from the anthologies backward.

## The child, the mimetic faculty and the Berlin memoir

One stratum of Benjamin's thought runs underneath both the Marxism and the theology and explains his lifelong choice of objects: the child's way of knowing. In two short texts of 1933, on the doctrine of the similar and on the mimetic faculty, he argued that human beings possess a gift for producing and recognising similarities, once exercised in magic and ritual, in the reading of stars, entrails and dances, and surviving in the modern world chiefly in children and in language. The child does not merely imitate persons; he imitates things, becoming himself a windmill, a train, a shop, and in that becoming he learns the world by rehearsing it in his own body. Language, for Benjamin, is the highest archive of this nonsensuous similarity: the word does not copy the thing, yet between the written sign and its meaning a mimetic residue persists, which reading at speed, like a flash, can still release. The doctrine matters to the profile for two reasons. It supplies the anthropology beneath the theory of experience, since the decay of the mimetic gift under the regime of shock is one more register of what modernity costs. And it supplies the method of the collector and the convolute builder: to know a thing by arranging oneself in similarity to it, by constellation rather than by category.

The memoir known as Berlin Childhood around 1900, composed in exile between 1932 and 1938 and published only after his death, is the doctrine enacted. Its short chapters proceed by objects and interiors: the loggia of the parental apartment, the sewing box, the telephone's arrival in the hallway, the zoo, the market hall, the boy's Christmas and his fevers, the stolen pleasure of reading in bed. Nothing is narrated as development; everything is held at the threshold where the child's perception and the historian's knowledge meet, and the city of the eighteen nineties is reconstructed as the prehistory of the exile who writes it, a man rehearsing, in the mimetic mode, the world whose destruction he is living through. The memoir also disciplines the profile's biographical method. Benjamin wrote his childhood as he wrote Paris, as a field of charged details from which an epoch can be read, and he left instructions, in effect, for how he is to be approached: not through the narrative of a career, but through the constellation of his objects, the library unpacked and pawned, the Klee angel carried into exile, the briefcase on the mountain path. This profile has followed those instructions, and readers who find its structure thematic rather than chronological are reading the life in its author's own genre.

## Toys, playthings and the child's second nature

Benjamin's writing on children was not a sideline indulged by a kindly reviewer; it was a laboratory of the mimetic doctrine, and the profile gives it a short section for the sake of the system. In reviews and essays of the late nineteen twenties, on old toys in the Märkisches Museum, on Russian picture books, on the construction kit and the doll's house, he argued against the adult fantasy that the toy is the child's scaled down version of the real world. The toy, in his account, is the site of the child's own production: the child does not imitate the adult world so much as rework its materials, building from the scraps and discards of the grown up workshops a world of his own in which the laws of similarity, not of utility, decide what a thing can be. Play is repetition, but repetition with a difference the doctrine insists on: the child handles the same object again and again not to master it by routine but to extract from it, each time, a new constellation, and in this the child's play is the innocent form of the collector's and the historian's labour alike, the rescue of things by their rearrangement. The essays on toys belong with the radio talks for children and the Berlin memoir as a single sustained attention, unusual among the theorists of his century, to the child's perception as a cognitive power with its own dignity. They also supply the artwork essay's concept of play with its anthropology. When the second version makes play the school of the second technology, it is drawing on a decade of watching children turn the refuse of the adult world into kingdoms, and conclude, correctly, that the species learns its apparatuses first in the nursery.

## Allegory and melancholy: the Baroque book's slow vindication

The rejected thesis deserves a section of its own, because the method it announced governs everything Benjamin wrote afterward, including the artwork essay. The Origin of German Tragic Drama takes up the Trauerspiel, the mourning play of the German Baroque, a corpus of court dramas by authors such as Gryphius, Lohenstein and Hallmann that the literary histories had dismissed as bombastic failures, failed tragedies clogged with corpses, emblems and interminable speeches. Benjamin's wager was that the failure was the scholarship's, not the plays'. Read on their own terms, he argued, the mourning plays embody a coherent and devastating vision: history experienced not as a meaningful progression but as a process of decay, nature as a field of ruins, the sovereign as a creature paralysed between tyranny and martyrdom, and the stage littered with the objects, crowns, skulls, daggers, through which that vision thinks.

The instrument of the vision is allegory, and the book's theoretical labour is to rescue allegory from the contempt into which the Romantic theory of the symbol had thrown it. In the symbol, as the classicist tradition praised it, meaning and form fuse in a beautiful instant, the idea shining through the image in organic unity. In allegory, the relation is arbitrary, mournful and temporal: the allegorist heaps images whose meanings are assigned by convention and knowledge, and any object can be made to mean anything, because the world itself, emptied of immanent meaning, offers its things to be read. Benjamin's notorious sentence on this, paraphrased here to its bones, runs that allegories are in the realm of thoughts what ruins are in the realm of things. The ruined object is not a defect of the method. It is the method's honesty: the Baroque looked at a world from which transcendence had withdrawn, and allegory is the gaze that lingers on the fragments and finds, in their very brokenness, the script of history. Melancholy, the humour of the period's medicine and of Dürer's famous engraving, is the disposition of the allegorist, the brooding attention that refuses to let dead things go.

The examiners of 1925 can hardly be blamed for failing to see a career in this, and the book's cold reception in 1928 confirmed the failure. Its vindication came slowly and then completely. The concepts it forged, allegory against symbol, the ruin as the truth of the historical object, the constellation as the form of presentation, the idea as a monad in which a whole epoch is legible in miniature, became the load bearing members of the later work. The Arcades are the Trauerspiel method applied to the nineteenth century: Paris read as the Baroque stage was read, its arcades and commodities as emblems in a book of nature turned to stone. The artwork essay inherits the same discipline: approach a mass phenomenon, film, with the patience the allegorist brings to a skull on a stage, and read in its technical constitution the fate of an entire organisation of experience. A reader who finds the later work's method abrupt has usually skipped the book where it was earned, and examiners in the German tradition have been known to test for exactly that omission.

### Was the Baroque book a failure when it appeared?

By its moment's measures, yes: it was barely reviewed, sold poorly, and confirmed its author's exile from the professoriate that had rejected the manuscript. By the measure of its afterlife, no work of Benjamin's has travelled into other disciplines: art history took its allegory, literary studies its theory of the ruin, and the Arcades Project is unthinkable without its method.

## Criticism as mortification: the Goethe essay and the life of works

The essay on Goethe's Elective Affinities, composed between 1919 and 1922 and published in the middle of the decade, is the major work of Benjamin's pre Marxist years and the place where his concept of criticism receives its charter. Its starting distinction, mentioned in this profile's first section, deserves its full statement. Every significant work, Benjamin proposes, has a material content, the Sachgehalt, what it presents and arranges from the stuff of its world, and a truth content, the Wahrheitsgehalt, what the work discloses about that world when its own moment has passed. At the work's appearance the two coincide so closely that the truth content hides inside the material; as the work ages, the material content steps forward, becomes dated, curious, documentary, and precisely thereby releases the truth content to a later reader. Criticism is the name of the operation that accelerates this release, and Benjamin's figure for it is violent and exact: if commentary is the chemist analysing wood and ash, criticism is the alchemist for whom only the flame itself preserves an enigma, that of a secret of life; if the work is a funeral pyre, its commentator stands before it as chemist, its critic as artist, and critique is the mortification of the work, not its beautification, the destruction of its beautiful semblance so that its truth can be read in the ruins. The formula explains the whole later trajectory. The mortification of the Baroque drama releases allegory; the mortification of the Paris arcade releases the dream of the century; and the artwork essay performs the operation on the auratic work itself, whose semblance, uniqueness, distance, cult, is dismantled so that the truth of its changed social function can appear. It also explains his practice as a reviewer, the most feared in Weimar Berlin, who treated mortification as a form of respect: the works he destroyed in print were the ones whose truth contents he had taken seriously, and the politeness of mere appreciation was reserved for the negligible. Students who meet the tenderness of the late Benjamin, the rescuer of discarded things, should set beside it this early doctrine of the critic as the work's executioner, since the rescue and the execution are one gesture performed at different moments of a work's life: mortify the semblance, rescue the truth.

## Naples, porosity and the street as a room

In 1924 Benjamin travelled to Naples with Asja Lacis and wrote, jointly with her, the short essay that first shows his urban method at work, a full three years before the Arcades began. Naples, as the essay reads it, is a city built on porosity: porous like the local stone, its architecture refuses the fixed boundary between private and public, building and street, festival and weekday. The courtyard is a stage, the balcony an auditorium box, the housewife's chair migrates to the pavement, and the sacred and the profane interpenetrate in every shrine and procession. Nothing is finished, nothing is fenced: improvisation, the art of making do with whatever the situation offers, is the city's technique of existence, and poverty, which in the northern city isolates, here collectivises, because no one can afford the sealed interior in which the bourgeois of Berlin conducts his life. The essay's significance for the profile is methodological. Here already are the moves the Arcades will systematise: the city read through its smallest transactions, the refusal to separate architecture from conduct, the conviction that a form of life announces itself in the handling of thresholds. Paris will be Naples read backward, the capital of the commodity where porosity has been roofed in glass and put to work selling. The line from the Naples essay of 1925 to the convolutes of 1927 onward is direct, and it passes, as so much in Benjamin does, through a collaboration his individual bibliography tends to absorb: the essay bears two authors, and the second is the woman who turned him toward materialism.

## One-Way Street and the aphorism as urban form

The book Benjamin published in 1928 beside the Trauerspiel study shows the same method turned to the present tense of his own city. One-Way Street is a sequence of short prose pieces, from single sentences to essays of a few pages, arranged under headings borrowed from the signage and furniture of the modern street: petrol station, breakfast room, stamp shop, lost and found office. Its subjects are the materials of Weimar modernity at ground level: the cluttered bourgeois interior with its plush and its dust, the new objectivity of the office, political leaflets, dreams on waking, the etiquette of the telephone, the child's Christmas, the art of not getting lost. The writing performs its theme. Just as the street directs the pedestrian by signs rather than by arguments, the book proceeds by placard and glimpse, trusting arrangement over exposition, and its most quoted sentence, in substance that the construction of life rests far more on the power of facts than of convictions, announces a materialism of the object against the materialism of the programme. Reading it alongside the scholarly apparatus of the Trauerspiel book, published the same year to the same indifference, a student can watch Benjamin's two registers part company: the monograph that thinks through emblems across centuries, and the miniature that thinks through a shop sign across a paragraph. The Arcades would find the form in which the two registers fuse, the convolute, where the miniature's compression serves the monograph's ambition. One-Way Street is the proof, earlier than any theory of montage he wrote, that the fusion was a practice before it was a doctrine.

## Funded and published by the Institute, never a member of it

The correction about membership deserves a full section, because the error is not a trivial slip of institutional bookkeeping. Reference works describe Benjamin as a leading figure of the Frankfurt School, and the description smuggles in assumptions about the work: that it was produced inside a collaborative research programme, that it answered to collective themes set by the Institute for Social Research, and that its divergences from Adorno and Horkheimer were family disagreements within an agreed framework. The record shows something more interesting. Benjamin was an external contributor, first courted and then maintained by the Institute, whose commissions gave him subjects and whose money kept him alive in exile, but whose intellectual direction he repeatedly resisted, sometimes in terms its leading members found exasperating. His relation to critical theory is that of its most gifted fellow traveller, and the friction of that position is legible in nearly everything he wrote after 1933.

The connection began in the nineteen twenties through Theodor Adorno, who had attended Benjamin's lectures as a schoolboy enthusiast in Frankfurt and became his most devoted younger reader, and through the Institute's journal, which under its earlier title published Benjamin's reviews and essays. When the Nazis came to power in 1933 the Institute, its endowment already transferred abroad, re-established itself first in Geneva and then in New York, attached to Columbia University. Benjamin, who left Germany in March 1933 and settled in Paris, became one of the dispersed network of stipendiaries through whom the Institute maintained a European presence. The arrangement from 1934 onward paid him a modest monthly subvention, regular enough to plan by and small enough to require supplementation by journalism, in return for essays and reviews for the Zeitschrift für Sozialforschung, the journal through which the Institute addressed the scholarly world.

The essays he supplied mark the high points of his exile production: a study of the collector and historian Eduard Fuchs, a major essay on Baudelaire that the editors pressed him to restructure, and late pieces on the storyteller and on motifs in Baudelaire that count among his finest work. But each transaction carried tension. The New York office, and Max Horkheimer in particular, expected contributions that conformed to the journal's standards of sociological presentation, and regarded Benjamin's montage methods and theological vocabulary with institutional suspicion. Benjamin, for his part, regarded the stipend as the purchase of work, not of obedience, and negotiated, delayed and quietly defied editorial instruction with a persistence that fills the correspondence of both sides. The definitive account of what the Institute was, as an organisation with a programme, is given in the [Frankfurt School complete guide](/2015/06/01/frankfurt-school-complete-guide/), and it shows how far Benjamin stood from the centre he is so often placed inside.

### What exactly did the Institute pay Benjamin to do?

The subvention was a working arrangement, not a salary and never a post. From 1934 the Institute paid a monthly sum that kept the Paris exile viable, in exchange for reviews, reports and essays for its journal. Horkheimer's office expected presentation and pressed for revisions Benjamin often resisted. He remained a contractor: funded, published, argued with, and outside the membership.

The clearest index of the relationship is editorial. When Benjamin submitted the central portion of his Baudelaire project in 1938, a study of Paris in the Second Empire built around the flâneur, the crowd and the phantasmagoria of the commodity, Adorno answered with a long, severe letter charging that the material had been arranged rather than theorised, that the dialectical mediation between base and superstructure was missing, and that the work stood at a crossroads where magic and positivism met. Benjamin accepted some of the pressure, withdrew the piece, and recast its substance into the more conventionally argued essay on Baudelaire's motifs that appeared in the journal in 1939. The concession was real, and so was the resentment it left. A member might have experienced the episode as internal discipline. Benjamin experienced it, and wrote of it to friends, as the price of bread.

It is worth being exact about what the non-membership does and does not imply. It does not imply estrangement. Adorno was his closest intellectual interlocutor for a decade, the correspondence between Gershom Scholem in Jerusalem and Benjamin in Paris forms one of the great documentary friendships of the century, and the Institute's money, whatever its conditions, quite literally sustained the production of the Arcades materials and the late essays. Nor does non-membership diminish the work's proximity to critical theory: the analysis of culture under monopoly capitalism, the suspicion of progress, the interest in experience and its decay, all run parallel to the Institute's concerns. What the correction changes is the direction of explanation. The divergences that matter, above all the quarrel over the artwork essay examined later in this profile, were not settled by any shared doctrine, because no shared doctrine bound the parties. They were negotiated between an institution and an independent, and the independence is the reason the work sounds like nothing else the school produced.

## The Eduard Fuchs essay: cultural history written for the Institute

The longest continuous piece Benjamin produced for the Institute's journal, the 1937 essay on Eduard Fuchs, collector and historian, is the clearest specimen of what his commissioned work looked like when the commission and the inclination coincided, and it belongs in this profile because its argument prefigures the theses on history. Fuchs, a socialist scholar and collector of Daumier's lithographs and of erotic art, had attempted a cultural history from below, in massive illustrated volumes that treated caricature, manners and the image of women as the documents of an epoch. Benjamin's essay takes Fuchs as the occasion for a theory of cultural history itself. A cultural history that accumulates treasures without asking after the labour and the barbarism sedimented in them, he argues, remains an inventory of spoils; the historical materialist must approach the cultural treasure as a document whose transmission from hand to hand is itself part of the history of domination, and must brush the received image of the past against the grain of its admirers. The collector reappears here in political dress, since Fuchs's collecting was itself a rescue of the despised and discarded, of caricatures and popular images the academies ignored, and Benjamin defends the method while correcting its positivism: the material must be torn from the continuum of an edifying narrative and set into the constellation of the present need. Readers who know the seventh thesis of 1940, quoted earlier in this profile, will recognise its dress rehearsal in the Fuchs essay of 1937, and the continuity matters for the fairness of the record: the theses' most famous ruling was not an improvisation of the catastrophe but a position worked out, in an Institute commission, three years before the catastrophe arrived.

## The turn to materialism: Capri, Moscow and the long shadow of Brecht

Benjamin's Marxism arrived late, by conversion rather than inheritance, and it never displaced the earlier strata of his thought. Through the early nineteen twenties his writing moved in a climate of linguistic philosophy and theological speculation, producing the famous early essay on language as such and the theory of allegory developed in the Trauerspiel book. The pivot came in the summer of 1924 on Capri, where he met Asja Lacis, a Latvian Bolshevik theatre director recovering from illness, who spoke about communism not as a doctrine but as a set of practices she had worked in, and who introduced him to the theatre of Bertolt Brecht. Benjamin read Capital in her company and began the reorientation he later described as a turning toward the material of the nineteenth century city. The sequence matters. His Marxism entered through theatre and through a city, not through party schooling, and it retained the marks of that entry for life: a preference for the concrete configuration over the abstract law, for the constellation of details over the deduction.

Brecht, whom he met personally in 1929, became the second pole of his intellectual life, counterweight to Scholem's theology and to Adorno's philosophy alike. The summers Benjamin spent at Brecht's house in Svendborg in Denmark, in 1934, 1936 and 1938, were working idylls of conversation, chess and joint projects, and they pulled his criticism toward the theatre, toward didactic forms, toward an aesthetics of interruption and estrangement designed to make audiences think rather than empathise. Adorno watched the influence with alarm and said so; Scholem watched it with grief and said so. Both suspected that the rigour of Benjamin's dialectics dissolved under Brecht's charm into a crudity the older Benjamin would have scorned. The suspicion was not groundless, and the correspondence leaves passages where Benjamin defends positions on Brecht's authority that sit awkwardly with his own finer instruments. But the influence also gave him the artwork essay's central wager: that a change in the technical basis of art might change what art is for, a proposition no purely Adornian framework could have generated. The profile of that other pole, his friend and critic, is given in the [Theodor Adorno intellectual profile](/2015/06/15/theodor-adorno-intellectual-profile/), and the quarrel between the two men over this essay is the subject of a later section here.

### What did Benjamin take from Brecht that his other friends feared?

From Brecht he took a method and a permission. The method was estrangement: present the familiar as strange so that its historical, changeable character becomes visible, interrupting empathy before it can settle into consumption. The permission was to value the apparatus itself, the theatre, the radio, the film studio, as a site where technique could be turned to new functions.

The Moscow journey of 1926 and 1927 shows the limits of the conversion, and its honesty. Benjamin went with sympathies and returned with a diary that records the theatres, the queues, the street trade and the party functionaries in prose of unmatched attentiveness, while withholding the commitment his hosts and his Berlin friends expected. He judged that his Russian was inadequate, his position that of a guest, and the diary registers a mind cataloguing a revolution's daily texture while declining to sign its ledger. The pattern repeats his whole life. He was, temperamentally and methodologically, a thinker of the threshold: attached to the Marxist analysis of capitalism as the indispensable map of his century, allergic to every apparatus that demanded his signature. The criticism that resulted has been claimed by Marxist literary criticism as one of its founding bodies of work, a claim examined on its own terms in the companion study of [Marxist literary criticism](/2020/07/01/marxist-literary-criticism-explained/), where his categories take their place in a longer tradition of reading literature against the economy that produced it.

Between the conversion and the exile stands the work on Paris, and it is time to give the Arcades their own section, because the unfinished project is the centre of gravity of everything else. The artwork essay itself began as a kind of prospectus for the Arcades method, and the theses on history were written as its theoretical armature. To understand either, the reader needs the ruin they were both designed to serve.

## Among the Western Marxists: Lukács, Bloch and the philosophy of praxis

Benjamin's turn to materialism did not happen in a vacuum, and the profile should place him on the map of the Marxism that formed in the same decades outside the parties' control. Georg Lukács's History and Class Consciousness of 1923, with its theory of reification, the process by which the commodity form stamps every relation until human activity confronts its makers as an alien, contemplative fate, supplied the generation's shared instrument; Benjamin's phantasmagoria is reification rendered as urban dream life, and his collector is the figure who refuses the contemplative posture Lukács diagnosed, handling things until their spell loosens. Ernst Bloch, whose Spirit of Utopia he had read with a mixture of exasperation and recognition, offered the complementary heresy: a Marxism of hope, of the not yet conscious and the daydream's surplus, in which the theological stratum Benjamin kept is not an embarrassment but the theory's engine. Karl Korsch's independent Marxism and the young Gramsci's prison meditations, unknown to him until too late, complete the constellation that later scholarship names Western Marxism, and the Frankfurt School article in this series treats the constellation's institutional form. Benjamin's place in it is the eccentric one his biography predicts: the only figure of the school of thought who never held a chair, a party card or a finished system, whose Marxism was worked out on the materials of cultural history and whose categories, aura, phantasmagoria, the dialectical image, entered the tradition's vocabulary while their author remained, institutionally, its guest.

## The Moscow diary: a conversion tested at zero degrees

The journey to Moscow in the winter of 1926 and 1927, already touched in this profile, deserves its own paragraph of depth because the diary it produced is the finest test of Benjamin's method before the method had a name. He went for mixed reasons, an encyclopaedia commission, the wish to see the revolution's capital at first hand, and the presence of Asja Lacis, convalescing in a sanatorium there, and he stayed for two months in a hotel room in the Tverskaya, in a cold so complete that the ink and the categories froze alike. The diary he kept, published half a century later, records the city with the ragpicker's attention trained on a society rather than a century: the sledges and the street traders, the beggars and the party functionaries, the theatres where Meyerhold's productions rearranged the very furniture of perception, the museums where the revolution was teaching itself its own pedigree, the endless meetings in which a decision ripened like weather. Its political intelligence is inseparable from its hesitations. Benjamin weighed joining the Communist Party and set out the arguments with legal care, the discipline required, the Russian he lacked, the independence his work required, and concluded in a deferral that history has judged more kindly than his friends did at the time. What he brought home was not a commitment but an education: the sight of a society attempting, in poverty and under siege, to build the collective forms whose European absence his later work diagnoses, and a prose instrument, the diary of charged notations arranged without commentary, that is the convolute method in rehearsal. Readers of the Arcades who wonder where its author learned to build an argument out of street furniture can be shown the Moscow winter of 1926, where the stakes of the arrangement were a revolution's daily bread.

## The triangle that held him: Scholem, Brecht and Adorno

Benjamin's thought was formed in a triangle of friendships, each vertex pulling toward a different pole of his work, and the surviving correspondence lets a reader watch the forces act. Gershom Scholem, met in 1915 when both were young men arguing about Judaism, mathematics and the war, emigrated to Jerusalem in 1923 and became the century's great historian of Jewish mysticism. His letters to Benjamin, learned, affectionate and increasingly exasperated, press the theological case: that the metaphysical core of the work was its truth, that the Marxist vocabulary sat on the surface, and that Palestine, not Paris, was where the thinker and his library belonged. Benjamin answered with love and evasion, agreeing about the theology, disputing the geography, and never boarding the ship. The friendship endured the disagreement, and it was Scholem who, with Adorno, edited the letters after the death, annotating the record of a thirty year argument about what his friend had really believed.

Brecht is the second vertex, and the record of the summers at Svendborg, the long talks, the shared projects and the theoretical provocations, shows a Benjamin deliberately schooling himself in a coarseness he found medicinal. Brecht's theatre of estrangement, his didactic plays, his doctrine that the apparatus must be changed and not merely supplied with new content, enter Benjamin's criticism of the nineteen thirties at every joint. Where Scholem feared the contamination, Adorno feared it more precisely, as a philosopher watching dialectics traded for slogans. The third vertex is therefore Adorno himself, the youngest of the three friends and the most relentless reader, whose letters combine veneration with an almost pedagogical severity, as though the older man's genius required guarding against its own admirers. It is not too much to say that the artwork essay was written into this triangle: Brecht supplied its wager on the apparatus, Adorno supplied the critique that forced its revisions, and Scholem, from Jerusalem, supplied the standing reminder that its author had once written, and might write again, as a theologian.

The triangle also explains the texture of the prose, which readers often register without locating its source. Benjamin writes as a man accustomed to being read by three incompatible masters, and the sentences carry the marks: the theological term placed beside the factory statistic, the aphorism that opens like a parable and closes like a memorandum, the argument that concedes to materialism in one clause and reserves a messianic remainder in the next. To ask which vertex was the true Benjamin is to repeat the error each friend made in turn. The work is the resultant of the forces, and its peculiar stability, the way it holds positions its contemporaries experienced as contradictions, is the stability of a structure braced from three sides.

## Surrealism, profane illumination and the outmoded

The immediate ancestor of the Arcades is an essay of 1929 on Surrealism, subtitled the last snapshot of the European intelligentsia, and it shows Benjamin taking the measure of the Paris avant garde with the double judgement that became his signature. The Surrealists had discovered, he argued, the revolutionary energies that appear in the outmoded: in the first iron constructions, the first factory buildings, the earliest photographs, the objects that have begun to go extinct, the grand pianos in the drawing rooms of a fading bourgeoisie. Louis Aragon's novel of the Passage de l'Opéra, an arcade condemned to demolition, was for Benjamin the breviary of this discovery, a mythology of the modern city in which the discarded spaces of commodity culture radiate a profane illumination, his term for a materialist, anthropological inspiration that reaches the reader not through doctrine but through the charged perception of things. Yet the essay also marks the limit with precision. Surrealism's cult of evil, its aestheticised revolt, its refusal to win the energies of intoxication for the revolution rather than for art, held it at the threshold it had found; the snapshot remained a snapshot. Benjamin's own Paris project can be read as the attempt to complete the movement Surrealism had arrested: to take the outmoded arcade from the poets and deliver it to the historians, converting profane illumination into the dialectical image, and the mythology of the modern into its materialist critique. Teachers pairing texts for a seminar will find no clearer demonstration of his way with a movement: enter its discovery completely, honour it exactly, and then show the door it did not walk through.

## The hashish protocols: experiment as a discipline of perception

A documented episode of the Weimar years belongs in a full profile, not for its colour but for its method. Between 1927 and 1934 Benjamin participated in a series of drug experiments, chiefly with hashish, conducted and recorded with medical friends in Berlin and Marseille, in which dosages were noted, sitters assigned, and protocols written up with the same care he gave to a review. The writings that resulted, published only long after his death, are neither confessions nor celebrations. They are laboratory notes on perception: on how the drug loosens the threads of the ego, lets an ordinary room unfold ornamental infinities, sharpens the sense for similarities and correspondences, and then releases the subject into a profundity that on sobriety reads as a tissue of trivialities held together by euphoria. Benjamin's interest, consistent with the mimetic doctrine, was anthropological and critical. Intoxication interested him as a controlled derangement of the very faculties his theory of experience describes, the similarity sense above all, and as a poor, private rehearsal of the collective illuminations that religion and revolution organise publicly. The Surrealism essay's phrase about winning the energies of intoxication for the revolution belongs to this dossier, and the Baudelaire book's attention to wine and hashish in the poems extends it. The profile records the episode in this register only: a researcher investigating, on himself and under protocol, the border where perception becomes vision, and filing the results where they belonged, in the archive of an anthropology of modern experience.

## The Arcades Project: an unfinished method, not a lost masterpiece

In 1927 Benjamin began collecting material on the Paris arcades, the glass roofed shopping passages built in the first half of the nineteenth century, and the project grew until it consumed his working life. The arcades were for him the dream houses of the collective: iron and glass constructions in which the commodity first displayed itself as spectacle, where the flâneur wandered in the crowd he observed, where fashion, prostitution, gambling and the panorama all announced a new organisation of experience. His claim, never systematised because the work was never finished, was that the nineteenth century had dreamed the twentieth in these spaces, and that to read the dream correctly was to wake from it: the phantasmagoria of progress, novelty and abundance concealing the hell of repetition and exploitation beneath. Charles Baudelaire, the poet of Paris under the Second Empire, stood at the project's centre as the lyric witness of the transformation, and around him Benjamin arranged the century's other signatures: Baron Haussmann's demolitions and boulevards, the world exhibitions, the collector, the gambler, the detective story, the poetry of the crowd.

The form of the work is its argument. Benjamin organised his materials into dossiers he called convolutes, lettered files of quotations, commentary and observation, intending to compose the book by montage: to let the cited matter speak in constellations, with his own prose reduced to arrangement and brief intervention. He described the method as literary montage and insisted he had nothing to say, only to show. Surviving drafts of an exposé, prepared in 1935 to interest the Institute and revised in French in 1939, sketch the architecture: Paris as capital of the nineteenth century, its dream images itemised, its awakening assigned to a historical materialism that reads the past at the moment of danger. The actual manuscript at his death comprised hundreds of pages of convolutes, which he left in Paris in the care of the writer Georges Bataille, who hid them in the Bibliothèque Nationale; they survived the occupation and were published in German in 1982 and in English in 1999 as The Arcades Project.

### How does a convolute do its work?

A convolute is a dossier on a motif, the arcade, the barricade, the collector, fashion, boredom, filled with quotations Benjamin copied by hand and remarks of his own. The method is montage: citations are placed in constellations where their juxtaposition generates the argument, so that a department store catalogue and a Baudelaire sonnet illuminate the same dream of the century.

Two disciplines govern how this article treats the project, and both follow from the state of the evidence. First, the Arcades must be presented as an unfinished method rather than a lost masterpiece. The romance of the interrupted magnum opus flatters the material into a coherence its author never achieved; what exists is a workshop, magnificent in parts, contradictory in plan, whose outlines shifted between the exposé of 1935 and that of 1939. Second, its influence is nonetheless real and traceable: the convolute method, the rescue of discarded detail, the idea of the dialectical image in which past and present flash into a constellation, have passed into historiography, art history and cultural studies as usable instruments, detached from the book that was to house them. The wider field in which those instruments circulate is mapped in the survey of [Marxist cultural theory](/2020/06/01/marxist-cultural-theory-guide/), where Benjamin's unfinished Paris book stands among the tradition's most consequential fragments.

### What is a dialectical image?

The term names the moment when past and present recognise each other in a flash. The resulting constellation is not the illustration of a thesis but the thesis in arrested form: the arcade of 1840 and the shopping mall confront one another, and their shock makes a history legible at a glance.

The Arcades also explain the two works of the exile that outgrew their parent. Alarmed that the method's theoretical basis remained unstated, Benjamin drafted in 1935 a short programmatic essay applying the analysis of dreaming collectives and phantasmagoria to the art of his own time, and that draft became the artwork essay. Five years later, with France collapsing, he composed the theses on the concept of history as the methodological confession the Arcades had always lacked. The essay and the theses are thus best read as the twin gateposts of an unbuilt house, and the sections that follow take them in that order: first the essay, its versions and its corrections; then the late theses and the theology they carry.

## The Hornberg exchange: the method confessed and contested

In May and August 1935, Benjamin and Adorno conducted, in two long letters around the first Arcades exposé, the methodological debate of which the artwork quarrel of the following spring was the sequel, and its terms belong in this profile because they define the method the whole project obeys. Benjamin's letter of 9 May, written from Paris, confesses the design: the book was to perform a Copernican turn in historical perception, abandoning the view of the past as a fixed point toward which the present gropes its way, for the view of the past as the point that the awakening present recognises as its own prehistory, so that what has been comes together in a flash with the now to form a constellation. Adorno's reply from Hornberg, admiring and alarmed in equal measure, accused the design of an undialectical materialism in its treatment of the dream: to set the century's dream images simply against a waking reality, he argued, was to relapse into a positivism of the given, and the phantasmagoria had to be shown as produced by the commodity form itself, its spell broken from within the object rather than by the historian's alarm clock. The criticism struck home, the convolutes on the theory of knowledge record Benjamin working through it for years, and the doctrine of the dialectical image that emerged, at once dream image and its interruption, bears the mark of the correction. The profile's account of the artwork essay's versions showed the same pattern at a smaller scale: an argument of Benjamin's that exists, in its final form, because Adorno disputed its first. Whatever the Frankfurt School was institutionally, this is what it was intellectually for Benjamin: the resistance that turned his provocations into positions.

## The two exposés: the book in blueprint

Twice Benjamin was obliged to state what the Arcades would be, and the two statements, because they disagree instructively, are the best map of the project a reader can hold. The exposé of 1935, written in German to secure the Institute's support and titled Paris, Capital of the Nineteenth Century, arranges the century under six heads, each yoking a material formation to a personage: Fourier and the arcades, Daguerre and the panoramas, Grandville and the world exhibitions, Louis Philippe and the interior, Baudelaire and the streets of Paris, Haussmann and the barricades. Its argument moves from production to dream: the new means of iron and glass construction generate wish images in which the collective seeks to abolish the scarcity and the social order that produced them, mingling the new with the residues of a classless primal past, and the historian's task is to read the wishes in the buildings before they harden into myth. The exposé of 1939, written in French at the Institute's request and shifting the centre of gravity to Baudelaire, darkens every term: the phantasmagoria is thicker, the awakening more precarious, the poet's heroism more plainly the heroism of a man fencing with a world arranged against lyric poetry itself. Between the two blueprints the convolutes kept growing, files on boredom, on the collector, on fashion, on prostitution and gambling, on the theory of knowledge itself, until the archive obeyed a logic of accumulation its author's plans could no longer summarise. Scholars dispute whether the book was approaching composability or had structurally outgrown it, and the dispute is instructive for the profile's discipline: the correct description of the Arcades is neither a finished cathedral minus its roof nor a heap of stones, but a quarry with two façades designed, from which other builders have been taking worked stone ever since.

## Convolute N: dialectics at a standstill

One convolute of the Arcades stands apart from the dossiers on fashion and gambling, and scholars reach for it first, because it is the project's theory of itself. Convolute N, titled on the theory of knowledge and the theory of progress, assembles Benjamin's reflections on how the book's history is to be known at all. Its rulings, assembled from fragments, compose the most compact statement of his method. Knowledge, against the positivism of accumulated facts, comes in the constellation: the object is not explained by its place in a sequence but read in the configuration where past and present meet. The dialectical image is dialectics at a standstill, a phrase he set against the Hegelian flow: not the synthesis that resolves a contradiction into motion, but the arrest in which the contradiction becomes legible, the then and the now held in a single charged figure. Progress is the convolute's standing target, redefined from within by the theory of knowledge itself, since a history organised as progress cannot see the constellation, which flashes up only to a present in danger and vanishes if the moment is missed. Teachers of historiography assign these pages as the theory behind the theses' practice, and researchers who cite the theses without them are quoting a conclusion without its premises. The profile records the convolute's existence and its office here, and directs the reader to it as the third member, with the essay and the theses, of the exile work's theoretical core.

## Origin as an eddy: the prologue's gift to the method

The Trauerspiel book opens with a prologue on the theory of knowledge that its first readers found the most forbidding part of a forbidding volume, and the concept it mints there, origin, quietly governs the method of everything discussed in this profile. Origin, Benjamin stipulates, is not genesis: it does not name the process by which a phenomenon came into being, to be recovered by digging back to a first instance. Origin is an eddy in the stream of becoming, a whirlpool that draws the material of genesis into its rhythm and restores, in the mature phenomenon, what was prefigured in its emergence and prefigured again in its decay. Hence the scholar's double attention that this profile has met in every section: the artwork essay reads film simultaneously as the newest medium and as the fulfilment of tendencies visible in the earliest photograph; the Arcades read the passage of 1830 by the department store of 1930; the collector attends to his object at the twin moments of its origin and its rescue. The prologue's other gift is the doctrine of the idea as constellation, the arrangement in which phenomena, mortified into their elements by the critic's analysis, are regrouped around a truth none of them contains alone. Readers who find the prologue's vocabulary scholastic are not wrong about the vocabulary, which is the last scholasticism of a mind formed by the German university it failed to join. They are wrong if they conclude that the doctrine stayed in the prologue. It is the operating system of the oeuvre, and the profile has been written on the assumption, which the record supports, that Benjamin's readers deserve to have the system named.

## The flâneur, the crowd and the detective: figures of the Baudelaire book

The Arcades' human centre is a figure Benjamin found in Baudelaire and in Edgar Allan Poe's tale of the man of the crowd: the flâneur, the strolling observer who makes the street his dwelling and the crowd his element. The flâneur is a detective before the profession of detection had its literature, reading faces, trades and destinies off the passing multitude, and Poe's narrator, following an unknown old man through the London crowds for a day and a night, supplied Benjamin with the type in its pathological purity, the man who is only at home in the mass and panics in solitude. Around this figure the Baudelaire materials arrange a whole society. The crowd is the veil through which the familiar city beckons to the flâneur as phantasmagoria, now a landscape, now a room; the department store is the flâneur's last haunt before his degeneration into the sandwich man, the human billboard, where the observer of commodities becomes himself a commodity walking. Baudelaire's lyric achievement, in Benjamin's reading, was to have registered this world in the very grain of his verse: the shock experiences of the crowd, the jostle, the fleeting glance across it that his sonnet to a passing woman immortalises, are worked into the poems as their hidden subject, the price of sensation paid in the coin of a dissolving aura.

The analytical stakes of these figures exceed literary portraiture, and Adorno's objection to the 1938 draft, that the motifs stood insufficiently mediated, was in part an objection to letting them pose as self explanatory. Benjamin's defence, enacted in the rewritten essay, was to tie each figure to its economic ground: the flâneur's leisure rests on the market's need for a scout of novelties, the crowd's shock regime on the factory's discipline and the traffic's signals, the lyric's decay on the same devaluation of experience that the storyteller essay mourns. The figures are thus the Arcades' way of doing sociology in images, and they have had the afterlife of images: the flâneur walks through a thousand subsequent studies of cities, consumption and modernity, sometimes credited, sometimes naturalised as common property. The profile's discipline applies here as elsewhere. The figure is Benjamin's by construction, assembled from Baudelaire, Poe and the Parisian feuilletons, and citing it without its construction is borrowing the glow again.

## Phantasmagoria: the dream world of capital

One Arcades term requires its own unpacking, because it does the work in Benjamin's Marxism that ideology does in more orthodox vocabularies, with a difference he insisted on. Phantasmagoria was the name of an optical entertainment of the revolutionary era, a magic lantern show in which ghosts were projected for a paying audience that half knew the trick and surrendered to it anyway. Marx had used the word for the commodity form's spectral objectivity, and Benjamin extended it into the master image of the nineteenth century's dream life. The world exhibitions were phantasmagorias, pilgrimage sites to the fetish commodity where use value receded and display enthroned itself; the arcades were phantasmagorias of the interior; fashion, Haussmann's boulevards, the panorama's painted infinities, all belonged to the same economy of enchantment, in which the new appears eternally as the newest while the relations underneath repeat themselves. His formula for the dream's structure, again in substance rather than in quotation, is that the century experienced the ever new and the ever same as a single spell, novelty as the eternal return of the identical, and that hell, in a phrase he borrowed and sharpened, is the discovery that nothing new can happen.

The difference from a crude theory of ideology is the point. The dream is not a lie told by rulers to the ruled; it is a collective sleep in which the dreaming class itself is immersed, and its images contain, distorted, the wishes of the dreamers: for abundance without toil, for a world of things arranged for human flourishing, the utopian surplus that Fourier's phalansteries and the arcades' iron construction genuinely embody. The historical materialist's task is therefore not to debunk the dream from outside but to accomplish the awakening from inside, seizing the dream image at the moment when the dreamer stirs, and reading in the discarded objects of the recent past the wishes their owners could not fulfil. This is why the Arcades attend to refuse: the outmoded commodity, the demolished passage, the fashion of the season before last, where the spell is broken and the wish stands exposed. Marxist cultural analysis in this register is a rescue operation on wishes, and its difference from the exposure of false consciousness explains both its fertility for later cultural studies and the unease of more orthodox readers, who found the dreamer treated with a sympathy their doctrine had not budgeted for.

## The interior, the trace and the detective story

A further convolute of the Arcades deserves a section, because it binds the collector to the crowd and completes the profile's account of Benjamin's Paris. Under Louis Philippe, he argued, the bourgeois for the first time made the interior the universe of his private life: the apartment became a padded case for the person, upholstered, draped and crowded with objects whose function was to register and preserve the traces of their owner's existence, as a detective preserves the traces in a room where a crime has occurred. The passion for traces is the interior's psychology. The dweller wishes the world to bear his imprint everywhere, on the covers his wife crochets for his chairs, on the cases and sheaths and boxes in which his instruments retire, and the collector's mania is this bourgeois wish raised to a method. Its detective is not far to seek: the same century that padded the interior invented the detective story, in which the trace becomes evidence and the crowd becomes the hiding place of the criminal, Poe's tale supplying the genre's primal scene and Baudelaire's Paris its richest beat. Benjamin read the two inventions together, the plush room and the police method, as complementary technologies of a class that wished simultaneously to leave its mark on everything and to disappear into the mass. The argument has aged into common property among historians of privacy and of policing, usually without its author's name attached, and it exemplifies the pattern this profile has insisted on from the start: the portable Benjamin, the supplier of detached insights, travelling farthest precisely where the convolute method, the placing of the crocheted antimacassar beside the birth of criminology, has been forgotten as the insight's source.

## The collector, the ragpicker and the rescue of things

A third figure walks beside the flâneur in Benjamin's Paris, and he is the author's self portrait in work clothes: the collector. In a talk of 1931 on unpacking his library, Benjamin described the collector's passion as a child's relation to the world renewed in the adult, the acquisition of an old object as its rebirth, and the collection itself as a magic encyclopaedia whose secret entry is the fate of the things it holds. The collector, in his analysis, liberates objects from the drudgery of being useful: he removes them from the circuit of use and exchange into a configuration where they are studied, loved and completed by the knowledge of their origin, their period, their provenance. His tact is a form of historical knowledge that the owner's catalogue knows and the market does not. It is easy to hear the autobiography in this, the son of an antiques dealer whose library was assembled, pawned, dispersed and reassembled across a lifetime of rented rooms, and whose notes for the Arcades were quite literally a collection of quotations, rescued fragments mounted in a new order.

Beside the collector stands his proletarian brother, the ragpicker, the chiffonier of Baudelaire's Paris, who gathers by night with hook and basket the refuse the city has discarded by day, cataloguing and collecting everything the capital in its ferment has thrown away. Benjamin saw in the ragpicker the unacknowledged portrait of his own method and of the historical materialist's calling as he understood it: to attend to what progress discards, to refuse the victors' inventory of the past, and to build the account of an epoch out of its leavings. The two figures divide the method between them. The collector supplies the love of the singular thing, the refusal to let it dissolve into an average; the ragpicker supplies the politics, the insistence that the truth of a society is in its refuse heap. Every major work enacts the partnership: the convolutes are a ragpicker's basket arranged by a collector's hand, and the theses' command to seize the memory of the oppressed as it flashes up is the ragpicker's ethic stated as a theory of history.

## The author as producer: the argument before the essay

Two years before the artwork essay, Benjamin stated its political logic in an address that readers of the essay should know, because it supplies the criterion the essay assumes. The Author as Producer, delivered in Paris in 1934 to an audience of anti fascist writers, argues that the correct political tendency of a work cannot be separated from its literary quality, because the quality consists precisely in the work's position within the relations of literary production. The fashionable commitment of the engaged writer, supplying revolutionary content to an unchanged apparatus of bourgeois publishing and theatre, leaves the apparatus, and with it the reader's position as consumer, exactly as it found them. What matters is the functional transformation of the apparatus itself: the writer who turns readers into collaborators, the newspaper whose letters column dissolves the distinction between author and public, the theatre whose staging makes the audience an assembly of interested persons rather than a crowd of spellbound customers. Brecht's epic theatre is the address's standing example, and its doctrine of the apparatus is the artwork essay's political skeleton: a medium is to be judged not by the messages it carries but by what it makes of the people who produce and receive through it.

The address also sharpens the profile's central correction. A reader armed with the apparatus criterion cannot mistake the artwork essay for technological determinism, because the criterion is institutional, not technical: the question is never what film, photography or radio do by their nature, but what positions they create, who is made a producer, who is left a spectator, and whether the apparatus is being supplied or transformed. The determinist reading survives only where the address is unknown, which is to say, in the great majority of summaries. The two texts should be read as a pair, and teachers who set the essay without the address are omitting the load bearing wall.

## The radio voice: enlightenment for children and the listener as producer

The freelance years had a medium that posterity, dazzled by the essays, tends to forget: Benjamin was one of the most inventive radio authors of the Weimar Republic, broadcasting from Berlin and Frankfurt between 1927 and 1933 in several dozen programmes, and the radio work matters to this profile because it is the artwork essay's politics practised before it was theorised. His broadcasts for children, delivered in a calm, unpatronising voice, took the young listeners through stamp albums and post offices, Pompeii and the Berlin of their own streets, treating curiosity as an intellectual power and the child as a producer of knowledge rather than a vessel for it. His talks for adults ranged over literature, cities and the curiosities of the book trade, and with Brecht he planned listening models, experimental programmes designed to test what the apparatus could be made to do. His theoretical fragments on radio state the doctrine plainly, in substance: the distinction between broadcaster and public is maintained by the apparatus in its present form, not by the medium's nature, and the listener must be turned from an audience into a collaborator if radio is to be more than distribution. When the Nazis took power the voice was silenced, Jewish and left authors being purged from the stations at once, and the episode closed. But the profile should register what the record shows: the theorist of the politicised apparatus had spent six years inside the most modern apparatus of his day, learning its grain from the speaker's side of the microphone, and the essay's confidence about media was an insider's confidence, not a litterateur's guess.

## Left melancholy: the intelligentsia judged

Benjamin's political judgement of his own class of people, the literary intelligentsia, survives in a review of 1931 whose title became a diagnosis: Left Wing Melancholy. Its target was the poetry of Erich Kästner and the posture it exemplified, a leftism of feeling that had hardened into routine, in which the political struggle was transposed into an object of amusement and the poet's despair became one more commodity for the stratum it claimed to indict. The review's severity has a precise structure. It does not accuse the melancholic left of insincerity; it accuses it of a function, the transformation of political misery into aesthetic consumption, of revolutionary reflexes into attitudes struck for an audience of the converted, and it names the product, the negation of everything, as the contemplative twin of the fascism it opposes in words. The piece belongs in this profile because it certifies, from within his most political decade, the standard the Part Six neutrality of this series requires for his own side as much as for any other: positions are to be graded by their function and their honesty, not by their professed allegiance, and Benjamin graded his own milieu hardest of all. It also explains a feature of the artwork essay that can otherwise seem merely perverse, its refusal to supply the progressive reader with comfortable slogans. The writer who had anatomised left melancholy in 1931 was not going to manufacture it in 1936 with a film camera.

## The illustrated press and the worker photographer

A final Weimar context completes the profile's evidence for the artwork essay's insider knowledge of the media. The illustrated magazines of the republic, above all the mass circulation Arbeiter Illustrierte Zeitung, had made the photograph a political instrument of the first rank, and the worker photography movement, which put cameras in the hands of amateur correspondents across industrial Germany, attempted exactly the transformation of the apparatus that Benjamin's address of 1934 would theorise: the consumer of images retrained as their producer, the caption restored to its office as the image's director, the picture page converted from a shop window into a tribunal. John Heartfield's photomontages for the magazine, which disassembled the press photograph's authority with scissors and pasted its fragments into accusations, drew Benjamin's admiration as the politicisation of art in practised form, the apparatus turned against the phantasmagoria it ordinarily served. When the artwork essay argues that the distinction between author and public is about to lose its basic character, that the reader is at all times ready to become a writer, it is not prophesying in a vacuum. It is generalising a movement its author had watched at close quarters in the last years of the republic, and the fate of that movement, extinguished in 1933 with the republic itself, is the shadow in which the essay's hope and the essay's warning were both composed.

## Three versions of one essay: the textual history

The facts of composition are these. Benjamin drafted the artwork essay in the autumn of 1935, working at speed in Paris, and this manuscript, the first version, remained unpublished in his lifetime. Dissatisfied, and pressed by the prospect of publication in the Institute's journal, he rewrote the essay in the first months of 1936, producing the second version, longer, more carefully structured, and prefaced by an explicit statement of purpose. The Institute's journal could not print the German text in full for a German speaking exile readership that no longer legally existed, and the essay appeared in May 1936 in French translation, cut and adjusted with Horkheimer's involvement, in the Zeitschrift für Sozialforschung. Between 1936 and 1939 Benjamin revised again, producing the third version, tightening the exposition, expanding the notes and letting some formulations harden. This third text was the one Adorno published in the collected Schriften of 1955, and the one Harry Zohn translated for the 1968 Illuminations volume through which the English speaking world discovered Benjamin. When the German collected works, the Gesammelte Schriften, reached the essay, the editors printed the second version as the authoritative text, and the Harvard Selected Writings later followed that judgement, presenting the second version in English under a corrected title.

### Why did the essay appear in French before it appeared in German?

The Institute's journal served a scattered readership, and a Paris audience existed for a French text that could not exist for a German one in 1936. Horkheimer arranged the translation by Pierre Klossowski and negotiated cuts he judged imprudent. The French publication was both rescue and compromise: the argument reached print in its author's lifetime only in translation, abridged, under caution.

The differences between versions are not cosmetic. The second version states the essay's political programme in its opening pages with a directness the third redistributes: the concepts of aura, cult value and exhibition value are introduced as instruments forged for a specific struggle, useless, as the preface says in substance, for the purposes of fascism, and formulated for the revolutionary demands on the politics of art. The third version, which most readers know, smooths some of this away, adds the celebrated discussion of film's shock effect and the absent minded examiner, and deepens the treatment of Dadaism and of the distracted masses. Neither text is a rough draft of the other. They are two states of an argument whose temperature changed as the European situation darkened and as its author's hopes for the medium of film cooled into a more guarded formulation. A reader citing the essay is therefore always citing a version, and a responsible citation says which.

Why did the translations diverge so sharply, a question the FAQ set also takes up? Because the text they translated was itself moving. Zohn worked from the third version as printed in 1955 and made the choices of a literary translator of his moment, preferring the concrete and mechanical phrase to the abstract German compound. The Harvard translators worked later, with the critical edition's apparatus behind them, and chose fidelity to the term of art over idiom, rendering Reproduzierbarkeit as reproducibility throughout the Selected Writings and thereby quietly rewriting the essay's reception: a generation of students met a different title, and in time a different argument, from the one their teachers had underlined. Translation here is not a veil over a stable object. It is one of the object's states, and the versions table treats it as such.

## What the second version knows that the third forgets: second technology and play

The scholarly preference for the second version, recorded in the table, is not an editor's fetish for the earlier text; the second version contains an argument the third let go, and the profile should state it, since it is the strongest internal proof that the essay is no deterministic pamphlet. In the second version Benjamin frames the whole analysis within a theory of two technologies. The first technology, born with early humanity, sought mastery over nature with the human being himself at stake, its works achieved through sacrifice and ritual, at maximum distance between man and the forces he conjured. The second technology, whose origin lies, he says, in the moment when, in an unconscious ruse, human beings first began to distance themselves from nature, aims at something opposite: not mastery but interplay, an increasing play room, the German Spielraum carrying both senses of a room for play and a margin of freedom, between humanity and its environment. The second technology's school is play itself, in which experiment, variation and repetition without identical stakes rehearse the species for its dealings with the apparatus, and film is its classroom because the film strip's very material, endlessly reprintable, invites the trial run rather than the unique deed. On this framing the essay's wager acquires its exact sense. The question posed by reproducibility is whether the play room opened by the second technology will be used for training a collective mastery of the apparatus or surrendered to those who stage the old sacrifices with new machines, the aestheticised war of the ending being precisely a first technology ritual, human sacrifice included, conducted with second technology means. The third version, compressing this framework, left the essay more quotable and less argued. Restoring it is the single largest gain a reader makes by following the table's advice, and it is the reason this profile has treated the versions question as the essay's first philosophical fact rather than as bibliographical garnish.

## The reproducibility correction: a capacity, not a process

Everything in the profile converges on this section, because the mistranslation is the single most consequential error in the essay's reception, and correcting it changes the argument a reader can honestly attribute to Benjamin. The German title runs Das Kunstwerk im Zeitalter seiner technischen Reproduzierbarkeit. The contested word is an abstract noun naming a property: the artwork's reproducibility, its being reproducible, in the age characterised by technical means of reproduction. English offered two paths. The path of mechanical reproduction names a process, something done to the work by machines, photography and film conceived as external instruments that copy an original. The path of technological reproducibility names a capacity, a condition internal to the work under modern technical circumstances: the work is now made, from the start, for and within a world where its reproduction is intrinsic to its existence. The reproducibility correction is the restoration of the second reading, and this article uses the corrected term throughout.

### Is mechanical reproduction a mistranslation?

yes. The German term is technological reproducibility, describing a capacity of the artwork under modern technical conditions rather than a mechanical process applied to it. The familiar English title has encouraged readings of the essay as technological determinism, which the argument does not support.

The difference is not a nuance for translators alone; it governs the essay's logic at three points. First, the direction of change. On the mechanical reading, technology acts on art from outside, and the essay becomes a story about machines, which can then be celebrated or deplored as an attitude toward machinery. On the corrected reading, the change is in the work itself and in the relations around it: a photograph has no meaningful original of which copies are degraded instances, a film is constituted by its prints, and the category of authenticity loses its grip because the work was designed for reproducibility from the first day. Second, the fate of aura. Aura withers, in the argument, not because machines stamp out copies but because the social basis of the unique, distanced, cult embedded work dissolves when the work's mode of existence changes. Third, the politics. A determinist reading makes the outcome automatic and the essay's closing appeal to a politicisation of art unintelligible: if machinery decides, no reply is needed. On the corrected reading the outcome is contested, the same capacities serve opposed functions, and the ending follows as an argument rather than a slogan. The correction therefore rescues the essay's coherence at exactly the point where the familiar reading makes it incoherent.

It should be recorded, in fairness to the older translation, that the German does concern film and photography, media whose reproduction is indeed mechanical in part, and that Benjamin's own French publication of 1936 accepted a title speaking of reproduction mécanisée. The correction is not an accusation of incompetence against Zohn, whose Illuminations volume carried Benjamin into English with Hannah Arendt's sponsorship and changed two disciplines by doing so. It is a claim about what happened next: a generation of summaries hardened the title into a doctrine, teaching the essay as the proposition that machines liberate art, a proposition the text refuses at its climax. Restoring the term restores the refusal.

## A century of argument before the essay: photography's first reception

Benjamin's essay enters a debate already a century old, and its originality shows more clearly against the debate's opening positions. When the daguerreotype was announced in 1839, the first responses divided between enthusiasm for a medium that let nature draw herself and a painter's horror at a plate that registered every detail without selection. By 1859 the division had produced its canonical statement of the prosecution: Baudelaire, in his review of the Salon of that year, denounced photography as the refuge of failed painters and a material obsession of the crowd, an industry that, by invading the territory of the impalpable and the imaginary, corrupted French art's already fragile respect for the dream. Benjamin knew this text intimately, and his Little History quietly reverses its valuation at every point: the detail the Salon feared is the clue the detective wants, the crowd's appetite for images is a historical force to be analysed rather than a vulgarity to be deplored, and the painter's displaced skills migrate into the new medium's own art of evidence. The artwork essay inherits the reversal and generalises it into method. Where the nineteenth century asked whether photography was art, Benjamin asked what the existence of photography did to the category of art itself, and the displacement of the question, from the medium's status to the category's fate, is the move his successors in media theory have repeated ever since, usually in his vocabulary. The student should hold the sequence firmly: a century of argument about whether the new image deserved admission to the temple precedes the thinker who announced that the temple's function had changed.

## Photography first: the optical unconscious and the clearing of Atget

The artwork essay's argument was rehearsed four years earlier in a shorter text that many readers find the most beautiful Benjamin wrote, the Little History of Photography of 1931, and the profile gives it a section because the aura concept is born there in a more delicate form. Surveying the medium's first decades, Benjamin lingered over the early portraits, the long exposures in which the sitters, obliged to hold still in bright light, seemed to grow into their images and acquire a duration that later snapshots lost. It was in these photographs, he argued, that aura had its last refuge: the cult of remembrance of the absent or the dead, the unique appearance of a distance in the face of a beloved person preserved in albumen. The decline he then traced was technical and social together: faster lenses and commercial studios industrialised the portrait, retouching manufactured a bogus atmosphere, and the aura was faked up as a commodity effect, a purplish haze applied to conceal the very process that had abolished the real thing.

Against this decline the essay sets the Paris photographs of Eugène Atget, who photographed the empty streets, shop windows and staircases of the old city around 1900, largely for other artists' use, and whose work Benjamin praised in the essay's most consequential image: Atget photographed the deserted streets like the scene of a crime, and the scene of a crime is also deserted, photographed for the purpose of establishing evidence. With Atget, photography becomes forensic: the image as clue, the city as a case to be reconstructed, the beholder as a detective whose attention the picture trains. Here is the artwork essay's distraction and testing in embryo, and here too is its famous sibling concept, the optical unconscious. Just as psychoanalysis discloses an instinctual unconscious in slips and dreams, the camera discloses a visual one: in the fraction of a stride frozen by the shutter, in the enlargement that reveals the structure of a plant or the gesture of a hand, spaces are brought to consciousness that the naked eye inhabits without seeing. The camera does not copy the visible world. It interrogates it, and the interrogation changes what the photographed thing can mean.

### What is the optical unconscious?

The camera discloses what the eye cannot see: the fraction of a stride between two steps, the structure enlargement reveals, the physiognomy of objects at scales vision skips. As psychoanalysis exposes an unconscious through slips and dreams, photography exposes another through its operations. It converts the camera into an instrument of knowledge, and the beholder into an examiner.

## The returned gaze: aura's second definition in the Baudelaire essay

A reader who compares the two exile treatments of aura meets a refinement that the profile should record, because it answers the commonest classroom confusion. In the Baudelaire essay of 1939, aura is defined again, in substance, through the returned gaze: to experience the aura of a phenomenon, Benjamin writes there, is to invest it with the ability to look back at us, the capacity we grant the beloved face, the portrait whose eyes follow us, the landscape at the hour when it seems to regard the wanderer. The definition does not contradict the artwork essay's distance formula; it specifies the social transaction the distance protects. The auratic object is one we treat as capable of returning our regard, and the mass production of images dissolves the relation from both sides, since the reproduced face is precisely the one that cannot look back, its gaze exhausted in advance by infinite availability. His example, drawn from Baudelaire, is the crowd's opposite: the eyes of the passing woman in the sonnet, whose single returned glance across the street is figured as a rebirth, valuable because it will never recur. Set the two definitions side by side and the concept's whole range becomes visible: distance and tradition on one page, reciprocity and loss on the other, and the photograph standing between them as the instrument that teaches a generation to stop expecting the world to look back. Examiners reward the candidate who can state both definitions and name their texts; journalists who use the word for mere atmosphere have read neither.

## The poet's halo in the mud: Baudelaire and the price of the street

Benjamin found the emblem of his whole argument about aura in a prose poem of Baudelaire's, and the profile would be incomplete without it, since he returned to it as the summa of the lyric's fate. In the little prose piece on the loss of a halo, the poet crosses a boulevard, leaping through the mud and dodging the carriages of Haussmann's new traffic, and his halo slips from his head into the mire of the macadam. He declines to retrieve it. Someone less worthy may pick it up and wear it; the poet, meanwhile, enjoys his incognito, free to move through the low places of the city like any other mortal, and the loss is registered not as tragedy but as the price and the liberation of the lyric vocation in the age of traffic. Benjamin's reading draws the moral in his own key. The halo is the aura in the form of a personal attribute, the nimbus of the inspired singer whose authority rested on distance from the crowd, and its loss in the street is the precise moment lyric poetry accepts its new conditions: immersion in the mass, exposure to shock, the exchange of consecration for incognito. That Baudelaire stages the loss as a comedy, and profits by it in freedom, is the reading's sharpest point, since it disposes of the elegiac Benjamin of the summaries from within his master's own work. The poet does not weep over the halo. He checks the traffic, pockets his anonymity, and gets on with the poems, which are greater for the mud on their maker's shoes.

## Aura: the unique appearance of a distance

Aura is the essay's most borrowed and most abused word. In the flattened usage of the summaries, it names a glow, a charisma, the mystique of the masterpiece, and Benjamin is made into its mourner, a nostalgic regretting that reproductions lack the magic of originals. The text defines something colder and more precise. In the artwork essay, and in a cognate formulation in his short history of photography written a few years earlier, Benjamin defines the aura as "the unique phenomenon of a distance, however close it may be": the experience of an object, or a mountain range, or a branch at rest, as set apart in its here and now, embedded in a tradition, unapproachable in the specific sense that the beholder cannot make it fully present or fully his own. Distance here is not metres. It is the object's residue of uniqueness and duration, its testimony to the history it has experienced, above all its embeddedness in ritual, which first gave artworks their function and their authority.

Unpack the definition and the sociology becomes visible. The auratic work is unique, and its uniqueness is inseparable from its place in a tradition: the statue in the temple, the altarpiece in the chapel, the portrait in the ancestral hall. Its value is cult value, realised in worship or in the secularised rituals of art religion that succeeded worship, and its mode of existence includes concealment as much as display, since many cult objects were seen rarely, by priests, on feast days, or not at all. Reproducibility dissolves this configuration not by damaging objects but by detaching the reproduced thing from the domain of tradition altogether. The copy meets the beholder halfway, in his own situation: the cathedral leaves its locale to be received in the studio of the art lover, the choral masterpiece is heard in the parlour. What withers in the age of technological reproducibility is the aura of the work, and the withering is a social symptom before it is an aesthetic one, the sign of masses whose desire is to bring things closer, spatially and humanly, overcoming uniqueness by extracting sameness even from the unique object through its reproduction.

### What happens to a work when its copy can stand anywhere?

The work loses the authority of its here and now. A reproduced painting can hang in a textbook, a waiting room and a student's wall, and is received inside the beholder's situation rather than on the ground of the tradition that housed it. Authenticity, which depended on the object's duration and testimony, ceases to organise its value.

Two clarifications keep the concept honest. First, Benjamin does not simply lament the loss. The decay of aura emancipates the work from its parasitic dependence on ritual, and for the first time in the history he sketches, art is freed to take its stand on another practice: on politics. The sentence carries both a diagnosis and a wager, and the wager's outcome is exactly what the essay's end contests. Second, aura is not confined to artworks. Benjamin applies the term to natural objects and to early photographs, whose long exposures let the sitter grow into the picture, and later writers have extended it to persons, commodities and places. The disciplined use, however, is the one tied to the historical argument: aura names the configuration of uniqueness, distance and tradition that a changed technical basis renders untenable, and arguments that deploy the word without that argument are borrowing the glow the concept was designed to dispel.

## Cult value, exhibition value and the education of distraction

The essay's engine is a polarity. Works of art, Benjamin argues, are received with two different accents: one on their cult value, the other on their exhibition value. The first poles the work toward invisibility and ritual presence; the second toward display, circulation and the crowd. Prehistory's cave painting, made for spirits, and the medieval Madonna, veiled for most of the year, stand near the cult pole; the photograph and the film, made to be shown everywhere at once, stand near the exhibition pole, and the entire history the essay compresses is the migration of art between the two. The migration is quantitative before it is qualitative: as exhibition value becomes absolute, as with film, where the work's transportability and audience are constitutive, the very criteria by which art was judged, creativity, genius, eternal value, mystery, are exposed as the theology of an earlier configuration. Art criticism that clings to them is not defending art. It is defending the cult under another name.

### How do cult value and exhibition value differ in practice?

Cult value rests on the work's existence and its place in a rite: the statue works by being there, whether or not anyone sees it, and concealment raises its power. Exhibition value rests on the work's being seen: the portrait bust, made to be displayed, begins the migration, and the photograph completes it, since a print is made for circulation.

Film is the essay's test case and its protagonist. The film actor performs not for a present audience but for an apparatus, and the audience, identifying with the camera, tests the performance rather than submitting to it; the aura that surrounded the stage actor, the unique presence of his body in a shared space and moment, cannot survive the studio. Around the actor the essay builds an account of the cameraman as surgeon against the painter as magician, of shots assembled in montage rather than scenes enacted in continuity, of a public that receives the work collectively and in distraction. Distraction is the essay's most misunderstood term after aura. It does not mean inattention. Benjamin contrasts concentration, in which the beholder is absorbed by the work, with distraction, in which the work is absorbed by the beholder, the way a building is used by touch and habit as much as by sight. The masses, he argues, are absent minded examiners, and their mode of testing a film, half amusement, half appraisal, is a form of expertise in training: the capacity to meet the shocks of modern experience with a practised, collective attention. Here the essay's hopes for the medium are at their highest, and it was here that Adorno's objection fastened, as a later section shows.

The afterlife of the film argument inside film theory is a subject of its own, and the companion study of [Marxist film theory](/2020/08/15/marxist-film-theory-explained/) traces how the essay's categories were taken up, revised and contested by the discipline it helped to found. Within this profile the necessary point is the symmetry Benjamin insists on. The same technical conditions that let film train a distracted public also let it manufacture the cult of the star and the spell of the commodity; the essay calls film capital's answer to the withering of aura a compensating construction of personality outside the studio. Nothing in the mechanism guarantees its direction. That refusal of guarantee is the bridge to the essay's ending, the most quoted and least heeded pages Benjamin ever wrote.

## Architecture, Dada and the tactile: reception by use

A thread of the artwork essay that summaries consistently drop is its detour through architecture, and the detour is load bearing, because buildings supply the essay's prototype for the reception it predicts. Architecture, Benjamin observes, has always been received in a twofold manner, by use and by perception, by touch and by sight, and the tactile appropriation is accomplished less by attention than by habit: the dweller knows his building the way he knows his own coat, and even the distracted tourist, guided by touch, performs a genuine appropriation that no contemplation of a facade achieves. Buildings thus prove that a profound, collective, expert reception in distraction is not a degradation invented by cinema; it is the oldest mode in which the masses have ever taken possession of an art. Dadaism enters the argument as the hinge. The Dada work, built from buttons, tickets and refuse, assaulted its beholder like a missile, acquiring a tactile quality that thrust contemplative immersion out of the picture and prepared the audience, by outrage, for the film screen, where the work's shock effect meets the same touch, now organised and collective. The sequence architecture, Dada, film is the essay's quiet answer to the objection that distraction is mere passivity. Reception by use is the most demanding reception there is, because it is tested continuously against the thing itself, and a politics of art founded on it is founded on the only expertise the modern masses actually practise.

## The actor before the apparatus: Pirandello and the exile of the body

The artwork essay's account of film acting rests on an authority Benjamin is careful to cite, and recovering the citation shows the argument's workmanship. Luigi Pirandello's novel of the film industry, written during the First World War, had put the matter from the actor's side: the film actor, performing for a machine in a studio silence, feels his body lose its corporeality, evaporate, deprived of reality, life, voice and the noises caused by his moving about, and turn into a mute image that flickers on the screen and is then silent again in the midst of an eerie silence. Benjamin quotes the novelist's diagnosis and builds his own upon it. The stage actor's aura is the presence of a living person in an unrepeatable here and now shared with the audience; the film actor's performance is delivered to an apparatus that records, selects and reassembles it, so that the audience occupies the position of the apparatus itself, testing rather than witnessing, and the actor's person undergoes what Benjamin calls a kind of exile, his aura withering in the studio as surely as the cult image's withered in the exhibition hall. The observation extends beyond cinema. It is the essay's general law of the performed self under reproduction, stated in 1936, at the birth of the century of the broadcast voice and the recorded image, and a reader who follows it into the age of the podcast and the streamed confession will find the law still running, requiring the same correction this profile has applied throughout: not a lament for the exiled body, but an analysis of the new relations its exile creates, and of who owns the apparatus to which the performance is delivered.

## The star cult and the phony spell: capital answers aura

The artwork essay is equally unsentimental about what fills the vacuum where aura withers, and this section of the argument is the one the celebration reading cannot survive quoting. Film capital, Benjamin argues, answers the shrivelling of the actor's aura with the artificial build up of the star's personality outside the studio: the cult of the movie star, sustained by the money of the film industry, preserves not the unique aura of a person but the phony spell of a commodity. The phrase deserves the emphasis this profile gives it. The spell is phony because it simulates, by publicity, the very distance and uniqueness that the technical process abolished in fact, selling back to the public, as charisma, an aura manufactured to specification. Around the star the essay arranges the political economy of the new media without illusion: the same apparatus that permits the masses to face themselves in procession also permits the leader's voice and image to be everywhere at once; the property relations that own the studios decide which of the medium's possibilities is developed; and the corrupted concept of creativity, genius and eternal value, the theology of art detached from its social function, serves to dignify the arrangement. His counter example, here as in the Author as Producer address, is the figure in whom the audience recognises its own testing relation to the apparatus. He found it in Chaplin, whose tramp meets the machine world with the distracted expertise of the mass man himself, disassembling the gestures of labour and authority into their component shocks, and whose films the public could judge as experts because they were themselves the material being judged. The star cult and the tramp are the essay's two futures for film in miniature, and the fork between them is the ending's fork drawn early.

## The ending nobody quotes whole: aestheticised politics

The artwork essay does not close with art. It closes with war. In its final movement Benjamin turns to fascism and argues that the movement's deepest aesthetic operation is to give the masses expression while denying them rights: to let them appear, march, acclaim and finally fight, in spectacles that leave every property relation untouched. The aestheticisation of politics reaches its logical terminus in a war beautiful enough to be enjoyed as spectacle, and Benjamin cites the futurist celebration of combat, its gas masks, flame throwers and leaping tanks hymned as a new beauty, as the confession of the doctrine. Against this terminus the essay sets its single answering sentence in substance: where fascism aestheticises politics, communism replies by politicising art. The sentence is a programme under the conditions of the corrected reading, not a flourish. Since reproducibility has detached art from ritual and placed it on the terrain of politics, the only question left is which politics it will serve.

### Why does an essay about art end with fascism and war?

Because the argument leaves no other ending available. If reproducibility frees art from cult and grounds it in politics, then the decisive struggle over the new media is political, and fascism is the force that has understood this most clearly, converting politics itself into spectacle while preserving property. The war passage is therefore the essay's conclusion in the strict sense.

Read against this ending, the celebration misreading collapses. An essay that concludes with the enjoyment of destruction as an aesthetic pleasure, and with humanity's self alienation grown so great that it can savour its own annihilation as a delight, is not an unambiguous welcome to mass culture. Its hope is real but narrow and conditional: the media contain a training ground for a new, testing, collective reception, and that training can be turned. The pessimism is structural: the same media, under the relations that own them, build star cults, manufacture consent in images and prepare the spectacle of war. To ask whether the essay is optimistic or pessimistic in the tone of an interview question is to miss its construction. It is a fork, drawn in 1936, with the branches labelled.

The profile now turns to the man who read the fork and disputed which branch Benjamin had really drawn. The argument with Adorno was conducted in letters, at close range, over the very sentences discussed here, and it remains the sharpest contemporary reading the essay received.

## The quarrel with Adorno: the school arguing with itself

On 18 March 1936, from Hornberg in the Black Forest, Adorno wrote Benjamin a long letter about the artwork essay that remains the most searching critique it has ever received. The correspondence matters beyond biography because it stages, inside a single friendship, the central unresolved argument of Marxist aesthetics: whether the new media carry an emancipatory charge the critic should develop, or whether their integration into the culture industry is so complete that hope placed in them is self deception. Adorno, who would later give the pessimistic pole its classic form with Horkheimer, read Benjamin's essay as too Brechtian, too willing to celebrate the liquidation of aura and to trust the distracted laughter of cinema audiences as a school of revolutionary perception. Benjamin read Adorno's objections as the caution of a mandarin who could not forgive art for leaving the concert hall. The exchange should be presented as what it was: not a quarrel of temperaments, but the Frankfurt milieu's real internal debate about its own object, conducted before the positions hardened into the labels of optimism and pessimism that textbooks now assign.

Adorno's letter praises before it wounds, and the praise identifies what he took to be at stake: he called the essay one of the most powerful things Benjamin had written since the Trauerspiel book and agreed that the decay of aura was irreversible and real. The objections then fall into order. The concept of aura, Adorno argued, had been applied too simply, as if the auratic and the reproducible divided cleanly between a bad old art and a good new one, when in truth the autonomous work of bourgeois art was itself dialectical, carrying truth content precisely in its distance and difficulty, and its liquidation could not be greeted without remainder. The film audience's laughter, which Benjamin treated as potentially progressive, Adorno heard differently: he reported his own observation that the laughter of cinema audiences was anything but good and revolutionary, full rather of bourgeois sadism. The distraction thesis underrated the degree to which the culture industry had already organised distraction in advance. And the essay's treatment of film technique, its faith in the testable performance and the expert public, struck Adorno as an unmediated transference of categories from the production process to reception, the base and superstructure collapsed into one another without the dialectical labour the subject demanded.

Beneath the arguments lay two structural disagreements that no revision could have settled. The first concerned autonomous art. For Adorno, the difficulty and self enclosure of the advanced work, its refusal of communication, constituted its truth in a society of total exchange; the liquidation of aura threatened to deliver art wholly to the commodity. For Benjamin, autonomy was itself the last form of the cult, and its defence risked becoming a defence of ritual privilege at the moment history was abolishing the conditions that sustained it. The second concerned the masses. Benjamin's essay takes seriously the proposition that the collective reception of the new media trains capacities that did not exist before, and that a materialist criticism must locate the progressive possibilities inside the apparatus rather than in a protected enclave outside it. Adorno suspected that this located hope where capital was strongest. He put the point in the letter's most famous figure, describing the two positions, his own defence of autonomous art and Benjamin's wager on the politicised apparatus, as "torn halves of an integral freedom" which do not add up: a figure generous enough to concede the partiality of his own side, and exact enough to deny that either side could simply be adopted.

The friendship survived the letter, and the argument continued through the Baudelaire dispute of 1938 and beyond, but the positions never merged. Readers meeting the debate in anthologies often treat it as a preliminary sketch for the culture industry chapter of Dialectic of Enlightenment, where the pessimistic pole receives its monumental statement. That account, of what Adorno and Horkheimer built from the disagreement, belongs to the companion study of [the culture industry](/2015/07/15/culture-industry-explained/), and it should not be allowed to flatten the original exchange. In 1936 both men were still arguing about a living question with the outcome open, and Benjamin's reply, courteous, wounded and immovable in substance, conceded points of presentation while keeping the wager. The essay he revised afterward is the third version, and some of its deepenings answer Adorno directly. The debate is therefore part of the text's history: the versions table in this profile records states of an argument that its author's ablest reader was pressing from the other side.

### What actually separated Benjamin's hope for film from Adorno's fear of it?

The separation was over what the audience's distracted testing amounts to. Benjamin saw a collective that meets the work with tactile, habitual appraisal, an expertise forming inside mass reception that a politicised art could organise. Adorno saw the same distraction pre organised by the industry that sells it, and the audience's laughter as conformity wearing the mask of release.

## Letters as resistance: the anthology published under another name

In 1936, from exile, Benjamin performed an act of cultural salvage whose form deserves recording beside the essays. Under the pseudonym Detlef Holz he published in Switzerland an anthology titled Deutsche Menschen, German Men and Women, twenty five letters written between 1783 and 1883 by figures of the German tradition, Kant and Goethe's circle among them, selected and introduced with brief, exact commentaries. The book was designed, with a smuggler's care, to pass the Reich's borders and censors, and its purpose was legible to any German reader who opened it: to exhibit, out of the regime's own classical inheritance, a Germany of humanity, friendship, courage and private conscience, the very tradition the new rulers claimed and traduced. Benjamin described the undertaking to Scholem with unusual directness, as an attempt to set the image of a humane Germany before its present custodians and to rescue its documents for the readers who would need them afterward. The anthology is the theses' programme executed in miniature, four years early and under a false name: the tradition of the defeated, the private letter against the state archive, seized at the moment of danger and carried into print by an exile who signed it with a name of wood. It also stands as the gentlest evidence against the portrait of Benjamin as a mandarin of difficulty. The anthology is a popular book, made for strangers, priced and formatted for the pocket, and its author understood the gesture as among the most useful of his exile.

## The Paris years: a working life in the reading room

The material routine of the exile is documented well enough to be reconstructed, and the profile includes it because the routine is the Arcades' true machinery. Benjamin lived in a succession of modest rooms, in the last years at 10 Rue Dombasle in the fifteenth arrondissement, kept his books where friends could store them, and worked most days in the reading room of the Bibliothèque Nationale, where the convolutes grew in folders of handwritten slips and copied quotations, each item entered in his crabbed, minute script and keyed to the lettered dossiers. His research was deliberately voracious at the level of the minor and the ephemeral: fashion journals, guidebooks, feuilletons, police reports, the literature of the arcades' tenants and the panoramas' showmen, the whole subliterature through which a century confesses what its monuments conceal. Evenings went to correspondence, the letters to Scholem, to Adorno, to Horkheimer doubling as the progress reports of a research establishment of one. Money structured everything: the stipend's arrival dates are a rhythm in the letters, and the sale of books, the pawned library of a connoisseur forced to live as his own ragpicker, recurs as necessity rather than as pathos. The image of the reading room should correct the last of the popular errors this profile has catalogued. The Arcades were not dreamed up in cafés by a flâneur of genius. They were compiled, over thirteen years, by the most disciplined archival worker of his generation, in the seat he occupied at the national library until the summer of 1940 put an end to the sittings.

## Central Park: the fragments of the last Baudelaire plan

Between the rejected Baudelaire draft of 1938 and the rewritten essay of 1939 stands a sheaf of fragments Benjamin titled Central Park, after the New York green space whose artificial nature in the metropolis's heart struck him as an emblem, and the fragments are the best single exhibit of his late manner. They are aphorisms, reading notes and compressed arguments toward the Baudelaire book: on allegory as the armature of Baudelaire's genius, on the new as the quality that, independent of use value, belongs to the commodity and grows ever more intense until it confronts death itself, on spleen as the feeling that corresponds to catastrophe in permanence, on the heroism of the poet as that of a fencer duelling with the crowd. Several of the profile's recurring themes are struck here in their most quotable form, including the reflection that the unique appearance of a distance, the aura, is inseparable from the experience of the beloved's returned gaze, developed at length in the section of this profile devoted to the second definition. Scholars treat the fragments as the quarry from which the 1939 essay was built and as the nearest thing to the unwritten book's table of arguments. For the general reader they offer something else: proof that the montage method was not a mannerism of presentation but Benjamin's way of thinking under pressure, the fragments composed in the last full year of work keeping their edge even with the book around them unbuilt and the border already closing behind their author.

## Exile, internment and the last journey to Portbou

The last seven years of Benjamin's life were lived as a refugee, and the facts should be set down in the plain order in which they occurred. In March 1933 he left Germany for Paris, with intervals on Ibiza, in Italy and at Brecht's house in Denmark, and Paris became his base: a rented room, long days in the Bibliothèque Nationale gathering the Arcades materials, the Institute's stipend arriving from abroad, journalism placed where it could still be placed. The work of the exile years is disproportionate to their misery. The Fuchs essay, the storyteller essay, the three versions of the artwork essay, the Baudelaire book in its successive forms, translations, reviews conducted under shrinking names and shrinking journals: the bibliography of a man whose world was contracting to a reading room and a desk, and who answered the contraction by widening the archive.

War reorganised the precarity into danger. In September 1939, as a German national in France, Benjamin was interned by the French authorities in a camp at Nevers, housed in a disused factory and then a stadium's outbuildings, among thousands of anti Nazi refugees classified as enemy aliens. He organised lectures among the internees and continued to take notes. Intercession from French intellectuals secured his release after some ten weeks. He returned to Paris, completed the theses on history in the early months of 1940, and in June, as the German armies approached, fled south on foot and by train with his sister, reaching Lourdes and then Marseille. In Marseille the machinery of rescue that the Institute had assembled from New York began to move: an emergency visa for the United States, procured through Horkheimer's efforts, a Spanish transit visa, a Portuguese visa beyond it. The route ran over the Pyrenees on foot, by a smugglers' path above the town of Portbou, guided by Lisa Fittko, who had reconnoitred the crossing and later wrote the classic account of it.

### Why did Benjamin refuse the safer departures arranged earlier?

He did not refuse rescue; he delayed it, repeatedly, for reasons that mixed work, attachments and misjudgement. Scholem's invitations to Jerusalem met the objection that his materials, his language and his subject were in Paris. The Institute's New York was a stipend's destination, not a home for the Arcades archive, in September 1940.

On 25 September 1940 the party of refugees, Benjamin among them carrying a heavy black briefcase, crossed the mountains and reached Portbou. There the Spanish police informed them that a new instruction closed the border to persons without nationality, and that the group would be returned to France the following day. Benjamin, stateless since the Reich had stripped emigrated Jews of citizenship, ill with a heart condition the climb had strained, and convinced that return meant delivery to the Gestapo, took morphine he had carried for such a contingency and died in the night of 25 to 26 September. The following day the remaining members of the party were permitted to proceed. The death is generally recorded as suicide by overdose, and this article reports it in those terms and no others. Some details of the episode, the exact wording of the border instruction, the sequence of the police decisions, the contents and fate of the briefcase, remain debated among scholars, and the debate is noted here because the brief's discipline requires it: the record supports the account given, and it does not support the embellishments, the deathbed aphorisms, the recovered masterpiece in the briefcase, that legend has attached to it.

The theses on history, the work he had completed that spring, travelled to safety by other hands: a copy carried across the Atlantic by Hannah Arendt, another preserved by his friend Gretel Karplus, and the Institute published the text in a memorial volume in Los Angeles in 1942. The Arcades convolutes, hidden in the Bibliothèque Nationale, survived to be published four decades later. The grave at Portbou, in the municipal cemetery above the harbour, long bore an inscription from the theses; the works themselves performed the rescue their author had theorised, snatched from the enemy at the moment of danger by the people who carried them. The site has since acquired the memorial the record called for. In 1994 the sculptor Dani Karavan completed Passages at Portbou, a work named for the arcades and for the crossing its subject did not survive: a corridor of weathered steel descends the cliff toward the water in three sections, its final flight of steps ending at a glass panel set above the waves, and the panel carries, in the languages of the place, a sentence drawn from the manuscripts of the theses, in substance that it is more arduous to honour the memory of the nameless than the memory of the renowned, and that historical construction is devoted to the memory of the nameless. Pilgrims of a secular kind make the climb down and back in numbers the village did not anticipate, and the memorial's discipline matches the profile's: no statue, no likeness, no apotheosis, only a passage, a descent, and a sentence about the anonymous dead, among whom its author, in the terms of his own doctrine, is content to be filed.

## The theses on the concept of history: the angel and the storm

The eighteen short theses Benjamin wrote in the spring of 1940, with a handful of appended fragments, are the most concentrated statement of his thought and the text in which the Marxism and the theology meet without either withdrawing. Their announced enemy is a conception of history: the historicism that empathises with the victors, arranges the past as a sequence of triumphs, and hands the tradition of the oppressed over to the conformism that threatens to overwhelm it. Against this Benjamin sets a historical materialism retooled by a messianic claim. Every generation, the second thesis argues, is endowed with a weak messianic power, a claim on the past that cannot be settled cheaply, since the dead too belong to the account the present must render. History is to be seized not as an image that passes but as a constellation flashing up at a moment of danger; the historical materialist blasts the object out of the continuum of progress rather than narrating its place within it; and the calendar of revolutions, which shot at the clock towers of Paris in an earlier century, replaces the homogeneous, empty time of the timetable with a time filled by the presence of the now, the Jetztzeit in which every second is a narrow gate through which the Messiah might enter.

Into this framework the ninth thesis places the figure by which the whole sequence is remembered. Benjamin owned a small work by Paul Klee, Angelus Novus, bought in Munich in 1921 and carried through his exile, left with friends in Paris when he fled. His reading of the image is a paraphrase that has itself become canonical, and it is summarised here rather than quoted at length. The angel's face is turned toward the past: where a chain of events appears to the observer, he sees one single catastrophe, which keeps piling wreckage upon wreckage at his feet. He would like to stay, awaken the dead, and make whole what has been smashed, but a storm blowing from paradise catches his wings with such violence that he can no longer close them, and the storm propels him irresistibly into the future to which his back is turned, while the pile of debris before him grows skyward. The storm, Benjamin concludes, is what we call progress.

The seventh thesis supplies the sentence most often detached from it, the ruling that there is "no document of civilisation which is not also a document of barbarism," directed in context at the cultural treasures carried along in the triumphal procession of the rulers, whose provenance the historical materialist must regard with detachment because they owe their existence not only to the great minds who created them but also to the anonymous toil of their contemporaries. The sentence is a method, not a lament: it instructs the reader of any tradition to ask at whose expense the treasure was accumulated, and it is the germ of a hundred later programmes for reading culture against the grain. The theses end, in the appended fragments, with a reflection on the Jewish prohibition against investigating the future, which did not make the future homogeneous and empty for those who observed it, since every second was a gate. The last text Benjamin completed is thus a theory of rescue: of the past from the victors' narrative, of the dead from oblivion, of the present from the sleep of progress. That its author died within months at a closed border gives the text its unbearable reception history, but the text should be read as it was written, as an argument about how history can be done at all, and it is in that argumentative register that the theses have entered the working equipment of historians who share none of their author's theology.

## The eighth thesis: the state of emergency as the rule

The profile has quoted the theses' seventh ruling on documents of civilisation; its eighth is the ruling by which the sequence entered political thought, and it should be stated here in substance. The tradition of the oppressed, Benjamin argues, teaches that the state of emergency in which the present lives is not the exception but the rule, and that the astonishment with which contemporaries watched fascism's advance, their faith that it was an aberration progress would correct, was itself a product of the conception of history the theses exist to destroy. The task the ruling assigns is correspondingly exact: to attain a conception of history in keeping with the insight, and to bring about a real state of emergency, a phrase whose deliberateness has occupied commentators ever since, since it proposes to answer the regime's permanent emergency not with normality but with the oppressed tradition's own counter concept. The ruling's ancestry is documented in Benjamin's own work, in the Baroque book's theory of the sovereign, who decides on the exception and whose indecision the mourning plays anatomise, and in the critique of violence's anatomy of the police. Its posterity runs through the political theory of the later twentieth century, where the exception became a master category, often at several removes from the seminar in which the thesis was first read. A profile that has insisted on the record should note the proportion: the thesis is eight lines long, its author was an exile writing without a library, and the use made of it by constitutional lawyers and philosophers of law would fill the reading room where the Arcades lie.

## On language as such: names, the Fall and the bourgeois sign

The earliest of Benjamin's texts to survive entire, written in 1916 and unpublished in his lifetime, is an essay on language that sets out the metaphysics the later work never quite discarded. On Language as Such and on the Language of Man argues against what it calls the bourgeois conception of language, in which the word is a sign agreed upon for a thing and language a mere means of communication, an instrument for delivering information between minds. Against this it sets a doctrine drawn from Genesis: that every entity, animate and inanimate, communicates its mental being in language, that man's privilege is to name, translating the mute language of things into the language of names, and that the Fall is simultaneously the fall of language itself, from naming into judging, from the name into the sign, from knowledge of good and evil into the chatter of means and abstractions. The essay's audacity should not obscure its function in the oeuvre. It defines the poles between which all the later work moves: language as the medium in which things communicate themselves, against language as a traffic in signals; the name against the label; the constellation in which a thing is completed against the category under which it is filed. Even the artwork essay, at thirty years' distance, obeys the scheme. The cult image participates in the first language, the reproduced image is conscripted into the second, and the essay's question is whether the second language can be made, politically, to speak otherwise. A profile organised around a mistranslation is entitled to insist on this continuity: the thinker who at twenty four located the Fall in the degradation of the name into the sign was always going to have his most famous argument damaged by a title that turned a capacity into a mechanism, and the correction of that title is, in his own earliest terms, a small act of restoration.

## The translator's task: why the mistranslation would have grieved him

It is one of the neat severities of Benjamin's reception that the thinker of translation should have been mistranslated in his most famous title, and the connection is worth a section, because his early essay on translation supplies the philosophy under which the reproducibility correction becomes inevitable. The Task of the Translator, written in 1921 as a preface to his own rendering of Baudelaire, argues that a translation issues not from the life of the original but from its afterlife, the transformation and renewal the work undergoes as it survives into other languages and times. Translation is therefore not the copying of a meaning into a new vessel. It is a mode in its own right, in which the kinship of languages shows itself: the translator's task is to find the intention toward the language into which he translates, to release in his own language the echo of the original, and to let his own tongue be powerfully affected by the foreign one rather than domesticating it into smoothness. The famous image of the vessel runs the same way: fragments of a vessel that are to be glued together must match in the smallest details without being identical, and so translation, instead of making itself similar to the meaning of the original, must lovingly incorporate the original's way of meaning into its own language.

Read that essay beside the versions table and the ethics of the corrected title come into focus. Zohn's mechanical reproduction made the English title smooth and similar: it sounded like an English title, and it bent the original's way of meaning toward the idiom of the receiving language. The corrected technological reproducibility does the Benjaminian thing instead. It lets the German abstraction strain the English, preserves the foreignness of the concept, and by doing so preserves the argument, which concerns a property of works and not an industry of copying. The versions table is in this sense a small enactment of his own theory: the essay's afterlife across its states and translations is part of what the essay now is, and the reader who tracks the afterlife is practising the form of attention the author spent his life describing. His reception obeys his theory of reception, down to the errors, and the errors, once identified, are instructive in exactly his manner.

## Experience and poverty: the new barbarism

In 1933, the year of his exile, Benjamin published a short, fierce essay that prevents any simple reading of him as a mourner for a lost world of experience. Experience and Poverty begins from the same observation as the storyteller essay, that the generation which had gone to school on horse drawn streetcars now stood under an open sky in a landscape where nothing remained unchanged but the clouds, its stock of experience devalued by war, inflation and the collapse of every inherited authority. But the essay refuses the lament. A new, positive concept of barbarism is its proposal: the poverty of experience can be confessed and made a beginning, clearing the ground of the cluttered bourgeois interior, its plush, its ornaments, its hoarded inwardness, for a construction in glass and steel, transparent, cool, without aura and without secrets. He marshals the witnesses of the new poverty as allies: Descartes beginning philosophy from a single certainty, Einstein rebuilding physics from altered axioms, Paul Klee drawing like an engineer, the architects who designed houses as smooth shells that retain no traces, Brecht, and the glass utopias of Paul Scheerbart. Even Mickey Mouse appears, as the creature in whose cartoons a humanity alienated from itself rehearses its own destruction as a second nature and survives it, laughing.

The essay complicates the artwork argument in a way the profile is obliged to register. The same writer who anatomised aura's decay with such precision could greet the clearing of the interior with something like relief, because the aura of things had become, in the furnished bourgeois world, a kind of upholstery of the soul, a private atmosphere purchased as a defence against the shocks of the street. Poverty of experience, honestly admitted, was preferable to its simulation. This double movement, diagnosis without nostalgia, is the signature of Benjamin's maturity, and it is why the celebration and the lament readings of the artwork essay are equally wrong about its neighbour text of three years later: the essayist who in 1933 could praise glass for leaving no traces was not going to weep in 1936 over the traces' disappearance from art. He was going to ask what the disappearance made possible, and what it threatened, which is the fork the essay in fact draws.

## Experience and its poverty: the storyteller behind the essay

The artwork essay's diagnosis of a transformed perception rests on a broader account of experience that Benjamin had been building across the exile essays, and a profile that omitted it would leave the aura concept floating. His distinction, stated most fully in the essay on the storyteller of 1936, runs between two German words English flattens into one. Erfahrung is experience integrated and transmissible, the kind that accumulates in a life and a tradition and can be handed on as counsel; Erlebnis is the isolated lived event, the shock registered and discarded by a consciousness trained to parry stimuli. His claim was that modernity, and the First World War as its demonstration, had devalued the first in favour of the second: men returned from the battlefields grown silent, not richer but poorer in communicable experience, and the newspaper, with its daily flood of verifiable, disposable information, completed what the trenches began. The storyteller, the figure whose authority rests on a life that can be retold, is dying because the conditions of his art, boredom as a communal discipline, craft rhythms, the slow integration of the told into the listener's own substance, are being abolished by the very media the artwork essay examines.

The connection to the essay is exact. Aura is the form Erfahrung takes in the presence of an object: duration, distance, the work's growth into the beholder's world across time. The shock reception of film, its distracted testing, is Erlebnis organised into an aesthetic. Benjamin's position between the two is the same fork drawn elsewhere in his work. He refuses the conservative lament that would restore storytelling by decreeing reverence for it, and he refuses the progressive celebration that would call every shock an education. What he does, in both essays, is inventory the loss and the capacity in the same ledger, and the honesty of the double entry is why the storyteller essay has outlasted more confident cultural criticism of its decade. The Baudelaire essay of 1939 extends the account to lyric poetry itself, reading the crowd, the shock and the spleen of the poems as the register where a great talent processed the devaluation of experience into form. The three exile essays belong together, and readers who meet the artwork essay alone meet one blade of a pair of shears.

## The story against the novel: counsel, death and the borrowed life

The storyteller essay repays a second look at its architecture, since its argument about the novel is the part Benjamin's contemporaries resisted and the part posterity has quietly adopted. The rise of the novel, he argued, is the earliest symptom of the decline of storytelling, because the novel's birthplace is the solitary individual, neither receiving counsel nor able to give it, and its dissemination depends on the printed book, the very apparatus that detaches the tale from the web of living voices. The storyteller's authority, by contrast, is borrowed from death: it is at the moment of dying that a life first assumes a transmissible form, and the reader warms his shivering life at the death he reads about, receiving from the narrative the counsel that experience, ripened in a whole existence, alone can give. The information that displaces the story in the newspaper has opposite properties: it must be plausible, verifiable, exhausted in the moment of its novelty, and shot through with explanation, where the story forbore to explain and kept its strength, like the seed that remains germinative in the sealed chamber of the pyramid. Benjamin chose the Russian writer Nikolai Leskov as his storyteller's exemplar, a craftsman among whose tales the art survives because its world of handicraft, travel and oral transmission still stands, and he set against Leskov the modern figures of the novelist and the journalist as the storyteller's heirs and liquidators in one. The essay's elegy is exact about what dies: not narrative, which flourishes as never before, but the communicability of experience, the ability to exchange experiences in the coin of a life. Its bearing on the artwork essay is the bearing of a diagnosis on its sequel. The media that kill the story are the media whose political possibilities the essay then examines, and the continuity of the two texts, elegy and wager from the same hand in the same years, is the strongest internal evidence against the reading of Benjamin as either a mourner or a celebrant by temperament.

## Montage in the novel: Döblin and the crisis of the book

Benjamin's criticism kept returning to the novel, the form his storyteller essay had declared the story's liquidator, and his 1930 review of Alfred Döblin's Berlin Alexanderplatz shows the judgement in its productive form. The crisis of the novel, as the review argues, is the crisis of the interiorised hero and the sealed book: Döblin's montage, which cuts tram timetables, weather reports, advertising slogans and biblical verses into the narrative of the ex convict Franz Biberkopf, breaks the novel's shell and lets the city's material flood the form, staging, in Benjamin's approving analysis, an epic theatre by other means, where the reader is not invited to empathise with a soul but to register a world. The review matters to the profile for the standard it applies. The montage novel succeeds, in his account, not because it is modern in decoration but because it transforms the apparatus of the book as Brecht's theatre transformed the stage: the author becomes a producer, the reader an examiner, the material itself an actor. Where the storyteller essay had drawn the fork for narrative, lament for the story, suspicion of the novel, the Döblin review shows the third path the fork concealed, the novel remade by the techniques of the age of reproducibility until it recovers, at a higher level of artifice, the story's old intimacy with the world's stuff. Critics who file Benjamin as the novel's elegist have not read the review, and teachers can use the pair of texts to show his method at its fairest: the same thinker who buries a form in one essay waits, in another, at the graveside with a spade to help it dig itself out.

## Shock: Freud, Simmel and the training of the sensorium

The Baudelaire essay of 1939, On Some Motifs in Baudelaire, contains the artwork essay's missing psychology, and its central concept is shock. Benjamin took from Freud's Beyond the Pleasure Principle the notion of consciousness as a protective shield: the psyche survives the bombardment of stimuli by registering them as isolated incidents, assigning each a precise point in time, at the cost of their integration into experience. The more efficiently the shield works, the more the events it parries remain mere Erlebnisse, lived through and unassimilated. Georg Simmel's sociology of the metropolis supplied the urban setting, the intensification of nervous life in the crowd and the traffic, and Baudelaire's poetry supplied the evidence of a sensibility fencing with shocks as its daily exercise: the jolt of the crowd, the collision of glances, the gambler's coup, the worker's reflex at the machine, all repetitions of a single structure in which the living being practises defence until defence becomes its mode of perception. Film, in the artwork essay, belongs to this analysis as the art form whose very structure, the perpetual, abrupt change of images, corresponds to the shock form of modern experience and trains the sensorium that must survive it. Read together, the two essays answer the question the summaries leave open, why distraction should count as a form of attention at all: because a consciousness armoured against shock can only receive collectively, inattentively, by touch and habit, and the politics of art begins from that armour, not from an imaginary unarmoured beholder who exists nowhere in the modern city.

## Proust, involuntary memory and the other rescue

Benjamin's engagement with Marcel Proust runs from the translation work of the middle nineteen twenties, undertaken with Franz Hessel, to the essay of 1929 on Proust's image, and it feeds the theory of experience at its most delicate point. Proust's distinction between voluntary memory, which reports the past in the flat uniform of information, and involuntary memory, which releases it unbidden at the prompting of a taste, a sound, a texture, gave Benjamin the model of a past that can be rescued only by chance and attention together, never by the archive's deliberate search. His essay reads the great novel as an act of Penelope work reversed: not a weaving by day and unweaving by night, but a remembering that interweaves the lived day into the warp of forgetting, so that the recovered time bears the mark of the oblivion from which it was torn. The madeleine's lesson generalises across his work. The dialectical image is involuntary memory socialised, the moment when a fragment of the collective past surfaces unbidden in a moment of danger and demands to be seized; the collector's passion is the same structure applied to things; and the historian of the theses, who seizes a memory as it flashes up, is Proust's narrator retooled for the class struggle. The engagement also corrects a possible misreading of the artwork essay's coolness toward the unique object. The man who translated Proust knew exactly what the aura of a remembered room was worth, and the theory that announced its decay was written by the person in his generation least likely to underestimate the loss.

## Karl Kraus and the ethics of quotation

The long essay of 1931 on Karl Kraus, the Viennese satirist whose journal Die Fackel had waged a one man war on the corruption of language in the press, is the place where Benjamin theorised his own most characteristic act, the quotation. Kraus's satire worked by quotation: he printed his enemies' sentences and let them convict themselves, holding that the phrase, exactly reproduced, was the judgement. Benjamin's essay, in substance, generalises the practice into a theory of language and justice. To quote a text, in this account, is to tear it from its context and thereby both to destroy and to save it: to destroy its false life as part of an edifying continuum, and to save its claim by setting it in the constellation of the present, where it speaks with a force its origin concealed. His own aphorism on the subject, from One-Way Street, compares the quotations in his work to wayside robbers who leap out, armed, and relieve the idle stroller of his conviction. The whole Arcades method is this ethics of quotation at archive scale, and the theses' ruling that even the dead will not be safe from the enemy if he wins is its political form. The Kraus essay also preserves the sharpest statement of his doctrine of the dwarf, since it is here, years before the chess machine of the theses, that the misshapen little figure first does duty as the hidden agent of a justice that cannot appear in its own person. Critics who find the montage method arbitrary are invited to test it against this essay, where its jurisprudence is argued outright.

## Kafka and the world of the offices

The longest of the exile essays before the Baudelaire project, the 1934 study of Franz Kafka, shows Benjamin's instruments turned on a writer whose world resists every doctrine brought to it, and it earns a section here because the essay's solutions are the profile's themes in another key. Kafka's novels and stories present a universe administered by authorities whose laws are never stated: courts in attics, castle offices behind village inns, officials whose messages arrive garbled through chains of intermediaries. Admirers had read the world theologically, as the negative image of a withdrawn grace, or psychoanalytically, as a family romance. Benjamin read it as the codex of a forgotten prehistory pressing into a modernity of files and functionaries: the swamp world of myth, with its hybrid creatures and its hopeless litigants, surviving inside the offices of a rationalised administration, so that the most antiquated guilt and the most modern bureaucracy turn out to be the same room seen at different hours. His emblem for the method is the figure of the hunchback, the little hunchback of the folk song who causes every mishap in the house and whom the essay names, in paraphrase of its governing fancy, as the inmate of distorted life, who will disappear when the messiah comes and straightens the world, not by force, but by a gentle adjustment.

Two of the essay's judgements have proved durable. The first is its treatment of Kafka's assistants, students and minor figures, the indefatigable helpers and errand boys who populate the novels in swarms: Benjamin read them as the bearers of Kafka's hope, creatures not yet fully formed by the world of the offices, in whom the possibility of a life outside the file still stirs. The second is its famous refusal of the biographical key. Against the industry that read the work out of the author's neurosis, Benjamin insisted that Kafka's failure, his inability to finish, to marry, to leave Prague's insurance institute, was the condition of a purity in the work itself, the failure of a man who concentrated all his forces on not succeeding in the terms his world proposed. The essay was written for a newspaper's literary supplement and published in shortened form; the full text circulated privately and drew Scholem's sharpest theological demurral, an exchange in which the whole triangle of friendships can be heard arguing about one dead writer from Prague. It stands in the profile as evidence of range: the critic of film and photography was equally, and by the same method, the critic of the modern novel's deepest strangeness.

## Marxism and theology in one hand

Every account of Benjamin must settle the question his friends argued over in his lifetime: how the Marxism and the theology fit. Scholem, who knew him longest, insisted to the end that the metaphysical and theological stratum was the foundation and the Marxism a later scaffolding the work could lose; Adorno and others pressed from the opposite side, treating the theology as a vocabulary to be cashed out in materialist terms. The texts support neither amputation. The first thesis on history opens with the fable of the chess automaton, the mechanical Turk that always wins because a hunchbacked dwarf, a master of the game, hides inside and guides its hand; historical materialism, Benjamin says, is the puppet that can win only if it enlists the services of theology, which in the fable's own terms must keep small, unlovely and out of sight. The sentence is precise about the arrangement. The puppet is the method, the dwarf is the claim that the past makes on the present, the messianic charge that no purely economic history could generate, and the two work as one apparatus or lose.

The combination is therefore structural, not decorative, and it explains positions elsewhere that puzzle readers who expect consistency of the party kind. The rejection of progress as a storm rather than a staircase comes from the theological side, from a tradition in which redemption interrupts history rather than crowning it; the analysis of the commodity, the phantasmagoria, the dream world of capital, comes from the Marxist side, and does the interrupting work on the century's actual materials. Weak messianic power is the hinge term, deliberately scaled down from doctrine to a claim: not that redemption is scheduled, but that the past is not finished, that the dead have a lien on the living, and that a materialism without this lien degenerates into the social democratic faith in progress the theses were written to destroy. To treat the theology as eccentricity is to misread the mechanism; to treat the Marxism as a costume is to misread the archive of quotations, prices, street plans and fashion plates over which the theology was set to work. The thinker is the combination, and the profile's remaining judgements assume it.

## The theological political fragment: the profane order and the Messiah

A short text of about 1921, unpublished in Benjamin's lifetime and known as the theological political fragment, states the settlement between his two vocabularies with a precision the theses later assume, and a full profile should put it on record. Only the Messiah himself, the fragment argues, consummates all history: the order of the profane, the world of politics and happiness, cannot be built on the idea of the divine kingdom, and theocracy has no political meaning except as a warning against confusing the two orders. Yet the profane order is not thereby abandoned to meaninglessness. The quest of free human beings for happiness runs counter to the messianic direction as a force runs counter to another that can nevertheless assist it: the profane, precisely by being profane and pursuing happiness without theology's warrant, promotes the coming of the messianic kingdom as a counterweight promotes the motion it opposes. The fragment's closing figure assigns world politics its method and its name in a single breath, calling for a politics whose method is nihilism: not destruction for its own sake, but the refusal to let any earthly order pose as the kingdom, the clearing of idols so that the world can be passed through in the direction of its own transience. Twenty years separate the fragment from the theses, and the fidelity is total. The dwarf in the chess machine of 1940 is the fragment's settlement wearing a fable's clothes, and the storm called progress is the profane order divinised by social democracy into exactly the idol the young Benjamin had forbidden. Readers who ask where the theology lives in the materialism should be sent to these two pages before any others.

## The critique of violence: law, myth and the strike

A profile for a Marxism series must register the one text of Benjamin's that belongs to political theory proper, the essay of 1921 on the critique of violence, written before the Marxist turn and argued over by political philosophers ever since. Its scheme distinguishes the violence that makes law from the violence that preserves it: the founding violence of conquest and constitution, which installs a legal order and demands recognition, and the administrative violence, police and penalty, that maintains the installed order day by day, with the police named the spectral mixture of both, founding law in every intervention while claiming only to preserve it. Against the entire sphere of legal violence, which Benjamin gathers under the name of the mythic, bloody and bound to fate, the essay sets a contrasting figure drawn from Jewish tradition and from Georges Sorel's reflections on the general strike: a pure, divine violence that expiates rather than punishes, destroys law rather than installing it, and appears in politics, if anywhere, in the proletarian general strike conceived not as blackmail for concessions but as the pure means of a class withdrawing its labour from the legal order altogether. The essay is deliberately vertiginous, and its refusal to supply a programme is the point: written in the year of failed revolutions, it asks what a violence outside the cycle of law could even mean, and it leaves the question open where the theses of 1940 will find it again, in the doctrine that the state of emergency is the rule and in the resolve to bring about the real one. Scholars of political theory cite the essay independently of the aesthetic work, and the two lines of reception, rarely on speaking terms, are one more instance of the afterlife's division of its author into specialists' portions.

## The strongest objection: evocative where it should be analytic

The objection to Benjamin should now be stated at the full strength its proponents give it, because this series corrects misreadings in both directions and the admiring misreading is also a misreading. The objection runs as follows. Benjamin's method is essayistic and associative rather than argumentative. His central concepts are defined evocatively, by image and antithesis, rather than analytically, by conditions that could be checked; aura means distance, tradition, uniqueness and authenticity in shifting proportions, and can be found or withheld by a sympathetic reader at will. His claims arrive as constellations and aphorisms whose arrangement does the work an inference should do, so that disagreement slides off them: to contest a montage one can only offer another montage. And his enormous influence in cultural studies, the objection concludes, reflects this quotability rather than explanatory power; he is the most excerptible of theorists, a supplier of licences, aura, flâneur, dialectical image, angel of history, for arguments he never made, and the discipline that canonised him has rewarded suggestiveness over rigour precisely because suggestiveness is what it could use.

The answer begins by separating the assessable core from the penumbra. The artwork essay contains a specific historical claim about the changing status of the artwork: that as the technical basis of art shifts toward intrinsic reproducibility, the work's social function migrates from cult to exhibition, the criteria of authenticity and uniqueness lose their organising force, and reception becomes collective and testing in form. That claim can be, and has been, examined against the histories of photography, cinema, recording and broadcasting; it predicts discriminations, for instance between media born reproducible and media retrofitted to reproduction, that evidence can confirm or disappoint. The theses likewise contain a checkable methodological claim, that the archive of any tradition is structured by its victors, with consequences a working historian can apply and test against cases. Where the objection lands, it should be conceded to land: the surrounding material, the messianic vocabulary, the redemptive posture toward the discarded detail, resists the kind of testing the objection demands, and a reader who wants Benjamin's concepts to behave as instruments in a social science will find them behaving, deliberately, as something else. The concession defines the use. He is a theorist of the singular configuration, and his concepts are instruments for reading configurations; deployed as covering laws they fail in the way the objection describes, and the failure is a misuse before it is a refutation.

### How does the aura argument survive being corrected?

It survives the way the essay itself survived correction: by narrowing to the claim that bears weight. Stripped of the glow its borrowers added, the argument is that uniqueness, distance and embeddedness in tradition formed one historical configuration of the artwork, that reproducible media dissolve that configuration, and that art's function consequently migrates toward exhibition and politics.

## The afterlife: rescue, edition and the manufacture of a classic

Benjamin died almost unpublished in the language of his posthumous fame, and the making of the classic is a history of rescue operations that his own theses describe in advance. The Institute's memorial volume of 1942 carried the theses to a handful of readers. In 1955 Adorno edited the two volume Schriften for Suhrkamp, and the third version of the artwork essay entered German reading from that edition. Scholem and Adorno together edited the letters in 1966, supplying the life in documentary form. Hannah Arendt, who had known Benjamin in Paris and carried the theses manuscript across the Atlantic, introduced the English Illuminations of 1968 with the great essay that fixed his image for the Anglophone world: the pearl diver, the collector of quotations, the man out of place in every movement to which he belonged. The Gesammelte Schriften, appearing through the nineteen seventies and eighties under the editorship of Rolf Tiedemann and others, at last printed the versions in their order and the Arcades convolutes entire, and the scholarly Benjamin, the one this profile has insisted on, became available to any reader willing to do the work.

The popular Benjamin grew alongside, and the two have never quite coincided. The student movements of the late nineteen sixties found in him a Marxist unincriminated by any party's record, a theorist of media before media studies existed, a martyr whose death could be laid at the door of the century. Cultural studies took the artwork essay as a founding charter, often in the celebration form this article has corrected; film studies took the distraction thesis and the cameraman's surgery; historians took the theses and the injunction to brush history against the grain; art history took the Arcades and made the nineteenth century legible in a new key. Each taking was partial, and the transmission test that this series applies to portraits of earlier thinkers applies here too: ask of any Benjamin in circulation which texts, in which versions, through which translation, the portrait's author could have read, and the portrait's omissions announce themselves. The most useful tribute paid him in the last decades may be the least glamorous: the slow editorial labour that replaced the portable Benjamin of the anthologies with the archival Benjamin of the collected works, and the field's gradual, unfinished adjustment to the replacement. That labour continues institutionally: since 2008 a critical complete edition, the Werke und Nachlaß, has been appearing volume by volume with Suhrkamp, printing the works with their drafts, variants and surviving papers in a form intended to supersede every earlier presentation, and its volumes on the early writings and the Arcades materials have already altered small certainties of the record, dating letters, separating drafts that the older edition had fused, and restoring passages the first editors had set aside. For the working scholar the consequence is practical. Citations formed on the Gesammelte Schriften remain serviceable for the major essays, but any argument that turns on the exact state of a text must now reckon with two critical editions rather than one, and the protocol this profile has urged, version, translator, edition, acquires a fourth term. Benjamin, who wrote that a work's afterlife is the medium in which its truth content ripens, is receiving an afterlife of editions to match the doctrine, each generation's editors mortifying the previous presentation so that the text can be rescued again.

## The papers divided: an archive between two cities

The survival of Benjamin's manuscripts is a history of narrow escapes and divided custody that the afterlife section of this profile summarised too quickly, and the record deserves a paragraph of its own. What he left in Paris was rescued in parts: the Arcades convolutes hidden in the national library, other papers with friends, a portion of the library dispersed or confiscated, the books he had stored in Denmark eventually recovered. What he carried was lost with him, the briefcase on the Portbou path passing into a legend the record cannot settle. What surfaced after the war gathered in two centres whose rivalry is itself a chapter of his reception: the papers Adorno assembled in Frankfurt, foundation of the Suhrkamp editions, and the manuscripts and letters in Gershom Scholem's keeping in Jerusalem, including the copies Scholem's devotion had preserved of texts whose originals vanished. The two friends who edited the letters jointly in 1966 were also the two executors of incompatible portraits, and scholars of the next generation learned to read the Benjamin archive as a divided estate, its parts catalogued in different cities under different dispensations, until the consolidated archive in Berlin, assembled after reunification in the Academy of Arts, gave the Nachlass a single address at last. The profile notes the division without adjudicating it, since the division is the fact: the thinker of the constellation left a constellation for an archive, and the editors who assembled his works performed, on his own leavings, the operations of rescue and mortification his theory describes.

## The angel itself: the picture's itinerary

The small Klee picture at the centre of the ninth thesis has an itinerary of its own, and the profile's method, which has followed objects throughout, should follow this one to its wall. Benjamin bought Angelus Novus in Munich in 1921, a work in ink, chalk and watercolour showing a figure with raised hands, staring eyes and wings arrested mid gesture, and he hung it in his Berlin rooms through every change of address, calling it, in a letter, the piece of his possessions he valued in a class of its own. When he fled Berlin in 1933 the picture went into the keeping of friends in Paris, and through the occupation it survived in hiding, as its owner's manuscripts survived in the national library, by the vigilance of the same circle. After the war it passed to Theodor Adorno, and from Adorno's estate to Gershom Scholem in Jerusalem, who lived with it for years before bequeathing it, at his death in 1982, to the Israel Museum, where it hangs in the modern collection. The itinerary invites the reading Benjamin taught: a fragile sheet of paper, an image of arrested flight, rescued repeatedly by the loyalty of friends, divided between the two cities of its owner's divided afterlife, and arriving at rest in the museum of the state its owner's oldest friend helped to build and its owner never chose to join. No allegory could be more exactly Benjaminian if it had been composed, and the fact that it was not composed, that it is simply the record, is the reason the profile ends its reception history here. The angel's wings, in the picture, are still open. The storm is still blowing. The museum label, as is proper, says nothing about any of this.

## Agesilaus Santander: the secret name and the angel reversed

In August 1933, on Ibiza, in the first summer of his exile, Benjamin wrote a short autobiographical text of extraordinary strangeness and gave it a title that is a signature in code. Agesilaus Santander, the name under which the text presents its subject, is an anagram, as Scholem later demonstrated, of Der Angelus Satanas, the angel Satan, and the text is a kind of anti memoir in which the author, renamed, surveys the rooms of his life as a series of thresholds guarded by a patience he describes as his cardinal virtue. The angel of the title is the anti type of the Angelus Novus of seven years later: where the Klee angel is driven backward by the storm of progress, the Santander angel is the power that waits, that lays its hand on the chosen person and forbids him every exit until the work is done, the daemonic companion of a life that understood itself as assigned. Scholars read the fragment alongside the two birthday texts Benjamin composed for himself in exile as the private mythology beneath the public theory, the place where the theology of the theses shows its biographical root: a man who experienced his own existence as a task imposed by an inscrutable appointment, and who built from that experience a philosophy of history in which the appointment is generalised to a generation. The profile records the fragment as the afterlife section's fitting close. The writer who taught his century to read constellations left one in his own name, and the decipherment of it, by the friend in Jerusalem who had argued with him about angels for twenty years, is the last of this life's scenes in which the method and the biography are indistinguishable.

## The historians' Benjamin: microhistory and the detail defended

One branch of the afterlife runs outside literary studies altogether, and a profile that claims authority should register it. Working historians took from Benjamin a licence and a method for the small scale: the conviction that a discarded detail, an outmoded fashion, a minor official's memorandum, can hold an epoch the way his monadology promised, legible entire in the fragment for a reader who knows how to constellate it. Carlo Ginzburg's evidential paradigm, the conjectural method he traced from art connoisseurship through Freud to the detective story, belongs to the intellectual neighbourhood Benjamin had already mapped with his scene of crime at Atget's empty streets; the history of everyday life practised in Germany found in the convolutes a charter for its attention to refuse and routine; and the material culture studies of the past decades are unthinkable without the collector's defence, mediated through the Arcades, of the object's testimony against the document's. The influence was rarely a borrowing of conclusions. It was the transmission of a permission: to treat the minor as evidence, the discarded as a document, and the arrangement of fragments as an argument, which is to say, the transmission of the method this profile has followed in assembling the life itself out of letters, stipends, reading rooms and a small picture of an angel.

## The photography writers' debt: Sontag, Barthes and the argument continued

The most visible continuation of Benjamin's work outside the academy runs through the writing on photography of the following generation, and the profile closes its reception account with it because the debts are specific enough to be checked. Susan Sontag's On Photography of 1977 is inconceivable without the Little History and the artwork essay behind it: the photograph as a memento mori, the camera as an instrument that miniaturises and appropriates the world, the image collection as a way of furnishing experience without undergoing it, are Benjamin's themes restated in an American idiom and aimed at a photographic culture he did not live to see. Roland Barthes's Camera Lucida of 1980, written in mourning and in dissent from the semiology its author had helped to found, answers Benjamin from the other side of the argument, relocating photography's power from the political economy of reproduction to the wound of the singular detail, the punctum that pricks a single beholder, and finding in the old portrait of his mother the returned gaze that the Baudelaire essay had made the sign of aura. Neither writer is a disciple; both are interlocutors working the same seam, and the fact that the century's two most read books on photography conduct their arguments on his ground, the one extending his sociology, the other contesting it with his own concept of the gaze, measures the ground's extent better than any citation count. The argument, as this profile has presented it from the start, remains open precisely because it was built to be continued rather than concluded.

## Which Benjamin to read first

The question the FAQ set also answers has a practical form worth one short section. Begin with the artwork essay, since it is the door through which nearly every reader enters, but begin with it properly: the second version, in the Harvard Selected Writings translation, read against the versions table in this profile, so that the concept arrives with its corrected name and its stated purpose. Follow it with the theses on the concept of history, which take less than an hour to read and a career to absorb, and which reorganise everything the essay seemed to say. Then the storyteller essay, for the theory of experience that underwrites both. The Trauerspiel book and the Arcades are the deep water, to be entered once the instruments are familiar, and the memoir of a Berlin childhood is the gentlest path for a reader who wants the sensibility before the system. What to avoid is the order of the anthologies, which begin with the excerpts that confirm the celebration reading and never reach the texts that correct it.

One supplement to the order deserves emphasis, because it costs nothing and changes everything downstream: read the letters alongside the essays, at least the exchanges with Adorno on the artwork essay and with Scholem on Kafka and on the theology. Benjamin's published texts are famously compressed, their arguments folded into constellations that a first reading can mistake for assertions, and the correspondence is where the folding is undone in real time, objection by objection, concession by concession. A reader who has watched the Hornberg letters do their work on the Arcades design, or Adorno's March letter meet its courteous and immovable reply, possesses the arguments in their tested form and can thereafter hear, inside the polished sentences of the essays, the voices they were written to answer. The memoir of the Berlin childhood, finally, is the one text that can be read at any point in the sequence without preparation and without loss, since its method is its own explanation; many teachers use it as the first assignment of a course and the artwork essay as the second, and the order has the warrant of the life itself, which began in those interiors and ended by theorising their abolition.

A note on study practice closes the profile's main body. Benjamin's concepts reward the same filing discipline he practised in the convolutes: a card for each concept, its definition in his words, its version and translation, its counter case. The companion study tool at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) supports exactly this card based method for the series, and readers building a Benjamin file will find the versions table transfers to it intact. Keep the file in the order of the questions it answers rather than of the books it came from, and the versions, the translations and the corrections will look after themselves.

## For students and exam candidates

Benjamin questions in examinations reward the corrections in this profile, because examiners build their marking schemes around the distinctions the summaries blur. Know first that he was never a member of the Frankfurt School, and be able to say what he was instead: a stipendiary contributor to its journal, funded and edited by the Institute while remaining outside its membership. Know second that the essay's subject is technological reproducibility, a capacity of the artwork, and that the mistranslated title is itself an examinable point about how a term of art shapes an argument. Know third the internal architecture of the essay: aura defined through distance and tradition, the polarity of cult value and exhibition value, film as the test case, distraction as a mode of appraisal, and the closing fork between aestheticised politics and politicised art. Anchor answers in versions and dates, quote sparingly and exactly, and when a question asks whether Benjamin celebrated mass culture, answer with the ending: an essay that closes on the aesthetic enjoyment of destruction is issuing a warning with a hope inside it, not a welcome. The examination treatment of the whole school, with model answers across its figures, is gathered in the Frankfurt School exam answers article in this series.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The artwork essay is the most teachable long text in the Marxist criticism of culture, and the versions problem makes it more teachable rather than less. Set the second version as the extract, supply the versions table from this profile as a handout, and open the seminar with the title: ask the class what mechanical reproduction would mean, let the determinist reading form fully, and then introduce the German term and watch the argument reassemble itself around a capacity rather than a machine. The Adorno letter of March 1936 makes the ideal second text, since it gives students a contemporary reader of genius disputing the essay's wager from inside its own milieu, and the ensuing discussion, whether the audience's distraction is training or capture, runs itself. Handle the death at Portbou in one factual paragraph, as this profile does, and resist the seminar's gravitational pull toward the legend; the hour is better spent on the theses' seventh ruling about documents of civilisation, applied to a set text the students already know, where it reliably produces the best writing of the term. Benjamin's own radio talks for children, models of gravity without condescension, repay reading aloud as a lesson in what exposition can be.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier on Benjamin is editorial and archival, and the first duty of anyone citing him is the protocol this profile has repeated: name the version, name the translator, and where the argument turns on a term, check the German. The Gesammelte Schriften and the Harvard Selected Writings between them make the protocol practicable for the artwork essay, and the convolutes of the Arcades are available in reliable editions in both languages for the motifs they treat. Open problems remain where a researcher can still contribute: the reconstruction of the essay's first version and its relation to the Arcades drafts, the exact circumstances and documents of the Portbou crossing, the contents of the lost briefcase, and the systematic comparison of the Baudelaire materials against the exposés of 1935 and 1939. Journalists borrowing the vocabulary should note its tolerances: aura is a term with a definition, not a synonym for atmosphere or prestige, and the angel of history is an argument about progress, not a decoration for any scene of ruin. The tradition to which these instruments belong, and against which their originality measures itself, is charted in the companion article on Marxist literary criticism linked earlier in this profile.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Walter Benjamin?

Walter Benjamin (1892 to 1940) was a German critic and essayist whose work joined Marxist cultural analysis, Jewish religious thought and a literary critic's attention to detail. Barred from an academic career after his Baroque thesis was withdrawn at Frankfurt, he earned a precarious living as a reviewer, translator and radio broadcaster, worked for thirteen years on an unfinished study of the Paris arcades, and spent his final years as a refugee in Paris. The essay on art and reproducibility and the theses on the concept of history, both products of that exile, made him after his death at the Spanish border one of the most influential critics of the century.

### Q: Was Benjamin a member of the Frankfurt School?

No. Benjamin never held a post at the Institute for Social Research, whose school the name denotes, and he belonged to none of its internal deliberations. What he had was a working arrangement: from 1934 the Institute paid him a monthly subvention in exile and published his essays and reviews in its journal, in return exercising editorial pressure he frequently resisted, most visibly over the Baudelaire manuscript of 1938. Adorno was his closest interlocutor and most devoted reader, which explains the persistent confusion, but the relation was that of an institution and a gifted, difficult external contributor. The distinction matters because no shared doctrine bound him, and his divergences from the school's line were never settled by membership discipline.

### Q: What is aura in Benjamin's artwork essay?

Aura names the configuration that gathers around a unique work embedded in a tradition: its unrepeatable presence in a particular place, its duration through history, and the distance it keeps from the beholder however near he stands, rooted in the work's original function in ritual and cult. A statue in a temple or a painting in an ancestral gallery possesses authority partly because it cannot be had anywhere, by anyone, at any time. Technological reproducibility dissolves the configuration by detaching the work from its setting and meeting the beholder in his own situation. Aura is therefore a historical category, not a synonym for atmosphere, charisma or artistic quality, and its decay is for Benjamin a social process before it is an aesthetic loss.

### Q: Is mechanical reproduction a mistranslation?

It is, and the error has shaped the essay's reception. The German compound names a property, the work's reproducibility under the technical conditions of the age, where the English phrase names a mechanical process performed on the work by copying machines. The first formulation makes the change internal: media such as photography and film are constituted by their multiples, so the very question of an original loses its hold. The second makes technology an external force acting on art, and readers accordingly took the essay for a deterministic story about machines. The Harvard Selected Writings restored the term as technological reproducibility, and the correction restores with it the essay's real claim, that art's mode of existence, and with it its function, is what changed.

### Q: What is the angel of history?

The angel of history is Benjamin's reading of Paul Klee's small picture Angelus Novus, which he owned from 1921, placed at the centre of the ninth of his theses on the concept of history. The angel faces the past and sees not a chain of events but a single catastrophe piling wreckage at his feet; he would stay to awaken the dead and repair the damage, but a storm from paradise has caught his wings and drives him backward into the future while the debris mounts. The storm is what is called progress. The figure compresses the theses' whole charge against the idea of history as improvement: from the standpoint of the victims, the continuum is a disaster, and progress is the wind that prevents rescue.

### Q: How did Benjamin combine Marxism and theology?

He built the combination into the method rather than decorating one side with the other. The opening thesis on history pictures historical materialism as a chess automaton that wins only because a hidden dwarf, theology, guides its hand: the materialist analysis supplies the machinery, the theological stratum supplies the claim of the dead upon the living and the possibility that history can be interrupted rather than merely continued. Hence his scaled down formula of a weak messianic power belonging to every generation, and his rejection of progress as a storm rather than a staircase. Neither element is a metaphor for the other. Remove the theology and the critique of progress loses its ground; remove the materialism and the theology loses its object, the commodity world of the nineteenth century archive.

### Q: What is the Arcades Project?

The Arcades Project, or Passagenwerk, is the unfinished study of nineteenth century Paris on which Benjamin worked from 1927 until his death. Its subject was the glass roofed shopping arcades and everything they concentrated: the commodity on display, fashion, the flâneur, the collector, Haussmann's rebuilt boulevards, the world exhibitions and Baudelaire, its lyric witness. Benjamin assembled the work as convolutes, dossiers of copied quotations and commentary meant to be composed by montage, letting the century's own materials form constellations rather than receiving a narrative. Two exposés, from 1935 and 1939, sketch the plan; the dossiers survived the war hidden in the Bibliothèque Nationale and were published in German in 1982 and in English in 1999, becoming one of the most influential unfinished books in the humanities.

### Q: Why did Adorno criticise Benjamin's artwork essay?

Adorno's long letter of March 1936 praised the essay's power and then disputed its wager. He argued that Benjamin had liquidated aura too cheerfully, underrating the truth content carried by autonomous art's very distance and difficulty, and that he had trusted the cinema audience too readily: the laughter Adorno observed in cinemas struck him as conformist and cruel rather than as a revolutionary expertise in formation. He objected too that the essay transferred categories from production to reception without the dialectical mediation the subject required, reflecting, in his view, Brecht's influence at its most simplifying. His summary figure conceded partiality on both sides, calling the two positions torn halves of an integral freedom that do not add up. The friendship survived; the disagreement was never resolved.

### Q: How did Walter Benjamin die?

Benjamin died at Portbou, on the Spanish side of the Pyrenean border, in the night of 25 to 26 September 1940. He had fled Paris in June, obtained through the Institute's efforts an emergency visa for the United States with Spanish and Portuguese transit visas, and crossed the mountains on foot with a guided party of refugees. At Portbou the Spanish police announced that a new instruction closed the border to the stateless and that the group would be returned to France the next day. Stateless, ill and convinced that return meant delivery to the Gestapo, he took morphine he had carried against such a moment. The death is generally recorded as suicide by overdose; some details of the border decision remain debated, and the rest of the party was allowed to proceed the following day.

### Q: Which Benjamin essay should you read first?

Read the essay on the work of art first, since it is the most complete short statement of his method and the door to everything else, but choose the state of the text deliberately: the second version, in the Harvard Selected Writings translation, where the title carries the corrected term and the political programme stands in the open. Follow it with the theses on the concept of history, eighteen short texts that reframe the essay's hopes inside a philosophy of rescue, and then with the storyteller essay for the theory of experience that supports both. The Baroque book and the Arcades reward readers who arrive with those instruments in hand. Beginning with the famous excerpts in anthologies risks meeting the celebration reading first and the argument only later, if at all.

### Q: How does the reproducibility correction change the artwork essay's thesis?

It relocates the change the essay describes. Under the familiar title, the thesis reads as a claim about what machines do to art, and the reader is invited to cheer or mourn the copying process. Under the corrected term, the thesis concerns what kind of object the artwork has become: one whose existence is constituted by reproducibility, for which the original copy distinction and the authority of authenticity no longer organise value. That relocation explains the essay's sequence, the withering of aura, the migration from cult to exhibition value, and its political ending, since a change in the work's mode of existence, unlike a copying technique, is a fact about social relations that opposed forces can contest. The correction therefore converts the essay from a prophecy about technology into an analysis of a transformation still available to argument.

### Q: What should a reader check in the version of the essay they are citing?

Three things, in order. First, the version: three states of the essay exist, composed between 1935 and 1939, and the second is the one scholarship treats as standard, while the third is the one that circulated most widely; their emphases differ enough that an unattributed quotation may support a claim its author later redistributed. Second, the translator and edition: the 1968 English version in Illuminations and the Harvard Selected Writings render the key term differently, and the title on the contents page announces which argument the reader is holding. Third, the notes, which Benjamin expanded between versions and which carry qualifications the main text lacks. A citation that names version and translator protects the reader against building on a sentence the essay itself revised.

### Q: Why did the translations diverge so sharply?

Because the original would not sit still, and because translators answered to different moments. The German text existed in three versions before any complete English one appeared, and the first English translator worked from the third version as printed in the postwar German collected writings, choosing an idiomatic, concrete phrase for the abstract German compound at the title's centre. Decades later the Harvard translators worked with the critical edition's apparatus, which had established the second version as authoritative, and chose fidelity to the term of art over smoothness, rendering the compound literally throughout. Institutional settings diverged too: a literary volume introducing an unknown author to English readers in 1968 had different pressures from a scholarly edition stabilising a classic. The divergence is thus a compound of a moving source, a contested key term and two translation philosophies, which is why the versions table treats translations as states of the work.

### Q: How does the aura argument survive its corrections?

By narrowing to the claim that can bear the weight. The editorial corrections of the last decades, the second version restored, the key term translated exactly, strip away the reading in which a prophet of pop celebrated the copies; what remains standing is a historical mechanism. A configuration of uniqueness, distance and ritual embeddedness once organised the artwork's authority; media constituted by reproducibility dissolve that configuration; the work's function consequently migrates toward exhibition and toward politics. Each element of that sequence can be examined against the record of photography, film and broadcasting, and the argument survives the examination better than the mood it is usually quoted for. Critics who press on its genuine weak points, the shifting proportions of the definition itself, now engage a stated mechanism rather than an atmosphere, which is what survival means for an argument.

### Q: What separates Benjamin's hopes for film from Adorno's fears?

A judgement about what mass reception trains. Benjamin watched the cinema audience test the film the way a user handles a building, absent mindedly and expertly at once, and he saw in that distracted appraisal a new collective capacity, a perceptual training that a politicised art could organise against the spectacle of fascism. Adorno, listening to the same audience, heard its laughter as administered conformity and saw distraction itself as the industry's product, packaged in advance by the same firms that owned the studios. The separation is not between hope and despair as temperaments. It is between two readings of a single phenomenon, the distracted crowd, which Benjamin took as evidence that reception had changed its nature and Adorno took as evidence that capital had changed reception's nature for it. Their correspondence of 1936 remains the sharpest record of the divide.
