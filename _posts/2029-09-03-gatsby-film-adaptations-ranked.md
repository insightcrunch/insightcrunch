Four times in a century, a studio has decided that Fitzgerald's novel would make a film, and four times the result has divided its audience. Every Great Gatsby film adaptation ranked here confronts the same obstacle, and the obstacle is not the parties or the period or the tragedy. It is that the novel's power lives in a retrospective narrating voice, and cameras do not have one. This article ranks the versions, states the criteria openly, and argues that the ranking is a map of how four eras handled a single unsolved problem.

![Every Great Gatsby Film Adaptation Ranked](/assets/images/blog/blog-102.webp)

The framework this article uses is what we call the narration test. Every other criterion, period detail, casting, visual invention, box office, sits downstream of one question: what does the film do about the fact that the book is Nick telling you something afterward, from a position of moral confusion, with hindsight he did not have at the time? Rank the adaptations by how directly they confront that question and the order becomes clear, and more interestingly, so does the reason each one fails in its own way.

## The Facts of the Four Adaptations

Before any judgment, the record. Getting these details right matters, because a good deal of what circulates about these films is wrong.

The first adaptation appeared in 1926, barely a year after publication. Herbert Brenon directed it for Famous Players-Lasky, and Paramount released it on November 21 of that year. Warner Baxter played Gatsby, Lois Wilson played Daisy, and Neil Hamilton played Nick, with William Powell and Georgia Hale in support. It ran eighty minutes. It drew not only on Fitzgerald's novel but on Owen Davis's Broadway play, which George Cukor had directed earlier the same year. The film is lost. Every print appears to have gone, and what survives is a trailer of about a minute, preserved by the Library of Congress. Fitzgerald and his wife reportedly saw it and walked out.

The second came in 1949, directed by Elliott Nugent for Paramount and released on July 13. Alan Ladd played Gatsby, with Betty Field as Daisy, Macdonald Carey as Nick, Barry Sullivan as Tom, Shelley Winters as Myrtle, and Howard Da Silva as Wilson. Richard Maibaum produced and co-wrote with Cyril Hume. It ran ninety-one minutes. The Production Code shaped it heavily, requiring a moralizing frame that the novel does not contain.

The third arrived in 1974, directed by Jack Clayton, produced by David Merrick, and written by Francis Ford Coppola. Robert Redford played Gatsby, Mia Farrow played Daisy, Sam Waterston played Nick, Bruce Dern played Tom, Karen Black played Myrtle, and Lois Chiles played Jordan. Howard Da Silva returned, this time as Wilson again, having appeared in the 1949 version. Paramount released it on March 29, 1974. It ran one hundred forty-six minutes on a budget of about seven million dollars and took roughly twenty-six and a half million. It won two Academy Awards, for costume design and for adapted score, and three British awards including cinematography. Coppola later said the finished film did not follow the screenplay he wrote.

The fourth is Baz Luhrmann's, released in 2013, co-written by Luhrmann and Craig Pearce and distributed by Warner Bros. Leonardo DiCaprio played Gatsby, Tobey Maguire played Nick, Carey Mulligan played Daisy, Joel Edgerton played Tom, Elizabeth Debicki played Jordan, and Isla Fisher played Myrtle. It was shot in Australia in three dimensions, ran one hundred forty-two minutes, and grossed over three hundred fifty million dollars worldwide, making it Luhrmann's most commercially successful film. It won two Academy Awards, both for Catherine Martin, in production design and costume design. Jay-Z served as executive producer on the score.

Television versions exist as well, including episodes in the nineteen fifties and a made-for-television film in 2000 with Toby Stephens, Mira Sorvino, and Paul Rudd. This article treats the four cinema releases as the principal adaptations, which is standard, while noting that the television versions occasionally solve problems the films do not.

### What are the principal Great Gatsby film adaptations?

There are four cinema versions: the lost 1926 silent directed by Herbert Brenon, the 1949 Paramount film directed by Elliott Nugent with Alan Ladd, the 1974 film directed by Jack Clayton with Robert Redford, and Baz Luhrmann's 2013 production with Leonardo DiCaprio.

## Fourth: The 1926 Silent

Ranking a film nobody has seen in decades requires honesty about what can and cannot be claimed.

What can be claimed: it existed, it ran about eighty minutes, and it took its shape as much from Owen Davis's stage version as from the novel. That second fact matters more than it sounds. A stage adaptation has already made the crucial decisions about Nick, because a play cannot easily reproduce retrospective narration either, and the usual theatrical solution is to reduce the narrator to a character who observes. A film built on that foundation inherits the reduction.

There is one documented alteration worth recording. Meyer Wolfsheim, whose depiction in the novel draws on antisemitic caricature that modern readers rightly find troubling, was renamed and reassigned to a different background in the 1926 version. Whatever the motive, the change tells us the film was willing to rework the source substantially.

Contemporary reaction appears to have been unenthusiastic, and the account most often repeated is that the Fitzgeralds disliked it enough to leave. That anecdote is well attested but it is still an anecdote, and it should be reported as such rather than treated as criticism.

What survives is the trailer, roughly a minute, held by the Library of Congress and included on home video releases of the 2013 film. It shows party footage and a small amount of performance. It is not enough to judge a film by, and this article declines to pretend otherwise.

The reason the loss matters is that 1926 is the only version made while the Jazz Age was still happening. Every subsequent adaptation is a period piece reconstructing a decade from outside it. The 1926 film was a contemporary story, shot by people wearing the clothes, and whatever its quality, that vantage point is unrecoverable. Its disappearance is the largest single gap in the novel's screen history.

There is one further reason to keep it in the count. A film made in 1926 could not know that Gatsby would become a set text, an archetype, and a shorthand for an entire decade. Every version since has been made under the weight of that reputation, and the weight shapes the choices: the reverence of 1974, the defiance of 2013. The 1926 production had no reputation to honor or resist, which makes it the only adaptation that treated the story as a story.

### Why is the 1926 silent version lost?

Nitrate film stock decayed and studios routinely destroyed prints once theatrical runs ended, since silent films were assumed to have no further commercial value. Roughly three quarters of American silent features are gone. Only a one-minute trailer of the 1926 Gatsby survives.

## Third: The 1949 Version

The 1949 film is the most neglected of the four and the most instructive about what censorship does to an adaptation.

Alan Ladd was a plausible piece of casting, and in some respects a better one than the versions that followed. Ladd's screen persona carried a genuine sense of criminality and self-invention, both of which Gatsby requires and both of which later stars, cast for glamour, supplied less readily. When Ladd's Gatsby claims a background, the audience half doubts him, which is the correct response.

The film's problem is structural and external. The Production Code required that criminal conduct be shown to be punished and that the moral position of the film be unambiguous. Fitzgerald's novel is built on moral ambiguity: Nick admires and disapproves simultaneously, and the book never resolves the tension. A film obliged to resolve it must supply an ending the novel refuses, and the 1949 version does exactly that, framing the story so the audience knows what to think.

The result is a competent picture that has removed the thing that makes its source valuable. Everything about the plot survives; the argument does not.

There are compensations. The visual style borrows from the crime pictures Paramount was making at the time, and the harder, shadowed look suits the material better than the soft period gloss of 1974. The valley of ashes sequences carry more genuine grimness than any later version manages. And the film treats Gatsby's criminal associations as real rather than decorative, which is an accuracy most adaptations lose.

The narration test is failed in an interesting way. The film does not avoid the problem of Nick; it replaces him. Instead of a compromised narrator whose judgment we must weigh, it supplies an authoritative moral frame that tells the audience the verdict. That is the opposite of the novel's method, and it is why the film ranks third despite doing several things better than the version above it.

## Second: The 1974 Version

Clayton's film is the most faithful adaptation ever made of the novel and one of the least satisfying, and the relationship between those two facts is the most useful thing about it.

Coppola's screenplay follows the book closely. Scenes appear in order, dialogue is frequently lifted intact, and the plot is reproduced with a completeness no other version attempts. The production spent seriously on period accuracy, and the results are genuinely beautiful: Douglas Slocombe's cinematography won a British award, John Box's art direction won another, and Theoni V. Aldredge's costumes took an Academy Award, with Ralph Lauren contributing to the men's wardrobe. Nelson Riddle's adapted score won the film's second Oscar and remains the best musical treatment any Gatsby film has produced, because Riddle understood the period from having lived near it.

And the film is cold. This was the consensus at the time and it has not shifted. Roger Ebert gave it two and a half stars and observed that reading the novel would take about as long and would be the better use of the time, which is the sort of verdict a film does not recover from. Variety's notice was split, praising the physical beauty while noting the shallowness of the people on screen.

The coldness has a specific cause, and it is the narration test. Nick's voice is what supplies the novel's warmth, its irony, and its moral pressure. Waterston plays Nick well, but the film gives him almost nothing to do beyond witnessing, because the retrospective commentary that constitutes his role in the book has no natural place in a scene. Strip out Nick's afterward-voice and what remains is a story about beautiful people behaving badly in beautiful rooms, observed without comment. That is precisely what the 1974 film is.

Redford's casting compounds the problem. Redford was among the most glamorous stars in the world, and Gatsby is a man performing glamour he does not naturally possess. When Redford performs it, the performance is invisible, because he already has the thing being performed. Ladd, less handsome and more obviously constructed, was closer to the character. Farrow's Daisy has been criticized as mannered, though the reading is defensible: her Daisy is a woman playing at fragility, which is arguably what the text supports.

### Which adaptation is most faithful to the plot?

The 1974 version, by a clear margin. Coppola's screenplay follows the novel's events in order and reproduces much of its dialogue directly. That fidelity did not produce warmth, since what the film could not reproduce was Nick's retrospective voice, which supplies the book's irony and moral pressure.

The film's lasting influence is aesthetic rather than interpretive. Its costumes and production design shaped how two generations pictured the Jazz Age, and its visual vocabulary is still what people mean when they say something looks like Gatsby. That is a real achievement and it is not the same as a successful adaptation.

## First: The 2013 Version

Luhrmann's film is the most divisive of the four and it is ranked first here for a specific reason, which the article will defend rather than assert.

Start with what it does. The film is loud, saturated, digitally extended, and shot in three dimensions. The party sequences use contemporary music, with a score executive-produced by Jay-Z that mixes hip hop, jazz, and contemporary pop rather than reproducing period recordings. Catherine Martin's production and costume design won both of the film's Academy Awards. It took more than three hundred fifty million dollars worldwide against a budget variously reported between one hundred five and one hundred ninety million.

Critical response split hard. Reviewers praised and attacked the same elements, sometimes in the same notice: the visual style, the music, the performances, the direction. Audience response ran more favorably than the reviews, and Fitzgerald's granddaughter spoke well of it publicly.

Now the argument for first place. Alone among the four, this film treats the narration problem as a problem and invents a solution. Luhrmann and Pearce place Nick in a sanatorium, recovering, encouraged by a doctor to write the story down. The invented frame does something no other adaptation attempts: it makes the film explicitly retrospective, gives Nick a reason to be composing rather than merely remembering, and licenses the device of putting Fitzgerald's sentences on screen as written text.

That last move is the one critics mocked most and the one that most deserves defense. When prose appears on screen in this film, the audience is reading Fitzgerald rather than watching an actor approximate him. It is inelegant. It is also the only time in a century of adaptation that a film has acknowledged that its source is a piece of writing whose value is in the writing.

The framing device carries a cost, and it should be stated. Making Nick an alcoholic patient converts a narrator whose unreliability is subtle into one whose unreliability is clinical, which flattens the ambiguity. In the novel we doubt Nick because his stated principles and his conduct diverge. In the film we doubt him because he is unwell. That is a weaker and less interesting form of doubt.

The anachronistic music is best understood the same way, as an interpretive claim rather than a marketing decision. Luhrmann has explained the logic directly: the jazz in Fitzgerald's parties was not heritage music, it was the disreputable, current, slightly dangerous sound of the moment, and reproducing period jazz for a modern audience produces quaintness rather than danger. Substituting a contemporary equivalent preserves the effect at the cost of the accuracy. Whether that trade is worth making is a legitimate disagreement, and this article's position is that it is defensible and that most objections to it are objections to the trade being visible rather than to the reasoning. The full case is developed in [the 2013 Baz Luhrmann Gatsby analyzed](/2029/09/10/gatsby-2013-luhrmann-film/).

What the film loses is interiority. The novel's quietest passages, Gatsby alone on his lawn, Nick's retrospective judgments, the final pages, all resist the maximalist treatment, and the film handles them less well than it handles the parties. A viewer who values those passages most will rank this film last rather than first, and that ranking is coherent.

## The Casting History and What It Reveals

Set the four Gatsbys beside one another and a pattern appears that individual reviews miss.

Warner Baxter in 1926 was a capable leading man two years away from an Academy Award, cast at a moment when the character had no cultural weight at all. Nobody watching in 1926 knew Gatsby was going to become an American archetype, which means the casting was made on ordinary commercial grounds. That innocence is unrecoverable.

Alan Ladd in 1949 is the most textually accurate casting of the four, and the film around him is the third best. Ladd's persona was built on characters with something to conceal, and his physical presence carried a hardness that made the bootlegging plausible. Gatsby is a criminal who has manufactured a gentleman, and Ladd looked like a man performing a role he had studied. That is precisely the effect the novel describes when Nick calls Gatsby's personality "an unbroken series of successful gestures."

Robert Redford in 1974 is the opposite case. Redford was among the most straightforwardly glamorous stars alive, and casting him inverts the character's central mechanism. The novel's Gatsby has assembled an appearance of effortless class out of catalogue references and rehearsed manners, and the seams show to anyone paying attention, which is why Tom can dismantle him in a hotel room. When the actor genuinely possesses what the character is faking, the fakery becomes invisible and the confrontation loses its meaning.

Leonardo DiCaprio in 2013 splits the difference and is, on balance, the most successful of the four. DiCaprio arrived with a public persona built on both glamour and evident effort, and his performance foregrounds the strain. The moments where his Gatsby's composure cracks are the film's strongest, and they are strongest because the composure was visibly constructed in the first place.

The Daisys tell a related story. Lois Wilson, Betty Field, Mia Farrow, and Carey Mulligan represent four different solutions to a character the novel deliberately underwrites. Fitzgerald gives Daisy little interiority, since we see her almost entirely through Gatsby's projection and Nick's discomfort. An actress must therefore either supply an interior the text withholds, which contradicts the design, or play the absence, which risks appearing thin. Farrow and Mulligan both chose versions of the second option and both were criticized for it, which suggests the criticism is aimed at the novel rather than at the performances.

Elizabeth Debicki's Jordan in the 2013 version is worth singling out as the most successful supporting performance in any of the adaptations. Jordan is a minor character on the page whose function is to model a kind of amused, dishonest self-possession, and Debicki plays exactly that function without inflating it.

### Which Gatsby casting is closest to the character?

Alan Ladd's, in the 1949 version, because his screen persona carried a genuine air of criminal self-invention. The novel's Gatsby is a manufactured gentleman whose seams show under pressure, and casting an actor who already possesses effortless glamour, as the 1974 production did, erases the mechanism.

## The Party Sequences Compared

Every adaptation devotes disproportionate resources to Gatsby's parties, and this is the one area where film has an advantage over the page.

The novel's party writing is already cinematic in the useful sense: crowded, kinetic, driven by movement and overheard fragments rather than by reflection. Fitzgerald suspends normal narrative attribution in these passages, so voices arrive without speakers and actions occur without agents, producing a deliberate blur. Film reproduces blur easily. It is, in fact, one of the few things film does better than prose.

The 1974 version stages its parties as lavish period reconstruction, and they are beautiful and slightly airless. The extras behave. The music is authentic. Nothing feels dangerous, which is a problem, because the novel's parties are attended by people Nick finds faintly appalling and describes with an amusement that curdles.

The 2013 version stages them as controlled chaos, with digital extension, rapid cutting, and contemporary music, and the sequences are the film's most admired and most attacked element. The defense is that the excess is accurate to the effect rather than to the appearance. A viewer in 2013 watching a period-accurate party would see a quaint historical curiosity. Fitzgerald's original readers saw something current, loud, and slightly disreputable. Reproducing the effect requires abandoning the appearance.

The 1949 version, working with a smaller budget and a stricter code, keeps its parties modest and compensates with atmosphere. The result is arguably the most morally uncomfortable of the three viewable versions, because the restraint leaves room for the sourness the novel attaches to these gatherings.

What all four demonstrate is that the parties are the most transferable material in the book, which is exactly why they dominate the popular image of the novel. Readers who came to Gatsby through the films tend to remember it as a book about parties. It is not. The parties occupy a small fraction of the text and stop entirely after chapter six. The distortion is a direct consequence of what film does well. The symbolic weight the parties carry on the page is examined separately in our study of the novel's spectacle.

## The Endings Compared

If the parties show what film gains, the endings show what it loses.

The novel ends with roughly three pages of Nick's retrospective meditation, delivered from a position of moral exhaustion, moving from a specific evening on Gatsby's lawn to a general claim about human striving. There is no action. There is no dialogue. There is a man on a beach thinking, and the thinking is the point.

The 1949 version resolves the ending morally, supplying the judgment the Production Code required, which is the clearest possible demonstration of what happens when an external authority is imposed on a text built from ambiguity.

The 1974 version reproduces the closing material as voice-over and lets the images carry the rest. It is respectful and it is inert. Waterston delivers Fitzgerald's lines competently over shots of an empty house, and the sequence feels like a recital rather than a conclusion, because voice-over over images is a report of thought rather than thought itself.

The 2013 version puts the final sentence on screen as text, which drew derision at the time. The move is clumsy. It is also the only honest response available. Faced with a passage whose value lies entirely in its wording, the film shows the wording. Every alternative is a paraphrase, and this particular passage does not survive paraphrase.

The pattern is exact. Where the source is scene, film matches or exceeds it. Where the source is voice, film either omits, recites, or displays the text, and none of the three is equivalent. That is the whole adaptation problem stated in a single comparison.

## Common Misconceptions About the Adaptation Record

Several claims circulate widely and are wrong, and a student writing about adaptation should avoid repeating them.

The first is that there are three Gatsby films. There are four cinema versions, and forgetting the 1926 silent removes the only one made while the world it depicts still existed. It is understandable that a lost film drops out of popular accounts, and it should not drop out of a serious one.

The second is that the 1974 version was a critical disaster. It was not. It received mixed notices, won two Academy Awards and three British awards, took roughly four times its budget, and has retained a substantial afterlife through its influence on fashion and design. Ebert's review was cool rather than hostile. Reducing the record to failure is inaccurate.

The third is that Luhrmann invented the idea of scoring Gatsby with contemporary music. He did not, though he pursued it further than anyone else. Adaptations have always negotiated the gap between period sound and current audiences, and Nelson Riddle's 1974 score is itself an arrangement rather than a straight reproduction of nineteen twenties recordings.

The fourth is that the 2013 film was a commercial disappointment. It was the reverse: the highest-grossing film of its director's career, with worldwide receipts over three hundred fifty million dollars, and it won both of the Academy Awards for which it was nominated.

The fifth is that Coppola's screenplay is what appears on screen in 1974. Coppola has stated publicly that the finished film departed substantially from his script, which complicates the common practice of attributing the film's fidelity or its coldness to him.

### What is the most common error about the Gatsby films?

Counting three adaptations instead of four. The lost 1926 silent regularly disappears from popular accounts, which removes the only version made while the Jazz Age was still in progress. Every subsequent adaptation is a period reconstruction assembled from outside the decade it depicts.

## The Reception History as Its Own Record

The critical response to these films across a century is a document in itself, and reading it chronologically shows something the individual reviews do not.

The 1926 reception is thinly recorded. The film arrived as a routine adaptation of a recent stage success, which is how the trade treated it, and Fitzgerald's novel had not yet acquired the reputation that would make its adaptations newsworthy. The most repeated piece of contemporary response is the Fitzgeralds walking out, which is anecdote rather than criticism. The absence of a serious review record is itself informative: nobody in 1926 thought a Gatsby film was an event.

By 1949 the situation had changed but not enough. Fitzgerald had died in 1940, the posthumous publication of his unfinished novel had begun a reappraisal, and Gatsby was on its way to becoming a set text. The 1949 film was nonetheless received as a Paramount programmer rather than as a literary occasion, and it slipped out of circulation quickly.

The 1974 release was the first time a Gatsby adaptation arrived as a cultural event. The marketing was enormous, the tie-in merchandising extensive, and the expectation correspondingly high. The reviews that followed were mixed in a particular way worth noticing: almost every notice praised the production and doubted the result. Variety admired the physical beauty and noted the shallowness of the people on screen. Ebert gave it two and a half stars out of four and suggested reading the novel instead. The consensus was not that the film was incompetent but that it was hollow, which is a harder charge to answer.

The 2013 reception split rather than settled. The same elements drew praise and attack, frequently from adjacent reviewers and occasionally within a single notice. The visual style was called either exhilarating or exhausting. The music was called either a legitimate translation or a marketing exercise. What the reviews agreed on was that the film had made choices, which is not what critics had said about 1974.

Two observations follow. The first is that critical response to Gatsby adaptations has consistently tracked the same anxiety: whether the film has captured something the reviewer regards as essential and un-filmable. That anxiety appears in 1974 as a complaint about coldness and in 2013 as a complaint about excess, and both complaints are the same complaint wearing different clothes.

The second is that audience response and critical response diverged sharply in 2013 and did so less in 1974. The later film was received warmly by viewers and coolly by many reviewers, which suggests that the objections were partly professional rather than universal. A student writing about reception should note the gap rather than reporting the critical verdict as the verdict.

### Why did critics respond so differently to the 1974 and 2013 films?

Both were charged with missing something essential, but from opposite directions. The 1974 version was called beautiful and emotionally hollow. The 2013 version was called overwhelming and superficial. The shared underlying complaint is that neither reproduced the reflective voice reviewers valued most.

## What the Adaptation Record Reveals About the Novel's Structure

Reading the four films as a set functions as a structural analysis of the source, because what survives adaptation and what does not is not random.

The first finding concerns proportion. All four films expand the parties and compress the final chapters. On the page, the party material occupies a modest share of the text and stops after chapter six, while the last three chapters carry the accident, the murder, the funeral, and the closing meditation. On screen the ratio inverts. The reason is that the novel's back half is dominated by aftermath and reflection, which are the hardest things to stage, and its front half is dominated by spectacle, which is the easiest.

That inversion has consequences beyond the films. The popular image of this novel as a book about glamorous parties is largely a product of adaptation, and it misrepresents a text that is substantially about a funeral nobody attends. Students arriving from the films frequently carry the distortion into their essays, writing about excess and spectacle when the book's argument is about aftermath.

The second finding concerns Tom Buchanan. Every adaptation makes Tom more straightforwardly villainous than the novel does, and the reason is structural rather than interpretive. On the page Tom is genuinely frightening because his cruelty is casual and socially sanctioned, and Nick's discomfort with him is complicated by the fact that they are the same class. Film needs an antagonist whose function is legible in a scene, so Tom acquires clarity he does not have in the text. The hotel confrontation is the test case: on the page it is a slow social humiliation, on screen it becomes a fight.

The third finding concerns Gatsby's criminality. It shrinks in every version except the 1949, where the studio's crime-picture idiom accommodated it naturally. In the novel the bootlegging and the bond fraud are real, specific, and central to why Tom can destroy him. Adaptations tend to soften this because a romantic lead who is also a fraud complicates audience sympathy. The softening costs the story its central irony, which is that Gatsby's dream was financed by exactly the corruption the dream was supposed to escape.

The fourth finding concerns Nick's sexuality and his relationship with Jordan, both of which the novel handles with ambiguity and every film resolves. The McKee sequence in chapter two is elliptical on the page and either cut or clarified on screen. Jordan is given a conventional romance arc or dropped. Film's need for legible relationships strips out the deliberate uncertainty that makes Nick an interesting narrator rather than a reliable one.

Taken together these four findings describe a single pressure. Adaptation rewards legibility, and this novel is built on withheld judgment: an ambiguous narrator, a protagonist whose crimes are real and whose dream is sincere, an antagonist whose menace is social rather than physical, and an ending that refuses to state its moral. Every film has clarified something the book declines to clarify, and the accumulated clarifications are what separates the versions from their source.

### What do the films consistently change about the novel?

They expand the parties, compress the reflective final chapters, make Tom more legibly villainous, soften Gatsby's criminality, and resolve Nick's ambiguities. Each change increases legibility, which is what adaptation rewards and what a novel built on withheld judgment cannot afford to give up.

## The Patterns Across a Century

Set the four side by side and three patterns emerge that no single film reveals.

The first pattern is that fidelity and success are unrelated. The most faithful version is the coldest. The least faithful is the most alive. This is not an argument for infidelity; it is evidence that what needs to be transferred is not the plot. The events of this novel are ordinary. A wealthy man loves a married woman, a car accident occurs, a murder follows. Reproduce those events accurately and you have reproduced nothing that matters.

The second pattern is that each era adapted the novel into its own dominant mode. The nineteen twenties made it a society melodrama drawn from a stage hit. The nineteen forties made it a crime picture with a moral. The nineteen seventies made it a prestige literary costume drama. The twenty tens made it a spectacle blockbuster. In each case the novel was translated into whatever the period considered serious commercial filmmaking, which tells us more about the periods than about the book. This is why the ranking functions as a map rather than a verdict, and why the comparison rewards attention. Our broader treatment of that comparison sits in [Gatsby compared to its film adaptations](/2028/11/27/gatsby-novel-vs-films/).

The third pattern is the persistence of the narration problem. Four attempts, four different evasions or partial solutions, and no version that has genuinely rendered what Nick's voice does. The 1926 version inherited a stage solution that reduced him to an observer. The 1949 version replaced him with an external moral authority. The 1974 version kept him and gave him nothing to say. The 2013 version invented a frame that lets him write, which is the closest anyone has come and which still converts subtle unreliability into clinical unreliability.

### Has any adaptation solved the narration problem?

None has solved it. The 2013 version comes closest by inventing a frame in which Nick is explicitly composing the account, which licenses on-screen prose and makes the retrospection visible. The cost is that his unreliability becomes clinical rather than moral, which flattens the ambiguity the novel maintains.

The reason the problem is stubborn is worth stating precisely, since it explains all four outcomes. Cinema shows things happening. The novel is not primarily about things happening; it is about a man who watched things happen deciding, years later, what they meant, and being unsure. Film has tools for showing events and poor tools for showing a mind revising its judgment of events. Voice-over approximates it and always feels like a workaround, because it is one. The obstacle is examined at length in [why The Great Gatsby resists adaptation](/2029/12/10/why-gatsby-resists-adaptation/).

## Every Great Gatsby Film Adaptation Ranked: The Criteria Stated Openly

Rankings are only useful when the criteria are visible, so here are the four this article used and the weight each carried. Naming them also lets a reader reconstruct the order under different assumptions, which is the point of publishing a framework rather than a verdict.

Fidelity to plot was weighted lightly, for the reasons above. It measures whether the events survive, and the events are the least valuable part of the source. It is included because readers expect it and because it distinguishes the versions usefully, not because it predicts quality.

Performance was weighted moderately. Casting choices reveal how a production understood the characters, and the pattern across the four is informative: Gatsby has been cast for menace once and for glamour three times, and the menace version was closer to the text even though the film around it was weaker.

Visual achievement was weighted moderately. Two of the four films won Academy Awards in design categories, and design is where adaptations of this novel consistently succeed, because the novel supplies extraordinary material for it. This is also why design success can conceal interpretive failure, as the 1974 version demonstrates.

The narration test was weighted heavily and decided the order. It asks whether the film has recognized that its source is a retrospective first-person account whose narrator is compromised, and what it has done about that recognition. Recognizing the problem and failing scores above not recognizing it.

A fifth criterion, cultural influence, was considered and excluded. It would place the 1974 version first on the strength of its effect on fashion and period design, and it measures what an adaptation did to the world rather than what it did with its source, which is a different question from the one this article asks.

A reader who weights these differently will produce a different order, and that is the correct response to this article rather than an objection to it. Weight fidelity heavily and 1974 wins. Weight period authenticity heavily and 1974 wins again. Weight visual invention heavily and 2013 wins. Weight the treatment of Gatsby's criminality heavily and 1949 rises sharply. The framework is transparent so that it can be argued with.

### What criteria should rank a film adaptation?

Use fidelity to plot, performance, visual achievement, and reception as descriptive measures, then add a source-specific test. For this novel the decisive question is what the film does about Nick's retrospective narration, since that voice is where the book's meaning lives.

## Is One Film Definitively the Best?

No, and the article's own ranking should be read with that in mind.

The case against a definitive verdict is straightforward: the four films succeed at different things, and no criterion for combining those successes is neutral. The 1974 film is the best period reconstruction and the best score. The 2013 film is the most interpretively ambitious and the most watched. The 1949 film understands Gatsby's criminality best. The 1926 film had access to a moment nobody can revisit. These are incommensurable achievements.

There is a stronger version of the objection. Ranking implies the films were attempting the same thing, and they were not. Nugent was making a Paramount picture under Production Code constraints in 1949. Luhrmann was making a three-dimensional summer release in 2013. Judging them on a shared scale flattens the industrial and cultural conditions that shaped what was possible, which is a real distortion.

The answer is not to abandon ranking but to state what it is for. The order in this article is a device for making an argument about the narration problem visible. Reading the four films as four attempts at one obstacle produces insight that a neutral survey would not. The ranking is the argument's shape, not its conclusion.

Which is why the honest recommendation for a reader is a sequence rather than a winner. Watch the 1974 version to see the novel's world reconstructed with care. Watch the 2013 version to see what happens when a filmmaker treats the book as an argument to be answered rather than a story to be reproduced. Read about the 1949 version and the 1926 version to understand what censorship removes and what film history loses. The individual analysis of the Clayton film is in [the 1974 Great Gatsby film analyzed](/2029/09/17/gatsby-1974-film/).

## What the Adaptations Teach a Reader of the Book

The practical value of the adaptation history is that it isolates, by subtraction, what the novel is actually made of.

Watch any of these films and then return to the book, and Nick's voice becomes audible in a way it was not before. Having spent two hours with a version that lacks it, the reader notices the constant low commentary running under every scene: the qualifications, the reversals, the judgments Nick offers and then partly withdraws. That commentary is invisible on a first reading because it feels like the natural texture of the prose. The films make it visible by removing it.

The same subtraction works on the symbols. Every adaptation shows the green light, and in every adaptation it is a light. On the page it is a light that acquires meaning through repeated appearances and then loses it, which is a process rather than an object. Film can show the object and struggles to show the process, which is why the light in these movies feels like a prop with a caption attached.

There is a third lesson about the parties. All four films devote substantial resources to the party sequences, and the party sequences are where all four are strongest, because the novel's party scenes are already cinematic: crowded, visual, propulsive, driven by movement rather than reflection. That the films succeed exactly where the novel is least novelistic is a useful piece of evidence about what transfers between the forms.

Finally, the adaptations expose which parts of the book are plot and which parts are argument. Anything all four films kept is plot. Anything all four films dropped or fumbled is argument. Run that filter and the residue is remarkably consistent: Nick's opening frame, the enchanted objects passage, the retrospective judgments, and the closing paragraphs. Those are the parts that make it a great novel, and they are exactly the parts no camera has managed to hold.

## Closing Verdict

Every Great Gatsby film adaptation ranked in this article is placed by one question: what did it do about Nick?

By that measure the 2013 version leads, because it alone treated the retrospective narration as a problem requiring invention rather than an inconvenience to be managed. Its solution is imperfect and its excesses are real. It is still the only version that acknowledged what the book is made of. The 1974 version follows, most faithful and least warm, a beautiful reconstruction with the commentary removed. The 1949 version is third, hobbled by a censorship regime that required the moral clarity the novel refuses, though it understood Gatsby's criminality better than either successor. The 1926 version is placed fourth by necessity, since it cannot be seen.

The more durable finding is the pattern. A century of attempts has produced four intelligent, expensive, well-made films, none of which has rendered the thing that makes the source matter. That is not a failure of filmmaking. It is a demonstration that some effects belong to one medium, and that Fitzgerald built his novel out of exactly those.

Which is the strongest argument for reading it. The films will give you the parties, the clothes, the cars, and the death. The voice deciding what all of it meant, and never quite settling, is available in one place only.

A last word on how to use this ranking. Treat it as a set of questions rather than a scoreboard. Ask of any adaptation what it did with Nick, what it did with Gatsby's crimes, how it handled the last three chapters, and whether it made Tom simpler than he is. Those four questions will produce a defensible assessment of any future version, and there will be future versions, since the novel entered the United States public domain in 2021 and the barrier to adapting it has fallen to nothing. The next filmmaker to attempt it will face the same obstacle as the previous four, and the ranking above is best understood as a record of how the obstacle has been approached rather than a closed account of who approached it best.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do the Great Gatsby film adaptations rank?

By the criteria used here, the 2013 Luhrmann version places first, the 1974 Clayton version second, the 1949 Nugent version third, and the 1926 Brenon silent fourth. The deciding criterion is what each film does about Nick's retrospective narration, since that voice carries the novel's meaning. The 2013 film alone treats it as a problem requiring invention. A reader weighting fidelity or period accuracy more heavily would reasonably place the 1974 version first, and the article states its criteria openly so that the order can be argued with rather than accepted.

### Q: What are the principal Gatsby film adaptations?

Four cinema releases. The 1926 silent, directed by Herbert Brenon with Warner Baxter as Gatsby, now lost apart from a one-minute trailer. The 1949 Paramount film, directed by Elliott Nugent with Alan Ladd. The 1974 film, directed by Jack Clayton from a Francis Ford Coppola screenplay, with Robert Redford and Mia Farrow. And Baz Luhrmann's 2013 production with Leonardo DiCaprio and Carey Mulligan. Television versions also exist, including episodes in the nineteen fifties and a made-for-television film in 2000 with Toby Stephens, Mira Sorvino, and Paul Rudd.

### Q: What criteria rank the adaptations?

Four descriptive measures and one decisive test. Fidelity to plot records whether the events survive, weighted lightly since the events are the least valuable part of the source. Performance records what the casting reveals about how the production understood the characters. Visual achievement records design and cinematography, where adaptations of this novel consistently succeed. Reception records critical and commercial response. The narration test, weighted heaviest, asks what the film does about the fact that its source is a retrospective first-person account by a compromised narrator.

### Q: Which film best serves the novel?

None serves it fully, which is the finding rather than an evasion. The 2013 version serves the novel's argument best, since it treats the book as a piece of writing whose value lies in its prose and invents a device to acknowledge that. The 1974 version serves the novel's world best, reconstructing the period with care that has shaped how audiences picture the Jazz Age ever since. The 1949 version serves Gatsby's criminality best. A viewer should choose according to what they want the film to supply.

### Q: Is one film definitively the best?

No. The four succeed at incommensurable things and were made under industrial conditions that make any shared scale partly artificial. A 1949 Paramount release constrained by the Production Code and a 2013 three-dimensional summer blockbuster were not attempting the same task in the first place. The ranking in this article exists to make an argument about the narration problem visible, not to settle a question of quality. Readers who weight the criteria differently will produce different orders, and several of those orders are entirely defensible on the available evidence.

### Q: Why has each era struggled to film Gatsby?

Because the novel's power lives in a retrospective narrating voice, and cinema has weak tools for that. Film shows events happening. The book is about a man deciding, years afterward, what events meant, and remaining unsure. Voice-over approximates the effect and always feels like a workaround. Each adaptation has therefore either avoided Nick's commentary, replaced it with an external moral frame, kept him without giving him anything to say, or invented a device that makes the retrospection explicit at the cost of subtlety.

### Q: Why is the 1926 silent version lost?

Nitrate film stock decays, and studios routinely destroyed prints once a picture's theatrical run ended, since silent films were assumed to hold no further commercial value. Something like three quarters of American silent features are gone for these reasons. In the case of Gatsby, the last known viewing appears to have been in the late nineteen forties, during research for the Paramount remake. What survives is a trailer of roughly one minute, preserved by the Library of Congress and included as an extra on home releases of the 2013 film.

### Q: What happened to the 1949 Alan Ladd version?

It was released by Paramount in July 1949, ran ninety-one minutes, and fell into obscurity fairly quickly. The Production Code required a clear moral resolution, so the film supplies a framing judgment the novel refuses, which is generally regarded as its central weakness. It has been harder to see than the later versions for much of its life. Its strengths are real though under-discussed: Alan Ladd's screen persona carried a genuine sense of criminal self-invention that later, more glamorous casting choices lost.

### Q: Who directed the 1974 adaptation?

Jack Clayton directed it, working from a screenplay by Francis Ford Coppola, with David Merrick producing for Paramount. Coppola later stated that the finished film departed substantially from the script he delivered, which complicates any assessment of authorship. The production team included cinematographer Douglas Slocombe, production designer John Box, costume designer Theoni V. Aldredge, and composer Nelson Riddle. Aldredge and Riddle won the film's two Academy Awards, and Box and Slocombe took British awards for art direction and cinematography respectively.

### Q: Which adaptation has the largest audience today?

The 2013 Luhrmann version, decisively. It grossed over three hundred fifty million dollars worldwide on release, making it the director's most commercially successful film, and it has remained the version most widely available on streaming platforms and most often assigned or referenced in classrooms. For most viewers under a certain age, it is the only Gatsby film they have seen. The 1974 version retains a substantial secondary audience through its influence on fashion and period design rather than through repeat viewing.

### Q: Do the films agree on how to handle Nick's narration?

No, and the disagreement is the most interesting thing about the adaptation history. The 1926 version, drawing on a stage adaptation, appears to have reduced Nick to an observer. The 1949 version replaced his compromised judgment with an externally imposed moral frame. The 1974 version retained him but gave him little to do beyond witnessing, since retrospective commentary has no natural home in a scene. The 2013 version invented a framing device in which Nick is writing the account, which makes the retrospection explicit and licenses on-screen prose.

### Q: Which adaptation is most faithful to the plot?

The 1974 version, without close competition. Coppola's screenplay reproduces the novel's events in order and lifts a great deal of the dialogue directly, and the production invested heavily in period accuracy down to the automobiles and the tableware. The result demonstrates that plot fidelity and interpretive success are unrelated, since the film has been criticized for emotional coolness continuously since its release. What could not be reproduced was Nick's retrospective voice, and without it the reconstructed events read as handsome behavior in handsome rooms, observed without moral pressure.

### Q: Which adaptation departs furthest from the source?

The 1949 version departs furthest in substance, because the Production Code required a moral resolution the novel deliberately withholds, which changes the meaning of the story rather than its events. The 2013 version departs furthest in surface, adding a sanatorium framing device and a contemporary soundtrack while following the plot fairly closely. The distinction matters: one film keeps the events and changes the argument, the other keeps the argument and changes the texture, and only the second is defensible as interpretation.

### Q: Are the television versions worth counting?

They are worth watching and are conventionally excluded from the ranking of principal adaptations. The 2000 made-for-television film with Toby Stephens, Mira Sorvino, and Paul Rudd is the most substantial, and it occasionally handles Nick better than the cinema versions do, since television drama is more tolerant of narration than a theatrical release. Earlier television treatments appeared in the nineteen fifties. Students writing about adaptation may cite these usefully, particularly when arguing that the narration problem is a cinema problem rather than a screen problem.

### Q: How did each adaptation perform commercially?

The 1926 film's returns are not well documented and it appears not to have been a notable success. The 1949 version took roughly two million dollars and is generally regarded as a modest performer. The 1974 version cost about seven million and grossed around twenty-six and a half million, a solid result for the period. The 2013 version grossed over three hundred fifty million worldwide against a budget reported between one hundred five and one hundred ninety million, by far the largest commercial success of the four.

### Q: Should a student watch a film instead of reading?

No, and the adaptation history is itself the argument. Every film reproduces the plot and every film loses Nick's retrospective commentary, which is where the novel's meaning is located. A student who watches instead of reading acquires the events and none of the argument, which is exactly the material an essay is graded on. Watching after reading is genuinely useful, since the absence of the commentary in the film makes its presence in the book audible in a way a first reading rarely achieves.

### Q: Which film would help most before an exam?

The 1974 version, for a narrow purpose: it follows the plot closely enough to reinforce the sequence of events and the relationships between characters, and its period reconstruction helps with contextual questions. Use it as a memory aid rather than as a source of interpretation. The 2013 version is more interesting to write about but risks importing its invented framing device into an answer about the novel, which examiners notice. Neither substitutes for the text, and both are best watched after a full reading.

### Q: What does the ranking reveal about each decade?

That each era translated the novel into whatever it considered serious commercial filmmaking. The nineteen twenties produced a society melodrama drawn from a stage hit. The nineteen forties produced a crime picture with an imposed moral. The nineteen seventies produced a prestige literary costume drama. The twenty tens produced a spectacle blockbuster with a pop soundtrack. The book stayed constant while the containers changed, which makes the adaptation sequence a reasonably good index of shifting assumptions about what a respectable literary film looks like.

### Q: Has any adaptation solved the narration problem?

No adaptation has solved it, and the consistency of the failure is evidence about the novel rather than about filmmakers. The 2013 version comes closest by inventing a frame in which Nick is explicitly composing the account, which permits Fitzgerald's sentences to appear on screen as written text. The cost is that Nick's unreliability shifts from moral to clinical, since the film makes him a patient. In the book we doubt him because his principles and conduct diverge, which is subtler and more productive.
