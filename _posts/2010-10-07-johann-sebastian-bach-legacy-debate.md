## The verdict from inside his own family

The harshest early judgment on Johann Sebastian Bach came from inside his own household. In the years around his death in 1750, his sons were building careers in a musical world that prized clarity, grace and immediate charm, and they described their father's manner of writing as old-fashioned. Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach and Wilhelm Friedemann Bach had learned counterpoint at his table, copied his scores and absorbed his discipline. Their verdict was therefore not ignorance speaking. It was informed judgment from musicians who knew the fugues and chorales intimately and who nonetheless heard them as belonging to a passing order. That family verdict opens a question that runs through every later argument about reputation: why did the people who knew the music best think it was already out of date?

The context sharpens the puzzle. A father's style can embarrass ambitious sons for social reasons alone, since each generation advertises its difference. Yet the sons' reservations were specific and musical rather than merely fashionable. They pointed to dense part writing, to learned counterpoint carried into genres that rewarded lightness, and to a harmonic language that younger composers found heavy. A judgment with technical content deserves a technical hearing. Either the sons misread music they understood deeply, or they perceived something genuine that later admiration has smoothed away. Framing the debate in that way protects both sides from caricature at the outset.

One further fact gives the family verdict its force. The sons did not discard the inheritance. Carl Philipp Emanuel preserved manuscripts, supplied material to early biographers and treated the paternal scores as property of real value even while his own keyboard music pursued a contrasting expressive ideal. Admiration and reservation lived together in the same house. That combination is the honest starting point for any legacy debate, because it refuses the later habit of sorting listeners into believers and detractors. Proximity produced a mixed verdict, and mixed verdicts from qualified witnesses are usually the ones worth trusting as evidence.

Consider also what the sons stood to gain and lose. Heirs of a famous craftsman inherit both capital and shadow. Wilhelm Friedemann, the eldest and by many accounts the most gifted, saw his career falter despite talent that his father had trained with special care, and his later bitterness coloured recollections that biographers mined for fact. Carl Philipp Emanuel, established at the Prussian court and later in Hamburg, managed the paternal legacy with a curator's tact, answering enquiries and parting with manuscripts selectively. The verdict of old-fashionedness passed through those two different temperaments and interests. Discounting either testimony entirely, whether as filial ingratitude or as marketing for a new style, discards evidence that a careful historian can use once its angle of distortion is known.
## What his sons meant by old-fashioned

Old-fashioned had a precise meaning in the middle of the eighteenth century. It named a style built on strict counterpoint, on thoroughbass habits formed in an earlier generation, and on church genres that court taste had begun to treat as heavy furniture. The galant manner prized periodic melody, transparent texture and a singing line that any cultivated listener could follow at first hearing. Against that standard, a five voice fugue or a chorale harmonisation dense with chromatic passing notes could sound like scholarship displayed where entertainment was expected. The sons were measuring their father against a living market, and by that measure he was behind the direction of travel.

The criticism was musically coherent, and that point matters more than its politeness or rudeness. Carl Philipp Emanuel developed the empfindsamer Stil, a language of sudden pauses, sighing figures and intimate keyboard confession that defined itself against paternal density. His treatise on keyboard playing codified an art of expression that treated the single melodic line as the carrier of feeling. When such a musician calls counterpoint old-fashioned, he is defending a rival theory of what music is for. The disagreement concerned purpose, not competence, and it foreshadowed every later round of the same argument.

Geography reinforced the generational split. The sons worked in Berlin and Hamburg, cities oriented toward opera, public concerts and a paying audience of amateurs. Their father had spent his final twenty seven years in Leipzig, serving a town council, a university church tradition and a school choir. Court and city rewarded novelty and brilliance. A cantor's post rewarded dependable craft across the church year. Judged from Berlin, Leipzig habits looked provincial. Judged from Leipzig, Berlin habits could look thin. Neither vantage point was neutral, and remembering that fact disciplines any attempt to treat the sons' verdict as a final sentence on the music itself.

The vocabulary of the criticism repays attention as well. Terms such as learned, artificial and laboured recur in mid century commentary about strict counterpoint, while natural, pleasing and singing praise the newer manner. Those adjectives smuggle an aesthetic theory inside a description. To call a fugue laboured is to claim that effort should be invisible in finished art, a claim the older generation would have rejected as a confusion between courtly ease and artistic worth. The sons, educated by a father who valued the display of craft as a form of honesty, adopted the court's vocabulary when they crossed into the court's world. Their criticism therefore documents a change of allegiance between two coherent aesthetics. Neither aesthetic refutes the other. Each defines the surface on which the other looks excessive, and the exchange between them frames the conservative and radical cases that follow.
## The case that Bach was a conservative

The conservative case begins with genre. Across a long career, the composer devoted his largest energies to forms inherited from predecessors: the Lutheran cantata, the organ chorale prelude, the fugue, the suite of dances and the concerto grosso pattern established by Corelli and refined by Vivaldi. He founded no new genre that successors adopted under his name. He wrote no opera, the dominant public form of his century, and he showed little interest in the sonata designs that would define the next era. A musician so invested in inherited containers can plausibly be described as a consolidator, a figure who brought an older art to completion rather than opening a new one.

### Was Bach ahead of or behind his time?

Behind, on the evidence of genre and reception, is the answer the conservative case gives. He perfected inherited forms such as the fugue and the Lutheran cantata while younger contemporaries developed lighter galant styles, and his own sons described his manner as old-fashioned within his lifetime.

Christoph Wolff, whose documentary biography assembled the most complete record of the composer's working life, presented a figure rooted in the institutions that employed him: court Kapelle, town council and church. On Wolff's account, the music answers commissions, calendars and local resources rather than any programme of reform. The Goldberg Variations answer a patron's request. The cantata cycles answer the Leipzig church year. The Well-Tempered Clavier answers a pedagogical need. Each answer is executed at an extraordinary level, yet the questions were set by employers and tradition. A conservative, on this reading, is not a dullard. The term describes where initiative lay, and initiative lay mostly outside the composer.

Reception history supports the same reading. Within a generation of his death, public performance of the large choral works had lapsed, the manuscripts circulated among specialists, and the name survived chiefly among keyboard players and theorists who valued craft. A radical usually leaves a school agitating for change. The decades after 1750 show instead a connoisseur's afterlife, quiet and technical, until the nineteenth century revival rebuilt a public reputation on new foundations. The conservative case therefore rests on three independent supports: inherited genres, institutionally set tasks and a posthumous audience of specialists. Any defence against the charge has to answer all three supports rather than selecting the weakest one for easy rebuttal.

A further plank in the conservative platform concerns training and temperament. The composer was formed inside a family guild culture of town musicians, apprenticed through copying the works of predecessors such as Froberger, Pachelbel and Buxtehude, and he preserved that copying discipline across his life, transcribing Vivaldi as a grown master to learn what the Italian concerto knew. Guild formation produces custodians by design: mastery means absorbing a tradition completely enough to reproduce its best effects on demand. The conservative case notes, correctly, that contemporaries hired him in that spirit, as an authority on the established art of organ building and counterpoint. Where the case overreaches is in treating formation as destiny. Plenty of guild trained musicians innovated. Formation explains the tools at hand. It cannot by itself decide how far beyond precedent the tools were pushed, and that distance is the quantity the radical case sets out to measure.
## The case that Bach was a radical

The radical case starts inside the notes rather than with genre labels. Harmonic writing in the Chromatic Fantasia and Fugue, in the Actus Tragicus cantata and across the second book of the Well-Tempered Clavier presses against the limits of what contemporaries treated as permissible, using chromatic voice leading and remote modulations that later centuries associated with far younger composers. The opening chorus of the St Matthew Passion layers two choirs, a chorale sung by a separate group and an orchestral fabric of interlocking lines into a structure without real precedent in scale or density. If radical means extending the technical means of an art beyond received boundaries, the scores supply abundant evidence.

Formal experiment tells a parallel story. The Goldberg Variations build thirty variations on a bass pattern, grouping them through canon at widening intervals into an architecture that is also a feat of abstract planning. The Musical Offering takes a theme supplied by Frederick the Great and derives ricercars and canons of severe ingenuity, turning a court compliment into a laboratory of contrapuntal possibility. The Art of Fugue pursues one subject through an exhaustive series of fugal procedures and breaks off in a final movement where the composer's own name enters the thematic material in German letter notation. No mere custodian of tradition designs projects of that speculative character for no commission and no certain audience.

The radical reading also draws strength from the composer's indifference to fashion as a career strategy. Remaining in Leipzig after seeking advancement elsewhere, declining operatic opportunity and persisting with learned counterpoint while the market rewarded lightness can be read as conservatism. It can equally be read as refusal: a deliberate decision that the art's deepest resources mattered more than the taste of the moment. Laurence Dreyfus, writing on questions of performance and interpretation, treated the music as posing problems that each generation must solve afresh rather than as a settled inheritance, a view that supports the radical framing by stressing how unsettled the scores remain under the hands of performers.

The radical case also gains from examining the keyboard works as a sequence rather than as monuments. Book one of the Well-Tempered Clavier systematised playing in every key; book two, assembled two decades later, treats the same scheme with a freedom that borders on the fantastical in places, admitting dance types, Italian aria manners and fugal subjects of a chromatic restlessness the earlier book avoids. A custodian repeats a system. A restless mind returns to its own system and worries it into new shapes. The same pattern marks the organ works, where the Leipzig chorales of the final decade revise earlier Weimar settings toward denser and more speculative treatments. Self revision on that scale is a signature of artists who experience their own past solutions as problems. The radical reading names that experience accurately, whatever label history finally pins on the whole career.
## Why both cases use the same evidence

Place the two cases side by side and an odd regularity appears. Both cite the fugue. The conservative points to the fugue as an inherited form perfected late in its history. The radical points to what happens inside particular fugues, where chromatic subjects and stretto combinations exceed anything the form had previously contained. Both cite the Leipzig appointment. One side reads twenty seven years of church service as institutional loyalty. The other reads the same tenure as stubborn independence from court fashion. Both cite the sons. One side hears informed criticism. The other hears a generation defining itself by necessary exaggeration of its distance from a father.

That symmetry carries the first large lesson of the debate. The conservative and radical readings draw on identical evidence, which means the disagreement is about criteria, not facts. Nobody disputes the genres used, the posts held, the manuscripts preserved or the testimony recorded. The dispute begins when a listener decides whether novelty of form, novelty of content or indifference to fashion should count as the measure of forwardness. Each criterion is defensible. None is compulsory. Arguments that present a verdict on forwardness as though it were a finding about the scores themselves conceal a prior choice, and the concealment is where most published controversy on this question goes wrong.

The family question opened at the start can therefore be closed in a disciplined form. The people who knew the music best thought it out of date because they judged by the criterion of the living market, the direction of public taste in Berlin and Hamburg. By that criterion they were right, and later centuries did not refute them so much as change the criterion. Once posterity began to value depth of construction over currency of manner, the same scores that looked dated looked central. The verdict from inside the family stands as accurate testimony about 1750 and as a warning against treating any single criterion as timeless.

One more symmetry deserves recording before the argument moves on. Both sides quote the composer's famous description, preserved through the necrology prepared by Carl Philipp Emanuel and Johann Friedrich Agricola, of fugal procedure and of his own habits of work, including the insistence that industry could carry any ordinarily gifted person as far as talent had carried him. Conservatives cite the ethic of industry as guild virtue. Radicals cite the same sentence as a democratising manifesto, craft knowledge claimed for anyone willing to labour rather than reserved for inspired favourites. A single sentence sustaining both readings confirms the section's thesis from a fresh angle: the archive is stable, the interpretive instruments differ, and the instruments are chosen upstream of every verdict the debate produces.
## Is Bach the greatest composer: the claim stated properly

Stated carelessly, the greatest-composer claim is a pub argument. Stated properly, it has a definite shape. It asserts that across a large output in many genres, one composer sustained a level of structural command, expressive range and technical invention that no rival matches over a comparable body of work. The claim is comparative, it depends on the size and variety of the output admitted as evidence, and it quietly assumes that some single scale can rank artists whose aims differed. Getting the claim into that precise form is a precondition for arguing about it honestly, because most disagreement turns out to concern the hidden assumptions rather than the music.

### Is Bach the greatest composer of all time?

On the criterion of structural command across a large and varied output, the case is exceptionally strong, and advocates rest it on the fugues, passions and variation sets. Whether that criterion should decide the question is a separate judgment that the claim itself cannot settle.

Advocates usually marshal four bodies of evidence. The first is range under control: dance suites, keyboard cycles, concertos, cantatas, passions and chamber music all handled with the same command of part writing. The second is density of invention, the sense that even occasional pieces such as wedding cantatas or coffee house entertainments receive fully worked musical thought. The third is influence documented in named successors, from Mozart studying fugues in Vienna to Mendelssohn reviving the St Matthew Passion. The fourth is durability across taste regimes, since the music survived the galant turn, the Romantic era and the early music movement with standing intact. That is a formidable dossier, and dismissing it as fashion or propaganda fails to engage it.

Yet a question hangs over the dossier, and it deserves to be posed without evasion. If the greatness claim is so widely accepted among musicians and teachers, what is it actually resting on? Acceptance could rest on direct acquaintance with hundreds of works, or on a smaller canon of celebrated scores filtered through education, or on a criterion chosen because the preferred candidate excels under it. Those three foundations have very different strengths. The sections that follow take the dossier apart piece by piece, testing which parts record properties of the music and which parts record properties of the institutions that transmit reputations.

Precision about the claim also requires distinguishing its professional and public forms. Among performers and composers, the verdict often functions as a working premise rather than a theorem: conductors programme the passions as summits of the choral art, composers from Chopin keeping the Well-Tempered Clavier at hand to Beethoven lecturing pupils on the fugues treat the scores as a court of appeal. Among the wider public the same verdict arrives as received opinion, repeated in programme notes and obituaries of taste without inspection of the underlying catalogue. Those two forms have different vulnerabilities. The professional premise can be tested against practice and survives or falls by use. The public form is vulnerable to fashion alone, and its holders often cannot say which works the premise summarises. Arguments about the claim go astray when they attack the public form and declare the professional premise refuted, or defend the premise and declare the public form vindicated.
## The criterion problem behind the claim

Every ranking conceals a criterion. To say that one composer stands above Mozart and Beethoven is to say that some quality, present in unequal measure across their outputs, deserves to govern the comparison. Candidates for that governing quality include melodic gift, dramatic power, emotional directness, formal innovation, influence on successors and command of structure. Different choices produce different winners with mechanical reliability. Choose dramatic power and Beethoven advances. Choose melodic abundance and Mozart advances. Choose structural command and the Leipzig cantor advances. The ranking exercise therefore measures the chooser's values at least as faithfully as it measures the composers.

Honesty requires admitting that no neutral procedure exists for weighting those qualities against one another. There is no unit in which a fugue's architecture and an aria's melodic radiance can both be denominated, no exchange rate between construction and consolation. Committees and critics who announce rankings without stating weights are performing a ritual rather than conducting an inquiry. The ritual has social uses, since canons organise teaching and programming. It has no evidentiary force. A ranking with unpublished weights tells an audience whom the ranker admires, and admiration, however informed, is testimony rather than proof.

The criterion problem also explains why the debate never ends. Participants believe they are disputing facts about scores, and occasionally they are, when a date or an attribution is at stake. Most exchanges, though, pit one unstated weighting against another. A structuralist hears evasion when a melodist praises tunes. A melodist hears coldness when a structuralist praises architecture. Each side experiences the other as deaf to something obvious. Naming the mechanism does not dissolve the disagreement, but it relocates argument to the ground where progress is possible: a defence of why one quality should govern, offered openly and tested against what listeners across centuries have valued in practice.

Philosophers of art have a name for the underlying difficulty: incommensurability, the absence of a common measure between goods of different kinds. A melody's memorability and a fugue's combinatorial depth are both real properties, and no operation converts one into the units of the other. Rankings proceed anyway because institutions require them: syllabi need sequences, seasons need anchors, publishers need a canon to print. The criterion problem is therefore not a puzzle awaiting a clever solution. It is a permanent feature of comparative judgment under plural values. Accepting that permanence changes the ethics of the debate. Participants owe one another their weightings in advance, the way witnesses owe the court their interests. A critic who declares a winner while concealing the scale has committed the one dishonesty this debate cannot afford, since every other disagreement inside it is legitimate.
## Structural command as the criterion

Structural command names a cluster of abilities that can be examined in scores without resort to taste alone. It includes control of counterpoint, where independent lines sustain interest while obeying harmonic logic; command of large spans, where a movement of twenty minutes coheres; economy of material, where a whole cycle grows from a bass line or a single subject; and consistency, where the standard holds across hundreds of works rather than in a few peaks. On each component, evidence can be produced, quoted in notes and checked by any trained reader. That checkability is precisely why educators favour the criterion: it can be taught, tested and demonstrated at a desk.

Under this criterion the case reaches its maximum strength. The Art of Fugue demonstrates contrapuntal procedures of a completeness that no contemporary attempted. The B minor Mass assembles movements across decades into an edifice whose proportions reward prolonged study. The cello suites construct an illusion of polyphony on a single melodic instrument, implying harmonies that are never sounded in full. Analysts from Heinrich Schenker's generation onward treated such scores as proof that musical value could reside in voice leading architecture itself. John Butt, whose scholarship addressed performance and the theology embedded in the church music, situated that architecture within a culture of Lutheran devotion, giving the structural case an intellectual home in the composer's own world rather than in later theory alone.

Here the debate reaches its pivotal recognition. The greatest-composer claim depends on choosing structural command as the deciding criterion, and that choice is a choice, not a finding. Nothing in the scores instructs a listener to weight architecture over melody or drama. The preference has respectable defences: architecture is demonstrable, it survives translation across performance styles, and it correlates with the admiration of practitioners who work inside the music daily. Respectable defences, however, remain defences of a preference. Once that is granted, the argument changes character. It stops being a contest over a title and becomes an inquiry into which musical values deserve primacy, a question on which reasonable and informed people have differed for three centuries.

The checkability advantage deserves one more examination, because it explains the criterion's grip on institutions. An examiner can set a fugue exposition and mark the answer's handling of the dominant against rules a candidate can study. No examiner can set an exercise in being moving and mark it with comparable agreement. Professions gravitate toward the standards they can administer, and musical education is a profession before it is a priesthood. Structural command thus enjoys an administrative tailwind unrelated to its truth as a measure of worth. Naming the tailwind does not discredit the criterion, since the qualities it tracks are genuine and its results correlate with the admiration of practitioners who had every freedom to admire elsewhere. The tailwind warning simply instructs the reader to discount the criterion's institutional dominance when weighing its philosophical case, much as a court discounts testimony that a witness is paid to give, without assuming the testimony is false.
## What that criterion systematically favours

A criterion is never innocent. Structural command favours music that rewards analysis, and analysis favours works written for instruments and voices under a composer's close control, preserved in legible scores and transmitted through institutions that teach reading. It favours long careers with large surviving outputs, since consistency across hundreds of works is part of the claim. It favours cultures of notation over cultures of improvisation, where value lives in performance decisions that leave no durable text. It favours the study over the theatre, the desk over the dance floor. None of those biases makes the criterion illegitimate. Each bias does mean that its verdicts encode a picture of what music is before any particular composer is assessed.

The bias toward the written text deserves particular attention in this case. Keyboard and choral scores from Leipzig survive in unusual quantity because a family, a school and a network of pupils preserved them. Comparable improvisers whose art lived in performance left fragments. A criterion that counts only what can be examined on paper will crown the best documented builder, and documentation is partly an accident of archives. Nicholas Kenyon, editing a major collection of essays on the composer's reception, drew attention to how each era reconstructs a different figure from the surviving materials, a reminder that the archive shapes the argument before the argument begins. The structural case is strong on its own ground. Its ground, however, was landscaped by survival.

There is also a bias of scale. Structural command is easiest to display in large designs: masses, passions, variation cycles and multi movement suites. Miniaturists, song composers whose genius concentrates in three minutes, and dramatists whose effects depend on staging all compete at a disadvantage under a scale weighted toward architecture. A fair hearing for the criterion therefore needs a supplement: an account of why large scale construction should outrank intensity in small forms. Advocates rarely supply that account, because the prestige of the monumental feels self evident to those formed by conservatory training. Prestige acquired through training is real evidence of a tradition's values. It is weak evidence that the values are universal.

The favouring pattern extends to geography and gender in ways the debate names too rarely. A criterion of written structural command privileges the German and Austrian organ lofts and chapels where dense notation was the professional currency, over the Italian opera house, the French court ballet or the English theatre, where excellence wore other clothes. It privileges the professional men admitted to those lofts over the women whose musical labour, in households such as the composer's own, ran through copying, teaching and singing that left fewer signed scores for examiners to grade. Anna Magdalena's documentary invisibility, examined later in this article in connection with the cello suites, is partly an artifact of exactly this bias in what counts as evidence of command. None of that makes the fugues less masterly. It does mean the criterion's map of musical worth was drawn by the institutions that the criterion then crowns, a circularity its advocates should concede gracefully.
## What it systematically misses

Begin with what listeners report first. Many encounters with great music are encounters with a phrase that lodges in memory, a theatrical moment that changes breathing, a dance rhythm that organises a room. Structural command registers none of those events directly. A fugue can be impeccable and leave a particular listener unmoved on a particular evening, while a simple aria by Handel or a Mozart slow movement performs the act that listener came for. A criterion that cannot register the difference between being impressed and being moved will systematically undervalue composers whose gifts operate on feeling before reflection. The structural case answers that reflection deepens feeling over repeated hearings. Sometimes it does. Sometimes repetition merely deepens familiarity with machinery.

The criterion also misses historical effect. Music changes the world through performances, institutions and imitations as much as through scores. A composer whose innovations altered what every successor had to learn reshaped the art even if individual works show looser construction. The structural scale has no column for that reshaping, or rather it smuggles it in through the back door by counting influence separately when convenient. Consistency matters here. If influence counts, it counts for rivals too, and Beethoven's effect on the nineteenth century or Wagner's on the twentieth enters the ledger with full weight. If influence does not count, then the structural case must stand on the paper alone and abandon one of its favourite supporting arguments about Mozart's Viennese studies.

Finally, the criterion misses the social life of music. Cantatas were written for congregations who heard them once, amid sermons, in cold churches, as part of worship rather than as concert objects. Judging those works primarily as architecture reverses the priorities of their first hearers, for whom text, occasion and devotion governed. A listener is free to reverse those priorities; concert culture does so every week. The reversal should be acknowledged as a modern habit rather than projected backward as the music's own demand. What the structural criterion misses, in sum, is not marginal. It misses immediacy, consequence and occasion, three of the main reasons human beings have ever cared about organised sound.

A concrete test helps to fix what the criterion misses. Play the opening of the St Matthew Passion and the opening of a Handel anthem to a listener with no preparation, and ask what each passage is doing. The Handel will likely be summarised in emotional terms within seconds; the denser score may be summarised in spatial terms, as size, motion and weight. Both summaries record genuine properties, yet ranking instruments register the second kind of response far more readily than the first, because spatial and architectural vocabulary converts into marks and grades. The listener who wept at the Handel has not made an error that the listener who admired the architecture has avoided. Two instruments measured two properties, and a culture that owns only one instrument will keep misdescribing half of its own experience as ignorance.
## Melody as a competing criterion

Suppose melody governs instead. The governing question becomes: who invents phrases that listeners carry away, that singers shape careers around, that other composers borrow because the phrases seem inevitable once heard? Melodic judgment has its own evidence base. It includes the afterlife of tunes in quotation, arrangement and memory, the testimony of performers who stake evenings on a line's capacity to hold a hall, and the plain experiment of humming a score's themes a week after hearing it. By those tests the field reshuffles at once. Handel, Schubert and Mozart rise. Composers of dense polyphony, whose themes often serve as raw material for combination rather than as monuments in themselves, fall back.

The melodic case against the Leipzig master is specific rather than vague. Many of his subjects are deliberately neutral: scale patterns and arpeggiated figures designed to combine, invert and augment. Their excellence lies in what they permit, not in what they sing. Even celebrated themes, such as the subject of the Musical Offering, were supplied by a king rather than invented by the composer. Advocates reply that melody also lives in the total line of a movement, in the aria writing of the passions, in the sarabandes of the suites. That reply has force, and a fair melodist must concede the aria Erbarme dich as evidence of the highest order. The concession changes a rout into a contest. It does not restore the structural verdict, because the criterion has changed and the contest is being scored by new rules.

Melody also exposes a hidden assumption in the structural claim, namely that complexity of combination outranks memorability of line because combination is harder to achieve. Difficulty is a fact about production. Value is a judgment about experience. Conflating the two is the master error of craft based rankings across all the arts, visible whenever a technically fearsome novel or building is pronounced greater than a lucid one on grounds of effort. A melodist is entitled to insist that the finished experience, not the labour ledger, is the proper object of judgment. That insistence cannot be refuted from inside structural premises, which is exactly the point about competing criteria.

The melodic contest also has a documentary dimension that favours candour. Composers testify about one another through borrowing, and borrowing is melody's ballot box. Handel borrowed themes wholesale from contemporaries and predecessors, a practice his age accepted and ours documents in detail, because strong phrases were common currency. The themes of the Leipzig master were borrowed less often as tunes and more often as procedures: Mozart, Beethoven, Mendelssohn and Schumann took fugal subjects and working methods rather than singable lines. Even the famous borrowing of the Royal Theme in the Musical Offering runs from a king to the composer, not the reverse. That distribution of borrowing is exactly what the melodist predicts and the structuralist must explain. Procedures travel through schools. Tunes travel through throats. A criterion built on throats will always produce a different podium, and its podium will always look to the schools like a popularity contest, which is precisely the misunderstanding this article exists to retire.
## Emotional immediacy as a competing criterion

A second rival criterion prizes emotional immediacy: the speed and certainty with which music produces a felt state in a listener who brings no analytical preparation. Its evidence is physiological and collective. Audiences weep at particular moments across cultures and centuries, and the recurrence of those moments is data, however untidy. Under immediacy, drama becomes central, and composers who think in theatrical time, who understand delay, rupture and release as moral events, gain ground. The criterion favours the pause before a resolution, the entrance that reframes everything heard so far, the theme that returns transformed after suffering.

Immediacy has weaknesses that its advocates must own. It confuses familiarity with power, since music heard since childhood strikes deeper regardless of quality. It rewards volume, tempo and harmonic simplicity in ways that can flatter crude effects. It varies by listener temperament and by the acoustics of a room. A structuralist is entitled to call immediacy an unstable measure. The melodist and the dramatist can return the compliment with interest, because structural admiration varies by training in exactly parallel ways: the listener taught to follow invertible counterpoint feels its thrill, and the listener without that training feels little. Every criterion is somebody's cultivated response dressed as a yardstick. Maturity in this debate consists in admitting that fact for one's own side first.

Where does the Leipzig composer stand under immediacy? Mixed evidence again, which is itself informative. The passions contain passages of direct pathos that reach unprepared listeners without mediation: the alto aria with violin obbligato in the St Matthew Passion, the opening chorus's plea, the closing chorus's exhausted rocking rhythm. Against those stand long stretches of secco recitative and chorale harmonisation whose effect depends on text comprehension and liturgical familiarity. A dramatist rival builds whole evenings at the pitch of the best ten minutes here. An immediacy ranking therefore places the composer high among the moved and the moving, but not alone at the summit, and the difference between high and highest is the whole ballgame for the title claim.

Immediacy also has a temporal structure that rankings ignore. Some music delivers its whole effect within a minute and then repeats the delivery. Some withholds effect across long preparation and then pays compound interest. The second pattern describes the passions' large design, where an aria's consolation lands with its full weight only against the recitatives and chorales accumulated before it. Judging by first minute impact therefore discriminates against cumulative art in the same way that judging novels by their opening pages discriminates against slow builders. The discrimination is not a fallacy, since openings matter and many listeners reasonably decline long preparations. It is a bias with a direction, like every other bias catalogued in this argument, and an honest immediacy advocate will state its direction alongside the verdict it produces, granting the structuralist the same courtesy in return.
## The case for Mozart on those criteria

Mozart's case under melody and immediacy is formidable because it does not require special pleading. The operas join melodic abundance to dramatic timing: ensembles in The Marriage of Figaro accelerate social chaos into music of perfect lucidity, and the final scene of Don Giovanni stages judgment with a directness that needs no programme note. The piano concertos of the Vienna years turn a solo entrance into theatre, delaying, teasing and finally granting the theme listeners have been led to want. The Clarinet Quintet and the slow movement of the Clarinet Concerto supply phrases that singers envy. Each example can be tested by a sceptical listener in a single hearing, which is the immediacy criterion's home ground.

Mozart also competes better on structural ground than the standard contrast allows. The finale of the Jupiter Symphony combines sonata design with fugal combination in a display that analysts cite alongside anything in the contrapuntal literature. The string quartets dedicated to Haydn show learned writing placed at the service of conversational grace. Haydn's reported judgment to Leopold Mozart, that the young man had the greatest knowledge of composition of anyone known to him, is testimony from the leading craftsman of the age. A Mozart advocate can therefore fight on both criteria at once: win on melody and immediacy, draw or nearly draw on structure, and then ask what remains of a claim founded on structure alone once its margin narrows.

The honest structuralist reply concedes the narrowing and shifts to volume and consistency. Mozart died at thirty five. The output, though large, contains routine work from the Salzburg years and commissions fulfilled at speed. The rival claim rests on forty years of sustained production at the highest level across more genres, a ledger of consistency that a shorter life cannot match. That reply is real, and it reveals the ranking's dependence on yet another weighting: how much should longevity and bulk count against peak intensity? No score settles that weighting either. The Mozart case thus performs the service this article most values. It shows a great claim dissolving, under pressure, into a stack of smaller choices, each arguable and none compulsory.

The Mozart candidacy finally tests the debate's manners. Advocates of the Leipzig master sometimes answer it by diminishing the rival, pointing to the opere buffe's conventional numbers or to the speed of Viennese production as though facility were a stain. That manoeuvre concedes the argument it tries to win, because a claim that requires diminished rivals is a claim about a field, not about a summit. The strong form of the structural case has no need of it. Mozart can be granted melody, drama, precocity and his own formidable learned technique entire, and the structural case still stands on its different ground: duration, density and the unbroken level across four decades of institutionally unglamorous labour. Two summits of different ranges can both be real. Ranking insists on a single altitude table for terrain that was never surveyed with one instrument, and the Mozart case, pressed politely and in full strength, is the cleanest demonstration of that insistence failing.
## The case for Beethoven on those criteria

Beethoven's case rests on drama, transformation and consequence. Under immediacy, the opening of the Fifth Symphony, the slow movement of the Seventh, the choral finale of the Ninth and the late quartets' fractured lyricism all register without mediation. Under the criterion of historical effect, his case may be the strongest available for any composer: after the Eroica, the symphony carried ambitions it had not carried before; after the late quartets, the string quartet became a confessional genre; the figure of the composer as defiant individual, answerable to an inner standard, entered European culture largely through his example and has shaped artistic self understanding far beyond music.

Structural advocates sometimes treat Beethoven as a lesser contrapuntist, citing fugal movements that strain and occasionally sprawl, such as the finale of the Hammerklavier Sonata or the Grosse Fuge, overpowering as experiences and unruly as architecture. A Beethoven advocate accepts the description and contests the valuation. Strain, on that reading, is the sound of material resisting form, and the resistance communicates something that seamless mastery cannot: effort, risk and the possibility of failure inside the work itself. Where the Leipzig master represents completion, Beethoven represents striving, and whole centuries have preferred striving as the image of their own inner lives. A criterion that scores completion above striving is, once more, a preference and not a law of nature.

Beethoven also complicates the influence ledger in ways that discipline loose talk. Influence through scores studied in private, the channel by which Mozart absorbed fugal technique in Vienna, differs from influence through public myth, the channel by which Beethoven affected poets, painters and politicians who never read a note. Both channels are real. Counting only the first flatters text centred reputations. Counting both turns the ranking into cultural history, where the Leipzig composer's slower, quieter penetration of practice competes against Beethoven's rapid occupation of the imagination. Reasonable judges weight those currencies differently. The debate's persistence is explained by that sentence more completely than by any disagreement about the notes.

There is a biographical asymmetry between the two candidacies that honest advocates should declare. Beethoven curated his own myth with a showman's instinct, preserving conversation books, staging defiance before patrons and leaving posterity a narrative of heroic struggle that biographers from Schindler onward amplified, not always scrupulously. The Leipzig composer left petitions about salary, complaints about choirboys and a paper trail of municipal friction, then fell silent in the record for eighty years. A reputation market that rewards narrative will overvalue the documented striver and undervalue the undocumented craftsman until performers and editors supply the missing story, as Forkel and Mendelssohn eventually did. Part of the nineteenth century revival's energy was precisely that supply catching up. Recognising the asymmetry does not discount Beethoven's drama, which lives in the scores. It warns the court that one candidate arrived with an advocate and the other arrived with an archive.
## Does the pedagogical role inflate the reputation

No composer occupies the teaching studio as this one does. Counterpoint courses work through his chorales and fugues. Keyboard students climb from the Notebook for Anna Magdalena through the Inventions toward the Well-Tempered Clavier. Harmony textbooks quote his voice leading as the norm against which student exercises are corrected. That centrality generates a suspicion worth stating in its strongest form: the reputation may be an artifact of the syllabus. Teachers teach what can be taught, examiners examine what can be graded, and generations of musicians emerge loving the composer whose works formed their own competence, because affection for one's training disguises itself as judgment of the repertoire.

The suspicion gains support from the mechanics of advocacy. Musicians who spent adolescence inside the Inventions become the performers, critics and professors who programme, review and appoint. Their testimony about greatness is sincere and also interested, in the strict sense that their professional formation is bound up with the verdict. A comparison suggests itself with Latin in an earlier educational order, revered as the supreme language partly because every educated person had been measured against it. When a subject becomes the gate through which talent must pass, the gate acquires sanctity. Disentangling reverence for the music from reverence for the gate is the hardest methodological problem in the whole debate.

Yet the causal arrow also runs the other way, and any fair treatment must hold both directions in view. The works entered the syllabus because teachers across two centuries found no better instruments for teaching counterpoint, keyboard command and harmonic logic. Pedagogy selected the music for properties it genuinely has: clarity of procedure, inexhaustible variety within discipline, and models at every level of difficulty. His centrality in music education is both evidence of his standing and a cause of it, and the two cannot be fully separated. That sentence is not a compromise designed to offend nobody. It is a precise statement about feedback loops in cultural transmission, and it closes the question opened earlier about what the greatness claim rests on. It rests on a loop: real properties, selected by teachers, amplified by training, confirmed by the trained. A loop with genuine properties inside it is neither a fraud nor a pure measurement. It is how canons actually work.

A practical consequence follows for readers keeping their own notes on the argument. Separating score based claims from syllabus based claims in writing, with sources attached to each, prevents the two from merging into a single glow of authority; a reader who organises listening notes that way might keep them in the [offline encrypted notes app available from the VaultBook download page](https://vaultbook.net/download/), where private study records stay under the reader's own control rather than on a shared platform.

The inflation hypothesis can be tested, within limits, against cultures where the syllabus differs. Japanese musical education built its own piano culture around the same European canon and produced generations of performers for whom the keyboard works are central, alongside a devoted public for the passions under conductors such as Masaaki Suzuki. Soviet and Russian training, organised around a different ideology and a partly different repertoire hierarchy, nonetheless kept the Well-Tempered Clavier at the foundation of piano study, and Shostakovich answered it with his own cycle of preludes and fugues after hearing the work in Leipzig. Convergence across hostile or indifferent institutions weakens the theory that any single syllabus manufactured the standing. It strengthens the loop model offered in this section: properties in the scores keep getting selected independently, and each selection then amplifies the next. Loops explain persistence. They do not require conspiracies, and they do not dissolve into pure merit either.
## The argument that Bach is admired more than heard

A sharper version of the inflation charge concerns listening rather than teaching. It holds that the music is admired as an idea, praised as a monument and performed as a duty, while actual voluntary listening concentrates on a narrow band of works: the Goldberg Variations, the cello suites, the Brandenburg Concertos, the Air from the third orchestral suite, the Toccata and Fugue in D minor and a handful of keyboard pieces in transcription. The two hundred cantatas, on this charge, are revered in bulk and heard in samples. A reputation resting on a fraction of the output, the argument runs, belongs to the fraction rather than to the whole catalogue that the greatness claim cites as evidence.

The charge has a respectable pedigree in the composer's own reception. Nineteenth century revivalists themselves performed selections, arranged movements and extracted chorales for domestic use, building fame from fragments long before complete editions existed. Publishers followed the same logic, issuing albums of favourites while the cantatas waited for scholarly editions. Even the twentieth century complete recording projects, monumental as they were, sold largely to institutions and committed collectors. If the greatness claim requires acquaintance with the whole output, then almost nobody who repeats the claim satisfies its own condition, and the claim functions socially as a badge of seriousness rather than as a report of listening.

The defence answers on two levels. First, selective acquaintance is the normal condition of every large reputation: few admirers of Shakespeare have read the histories entire, and few lovers of Rembrandt could catalogue the etchings. A verdict based on the best known fifth of an output is incomplete, not worthless, provided the sample is drawn from across the range rather than from one corner. Second, the sample in this case keeps expanding under examination. Listeners who enter through the Goldberg Variations reliably proceed to the partitas, the passions and then the cantatas, and the cantata recordings of the period performance era found audiences far beyond the specialist circle that commissioned them in scholarly terms. Admiration preceding acquaintance is how most deep listening begins. The sequence is human, and condemning it proves too much against every demanding artist.

The admired more than heard charge also cuts at angles its proponents rarely notice. The same accusation, applied consistently, would convict nearly every large reputation in the arts, including the novelists whose complete works sit unopened on educated shelves and the painters known through five canvases reproduced everywhere. Fame has always run ahead of acquaintance, because fame is a social signal and acquaintance is a private labour. Singling out one composer for a universal condition suggests that the charge is really about something else: discomfort with the moral tone of the admiration, its tendency to present taste as virtue and ignorance of the cantatas as a defect of character. That discomfort is legitimate and worth keeping. Snobbery around this repertoire is real, documentable in any concert hall foyer, and it corrupts honest advocacy by giving sceptics an easy target. The remedy is to separate the music from its courtiers, a separation the music itself, heard plainly, performs without assistance.
## Evidence from concert programming and recordings

Programming data offers a check on both the inflation charge and its defence, because box office behaviour records what institutions believe audiences will attend. Orchestral seasons across Europe and North America in the decades before this article's date placed the Brandenburg Concertos, the orchestral suites and the violin concertos regularly, the passions at Easter as civic rituals in many cities, and the cantatas sparsely outside specialist series. Keyboard recitals told a friendlier story, with the Goldberg Variations functioning as a summit event pianists use to certify maturity. The pattern confirms concentration: a core of perhaps forty works carries the public reputation, exactly as the inflation charge alleges, while the long tail of the catalogue lives in festivals, university series and recordings.

Recording history complicates the picture in the defence's favour. The LP era produced multiple complete traversals of the organ works and keyboard cycles. The CD era added competing complete cantata cycles from conductors working with period instruments, most prominently the projects led by John Eliot Gardiner, Philippe Herreweghe, Ton Koopman and Masaaki Suzuki, each finding commercial release on major or specialist labels. A repertoire that nobody heard could not sustain rival complete cycles within two decades. Whatever the concert hall's caution, the recording market testified to a listening public willing to move through the catalogue in bulk, at home, at its own pace, returning to movements across years rather than applauding once and forgetting.

Both data sets can be true, and holding them together yields the balanced finding. Public concert life, constrained by rehearsal costs and subscriber habits, underrepresents the catalogue's bulk. Private listening, liberated by recording, explores it far more fully. The reputation is therefore neither simply inflated nor simply earned in the open. It is earned in private and ratified in public on the strength of a representative minority of works. Critics of the reputation should aim at the ratification process, where syllabi and seasons amplify one another. Defenders should stop pretending that the whole catalogue is common listening property. Precision about where listening happens dissolves much of the quarrel's heat.

One further body of evidence deserves weighing before the argument leaves reception for theology: the behaviour of performers choosing repertoire under no institutional compulsion. Jazz musicians from Jacques Loussier to the Modern Jazz Quartet built programmes from the inventions, fugues and chorales voluntarily, translating them into an idiom where no syllabus required them and no examination rewarded the labour. Wendy Carlos carried the Brandenburg Concertos to a mass audience through the synthesizer album Switched-On Bach in 1968, a commercial success no curriculum engineered. Guitarists and lutenists transcribe the suites and partitas because the material sustains their instruments' most serious recitals. Voluntary adoption across idioms is the closest thing reception history offers to a controlled experiment: remove duty, subsidy and examination, and observe what musicians still choose. The observation consistently returns the same repertoire, which the inflation charge must somehow accommodate.
## The faith question

Roughly two thirds of the surviving output is sacred music written for Lutheran worship: cantatas for the church year, passions for Holy Week, motets for funerals, masses and a Magnificat for festal days. That proportion forces a question no other major composer's reputation faces in the same degree. The works were not religious in decoration. They were functional theology, composed to preach, console and instruct within a confessional system whose doctrines the composer knew from inside, as cantor, as teacher of Latin school boys and as a householder whose Bible commentary, the Calov Bible with his own marginal markings, survives as documentary evidence of engaged belief.

Two broad positions organise the argument. The confessional position holds that the music's meaning is inseparable from the doctrine it serves, so that hearing the St Matthew Passion as generalised sorrow misses its specific movement from law to grace that the libretto expounds chorale by chorale. John Butt gave this position scholarly depth by connecting compositional choices to Lutheran habits of meditation and rhetoric, treating belief not as background colour but as a compositional resource. On this account, theological literacy is part of musical literacy for these works, and a listener innocent of the doctrine resembles a listener innocent of sonata form in Beethoven: capable of pleasure, barred from a layer of sense.

The universalist position answers that music exceeds its commission. The same composer set secular cantatas for coffee drinking and hunting parties with identical craft, borrowed sacred movements for secular texts and secular movements for sacred ones through the parody procedure, and thereby demonstrated that musical material migrates across doctrinal boundaries without damage. If a birthday ode can become a mass movement, the universalist argues, then the power resides in the musical thought rather than in the confession attached to it. Listeners from Mendelssohn, a baptised Lutheran of Jewish descent, to the largely secular audiences of Japanese concert halls, where the passions draw devoted followings, testify that the works communicate across every confessional line drawn around them.

Neither position should be permitted a cheap victory. The confessional reading risks making a concert ticket depend on a creed. The universalist reading risks emptying specific works of the very content their texts labour to state. The mature form of the question is not whether belief is required, but which aspects of the music each kind of listener is positioned to receive, and which aspects require study rather than faith. Study is available to anyone. That distinction moves the debate from gatekeeping to description, where evidence about scores and texts can actually be brought to bear.

The two positions also predict different concert experiences, and listeners can test the predictions directly. The confessional account predicts that following the libretto's theology in detail, chorale by chorale, will reorganise what a listener hears in the passions, turning decorative solemnity into argument. The universalist account predicts that a listener innocent of the doctrine but attentive to voices and drama will receive the work's essential communication intact. Both predictions come true in practice for different listeners, and sometimes for the same listener on different evenings, which suggests that the works support more than one complete mode of attention. Music of narrower design rarely survives that test. A cantata that rewards theological study, theatrical attention and pure contrapuntal inspection simultaneously is exhibiting a property worth naming plainly: layered address. Layered address, rather than any creed test, is what the faith debate keeps circling, and naming it lets both camps retire their least defensible claims.
## Can a non-believer fully hear this music

### Do you need to be religious to appreciate Bach?

No creed is required for appreciation, since the scores communicate through craft, drama and vocal expression that cross confessional lines. Theological literacy does add a layer of meaning in the cantatas and passions, and that layer is gained by study rather than by belief.

The word fully carries the whole difficulty. A non-believer can follow every note of the St John Passion, trace its chorale placements, feel the crowd choruses' violence and the arias' introspection, and weep at the final chorus. What such a listener does not do is consent to the work's own claim about what those events mean. The passion asserts that a particular death redeems the world. Hearing the music while declining the assertion produces a real and coherent experience, an aesthetic and human one, that differs from the experience the work was built to produce inside worship. Both experiences are genuine. Calling one of them complete and the other deficient requires a theory of what completeness means, and theories of completeness are where this argument properly lives.

Historical evidence cuts in both directions and should be reported evenly. Albert Schweitzer, theologian and organist, heard the cantatas as sermons in sound and built his interpretation on the composer's pictorial responses to sacred words. His reading demonstrates what doctrinal attention unlocks. Against it stands the documented devotion of performers and listeners outside the confession, including the Japanese Bach Collegium culture under Masaaki Suzuki, a Christian conductor working for audiences largely formed by other traditions, where attendance and recording sales testify to profound engagement without Lutheran formation. Engagement is measurable in behaviour: return visits, study, performance. By behavioural measures the non-believing audience passes every test except assent itself.

A workable settlement distinguishes three layers. Craft is audible to any attentive listener. Drama is audible to any listener who follows the words in translation. Doctrine is audible as structure to a student and as truth only to a believer. On that layered account the honest answer to the section's question is that a non-believer can hear everything the music does and can understand everything it says, while standing outside the one act, assent, that the music finally requests. Whether standing outside assent counts as missing the music or as hearing it with adult independence is a judgment each listener must make without borrowing authority from either camp.

Performers divide on the question in ways that illuminate it better than manifestos do. Singers of the evangelist roles describe the labour as preaching under musical discipline, responsible for a text whose claims they may or may not share, and the best accounts of that labour treat the tension itself as part of the performance's honesty. Conductors of period ensembles include committed believers and declared sceptics, sometimes in the same recording cycle across different works, without audible damage to either. What no serious practitioner defends is ignorance: the position that the words are packaging and only the notes are real fails every rehearsal test, because phrasing, tempo and balance in this repertoire are decisions about sentences before they are decisions about sound. Belief remains optional. Literacy does not. That asymmetry, established in practice by people with no theory to sell, is the nearest thing to a settlement the faith question has produced.
## What the scores themselves record about intent

Intent leaves traces, and in this case the traces are unusually concrete. The initials SDG, for Soli Deo Gloria, appear on sacred manuscripts, dedicating the work to the glory of God alone. The companion initials JJ, for Jesu juva, Jesus help, head many scores as a prayer before labour. The title page of the Orgelbuchlein announces a double purpose in plain words: instruction for beginning organists in performing a chorale in many ways, and honour to God with instruction of one's neighbour. The Well-Tempered Clavier's title page likewise joins utility and delight for musical youth desirous of learning. Dedication and pedagogy share the page without embarrassment. The composer evidently saw no border between serving God, teaching neighbours and pleasing listeners, and modern attempts to separate those motives impose a map the documents do not draw.

The Calov Bible, rediscovered in the twentieth century and preserved with marginal underlinings and comments in the composer's hand, shows a reader marking passages on music's role in worship and on diligence in one's calling. Christoph Wolff treated those markings as key evidence of a working theology joined to a working musician's pride in craft. The document resists two simplifications at once. It refutes the portrait of a craftsman indifferent to doctrine, since the markings engage sermons and commentary in detail. It equally refutes the portrait of a mystic indifferent to career, since the same archive contains salary petitions, disputes with employers and meticulous professional self defence. Belief and ambition shared one desk.

Intent, however documented, does not settle reception. A score can record its maker's purposes without binding later listeners to them, as the parody procedure inside the output itself demonstrates: the composer reassigned music between sacred and secular texts whenever a commission required it, treating his own intentions as revisable by occasion. That professional flexibility is the strongest internal evidence against any doctrine of fixed meaning. The manuscripts record a Lutheran cantor's purposes in full. The music's subsequent career, blessed by its maker's own habits of reuse, escaped those purposes almost immediately. Both facts belong in the record, and the faith debate stays honest only while both remain in view.

The dedications also speak in a second direction that advocates of the devotional reading sometimes underplay. The same title pages that honour God advertise the product with a tradesman's precision, naming the intended buyers, the skills taught and the pleasures promised, because printed music was merchandise and the composer, who engraved and sold copies of the Clavier-Uebung parts from his own house, understood his market. Piety and commerce share the title pages as comfortably as belief and ambition shared the desk. Later centuries separated those motives into rival portraits, the saint of the organ loft and the professional of the Leipzig book trade, and then argued about which portrait was genuine. The documents refuse the separation. Any account of intent that cannot hold both the prayer and the price list in one frame has edited the evidence before interpreting it.
## The attribution problem across the catalogue

A reputation built partly on bulk invites an awkward audit. Could any of the famous works turn out not to be his? The catalogue, assembled by Wolfgang Schmieder and published in 1950 as the Bach Werke Verzeichnis, runs to more than a thousand entries, and its appendix of doubtful and spurious works has grown across editions as scholarship tested attributions that earlier centuries accepted on trust. A meaningful portion of the catalogue's attribution is uncertain, including one of the most famous pieces bearing his name. That sentence should not scandalise anyone. Attribution uncertainty is the normal condition of early music, where works circulated in copies, title pages were added by sellers and pupils, and a famous name raised a manuscript's price.

The mechanisms of error are well documented. Copyists attributed pieces to the master of the household whose papers they handled. Publishers in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries attached the celebrated surname to anonymous keyboard pieces because it sold copies. Pupils' compositions survived among the teacher's papers and were absorbed into the catalogue by hopeful editors. Family transmission blurred generations, since works by Wilhelm Friedemann and Carl Philipp Emanuel, and by cousins and uncles across the sprawling musical clan, travelled in the same bundles. Modern scholarship disentangles such bundles with handwriting analysis, paper and watermark studies, stylistic comparison and documentary cross checking, and its verdicts revise the canon at the margins every decade.

Scale matters for proportion. The core of the reputation, the passions, the B minor Mass, the Brandenburg Concertos, the Well-Tempered Clavier, the Goldberg Variations and the great organ cycles, rests on autographs or on transmission too strong to shake. The uncertainty gathers at the catalogue's edges: short keyboard pieces, single arias, chamber works preserved in one copy, and pieces whose style sits oddly against the documented habits. A sceptic who hopes attribution research will dissolve the greatness claim will be disappointed, because the load bearing works are the secure ones. A defender who claims the whole thousand plus entries as certified property overstates the record. The truth has a shape: a granite centre, a contested perimeter.

The audit habit this section recommends generalises beyond one catalogue. Every pre-modern repertoire carries a penumbra of hopeful attributions: symphonies assigned to Haydn by copyists that scholarship has since redistributed among contemporaries, concertos travelling under Vivaldi's name on a publisher's optimism, madrigals gathered into famous collected editions on slender evidence. The discipline's tools improve steadily, and each generation's appendix grows more honest than the last. What distinguishes the present case is the ratio between centre and penumbra and the quality of the centre's documentation. A sceptic is entitled to press the penumbra hard. Pressing it tells the court a great deal about transmission and very little about the value of the Passacaglia, the passions or the Goldberg Variations, whose papers are in order. Debates that blur that distinction are trading on a confusion between archival science and aesthetic judgment, and the confusion serves polemic rather than knowledge.
## The disputed organ works

The organ repertoire illustrates the perimeter vividly, because its most celebrated item is also its most contested. The Toccata and Fugue in D minor, BWV 565, the piece that opens horror films and organ recitals alike, survives in a copy by the organist Johannes Ringk rather than in an autograph, and its style has troubled analysts for generations. Its octave doublings, its primitive fugal answers and its violin like figuration prompted scholars including Peter Williams to propose an origin as a transcription of a lost string work, possibly by another hand, or as a youthful experiment whose oddities later fame magnified beyond its documentary worth. No replacement attribution commands consensus. The work hangs in a scholarly limbo that concert life ignores completely.

Other organ attributions have fallen outright. The eight short preludes and fugues, BWV 553 to 560, long printed as youthful works, are generally assigned by modern scholarship to a pupil, possibly Johann Ludwig Krebs or Johann Tobias Krebs, whose studies with the master explain both the stylistic resemblance and the transmission. Several chorale settings once cherished in the Orgelbuchlein's wider orbit have migrated to the appendix. Each migration matters beyond its minutes of music, because it demonstrates the discipline working as it should: claims surrendered when paper evidence and stylistic tests turn against them, without damage to the reputation's core. A canon that can expel members is healthier than one that defends every entry.

The organ disputes also expose an uncomfortable asymmetry in public argument. Defenders of the reputation rarely mention BWV 565's status; attackers rarely mention that the Passacaglia in C minor, the great preludes and fugues of the Leipzig years and the trio sonatas stand beyond dispute. Selective silence is the tell of advocacy. The scholarly literature, by contrast, holds both facts simultaneously and grades confidence piece by piece. Readers of this debate should import that habit wholesale. Attribution is not a verdict on a composer. It is a filing system with confidence levels, and the confidence levels in this catalogue are published, argued over and revised in the open.

The D minor Toccata concentrates every difficulty of the organ evidence into nine minutes of music. Its transmission is thin, its style is double natured, part organ rhetoric and part string figuration, and its fame is entirely out of proportion to its documentation, which inverts the normal relationship between evidence and certainty. Organists cannot retire it, since audiences expect it and students demand it, so the profession lives with a divided practice: the piece is performed as a signature work and footnoted as a doubtful one, in the same programme books that print both facts without apparent embarrassment. That divided practice is actually a model of how to handle disputed attribution in public. Print the work, print the doubt, let neither cancel the other. The alternative habits, silent certainty and silent omission, both falsify the record, and the organ literature's long negotiation with BWV 565 shows a repertoire learning to carry uncertainty in the open.
## The Anna Magdalena authorship claim

### Did Anna Magdalena Bach write the cello suites?

No, on the consensus of scholarship that has examined the claim. The suites survive in a copy made by Anna Magdalena Bach in her role as the composer's copyist, and copying in her hand is evidence of transmission rather than of authorship.

The claim itself deserves accurate statement before judgment. Martin Jarvis, an Australian music scholar, argued from handwriting analysis of the surviving copy of the cello suites that Anna Magdalena Bach had composed the works, presenting his case at conferences from 2006 onward and in subsequent publications and film. His argument treated features of the manuscript, including corrections and the character of the script, as signs of a composing hand rather than a copying one, and situated the proposal inside a broader recovery of women's suppressed contributions to musical history. Stated at its strongest, the claim asked why a copy by a trained singer and professional copyist in the household should be presumed mechanical, and demanded that the burden of proof be shared rather than assigned entirely to the challenger.

The biographical setting gives the question its surface plausibility. Anna Magdalena, born Wilcke, was a professional court singer at Cothen who married the composer in 1721, ran a household of extraordinary musical density, copied parts for performance under deadline pressure and maintained the family notebooks through which much domestic repertoire survives. Her musicianship is documented and was considerable. A twentieth century claim that Anna Magdalena Bach composed the cello suites was argued from handwriting analysis and is rejected by the scholarly consensus, but it drew attention to how little is independently documented about the suites' origin. No autograph of the suites survives. The earliest sources are copies, hers among them, alongside a copy by Johann Peter Kellner. Where autographs vanish, speculation enters through the gap, and the gap in this famous case is real.

The reception of the Jarvis argument also belongs in the record, because it tested the discipline's fairness under pressure. Some responses engaged the handwriting evidence point by point and explained the copyist's habits it had misread. Others reached for ridicule, treating the claimant's discipline of forensic document examination and his base outside the German scholarly establishment as though either settled the musical question. The first kind of response is the only kind this article endorses, and it is also the only kind that persuades uncommitted readers. A consensus that cannot state a challenger's evidence before answering it has stopped arguing and started guarding. The consensus in this case did state the evidence, in journal exchanges and conference replies, and answered it on the manuscripts' own terms. That process, more than the verdict alone, is why the rejection deserves the confidence this article gives it.
## Why that claim is rejected and what it got right

The rejection rests on the distinction between composing and copying hands, a distinction palaeography makes daily in this repertoire. A copyist's manuscript shows corrections of copying errors: misread notes repaired, omitted bars restored at page turns, clefs adjusted to the exemplar. A composer's manuscript shows revisions of invention: passages recomposed, endings replaced, material redistributed. Specialists who examined the Anna Magdalena copy, including the cello scholar community that responded to Jarvis in print, identified the first pattern and not the second. The suites also cohere stylistically with the composer's other string writing of the Cothen years, the sonatas and partitas for solo violin above all, in figuration, in implied polyphony and in the architecture of dance movements. Stylistic continuity across a documented group is evidence, and in a case without autographs it is the strongest evidence available.

Documentary silence completes the rejection. A claim of this size, that the most celebrated cello works ever written were composed by the copyist credited on no title page, requires some trace beyond the contested manuscript: a payment record, a letter, a contemporary reference, an entry in the catalogues compiled by the sons who promoted their father's legacy and had every incentive to record family achievements. No such trace has been produced. The scholarly consensus therefore holds, and this article states it without qualification: Anna Magdalena Bach did not compose the cello suites on any evidence that survives scrutiny. Representing the claim fairly, as the preceding section did, is compatible with rejecting it plainly. Fairness concerns how an argument is heard, not whether it wins.

What the claim got right deserves equal plainness. It forced attention onto copyists as skilled musicians whose labour the catalogue renders invisible, and onto the evidential poverty surrounding works that fame treats as bedrock. It also exposed how quickly documentary gaps around women in musical households were filled with assumptions of passivity. Those corrections survive the claim's failure and improve the discipline. Scholarship advances partly through strong claims that fail on evidence while succeeding as audits of complacency. Filing the Jarvis episode under mere error wastes its uses. Filing it under suppressed truth wastes the evidence. The accurate file is the first one: a rejected claim that performed a genuine service on its way down.

One distinction protects future discussion of this episode from repeating its errors. Rejecting the claim that Anna Magdalena composed the suites is not equivalent to rating her musicianship low, and conflating the two has poisoned commentary in both directions. The documentary record supports a highly trained singer, a copyist trusted with the most complex scores in the household's economy, and a teacher within the family's musical enterprise. Those are substantial professional identities in a century that granted women few of them. Her husband's century also granted her no plausible path to signed publication as a composer, which is exactly why claims on her behalf attract sympathy. Sympathy, however, is not a source. The accurate tribute to her labour is to describe it precisely as the record shows it, indispensable and documented, rather than to promote it into a different labour the record does not show.
## Forgery, misattribution and the catalogue's edges

Beyond honest error lies a murkier territory. The eighteenth century music trade rewarded famous names, and pieces were dressed in them deliberately. Publishers issued keyboard works under the Bach surname that the family never wrote. Later, the Minuet in G major from the Notebook for Anna Magdalena, perhaps the most widely played beginner's piece on earth, turned out to be by Christian Petzold, a Dresden organist whose name fame discarded while retaining his tune. The St Luke Passion, BWV 246, performed and recorded under the great name for generations, is accepted by scholarship as a work the composer may have arranged or performed but did not compose. Each case refines the same lesson: the surname functioned as a brand long before the catalogue functioned as a filter.

The edges of the catalogue are where reputations should be audited, because edges reveal method. Schmieder's appendix of doubtful works is not an embarrassment to be hidden from admirers. It is the discipline's conscience in printed form, a standing admission that certainty has degrees. Works move between the main list and the appendix as evidence shifts, and the traffic in both directions across the 1950 catalogue and its 1990 revision demonstrates a system that corrects itself in public. Few other arts maintain so transparent a ledger of their own uncertainty. The catalogue's honesty about its perimeter is therefore evidence for the reliability of its centre, not against it.

This closes the attribution question in its disciplined form. Could famous works turn out not to be his? At the perimeter, yes, and some already have, including pieces generations of beginners and concertgoers loved under his name. At the centre, the documentary chain is of a different order: autograph scores, council records, contemporary copies by named pupils, and a family transmission that scholars have cross examined for two centuries. A debate that keeps centre and perimeter distinct can absorb any plausible future revision without drama. A debate that treats the catalogue as one undifferentiated block will lurch between credulity and panic with every journal article, which is a description of much popular writing on the subject.

Forgery in the strict sense, a work manufactured to deceive, is rarer in this catalogue than hopeful misattribution, and the distinction matters for judgment. A publisher who dressed an anonymous sonata in a famous surname committed a commercial sin against buyers, not an artistic one against the composer, and the piece itself stands or falls as music once its papers are corrected. The Petzold minuet did not become worse music on the day its author was identified, though millions of childhood lessons quietly changed their meaning. What the edges of the catalogue finally teach is narrative humility. Fame assigned the credit, the market multiplied it, and scholarship, working slowly with paper, watermarks and handwriting, repossessed it piece by piece. The centre held throughout that repossession, which is the single most reassuring fact available to anyone who wants the greatness debate conducted on evidence rather than on loyalty.
## The period performance quarrel as a question of authority

How the music should sound became, across the second half of the twentieth century, a quarrel about who holds authority over a text. The period performance movement proposed that authority lies with historical evidence: instruments of the types the composer knew, pitch standards near a semitone below the modern norm, temperaments that colour keys unequally, small choral forces in which each line is sung by one voice, and articulation drawn from treatises of the period. Its pioneers argued that Romantic habits, massed choirs, steel strung instruments and continuous vibrato, had buried the scores under anachronism. The movement's recordings of the cantatas and passions, from Nikolaus Harnoncourt and Gustav Leonhardt onward, changed what audiences expect the music to feel like: leaner, faster, more rhetorical, more dangerous.

The counter argument, in its strongest form, holds that the movement's claim to recovered authenticity exceeds its evidence and conceals modern taste wearing historical costume. Richard Taruskin, the most formidable critic writing in this territory, argued across essays collected in Text and Act that period performance tells us about twentieth century preferences for clarity, speed and emotional restraint at least as much as about eighteenth century practice. His charge was not that the research was worthless. It was that the rhetoric of authenticity converted debatable choices into moral obligations and marketed a preference as a restoration. On Taruskin's account, every generation performs the past in its own image, and honesty consists in admitting the transaction.

A middle position, occupied by many working performers, treats historical evidence as a discipline rather than a destination. Treatises constrain tempo and articulation; surviving instruments constrain sonority; payment records constrain ensemble size. Within those constraints, musicians make choices as musicians in every era have done, answering to the score, the room and the audience in front of them. Readers who wish to compare the contending approaches side by side on screen, movement by movement and choir by choir, can assemble the rival versions in the [browser-based presentation viewer at ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), laying the competing claims out visually before judging which authority persuades. The quarrel, properly understood, was never only about gut strings. It was about whether evidence or inheritance governs interpretation, and that question outlives any particular recording.

The authority question also has an economic anatomy that pure aesthetics misses. Period performance created institutions: specialist ensembles, festival circuits, recording contracts and conservatory departments whose livelihoods depend on the historical claim retaining its force. Mainstream orchestras and opera houses hold symmetrical investments in the inherited manner, in instrument collections, training pipelines and subscriber expectations. Neither investment proves its side insincere. Musicians overwhelmingly believe the doctrines their formation taught them, as musicians have in every century. The investments do explain the quarrel's temperature and its longevity, since a dispute about evidence that is also a dispute about livelihoods will be argued harder and settled slower than the evidence alone warrants. Readers weighing a particularly absolute claim from either camp are entitled to ask which institution pays the claimant, not to dismiss the claim, but to price its certainty correctly.
## Who decides what the text means

Beneath the quarrel over instruments sits a constitutional question for all performed art. A score underdetermines its performance: it fixes pitches and rhythms within conventions while leaving tempo, accent, phrasing, ornament realisation and emotional temperature to be decided in the act. Somebody decides. Candidates for the deciding office include the composer's documented intentions, the conventions of the composer's milieu, the performer's judgment, the scholarly editor's choices and the audience's accumulated expectations. Each candidate has a constituency. Each constituency can cite genuine goods that its candidate protects: fidelity, intelligibility, vitality, responsibility, pleasure.

The documentary record complicates every candidacy. Intentions survive partially, in treatises by Quantz and by Carl Philipp Emanuel, in payment records listing ensemble sizes, in a famous memorandum in which the composer specified the choir and orchestra he considered necessary for well ordered church music. That 1730 memorandum, addressed to the Leipzig council, is precious evidence about forces and standards. It says nothing directly about tempo in a given aria or accent in a given chorus, because those matters belonged to a shared professional culture that nobody needed to write down. The unwritten culture is exactly what two and a half centuries have eroded. Claims to recover it wholesale therefore overreach, and Taruskin's critique, introduced in the preceding section, retains its force wherever a performer presents inference as inheritance.

Performer judgment, the surviving candidate, carries its own risks, chiefly arbitrariness dressed as inspiration. The healthiest settlement visible in practice by the first decade of the twenty first century was a kind of constitutional monarchy: evidence reigns, judgment governs. Editions present the text with its variants visible. Performers state their forces, pitch and sources. Critics compare rival realisations against the evidence rather than against a single orthodoxy. Audiences, presented with meaningfully different versions of the same cantata, learn the deepest lesson the quarrel teaches: that a masterpiece is not a sound but a field of possible sounds, bounded by a text and by history, and inexhaustible inside those bounds. Authority, distributed in that way, belongs to no office and is exercised by all of them under mutual scrutiny.

Editors deserve a place in this constitutional account, because their decisions reach performers before any treatise does. The Bach Gesellschaft editors of the nineteenth century chose readings, regularised notation and occasionally completed fragments according to the taste of their time, and generations of performers obeyed those pages as though they were the composer unmediated. Modern critical editions expose their working, printing variants and source descriptions that let a performer audit the editor. That exposure changed the politics of the text permanently. Authority that must show its papers behaves differently from authority that merely announces itself, and the whole period performance quarrel can be retold as the slow transfer of the text from announced authority to audited authority. The transfer is incomplete wherever a famous recording's choices are imitated as gospel by students who never open an edition, which is to say it is incomplete everywhere, permanently, and the incompleteness is not a scandal but the normal condition of performed art.
## Transcription and whether it violates anything

Few practices test the idea of textual authority like transcription, and few composers have been transcribed as relentlessly. The output itself began the habit: the composer arranged Vivaldi concertos for keyboard, reworked his own violin concerto as a harpsichord concerto, and transferred movements between cantatas with a pragmatism that treated musical substance as portable across instruments and occasions. Successors extended the licence. Liszt arranged organ works for piano. Busoni built a performing career partly on keyboard transcriptions of the organ and violin literature. Stokowski orchestrated the D minor Toccata and Fugue, in the version through which film audiences met it in Fantasia. Guitarists, marimba players and saxophone quartets have followed, each migration raising the same question in a new accent: does moving the notes violate anything essential?

The violation charge assumes that a work's identity includes its original sonority, so that a chorale prelude on marimba is a description of the piece rather than the piece itself. The assumption has philosophical support, since timbre shapes perception of structure, and it has practical support wherever balance between voices depends on instrumental colour. Yet the composer's own practice blocks any purist verdict issued in his name. A musician who recast a violin concerto for harpsichord and choir cantata movements for a mass accepted the portability principle at the highest level of his art. Defending the scores against transcription by appealing to their maker's intentions therefore fails on the documentary record, which is the one court where the appeal could have won.

A better test asks what a given transcription reveals or conceals. Piano versions of the organ works reveal contrapuntal design to listeners with no access to an organ loft, while concealing registration colour. Stokowski's orchestration reveals rhetorical grandeur while concealing the original's registration logic and, in the D minor case, wrapping a disputed work in symphonic glamour its sources never claimed. Judged case by case under a reveal and conceal standard, transcription becomes a branch of criticism rather than a category of offence. Each arrangement is an argument about what the work essentially is, and arguments can be answered. That framing returns the practice to the debate's main current: criteria chosen, defended and tested, with no appeal to a purity the historical record does not contain.

The transcription question returns a final dividend to the main argument, because it separates two kinds of value that rankings habitually merge. A work whose excellence survives radical changes of sonority, language of performance and cultural setting is exhibiting portability, a property closer to the structural criterion's home ground. A work whose excellence evaporates outside its original medium, a stage effect, a singer's unique timbre, a dance floor's physics, may be no less excellent and far less portable. Supremacy claims quietly assume portability is the senior property, since portable works accumulate the performances, editions and syllabi from which reputations are built. The assumption deserves the same cross examination as every other weighting in this debate. A culture is entitled to prize the unportable, the effect that happens once in one room, and some of the most intense musical experiences on record are of exactly that kind. Transcription practice simply makes the hidden weighting visible.
## The charge of mathematical coldness

### Is Bach's music cold or emotional?

The scores answer emotional, on the evidence of the passions, the cantatas and the great minor key keyboard works, where harmonic and rhetorical choices serve grief, penitence and consolation directly. The coldness charge reflects analytical habits of description rather than the sound itself.

The charge has a history that explains its persistence. Nineteenth and twentieth century pedagogy presented the music through its procedures: fugue subjects, canonic devices, inversion, augmentation and the arithmetic of the Goldberg canons at expanding intervals. Textbooks needed describable features, and procedure is describable in a way that grief is not. Generations of students therefore met a composer made of mechanisms before they met the cantatas' arias of penitence or the passions' crowd scenes. The Goldberg Variations' ground bass became a diagram. The Musical Offering became a puzzle book. A reputation for mathematics accumulated in the classroom and then walked out into the concert hall, where listeners primed for machinery listened for machinery and, predictably, heard it.

Perception research of a homelier kind supports the priming account. Listeners told that a performance is a computer realisation reliably rate it colder than the identical performance attributed to a human. Expectation edits experience. A composer branded mathematical suffers that edit at scale, across a whole culture of programme notes and examination syllabuses. The counter evidence has to be experiential rather than argumentative, which is why this section of the debate so often stalls: one side cites the scores' construction, which is public and checkable, while the other side cites performances that undid the charge in a room, which are private and unrepeatable. Recognising the asymmetry of the evidence explains the quarrel's longevity without awarding it to either party.

The coldness charge also varies by instrument in a pattern that supports the priming account. On the organ, an instrument whose sound is produced at a mechanical distance from the player's body and whose literature arrives wrapped in counterpoint, the charge flourishes. In the voice, where text and breath force expression into the open, it withers; nobody has ever built a career calling the alto arias cold. Pianists divide the difference, some cultivating architectural clarity as an ethic and others, following Gould's example of singing eccentricity or the Romantic tradition of Busoni's pupils, treating the keyboard works as confession. A property of the music itself should not track the performing medium so obediently. A property of the music's presentation should, and does. That single observation does more work against the coldness charge than any anthology of passionate quotations, because it relocates the phenomenon from the scores to the interface where listeners actually meet them.
## The counter-case from the vocal works

Against the coldness charge, the vocal works constitute a standing refutation performed weekly somewhere in the world. The cantata Ich habe genug builds an entire evening of the soul from a bass voice, an oboe and strings, moving from weariness with life to a final aria of brisk readiness that no listener has ever described as arithmetic. The aria Aus Liebe will mein Heiland sterben in the St Matthew Passion suspends its line over an accompaniment without bass foundation, a sonority of deliberate fragility that analysts praise and congregations simply feel in the throat. The double chorus writing of the motet Singet dem Herrn partitions joy between two choirs tossing phrases like light between windows. Evidence of this character does not argue. It accumulates, performance by performance, until the charge begins to sound like a report about the charger's listening history.

Rhetoric, the period's own science of moving listeners, supplies the analytical bridge between construction and feeling. Lutheran musical culture inherited figures from oratory: musical phrases designed as exclamations, questions, sighs and climaxes, catalogued in treatises and deployed by cantors as preachers deploy them. John Butt's scholarship on this territory showed how deeply the composer's choices engage that rhetorical inheritance, down to the placement of a chorale phrase as a sermon's application. On that showing, the famous construction is not an alternative to expression. Construction is the delivery system expression used. A fugue can be a machine for thinking and an instrument of consolation in the same bars, and usually, in the church works, it is both at once.

The counter-case should nonetheless concede the charge's one true observation. Some works, especially the late speculative cycles, address the mind before the senses and withhold immediate warmth as a matter of design. The Art of Fugue does not woo. The canonic variations on Vom Himmel hoch present doctrine as procedure. A listener who begins there and generalises has made an error of sampling, not an error of perception, and the remedy is repertoire guidance rather than polemic. Heard across its full width, from wedding cantatas to the B minor Mass, the output is among the most emotionally various in the Western record. The coldness charge survives as a warning about entry points, and in that reduced and useful form it deserves to survive.

The vocal counter-case also answers a subtler version of the charge, which grants the passions their pathos but calls the instrumental majority sterile by comparison. The instrumental works answer in their own dialect. The Chaconne from the D minor violin Partita sustains a quarter of an hour of accumulating intensity on four strings, through variations whose emotional trajectory performers describe in the vocabulary of ordeal and arrival. The slow movements of the Brandenburg Concertos, the sarabandes of the cello suites and the aria of the Goldberg Variations all operate in the same register of suspended, singing introspection. Listeners who require words before they will grant feeling are describing a preference about evidence, not a discovery about the music. The instrumental case rests on whether organised sound without text can carry feeling, a question settled affirmatively by every dance floor and film score in the culture surrounding the debate.
## Reputation across three centuries

The reputation's career falls into three broad acts, and readers who want the underlying biography narrated in full, from Eisenach through Weimar and Cothen to the Leipzig decades, will find it in the companion account of [Bach's life and career](/2010/08/07/johann-sebastian-bach-life-and-career/), which this argument deliberately compresses. The first act runs from 1750 to the 1820s: neglect in public, preservation in private. Manuscripts passed through the family to pupils and collectors, the keyboard works circulated among connoisseurs, and Forkel's biography of 1802, built on information from the sons, presented its subject as a national patrimony awaiting recovery. The second act is the nineteenth century revival: Mendelssohn's 1829 Berlin performance of the St Matthew Passion, the founding of the Bach Gesellschaft in 1850 and its complete edition across half a century, and the absorption of the fugues into every serious pianist's formation. The third act is the twentieth century's double movement: monumental Romantic performance on one side, the period performance correction on the other, with the complete recording era arbitrating between them.

Each act rebuilt the figure it needed. The nineteenth century wanted a national classic and a Protestant monument, and edited, performed and framed the music accordingly. The early twentieth century wanted a master builder for an age of systems, and produced the architect of the textbooks. The period movement wanted a rhetorician and man of the theatre, leaner and stranger than the monument. Nicholas Kenyon's editorial work on the reception documents that sequence of reinventions with unusual clarity, and the sequence itself is data for the debate: a reputation that survives three incompatible reconstructions is either exceptionally substantial or exceptionally convenient as a screen for projection. Both explanations have defenders, and the evidence of the scores is summoned by each.

Set beside the parallel stories gathered under [musicians ignored in their own time](/2026/04/07/musicians-ignored-in-their-own-time/), the case is instructive precisely because it refuses the simple pattern. This composer was not ignored by his peers, who elected him to learned societies and sought his judgment on organs and organists across Germany. He was honoured as a craftsman and neglected as a public composer, a distinction the rags to reverence storyline usually flattens. His posthumous rise therefore teaches a subtler lesson about reputations: that professional esteem and public fame are separate currencies, that a fortune in one can sit in a vault for generations, and that conversion between them depends on editors, performers and institutions whose names belong in any honest history of the music's standing.

The third act's arbitrating technology deserves a word of its own, because recording changed the reputation's evidentiary basis. Before recording, standing rested on testimony: critics reported, editors printed, teachers assigned, and most admirers believed on credit. The complete recording projects converted credit into inspection. A listener in Osaka or Ohio could test the cantatas claim in a winter of evenings, pause, compare and return, building the kind of first hand acquaintance that earlier centuries reserved for professionals in a handful of cities. Democratised inspection did not end the debate; it changed who may enter it and with what evidence, weakening the courtiers and strengthening the sceptics who do their listening. If the reputation has survived that exposure with its centre intact, the survival counts among the strongest reception facts available, stronger than any single critic's verdict in any single century.
## What would have to be true for the greatest claim to fail

Structural closing requires precision about defeat conditions. For the greatest claim to fail, rather than merely to be doubted, several things would have to be true together. The structural criterion would have to be demoted, shown to measure a narrower good than its advocates claim. The consistency ledger would have to shrink under attribution audit, with load bearing works migrating to the doubtful appendix. The influence record would have to be reinterpreted as pedagogy's echo rather than practitioners' free choice. And a rival would have to consolidate the alternative criteria, melody, drama and immediacy, into a single candidacy that commands the allegiance structural command once commanded. Each condition is conceivable. Their conjunction is the claim's true exposure, and stating the conjunction fairly is more useful to sceptics than any amount of generalised doubt.

The debate's main positions can be set side by side for inspection.

| Claim | Strongest evidence for | Strongest evidence against | What would settle it | Current state of the argument |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Structural command makes him the greatest composer | Contrapuntal mastery sustained across the Art of Fugue, the B minor Mass and hundreds of cantatas | The criterion is chosen rather than discovered, and it favours written, large scale, analysis friendly music | An agreed weighting of musical values across criteria, accepted by rival camps | Open: the criterion commands professional assent and public dissent in roughly stable proportions |
| He was a conservative who completed an older art | Inherited genres, institutionally set tasks and a specialist posthumous audience | Harmonic and formal experiment inside the inherited genres exceeds contemporary practice | A shared definition of radicalism covering form, content and career conduct | Open: both readings cite identical evidence and differ on definitions |
| The reputation is inflated by pedagogy | Syllabus centrality forms the judges who then confirm the verdict | The syllabus selected the works for demonstrable teaching properties across two centuries | A comparison of standing among musicians formed outside the syllabus tradition | Partly testable: esteem persists across cultures with different curricula, weakening the pure form of the charge |
| Anna Magdalena composed the cello suites | Her copy is the earliest substantial source and no autograph survives | The manuscript shows a copyist's corrections, and no documentary trace supports her authorship | An autograph, a payment record or a contemporary attribution in her name | Settled against the claim by scholarly consensus, with the evidential gaps it exposed acknowledged |
| The D minor Toccata and Fugue is securely his | Unbroken performance tradition under his name since the nineteenth century revival | The sole early source is a pupil circle copy, and the style sits oddly against documented habits | A newly surfaced autograph or an independently dated contemporary source | Open: performed as his everywhere, filed as doubtful by scholarship |

The table's discipline is the point. Claims with different evidential standings are usually debated at one temperature, as though the cello suites question and the criterion question were the same kind of dispute. They are not. One is documentary and has been answered as firmly as documentary questions get answered. The other is evaluative and cannot be answered by documents at all. Half the confusion in popular argument about this composer comes from smuggling evaluative claims into documentary dress, or the reverse. Readers who keep the two currencies separate will find most published controversy on the subject quietly resolving itself into its components.

The defeat conditions and the holding conditions, stated in this and the following section, also expose an asymmetry worth naming. The claim fails by conjunction: several independent supports must give way together. It holds, under the structural interpretation, by accumulation: support after support arriving from independent directions, documents, practitioners, rival traditions and hostile institutions. Asymmetric structures of that kind are common in mature inquiries and rare in slogans. They explain why the debate frustrates partisans of both extremes. The sceptic must win everywhere at once. The advocate must merely keep winning somewhere. Neither position licenses complacency, since conjunctions do occur in history and accumulations do reverse, but anyone entering the argument deserves the true shape of the task rather than the fiction of a single decisive blow that either side could land.
## What would have to be true for it to hold

Symmetry demands the opposing specification. For the greatest claim to hold in a form stronger than professional courtesy, structural command would have to be defended successfully as the governing criterion, not merely asserted as the profession's habit. That defence would need to show that architecture in music does the work that melody and drama advocates claim for their criteria: that it produces, across repeated hearings and across cultures, the deepest and most durable responses listeners report. It would need the attribution audit to keep sparing the centre of the catalogue, as it has done so far. It would need the influence record, examined name by name in the companion study of [Bach's influence on music](/2010/09/21/johann-sebastian-bach-influence-on-music/), to keep showing practitioners choosing the works freely, from Mozart's Viennese fugue studies to the jazz musicians who harmonised their improvisations through his chorales, rather than merely inheriting them from teachers.

It would also need an answer to the diversity objection that does not cheat. The claim's strongest form covers many genres, yet opera, the era's dominant public genre, is missing entirely, and the orchestral repertoire is slender beside Haydn or Handel. Defenders answer that command within attempted genres, weighted by difficulty and consistency, outweighs coverage of every genre on the shelf. That answer is coherent. It is also, once again, a weighting decision, and its coherence does not convert it into a fact. A claim that holds only under a stack of defensible weightings holds in the way a legal judgment holds under a stated interpretation: firmly, within the interpretation, and open to appeal under another. Much confusion would end if advocates printed the interpretation on the label.

The wider ranking exercise, surveyed in the series overview of the [greatest composers of all time](/2025/04/07/greatest-composers-of-all-time/), displays the same architecture wherever it is attempted: a criterion selected, a candidate crowned, a controversy renewed at the next selection. Recognising the architecture does not make rankings worthless. It makes them legible. A ranking tells a reader exactly which values a musical culture rewards, which is precious evidence about the culture, and suggestive, though never conclusive, evidence about the music. On that understanding the claim for this composer holds as the most defensible ranking available under the values that professional musical culture has, in practice, rewarded for two centuries. Anyone who finds the claim unsatisfying has been given, by the argument itself, the correct address for the dissatisfaction: the values, not the notes.

A last holding condition concerns the testimony of composers, the witnesses with the most at stake in their own rankings. Across three centuries, practitioners who might have been expected to resent a predecessor's shadow instead studied the scores with a persistence that amounts to a professional plebiscite: Mozart arranging fugues for Baron van Swieten's circle, Beethoven directing pupils to the Well-Tempered Clavier, Mendelssohn and Schumann building institutions around the revival, Brahms editing and performing, Shostakovich answering with his own preludes and fugues. Plebiscites can be rigged by education, and the loop argument of this article applies to composers as much as to audiences. Even discounted for the loop, the pattern remains remarkable, because the witnesses include the very rivals, Mozart and Beethoven above all, whose candidacies the structural claim defeats. Testimony against interest is the strongest testimony any court recognises, and the composers' testimony about these scores has run, consistently and across every change of fashion, against their own competitive interest.
## The question the argument is really about

Underneath every round of this debate sits a single fork in the philosophy of music. One road treats music as a made object whose excellence resides in its construction, available to any examiner willing to learn the construction's language. The other treats music as an event between sound and listener whose excellence resides in its effect, available immediately and democratically to anyone present. The debate is not really about Bach; it is about whether music is best judged by what it builds or by what it does to a listener. The composer became the debate's permanent exhibit because his output maximises the first road's evidence while containing, in the passions and cantatas, some of the second road's most devastating exhibits as well. No other figure arms both sides so completely, which is why the argument returns to him whenever a culture wants to renegotiate its values.

The long view offered by the [history of classical composers](/2010/03/07/history-of-classical-composers/) suggests that the fork is permanent and that each era walks both roads in different proportions. Periods of system building exalt the architect. Periods of expressive hunger exalt the mover of hearts. The scores under discussion have outlasted several swings of that pendulum without losing their audience under either dispensation, a durability that may be the nearest thing to a verdict the evidence supports. Durability is not the same property as supremacy. It is arguably the more interesting property, since supremacy depends on a criterion while durability is a brute historical fact recorded in performances, editions and the working habits of musicians across three centuries.

A disciplined close therefore refuses the trophy and keeps the questions. Was he ahead of his time or behind it? The evidence answers: behind in genre, ahead in technique, indifferent in conduct, and the compound answer is more truthful than any single word. Is the reputation earned or manufactured? Both, in proportions that vary by work and by decade, through a feedback loop whose mechanism can be described exactly. Must a listener believe to hear? No, and a layer of meaning awaits the listener who studies what belief built. Is the most famous organ piece certainly his? No, and the centre stands anyway. An argument that can hold that many precise answers at once has done its work. The music, which never needed the argument, remains available for the only test that finally matters, the next hearing.

One practical inheritance remains for the reader who has followed the argument this far. The debate's tools transfer to every other reputation in the series and beyond it: demand the criterion before the verdict, ask which evidence a claim rests on and which it ignores, grade attribution by documentary confidence rather than by fame, separate a reputation's courtiers from its scores, and treat every authority as a witness with an angle worth knowing. Applied consistently, those habits will sometimes confirm a famous standing and sometimes dissolve one, and the user of the tools will not know in advance which outcome a given case holds. That uncertainty is not a defect of the method. It is the method's definition of honesty, and it returns the reader to the concert hall and the recording shelf in the only posture this article has argued for throughout: informed, unowned and listening.
## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why do people call Bach the greatest composer?

The title rests mainly on structural command sustained across an unusually large and varied output. Advocates point to the fugues, the passions, the B minor Mass, the variation cycles and the hundreds of cantatas, all showing control of counterpoint, harmony and large scale design at a level no contemporary matched. Teachers reinforce the verdict because the works demonstrate compositional procedure with unmatched clarity, so each generation of musicians meets the music as the standard for its own training. Critics of the title reply that the verdict depends on choosing structure as the governing criterion, and that melody or drama would crown other composers. The verdict's persistence across rival musical cultures is what any explanation of it has to account for.
### Q: Is Bach overrated?

The honest answer depends on which claim is being rated. The claim that the core works reward deep study is supported by scores, by three centuries of performance and by the testimony of practitioners from Mozart onward. The claim that the entire catalogue of more than a thousand entries is uniformly masterly goes beyond the evidence, since attribution is uncertain at the edges and even devoted listeners know a fraction of the output well. The claim that the reputation owes nothing to institutions also fails, because syllabi and seasons amplify it. Rated claim by claim, the music is formidable and the mythology around it is inflated. Precision about which claim is inflated, and which is not, does most of the work in this recurring argument.
### Q: Was Bach's style old-fashioned in his own time?

Yes, by the standard of the living market in the 1740s, and the witnesses were close ones. His sons Carl Philipp Emanuel and Wilhelm Friedemann described the paternal manner as old-fashioned while building careers on the lighter galant style that courts and cities rewarded. The charge had technical content: dense counterpoint, learned fugue and heavy chorale writing stood against an ideal of clarity, periodic melody and immediate charm. Older contemporaries who valued craft judged differently, and learned societies honoured him. Old-fashioned therefore describes a relationship to fashion at a particular moment, not a measurement of quality that later centuries were bound to accept. Posterity changed the criterion rather than refuting the witnesses, a distinction the next sections develop in full.
### Q: How did Bach's reputation change after his death?

Public performance of the large works lapsed within a generation, while manuscripts circulated among pupils, family members and connoisseurs who valued the keyboard music and the craft. Forkel published a biography in 1802 drawn from the sons' testimony, presenting the composer as a national inheritance. Mendelssohn's Berlin performance of the St Matthew Passion in 1829 reopened the public account, and the Bach Gesellschaft, founded in 1850, issued a complete edition across fifty years. The twentieth century then argued over the inheritance, with monumental Romantic performance challenged by period instrument practice, and complete recording projects carrying the catalogue to private listeners worldwide. Each stage of that career, neglect, revival and argument, rebuilt the figure in the image of its own needs.
### Q: Is Bach better than Mozart?

Under structural command, the case favours the Leipzig composer, whose contrapuntal consistency across forty years and many genres has no parallel. Under melody and dramatic immediacy, the case favours Mozart, whose operas, concertos and chamber music deliver phrase, character and theatrical timing that register at first hearing. Mozart also competes on learned ground, as the fugal finale of the Jupiter Symphony shows, while the rival competes on expressive ground in the passions. The comparison therefore reports the judge's criterion more reliably than it reports a fact about either composer, and the most defensible verdict names the criterion before naming the winner. Naming the criterion first turns a sterile contest into an argument about values that can actually be joined.
### Q: What does structural command mean in music?

Structural command is the demonstrated ability to organise musical material coherently at every scale. It includes counterpoint, where independent melodic lines interlock without harmonic confusion; large scale design, where a long movement or an entire cycle holds together; economy, where extensive works grow from a bass pattern or a single subject; and consistency, where the standard survives across hundreds of compositions rather than a few celebrated peaks. The quality matters to this debate because it can be examined in scores and taught in classrooms, which makes it the most checkable of the qualities used to rank composers, though checkability alone does not prove it should govern. Its nearest rivals, melody and emotional immediacy, measure different goods and produce different rankings with equal legitimacy.
### Q: Does teaching Bach in schools inflate his reputation?

Partly, and the mechanism is visible. Counterpoint and harmony teaching rely on the chorales, inventions and fugues because no other repertoire demonstrates procedure so clearly at every level of difficulty. Students formed on those models become the performers, teachers and critics who programme and praise the music, so training manufactures advocates. The arrow also points the other way, since the works entered syllabi across two centuries because teachers found genuine properties in them: clarity, variety within discipline and models for every stage. Evidence and cause circulate in a loop, and separating them entirely is beyond any method the debate has produced. The same loop appears wherever a repertoire becomes a profession's entrance examination, from Latin verse to orchestral excerpts.
### Q: Is Bach's religious music still meaningful to secular listeners?

The concert record answers yes in behavioural terms: the passions fill halls at Easter in cities of every confessional complexion, and recordings of the cantatas sell to audiences with no Lutheran formation. Secular listeners receive the craft, the drama and the vocal expression directly, and follow the texts in translation for the narrative and the poetry. What differs is assent to the doctrines the works expound, from law and grace to the resurrection hope of the Easter cantatas. Meaning therefore arrives in layers. A listener can receive most of those layers fully while standing outside the final one, and many listeners report that the music's seriousness about death and consolation speaks across that boundary. Layered meaning also explains why arguments about the works so often talk past one another without either side lying.
### Q: What did Bach write in his own manuscripts about purpose?

The manuscripts carry dedications that join worship, teaching and craft without separating them. Sacred scores bear the initials SDG, for Soli Deo Gloria, to God alone the glory, and many begin with JJ, for Jesu juva, Jesus help. The title page of the Orgelbuchlein announces instruction for beginning organists in developing a chorale, together with honour to God and instruction of one's neighbour. The Well-Tempered Clavier addresses musical youth desirous of learning and promises both use and delight. His annotated Calov Bible, preserved with underlinings in his hand, shows a reader engaging sermons and commentary on music's place in worship and on diligence in a calling. Dedications of that kind were conventional in the period, and the density and specificity of these examples exceed convention.
### Q: How do scholars decide if a work is really by Bach?

Scholars combine several independent tests because no single one is decisive. Handwriting comparison separates autographs from copies by named pupils and family members. Paper studies and watermarks date the physical sources. Documentary evidence, including council records, payment lists and early catalogues, either supports or contradicts a claimed origin. Stylistic analysis compares harmony, counterpoint habits and formal choices against the secure works, treating oddities as questions rather than verdicts. Verdicts are graded rather than absolute, which is why the catalogue carries an appendix of doubtful and spurious pieces, and why works move between the main list and the appendix as evidence accumulates. The graded appendix, revised across editions since 1950, is where the current state of every disputed case can be checked.
### Q: Which famous Bach works have disputed authorship?

The Toccata and Fugue in D minor for organ heads the list, surviving in a copy from a pupil's circle with stylistic features that prompted scholars to propose a string origin and even another hand, though no replacement attribution commands consensus. The eight short preludes and fugues for organ are generally assigned to a pupil, possibly Johann Ludwig Krebs. The Minuet in G major from the family notebook belongs to Christian Petzold. The St Luke Passion is accepted as a work the composer performed or arranged rather than composed. Against that perimeter, the passions, the B minor Mass, the Brandenburg Concertos and the great keyboard cycles stand on autographs and transmission too strong to shake.

### Q: Why do some people find Bach mathematical?

The impression usually begins in the classroom, where teaching presents the music through its procedures: subjects, answers, canons at set intervals, inversion and augmentation. Those devices are countable and examinable, so textbooks foreground them, and the Goldberg Variations in particular circulates as a diagram of canons at widening intervals. Expectation then edits listening, priming audiences to hear machinery. The scores themselves supply the corrective in the vocal works, where rhetorical figures serve grief, penitence and consolation, and in the passions, whose crowd choruses and arias operate theatrically. Listeners who start with the cantatas rather than the speculative late cycles rarely bring back a report of coldness. Starting point matters more than temperament in most such reports, which is why guided entry routes feature later in these answers.
### Q: Is period instrument performance more faithful?

It is more faithful to documented evidence about instruments, pitch, temperament and ensemble size, because those matters leave records: surviving instruments, treatises, payment lists and the composer's own 1730 memorandum on the forces proper church music requires. Faithfulness becomes less certain at the level of tempo, accent and emotional temperature, where the period's shared habits went unwritten and must be inferred. Critic Richard Taruskin argued that the movement also expresses twentieth century taste for clarity and speed, dressed in historical costume. A careful verdict separates the layers: evidence governs materials firmly, guides interpretation usefully and never performs the music by itself. Working performers increasingly treat the evidence as a discipline for choices rather than as a destination that removes choice.
### Q: How do I judge competing recordings of the same work?

Begin with the decisions a recording announces: instruments old or modern, choir large or one voice to a part, pitch, tempo and the edition used where the notes make those details public. Listen for balances that let inner lines speak, since counterpoint buried under a generalised wash fails the music's first requirement in any style. Compare a single movement across versions rather than whole works, attending to how each conductor phrases chorales and paces recitative. Note which version sends you back to the score or to another movement, since sustained curiosity is evidence about performance as well as about the listener and about the work itself. Keep the verdict provisional, because rooms, equipment and one's own history all vote.

### Q: How do I check whether a work's attribution is secure?

Start with the catalogue number and its placement: works in the main Bach Werke Verzeichnis list carry stronger transmission than those in the appendix of doubtful and spurious pieces, and reference works report the grading openly. Look for an autograph as the gold standard, then for copies by named pupils or family members with a traceable path to the composer. Check whether paper and watermark evidence has been published and whether the style has drawn published doubts, as with the D minor organ Toccata and Fugue. Treat programme notes that print a famous name without any of that apparatus as marketing rather than scholarship, and expect honest sources to state confidence levels. Confidence levels, stated plainly by honest sources, are the reader's best protection against both credulity and panic.
### Q: Which recordings make the emotional case for Bach?

The passions are the strongest witnesses, and the St Matthew Passion in versions that honour its double choir architecture, from period instrument accounts under conductors such as Philippe Herreweghe and John Eliot Gardiner to the great mid century readings, lets the crowd scenes and the alto arias make the case without advocacy. The cantata Ich habe genug in a fine bass performance compresses the argument into twenty minutes. The Goldberg Variations in Glenn Gould's 1955 recording present wit, melancholy and dance in sequence for keyboard sceptics. The cello suites in Pablo Casals's historic accounts carry the suites' singing line past every analytical barrier a listener brings. Entry through any one of those doors reliably leads listeners toward the others, which is part of the repertoire's documented behaviour.
### Q: Where should a skeptical listener start?

Start where the defence is strongest relative to the doubt. A listener who suspects coldness should begin with the St Matthew Passion's opening chorus and the aria Erbarme dich, or with the cantata Ich habe genug entire, since twenty minutes settles whether the emotional register exists. A listener who suspects inflation should begin with the Goldberg Variations and one Brandenburg Concerto, the works whose craft is most exposed to inspection. A listener who suspects period performance dogma should hear the same cantata in a period account and in a modern instrument account back to back. Small, well chosen samples answer scepticism better than dutiful marathons through the catalogue. Scepticism answered by sampling tends to stay answered, because the listener's own ears become the witness that matters.
### Q: Why do arrangements of Bach remain popular?

Arrangements persist because the musical substance survives translation unusually well. Counterpoint that lives in the relationships between lines can move from organ to piano, from violin to guitar, from orchestra to saxophone quartet, keeping its logic audible, and the composer himself set the precedent by recasting Vivaldi concertos for keyboard and his own concertos between instruments. Liszt and Busoni built piano repertoires from the organ works, Stokowski carried the D minor Toccata to film audiences, and jazz and popular musicians have mined the chorales and inventions for harmonic patterns. Each arrangement also functions as criticism, arguing by demonstration which features of a work are essential and which were costume. Each migration also recruits new listeners who would never have approached the original medium, extending the work's public by other means.
### Q: How does Bach compare with his own contemporaries?

Telemann was more famous during their lifetimes, more prolific by most counts and quicker to follow fashion, holding posts in Hamburg and publishing for a broad amateur market with a facility contemporaries admired. Handel built an international career on opera and oratorio, commanding public genres and royal institutions across two countries. Vivaldi defined the concerto's grammar from Venice. Against that company, the Leipzig cantor looks provincial in career terms and unrivalled in contrapuntal depth, keyboard command and the density of invention sustained across the church music. Posterity reversed the lifetime ranking almost completely, which is either posterity's correction of a market error or evidence that later criteria favoured the study over the stage. The reversal says as much about the criteria later centuries rewarded as it does about the musicians themselves.
### Q: What would change the verdict on Bach?

Evidence could change parts of it. A documentary discovery moving a load bearing work, a passion, the B minor Mass or a keyboard cycle, into the doubtful appendix would force genuine revision, as would proof that a celebrated cycle came from another hand. Nothing of that order has survived scrutiny, and the perimeter revisions have left the centre intact. The evaluative part of the verdict changes only when musical culture changes its weightings: a century that prizes immediacy over architecture will demote the composer regardless of documents, and a century that prizes construction will restore him. Watching which way informed taste moves, and noticing that the scores wait patiently under every regime, is the practical way to follow the question.
