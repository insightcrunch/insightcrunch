The most expensive plumbing failure a house can suffer is one you can prevent in an afternoon for the cost of a few foam sleeves and a shutoff valve turn. When you winterize your plumbing before the first hard freeze, you are not chasing a leak or reacting to a flood; you are spending thirty dollars and two hours to avoid a repair that routinely runs into the thousands once a frozen line splits and water pours into a wall, a ceiling, or a finished basement. That trade, a short seasonal routine against a mid-winter emergency bill, is the whole argument of this guide, and it is the one thing the reactive world of emergency plumbers almost never teaches, because there is no service call in a burst pipe that never happened.

Frozen pipes do not announce themselves. A supply line in an exterior wall can freeze overnight while you sleep, and the crack it opens may not release water until the ice thaws the next afternoon and the pressure behind it finds the split. By then the damage is already spreading behind drywall you cannot see. The good news is that the physics behind a frozen pipe are simple, the vulnerable spots in most homes are predictable, and the moves that protect them are cheap, quick, and well within reach of anyone willing to spend one Saturday before winter sets in. This guide walks the full seasonal routine in the order you should do it, explains the temperature reality behind the risk, hands you a safe thaw method for the day a line does freeze, and closes with a reusable checklist you can run every year.

## Why Winterizing Your Plumbing Matters More Than You Think

A pipe does not burst because it is cold. It bursts because water expands by roughly nine percent as it turns to ice, and a closed section of pipe has nowhere to put that extra volume. As ice forms and grows inside a line, it pushes a slug of trapped water ahead of it toward any closed faucet or fixture. Pressure builds in that trapped column until the weakest point in the pipe, often a spot far from the ice itself, gives way. This is why the split frequently appears in a section that was not even frozen, and why simply keeping a pipe from freezing solid is not the whole game; you also want to relieve the pressure that freezing creates.

Understanding that mechanism changes how you think about prevention. The goal is twofold: keep vulnerable lines above freezing wherever you reasonably can, and give any water that does start to freeze an escape route so pressure never reaches the bursting point. Insulation and heat handle the first goal. A slightly open faucet during a hard freeze handles the second. When people skip one of the two, they often get unlucky in exactly the way they did not expect, so the seasonal routine below treats both as non-negotiable.

It also explains why a pipe can survive one freeze and burst in the next. The spot that fails is the weakest point in a pressurized section, and whether it holds depends on the pipe's condition, the length of the trapped column, and how much ice forms before something gives. A line that came through a brief freeze intact may split in a longer or colder one, which is why past survival is no guarantee and why a pipe that has frozen before should be treated as a known weak point to protect, not a proven survivor to ignore. The safest assumption is that any exposed line can fail under the right cold, and to protect it accordingly rather than betting on its track record.

The cost side of this equation is what makes winterizing worth taking seriously rather than treating as an optional chore. A single burst supply line that runs unnoticed for even a few hours can release many gallons per minute into a structure. Water follows gravity into wall cavities, saturates insulation, wicks into subfloors, and drips down into rooms below. The plumbing repair itself, the actual pipe fix, is often the smallest line on the final bill. The larger costs are drying out the structure, replacing ruined drywall and flooring, treating for mold if the moisture sat, and repainting. Insurance may cover part of a sudden freeze event, but claims raise premiums, carry deductibles, and sometimes get contested when an adjuster decides the homeowner left the heat off or failed to take reasonable precautions. Prevention sidesteps all of it.

There is a stubborn myth worth killing early: the belief that frozen pipes are a far-north problem, a concern for Minnesota and Maine but not for milder regions. The opposite is closer to the truth. Homes in cold climates are built and plumbed with freezing in mind, with pipes routed through heated interior spaces, deeper burial for water services, and homeowners who already know the drill. Homes in mild climates are built assuming freezes are rare, so their pipes run through uninsulated attics, crawl spaces, exterior walls, and unprotected outdoor fixtures. When a rare hard freeze reaches a warm-climate region, those unprepared homes burst in enormous numbers all at once, overwhelming every plumber in the area for days. Every few winters a cold snap sweeps across a normally mild state and social feeds fill with photos of ceilings raining water and hardware stores sold out of pipe insulation. If you live somewhere that freezes only occasionally, you are arguably more exposed, not less, because your house was never designed to shrug off the cold and you may have never learned to winterize your plumbing at all.

Renters are not off the hook either. While a landlord owns the pipes, a tenant who leaves for a holiday and turns the heat off entirely can be the reason a line freezes, and lease agreements often make the tenant responsible for reasonable care, including keeping enough heat on to prevent freezing. Anyone responsible for a home in winter, owner or renter, benefits from knowing the routine.

## When to Winterize Your Plumbing: The Seasonal Calendar

Timing is the quiet failure point in freeze prevention. The tasks themselves are simple, but people put them off until a cold front is already in the forecast, and by then the outdoor faucet they meant to drain has a hose still attached and water sitting in the spigot. The rule is to complete the outdoor and preventive work in fall, well before the first hard freeze, not during the freeze itself.

Think of the winterizing calendar in three phases. The first is early fall, when the weather is still mild and comfortable to work in. This is when you handle the outdoor tasks: disconnecting and draining garden hoses, shutting off and draining outdoor faucets and hose bibs, blowing out or draining an irrigation system if you have one, and covering exterior spigots. Doing this early matters because a single overnight freeze can crack a hose bib that still has water in it, and outdoor work is far more pleasant in October than in a December cold snap. Early fall is also the right window to inspect and address insulation on exposed pipes before the temperatures that make the job urgent arrive.

The second phase is late fall, as the first frosts appear. This is when you finish the interior preparation: insulating any pipes that run through unheated basements, crawl spaces, garages, or attics; sealing the drafts and gaps around the foundation, rim joists, and any point where a pipe passes through an exterior wall; and locating your main water shutoff so you can find it in the dark if you ever need to. This is also the moment to make sure you know where every fixture shutoff is and that they actually turn, because a valve that has not moved in years can seize.

The third phase is reactive, triggered by the forecast rather than the calendar. When a hard freeze is coming, especially the kind of prolonged deep cold that drops nighttime lows well below freezing for a day or more, you take the day-of steps: opening cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls so household heat reaches the pipes, setting the thermostat to hold a steady temperature day and night rather than letting it drop overnight, and letting a trickle run from the faucets fed by the most vulnerable lines. These are the moves that carry a home through a genuine cold event, and they layer on top of the fall preparation rather than replacing it.

The best time to service or upgrade anything freeze-related is also the shoulder season, not the crisis. If you want a plumber to add heat tape to a chronically freezing line, insulate a crawl space properly, or reroute a pipe that freezes every year, book it in early fall when plumbers have open schedules, rather than during the January rush when every available pro is chasing burst-pipe emergencies and pricing reflects the demand. The general whole-home seasonal rhythm, of which plumbing is one part, is worth mapping onto a single annual routine; the broader [complete home maintenance calendar](/2027/12/01/home-maintenance-calendar/) ties the plumbing tasks here to the roof, gutter, heating, and exterior work that share the same fall window, and the dedicated [winter home prep and protection guide](/2028/07/15/winter-home-prep/) covers the non-plumbing side of getting a house ready for the cold.

### How early should you start winterizing your plumbing?

Start the outdoor tasks in early fall, weeks before the first hard freeze is forecast, while the weather is still mild. Hose bibs, garden hoses, and irrigation lines need to be drained before the first overnight freeze can crack them. Interior insulation and draft sealing can follow in late fall, before deep cold arrives.

## Where Your Pipes Are Most Vulnerable to Freezing

Not every pipe in a house is at equal risk, and knowing which ones are exposed lets you spend your effort where it matters instead of insulating lines that were never going to freeze. Freezing happens where a pipe sits in a space that gets significantly colder than the heated interior of the home, so the vulnerable spots are the ones separated from household heat by an uninsulated barrier, an exterior wall, or open air.

Outdoor fixtures are the most obvious and the most commonly damaged. Hose bibs, sillcocks, and spigots sit on or through an exterior wall, fully exposed to the outside air. Any water left sitting in them freezes readily, and because the valve body is often just inside the wall, a crack there can leak into the wall cavity rather than out the front where you would see it. A garden hose left attached makes it worse: the hose traps water against the spigot and prevents it from draining, so the whole assembly freezes as a unit. This is why disconnecting hoses is the single most repeated piece of winterizing advice.

Unheated interior spaces are the next tier. Basements that are not conditioned, crawl spaces under the house, attached garages, and attics all run close to outdoor temperature, and any water line passing through them is exposed. Crawl spaces are especially treacherous because they combine cold air, open vents, and pipes that run the length of the house, often with no insulation at all. A supply line stretched across a vented crawl space during a hard freeze is a classic burst location. Attics matter in homes where plumbing was run overhead, and garages matter because people leave the door open, park a warm car that then cools, and often have a laundry or utility hookup on the shared wall.

Pipes inside exterior walls are the sneaky category because they look protected. A supply line running up through the wall behind a kitchen or bathroom sink on the outside face of the house sits between the interior drywall and the exterior sheathing, and if that wall cavity has thin or poorly installed insulation, cold air reaches the pipe. These are the lines that benefit most from the day-of trick of opening the cabinet doors beneath the sink so warm room air can circulate into the cavity. Homes built before modern insulation standards, and additions or bump-outs where a wall was opened up, are the usual offenders.

The water service entry, where the main line comes into the house, deserves a look too, particularly in homes where the meter or the entry point sits in an unheated basement corner or a below-grade pit. In very cold regions the buried service line is deep enough to stay safe, but the transition where it emerges into the structure can be exposed. A water meter in an outdoor pit or an unheated garage is itself vulnerable in a hard freeze, and a frozen meter can crack, so in colder regions meter pits are insulated and, where allowed, the meter and the exposed connections given attention. If your shutoff and meter sit in a cold, exposed spot, that location goes on your list of areas to insulate and check.

Do not overlook the difference between a pipe that merely runs through a cool space and one that sits directly against or inside an exterior barrier. A line stapled to the underside of a floor in a vented crawl space, a pipe running up the inside face of an exterior wall, and a spigot line passing straight through the wall are all in the highest-risk category because cold reaches them from multiple sides with little insulation in between. A pipe centered in a warm interior wall, by contrast, rarely freezes. When you inventory your home, rank the spots by how directly the cold can reach the pipe, and spend your insulation and attention where the exposure is greatest.

The pattern across all of these is the same: a pipe carrying still water, sitting in air that drops below freezing, with nothing between it and the cold. When you walk your house before winter, follow your water lines with that lens and note every place a pipe leaves heated space. Those are the spots that decide whether you sail through a cold snap or spend a January morning on the phone with a flooded floor. The early tells that a line is starting to struggle, reduced flow, a faucet that sputters, frost forming on an accessible pipe, are worth learning too; the guide to [plumbing warning signs you should not ignore](/2000/07/01/plumbing-warning-signs/) covers the symptoms that mean a line is in trouble before it fails outright.

## Pipe Materials and How Each Handles a Freeze

A common misconception is that some pipe materials are freeze-proof. None are. What varies is how a given material fails when the water inside it freezes, and understanding that helps you judge your own risk and avoid a false sense of security about a plastic line.

Copper, the traditional supply-line material in many homes, is rigid and splits readily when ice expands inside it. A frozen copper line typically opens a lengthwise crack, and because copper is stiff, it offers little give before it fails. Copper is a reliable, long-lived material, but it earns no mercy from a freeze, and copper lines in exposed spots are classic burst locations.

Galvanized steel, found in older homes, is even more rigid and can fail at threaded joints as well as along the pipe body. Steel also corrodes internally over many years, narrowing the bore, which can make a partially blocked older line freeze more readily. Homes still carrying galvanized supply lines are often older homes with more exposed plumbing generally, so the material and the exposure compound.

PEX, the flexible plastic tubing now common in new construction and repipes, is more forgiving than copper or steel because it can expand somewhat as ice forms, and it sometimes survives a freeze that would split a rigid pipe. Sometimes, not always. PEX can still burst under a hard, prolonged freeze, and its fittings and connections can fail even when a run of tubing holds. The flexibility buys margin, not immunity, so treating PEX as freeze-proof is a mistake that leads people to skip protection on lines that still need it.

PVC and CPVC, used for some supply and many drain applications, become brittle in cold and can crack from the freeze itself or shatter more easily when disturbed while cold. CPVC used for hot and cold supply in some homes is rigid and vulnerable much like copper.

The practical point across all materials is that no pipe is safe to leave exposed on the assumption that it will survive freezing. PEX buys a little forgiveness, copper and steel and rigid plastics buy none, and every material benefits from the same insulation, draft sealing, heat, and trickle. When you walk your home to find vulnerable lines, identify the material where you can, give rigid copper and steel and CPVC lines in exposed spots the most attention, and do not let a run of PEX tempt you into leaving it unprotected.

## Protecting Well Systems and Pumps in Winter

Homes on a private well carry a set of freeze risks that municipal-water homes do not, and they are easy to overlook because the vulnerable equipment often sits outside the living space in a well house, a pit, or an unheated utility area. If your home draws from a well, winterizing your plumbing has to extend to the well system, or the first hard freeze can knock out your water supply entirely.

The pressure tank, the pump controls, and the pipes connecting the well to the house are the exposed components. In colder regions, the well itself and the buried supply line are below the frost line and stay safe, but the pitless adapter, the pressure tank, and the plumbing where the line rises into the house or a well house are where freezing strikes. A pressure tank sitting in an unheated basement corner, a garage, or a detached well house is exposed to the same cold as any other pipe, and a frozen pump or pressure switch means no water until it thaws, on top of any cracked line.

For a well house or a utility building housing the equipment, the standard protections are insulation of the walls and any exposed pipe, sealing the drafts, and a safe heat source such as a thermostatically controlled heat lamp or a heater rated for the space and kept clear of anything flammable. Some owners add heat tape to the exposed pipe and the pressure tank connections. The same fire-safety cautions that apply to heat tape and space heaters inside the home apply doubly in a small well house full of stored equipment, so the heat source must be appropriate and safely installed.

If a well-system home will sit empty and unheated through winter, the well and plumbing should be winterized like any vacant home, with the added step of addressing the pump and pressure tank per the equipment's guidance, since these hold water that can freeze and crack. Because well systems vary and the pump and tank are expensive to replace, having a well or plumbing professional winterize a seasonal well-system home is often the safer route, especially the first season when you are learning the specific setup.

During a hard freeze in an occupied well-system home, extend the day-of routine to the well equipment: make sure the well house or utility area holds enough heat, that any heat tape is working, and that the pressure tank and controls are not sitting in a draft. Losing the pump to a freeze is worse than losing a single fixture line, because it takes the whole water supply down until it thaws or is repaired.

## The Appliance and Fixture Lines People Forget

The obvious targets, the outdoor spigots and the exposed supply lines, get most of the attention, but a home has water running to appliances and fixtures that people forget when they winterize, and a forgotten line freezes just as readily as a remembered one. Walking the full inventory of where water goes in your home catches the lines that would otherwise be a surprise.

Washing machine supply hoses are a frequent oversight, especially when the laundry sits in a garage, a basement, or an unheated utility room on an exterior wall. The rubber or braided hoses feeding the machine hold water and can freeze, and the connection point at the wall is exposed. In a laundry area at risk of freezing, the same protections apply: keep the space heated, insulate the lines, and during a hard freeze consider shutting off and, if needed, draining the hoses, which many people do anyway to prevent the separate risk of a hose bursting under pressure.

Refrigerator water lines feeding an ice maker or a water dispenser run a small tube, often through a wall or under the floor to the fridge, and a section of that tube passing through an unheated space can freeze. Dishwasher supply lines share the under-sink cabinet with the sink plumbing, so keeping that cabinet reachable by household heat during a freeze protects them together with the sink lines.

Outdoor kitchens, wet bars, and second or basement bathrooms that see little use are easy to forget precisely because they are out of the daily routine. A rarely used basement bathroom on an exterior wall, an outdoor kitchen sink, or a bar sink in an unheated space all carry water lines that need the same winterizing as any other. For seasonal outdoor kitchens and bars, draining the lines for winter is the reliable move.

Pool, spa, and sprinkler equipment sit outside and hold water in pumps, filters, and lines that freeze and crack readily. Winterizing a pool or spa is its own process, typically involving draining or lowering water levels, clearing the equipment lines, and protecting the pump, and it should be done before the first freeze along with the rest of the outdoor water systems. An irrigation system, as covered earlier, needs its lines cleared or blown out.

Even a hose bib or a spigot on a rarely visited side of the house counts. The pattern is to inventory every place water flows, indoors and out, appliance and fixture, used and unused, and to make sure each vulnerable one is on your winterizing list. The lines that flood homes are often the ones nobody thought about because they were out of sight.

## At What Temperature Do Pipes Actually Freeze?

Water freezes at thirty-two degrees Fahrenheit, but a pipe carrying water inside a house does not freeze the instant the outdoor air hits that mark. The pipe has to lose enough heat for the water inside it to drop to freezing, and how fast that happens depends on how exposed the pipe is, how cold the surrounding air gets, how long the cold lasts, and whether any heat or moving water is keeping the line warm.

### At what temperature should you worry about frozen pipes?

The commonly cited threshold is around twenty degrees Fahrenheit for unprotected pipes, especially those in exterior walls or unheated spaces. That figure is a durable guideline, not a guarantee; a poorly insulated pipe in a windy, exposed spot can freeze at higher temperatures, while a protected interior line may hold well below it. Watch the forecast and your home.

The distinction that matters is between exposed pipes and protected ones. A well-insulated line running through heated interior space can hold up in extreme cold because the household heat keeps it warm. An exposed line in a crawl space or an exterior wall, with cold air on all sides, is on borrowed time once the temperature drops into the low twenties and stays there. Wind chill accelerates the loss because moving air strips heat from the pipe faster than still air, so a spot exposed to a draft or an open vent freezes sooner than the raw temperature alone would suggest. Duration compounds it: a brief dip to the low twenties overnight that warms up by mid-morning is far less dangerous than the same temperature sustained for a day or two, because sustained cold gives the pipe time to lose all its heat and the water time to freeze solid.

This is why the twenty-degree figure is a guideline rather than a rule. It reflects the point at which unprotected pipes in typical exposed locations start to fail in large numbers, which is why utilities and weather services often flag it as the temperature to take precautions. But your particular house has its own weak points, and a pipe in an especially exposed, windy, or poorly insulated spot can freeze at temperatures a few degrees warmer, while a protected line stays safe well below the threshold. Treat the low twenties as your cue to run the day-of routine, and treat any pipe you already know to be exposed as vulnerable at the first sustained freeze regardless of the exact number.

The practical takeaway is to watch the forecast for the combination that does the damage: sustained cold in the low twenties or below, especially overnight, especially with wind, especially after a stretch of mild weather that lulled you into leaving the outdoor faucet undrained. That combination is the burst-pipe recipe, and it is precisely when the fall preparation and the day-of moves earn their keep.

## The Fall Winterizing Tasks in Order

The fall routine is a sequence, and doing it in order means you never have to redo a step or work around one you skipped. Start outside while the weather is still workable, then move inside.

Begin with the garden hoses. Disconnect every hose from every outdoor spigot, drain it fully by stretching it downhill or coiling it from one end, and store it somewhere it will not be crushed or cracked by cold. A hose left attached is the number one cause of a cracked hose bib because it holds water against the spigot and stops the valve from draining. This single step, disconnecting the hoses, prevents a large share of outdoor freeze damage on its own and takes only minutes.

Next, shut off and drain the outdoor faucets themselves. Many homes have an interior shutoff valve on the line feeding each exterior spigot, usually just inside the wall in the basement or crawl space. Close that valve, then go outside and open the spigot to let the water between the valve and the spigot drain out. Leave the spigot open through the winter so any residual water has room to expand rather than build pressure. If your home has frost-proof or freeze-proof sillcocks, which are designed to shut the water off deep inside the heated wall rather than at the exterior face, you still need to disconnect the hose for them to work as intended, because an attached hose defeats the drain-back design. For spigots with no interior shutoff, an insulated faucet cover, a foam dome that slips over the spigot, adds a layer of protection, though draining is always better than covering alone.

If you have an in-ground irrigation system, it needs to be winterized before the first freeze, either by draining it at the low points or, more reliably, by having the lines blown out with compressed air. Sprinkler heads and shallow irrigation lines are highly vulnerable, and a cracked underground line is an expensive spring surprise. If you are not comfortable blowing out the system yourself, this is a task worth scheduling with an irrigation or landscaping pro in early fall.

With the outdoor work done, move inside and address insulation on exposed pipes. Foam pipe sleeves, the split tubes that snap over a pipe, are inexpensive and available at any hardware store, and they slip onto exposed lines in basements, crawl spaces, garages, and attics in minutes. Cover the full run of any pipe in an unheated space, paying attention to elbows and joints where sleeves need to be cut and fitted. For pipes in especially cold or drafty spots, or lines with a history of freezing, heat tape or heat cable, an electric element that runs along the pipe and warms it, is the stronger option, but it must be the correct type for the application and installed per its instructions, since improperly used heat tape is a fire risk. When in doubt about heat tape, treat it as a pro-adjacent task.

Then seal the drafts. Cold air reaches pipes through gaps in the foundation, around the rim joist where the framing meets the foundation, through open crawl-space vents, and around any point where a pipe or cable penetrates an exterior wall. Caulk and expanding foam close these gaps, and closing or covering crawl-space vents for the winter cuts the flow of cold air across the pipes below. A pipe that would freeze in a drafty space often stays safe once the draft is stopped, so draft sealing frequently does more than insulation alone.

Finally, do the knowledge work that costs nothing: locate your main water shutoff and confirm it turns, locate the individual fixture shutoffs and confirm they turn, and know where they all are before you ever need them in a hurry. A seized shutoff valve is worth discovering in October when a plumber can replace it calmly, not during a freeze when you need it to work right now. Knowing your shutoffs is also the foundation of handling any burst, which is why the [what to do in a plumbing emergency guide](/2001/06/01/plumbing-emergency-what-to-do/) leads with finding and using them.

### What is the right way to shut down an outdoor spigot for winter?

Disconnect and drain the hose, then close the interior shutoff valve feeding that spigot if one exists. Open the outdoor faucet to drain the water between the valve and the spigot, and leave it open through winter. Add an insulated faucet cover for extra protection, especially on spigots with no interior shutoff.

## What to Buy: Insulation, Covers, Heat Tape, and Tools

Winterizing needs very little in the way of materials, and knowing what to pick up before fall means you are not improvising during a cold snap when the hardware store shelves are already bare. The core kit is inexpensive and mostly reusable year to year.

Pipe insulation comes in a few forms. Foam pipe sleeves, the split tubes that snap over a pipe, are the most common and the easiest to install; they come sized to fit common pipe diameters, so measure or note your pipe size before buying, since a sleeve too large leaves a gap and one too small will not close. Thicker-walled sleeves insulate better for the coldest spots. Rubber or self-sealing foam insulation with an adhesive seam seals more tightly and suits pipes in the harshest locations. For elbows, tees, and valves where a straight sleeve will not fit, you cut and fit the foam or use fiberglass wrap, and you tape the seams so cold air cannot reach the pipe through a gap. Buy a little more than you think you need, since the joints and fittings eat material.

Insulated faucet covers, the foam domes that slip over an outdoor spigot and cinch tight, are a few dollars each and protect hose bibs, especially those without an interior shutoff. They are a supplement to draining, not a replacement, but on a spigot you cannot fully drain they add a meaningful layer.

Heat tape or heat cable is the tool for lines that freeze despite insulation, or for a run in a spot too exposed to protect otherwise. This is an electric heating element that runs along the pipe. It must be the correct type for the application, whether the pipe is metal or plastic and whether it runs indoors or outdoors, and it must be installed strictly per the manufacturer's instructions, because heat tape used incorrectly, overlapped on itself, or left on a damaged cord is a genuine fire risk. Buy heat tape rated for your specific use, read the instructions before installing, and if you are not confident, treat it as a task for a pro. Some heat cable is thermostatically controlled so it only powers on when the pipe gets cold, which is both safer and more efficient than a version you must remember to plug in.

For sealing drafts, a tube of caulk for gaps and cracks and a can of expanding foam for larger openings around pipe penetrations and the rim joist handle most of the air-sealing work. Foam-board or foam-block covers for crawl-space vents let you close the vents for winter and reopen them in spring.

The tools are minimal: a utility knife to cut foam sleeves, a tape measure to size pipe and insulation, and possibly a caulk gun. Stock the kit in early fall, store the reusable pieces where you can find them next year, and note in your home records what sizes and types fit your home so the next fall's shopping is a two-minute list rather than a guessing trip. Keeping that list and the record of what you insulated where is exactly the kind of detail worth saving in your home project notes so you are not relearning your own house every winter.

## The Drain, Disconnect, and Trickle Rule

If you remember nothing else from this guide, remember three cheap moves that prevent the large majority of frozen-pipe damage: disconnect and drain the outdoor hoses and faucets before the first freeze, insulate and seal the exposed lines, and let a trickle run from vulnerable faucets during a hard freeze. Call it the drain, disconnect, and trickle rule. Burst pipes spike every cold snap not because prevention is hard or expensive, but because people skip these three moves, and the freeze finds the gap they left.

The drain-and-disconnect half addresses the outdoor fixtures and the still water that freezes first. The trickle half addresses the pressure that freezing creates inside the lines you cannot fully protect. Each half handles a different failure mode, which is why doing only one leaves you exposed. A homeowner who diligently drains the outdoor spigots but never opens a faucet during a deep freeze can still lose an interior line that froze and burst from pressure. A homeowner who drips the faucets but leaves a hose attached to a spigot can still crack the hose bib. The rule works because it closes both gaps.

The reason this rule is worth naming and remembering is that it converts a vague sense that you should do something about winter into three concrete, repeatable actions with a clear trigger for each. Drain and disconnect in fall. Insulate and seal in fall. Trickle when a hard freeze is forecast. That is the entire prevention strategy for most homes, and it is why a small seasonal routine reliably beats a mid-winter emergency bill. The homes that flood are almost always the ones that skipped one of the three, and the pattern is consistent enough that you can predict, after any regional cold snap, exactly which corners the flooded homes cut.

### Is a dripping faucet enough to stop a freeze?

Yes, running a small trickle during a hard freeze helps in two ways. Moving water resists freezing better than still water, and more importantly, an open faucet relieves the pressure that builds between an ice blockage and a closed tap. That pressure, not the ice, is what usually bursts a pipe, so an open faucet gives it an escape route.

The trickle does not need to be a heavy stream. A steady drip to a thin trickle from the faucet served by your most vulnerable line is enough, and running both the hot and cold sides matters because both supply lines are at risk, not just the cold. Prioritize the faucets fed by pipes running through exterior walls, unheated spaces, or the lines with a freezing history, since those are where the trickle earns its small water bill. During an extended deep freeze, running the trickle through the coldest overnight hours is the window that counts, because that is when the lines lose the most heat and the water has the most time to freeze.

Some people worry about the water waste, and it is a fair consideration, but the volume from a trickle over a cold night is small measured against the cost of a burst pipe, and it is a temporary measure for the worst nights, not a permanent habit. Weigh the pennies of water against the thousands a flood can cost, and the trickle is the cheapest insurance in the routine.

## During a Hard Freeze: What to Do When Temperatures Plunge

When the forecast shows a genuine hard freeze coming, sustained cold in the low twenties or below, especially overnight and especially after mild weather, the day-of routine layers protection on top of your fall preparation. These are the moves that carry a home through the actual cold event.

Open the cabinet doors beneath sinks on exterior walls, in kitchens and bathrooms, so household heat can reach the supply lines hidden in those wall cavities. Those under-sink pipes are among the most commonly frozen because they sit in exterior walls with limited insulation, and warm room air circulating around them can be the difference between a line that holds and one that freezes. If you have small children or pets, move any cabinet cleaning chemicals out of reach first, since open cabinets put them within reach.

Hold your thermostat at a steady temperature day and night rather than letting it drop overnight. The nighttime setback that saves energy in normal weather works against you in a hard freeze, because the overnight hours are the coldest and the setback pulls heat away from the pipes exactly when they need it most. Keeping the heat steady, and setting it a few degrees higher than usual, keeps the interior spaces and the wall cavities warm enough to protect the lines. If you leave the house during a cold event, do not turn the heat off; keep it set high enough to prevent freezing, which is a lower bar than comfort but not off.

Let the vulnerable faucets trickle overnight, as covered in the rule above, running both hot and cold sides on the lines most at risk. If part of your house has a section you can isolate and it has pipes you are worried about, some homeowners choose to keep that zone warm or, for a high-risk line, to shut off and drain it rather than gamble.

Keep the garage door closed if you have water lines on the garage wall or a utility hookup there, since an open garage lets cold air pool against those lines. If you use a space heater to protect a specific cold spot, keep it well away from anything flammable and never leave it unattended, because a space heater near stored items or fabric is a fire hazard that trades one disaster for a worse one.

Move heat toward the cold spots rather than assuming it spreads evenly. Interior doors to unheated or rarely used rooms can be opened so warm air reaches pipes in those spaces, and a room on the cold side of the house that holds vulnerable plumbing benefits from having its door open and, if needed, a safely placed heat source. Basements and utility rooms that carry a lot of exposed pipe are worth keeping a few degrees warmer during the worst of a freeze. Conversely, keep the heated envelope closed to the outside: the goal is to push warmth to the pipes while keeping the cold out, so open the interior doors that share household heat and close the exterior ones that leak it. The whole-home side of holding a house warm and sealed through winter, including the heating system, insulation, and draft work that keeps the interior temperature up, is covered in the broader [winter home prep and protection guide](/2028/07/15/winter-home-prep/), and it works hand in hand with the plumbing-specific moves here.

Finally, watch for the early signs that a line is starting to freeze so you can act before it bursts. A faucet that slows to a trickle or stops entirely while others still flow normally often means the line feeding it is freezing. If you catch it at that stage, you can sometimes save the pipe by warming the area and keeping the faucet open, which is far better than discovering the problem when the ice thaws and the split releases. The point of watching is early intervention; a frozen pipe caught before it bursts is an inconvenience, while one that bursts unnoticed is a flood.

## How a Cold Snap Unfolds: A Timeline for Responding

Knowing the individual moves is one thing; knowing when to make each one during an actual cold event ties them together. A hard freeze follows a predictable arc, and matching your actions to it means you are never scrambling.

The lead-up is the forecast. A day or two out, when the forecast shows sustained cold in the low twenties or below, especially overnight and especially after a mild stretch, is your trigger to confirm the fall preparation is done. Walk the outdoor spigots to be sure the hoses are off and the faucets are drained, check that the exposed pipes are insulated and the drafts sealed, and pick up any pipe insulation or faucet covers you still need before the stores empty. This is also when to identify the faucets you will trickle and confirm your main shutoff turns.

As the freeze arrives, usually the first cold evening, run the day-of routine. Open the cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls, set the thermostat to hold steady day and night at a few degrees above your usual comfort setting, and start the vulnerable faucets trickling on both hot and cold sides. Close the garage door if lines run along the garage wall. If you have a well house or exposed equipment, confirm its heat is working. These moves go in place before you go to bed on the first hard-freeze night, because the overnight hours are when the pipes lose the most heat.

Through the coldest stretch, keep the routine running and watch for the tells. Check that the trickle is still flowing at each vulnerable faucet, since a trickle that died overnight means the line behind it is freezing. If a faucet slows, warm the area and keep it open to catch the freeze before it bursts. Do not switch off the trickle or drop the heat until the cold breaks, because a mid-freeze lapse is exactly when a marginal line fails.

As the thaw comes, stay alert rather than relaxing. Remember that a frozen pipe that cracked often does not leak until it thaws, so the warming afternoon after a freeze is a prime time for a hidden burst to reveal itself. Keep an eye and an ear out for water where it should not be as temperatures climb, check the ceilings and walls near known cold spots, and only wind down the routine once the freeze risk has fully passed. Matching your response to this arc, prepare on the forecast, act as it arrives, hold through the cold, and watch through the thaw, is how the routine actually protects a home rather than sitting unused because the timing got away from you.

## What to Do During a Winter Power Outage

A power outage during a hard freeze is one of the more dangerous combinations for plumbing, because the very cold snap most likely to knock out power is also the one most likely to freeze pipes, and the outage removes the household heat that was protecting them. If the electricity goes out during deep cold, the interior of the home begins cooling toward the outdoor temperature, and the pipes lose their protection just when they need it most.

The immediate moves are to conserve whatever heat remains and to relieve pressure on the pipes. Keep exterior doors and windows closed and limit trips outside to hold interior heat as long as possible. Keep the cabinet doors open under sinks on exterior walls so the residual warmth reaches those lines. Let the vulnerable faucets continue to trickle, since the pressure-relief benefit of an open faucet does not depend on the heat being on; an open faucet still gives freezing water an escape route even in an unheated house.

If the outage looks likely to last through the coldest hours and the home is cooling fast, consider whether to shut off the water at the main and drain the system to remove the water that could freeze. In a prolonged outage during severe cold with no backup heat, draining the plumbing is the surer protection than hoping the pipes hold, though it is a bigger step and one to weigh against how long the outage and the cold will last. A home with a generator or a safe alternative heat source can keep enough warmth to protect the pipes, but any alternative heat source must be used safely, never a device that produces carbon monoxide indoors, and space heaters kept clear of anything flammable.

For a home you will leave during a winter outage, or a vacant home where an outage could go unnoticed, this is exactly the scenario that argues for either draining the system in advance or installing a freeze alarm that alerts you when the temperature drops. The outage-plus-freeze combination is why relying solely on heat to protect an empty home carries real risk, and why draining is the safer choice for any home that will sit without reliable heat and without anyone present to react.

## Common Winterizing Mistakes That Still End in a Burst

Most burst pipes trace back to a small number of avoidable mistakes, and knowing the common failure patterns lets you check your own routine against them. These are the corners people cut, usually without realizing the risk they carry.

Leaving the garden hose attached is the classic. A hose holds water against the spigot and stops it from draining, so even a homeowner who meant to winterize the outdoor faucet loses it because the hose defeated the drain. Disconnect every hose, every fall, from every spigot.

Draining the spigot but forgetting the interior shutoff is a subtler version. Opening the outdoor faucet lets out the water in the spigot, but if the interior valve feeding it stays open, the line behind the spigot still holds pressurized water that can freeze in the wall. Close the interior valve first, then open the spigot to drain the section between them, and leave it open.

Running a nighttime thermostat setback during a freeze pulls heat away from the pipes at the coldest hours. The energy-saving overnight drop that makes sense in normal weather works against you in a hard freeze. Hold the heat steady, day and night, through a cold event.

Dripping only the cold faucet leaves the hot supply line exposed. Both the hot and cold lines run to a fixture, and both can freeze; the hot line is not warm when no one is using hot water. Trickle both sides on the vulnerable faucets.

Ignoring the garage, the crawl space, or the one exterior wall with a sink because it seems minor is how the forgotten line freezes. The vulnerable spot you skip is the one that fails. Insulating some exposed pipes but not others, or insulating without sealing the drafts that let cold air reach them, leaves the gap the freeze finds.

Insulating a pipe but leaving the draft that chills it is a frequent oversight, because insulation slows heat loss but a steady stream of cold air through a foundation gap can still overwhelm it. Seal the drafts and insulate; the two work together.

Turning the heat off entirely when leaving for a trip, in an undrained home, is among the most costly mistakes, because there is nothing to protect the pipes and no one to catch a problem. Either drain the system or leave the heat set to a freeze-safe temperature, and ideally do both.

And the dangerous one: thawing a frozen pipe with an open flame. Every winter this starts house fires and bursts pipes. Gentle heat only, never a torch.

Run your routine against this list each fall, and you close the gaps that account for the large majority of frozen-pipe failures. The homes that flood are rarely unlucky in some unforeseeable way; they almost always skipped one of these known steps.

## What You Can Do Yourself and What Needs a Pro

Most of the winterizing routine is squarely homeowner work. Disconnecting hoses, draining outdoor spigots, slipping foam sleeves onto exposed pipes, sealing drafts with caulk and foam, opening cabinet doors, adjusting the thermostat, and running a trickle are all safe, simple tasks that require no special skill and no license. This is the part of home care where a willing homeowner genuinely saves money by doing it, because the tasks are easy and the materials are cheap.

The line where a pro becomes worth calling runs along a few specific tasks. Heat tape and heat cable, while installable by a careful homeowner, must be the correct product for the pipe material and application and must be installed exactly per the instructions, because the wrong type or a bad installation is a fire risk. If you are adding heat tape to a chronically freezing line and you are not confident about the product and the wiring, a plumber or electrician handling it is money well spent.

Rerouting a pipe that freezes every winter is a pro job. Some homes have a line routed through a spot that will always be vulnerable, an exterior wall with no room for insulation, an exposed run across a cold crawl space, and the real fix is to move the pipe into heated space or reroute it entirely. That is licensed plumbing work, not a weekend task, and it is worth doing on a line that has frozen before, because a pipe that froze once will freeze again in the same conditions.

Whole crawl-space or basement insulation and air sealing, done properly, often exceeds what a homeowner wants to tackle, especially in a tight or dirty crawl space, and a professional insulation contractor or plumber can address the exposed runs comprehensively rather than piecemeal. If your winterizing every year feels like fighting the same cold spots, a one-time professional fix to the underlying exposure can end the annual battle.

And of course, a pipe that has already burst is a pro job for the repair, though the immediate response, shutting off the water, is yours to do first and fast. The [complete guide to hiring a plumber](/2000/01/15/hire-a-plumber-complete-guide/) walks through finding and vetting the right pro for the tasks above the DIY line, whether that is heat tape, a reroute, or a burst-pipe repair, and it covers how to tell a solid operator from a bad one so a freeze emergency does not push you into hiring the first name that answers the phone.

The honest division is this: prevention is overwhelmingly homeowner work, the exposed-pipe fixes and the burst repairs are pro work, and the smart move is to do the easy prevention yourself every fall so you rarely need the expensive pro repair at all.

## Spotting a Freezing Pipe Before It Bursts

A pipe that has frozen but not yet burst is a save waiting to happen, and the difference between catching it and missing it is knowing the tells. During a hard freeze, staying alert to these signs lets you act while the pipe is still intact rather than discovering the problem as a flood.

The clearest sign is a faucet that slows to a trickle or stops entirely while other faucets still flow normally. When one fixture loses pressure and the rest of the house is fine, the line feeding that fixture is very likely freezing. Reduced flow that gets steadily worse over a cold night, or a faucet that sputters and spits air, points the same way. Because you are watching the faucets you deliberately set to trickle, a trickle that dies overnight is a direct warning that the line behind it is closing with ice.

Frost or condensation forming on an accessible pipe, a pipe that feels intensely cold to the touch along one section, or a visible bulge in a line are physical tells on exposed plumbing you can see. In a bathroom or kitchen, a drain that suddenly smells or gurgles oddly in deep cold can occasionally signal a freezing issue lower in the system, though drain freezing is less common than supply-line freezing.

When you catch a line at the freezing stage, you can often save it. Keep the affected faucet open, warm the area around the suspected frozen section with gentle heat, and get more household warmth to the space. Catching it early means you thaw a pipe rather than repair a burst, which is the whole point of watching. If the flow does not return, or if you cannot reach the frozen section, move to the safe thaw steps below, and if you find any sign the pipe has already split, shut off the water.

These freeze-specific tells sit alongside the broader set of plumbing symptoms worth learning; the guide to [plumbing warning signs you should not ignore](/2000/07/01/plumbing-warning-signs/) covers the year-round signs that a line is in trouble, of which the freezing tells here are the winter subset. Knowing both means fewer surprises in any season.

## How to Safely Thaw a Frozen Pipe

Despite the best prevention, a line can still freeze, and how you thaw it decides whether you save the pipe or make things worse. The goal is to warm the frozen section gradually and safely, with the faucet open so melting water can escape and relieve pressure, and without ever using an open flame.

### How do you thaw a frozen pipe without causing a burst?

Open the faucet on the frozen line so melting water can flow. Warm the frozen section with a hair dryer, a heat lamp, a space heater kept clear of flammables, or towels soaked in warm water, working from the faucet end toward the ice. Never use a blowtorch or open flame, which can burst the pipe or start a fire.

Start by finding the frozen section. It is usually the coldest, most exposed part of the line feeding the faucet that stopped flowing, an exterior wall, a crawl space, or a spot near an outside vent. You can often locate it by feeling along accessible pipe for a very cold or frost-covered section, or by reasoning from which faucet lost flow. Open that faucet before you begin warming, both hot and cold sides if it is a mixing faucet, so that as the ice melts the water has somewhere to go and the melting itself signals your progress when flow returns.

Apply gentle, gradual heat to the frozen section, working from the faucet end back toward the blockage rather than starting in the middle. Starting near the open faucet lets the first melt water flow out immediately and keeps pressure from building behind the ice as you work. A hair dryer is the classic tool, moved slowly along the pipe. A heat lamp or a portable space heater aimed at the frozen area works for a length of exposed pipe, provided the heater is kept well away from anything that can catch fire and is never left unattended. Towels soaked in hot water and wrapped around the pipe, refreshed as they cool, warm it gently and reach spots a dryer cannot. Warm, not boiling, water poured over towels avoids thermal shock to the pipe.

The absolute rule is no open flame. A blowtorch, a propane torch, or any open flame is dangerous on two counts: it can heat the pipe so unevenly and so fast that it bursts on the spot, turning a frozen line into a spraying one, and it is a serious fire risk, especially in the wood-framed, insulation-filled spaces where frozen pipes live. Every winter, house fires trace back to someone trying to thaw a pipe with a torch. There is never a good reason to take that risk when gentle heat does the job safely.

If the frozen pipe is inside a wall and you cannot reach it, you have fewer options: turn up the heat in the house, aim warmth at the wall from an infrared lamp or a space heater kept safe, open the cabinet or the nearest access, and be patient. If you cannot reach the ice and cannot restore flow, or if you find the pipe has already split, shut off the water to that line or the whole house and call a plumber. And if at any point during thawing water begins to spray or leak, the pipe has cracked; shut off the water immediately and treat it as a burst, which the [emergency plumbing guide](/2001/06/01/plumbing-emergency-what-to-do/) covers step by step. Thawing a pipe you cannot fully see is a gamble, and knowing where your shutoff is turns a bad outcome into a manageable one.

A few situations call for a different response than the standard supply-line thaw. A frozen drain line, less common than a frozen supply line, does not carry pressurized water, so it is less likely to burst from pressure, but a frozen trap or drain in an exterior wall can still crack, and the fix is the same gentle warming. A frozen main water line, where the whole house loses water rather than a single fixture, is a larger problem: the freeze may be at the point where the service enters the structure, and if warming the accessible entry does not restore flow, or if the frozen section is in the buried line, this is a call for a plumber or the water utility rather than a homeowner thaw. An outdoor spigot or hose bib that froze because a hose was left attached should be shut off at the interior valve if you can, then thawed gently and inspected for a crack once it warms, since a frozen sillcock often reveals its split only when the water is turned back on in spring.

Above all, resist the temptation to rush a thaw with more aggressive heat. The gradual approach protects the pipe, and patience during a thaw is far cheaper than the burst that an open flame or a blast of extreme heat can cause. If a frozen pipe is beyond gentle warming, calling a plumber to thaw it safely is a small cost against the alternative.

## When a Frozen Pipe Bursts: Water Damage and What Happens

A frozen pipe that bursts is the outcome the whole routine exists to prevent, and understanding how the damage unfolds makes the case for prevention concrete. When the ice inside a line finally builds enough pressure to split the pipe, the crack does not always leak immediately. Often the ice itself plugs the split, so the pipe holds until it thaws. Then, as the frozen section melts and full water pressure returns to the line, water pours out of the crack at the pipe's normal flow rate, which for a supply line under household pressure is many gallons per minute.

That is the cruel timing of a frozen-pipe burst: the flood frequently arrives not during the cold, when you are watching, but during the thaw, when you may have relaxed or left the house believing the cold snap passed. Water escaping inside a wall or ceiling runs down framing, soaks insulation, saturates drywall, and pools on the floor below the leak. Because much of the path is hidden, a burst can release a great deal of water before anyone notices, and the damage compounds with every minute the water flows.

The repairs that follow a burst are rarely just the pipe. The plumbing fix, cutting out the split section and replacing it, is often the fastest and cheapest part. The larger effort is drying the structure before mold takes hold, which may involve professional water extraction, industrial dryers running for days, and opening walls and ceilings to reach saturated cavities. Then comes replacing ruined drywall, insulation, flooring, and trim, and repainting. If the water reached a lower level, the damage multiplies across floors. If it sat long enough to grow mold, remediation adds cost and time. This cascade, from a single crack to a multi-room restoration, is why a burst pipe is one of the most expensive things that can happen to a house in winter, and why the thirty-dollar fall routine is such a lopsided bargain against it.

The moment you discover a burst, the priority is to stop the flow: shut off the water at the fixture valve if you can reach it, or at the main shutoff, which is exactly why locating and testing that shutoff in the fall matters so much. After the water is off, containing and documenting the damage and getting a plumber in for the repair follows, but the shutoff comes first because every minute of flow adds to the bill. This is the sharpest illustration of the know-your-shutoff principle: the single move that turns a plumbing disaster into an inconvenience is knowing where the shutoffs are before you ever need them.

## The Cost of Skipping Winterization

It is worth being direct about the economics, because the whole argument for winterizing rests on the gap between what prevention costs and what a failure costs. On the prevention side, the materials are among the cheapest in home maintenance. Foam pipe sleeves, a few insulated faucet covers, a tube of caulk, and a can of expanding foam are inexpensive at any hardware store, and the labor is your own afternoon. Heat tape for a chronic cold spot costs more but is still modest. The entire seasonal routine for a typical home comes in low, and much of it is reusable year to year, since the foam sleeves and faucet covers last multiple seasons.

On the failure side sit the numbers that make prevention obvious. A burst supply line does not just cost the plumbing repair; it costs the water extraction, the structural drying, the replaced drywall and flooring, the possible mold remediation, and the repainting, and those restoration costs dwarf the plumbing itself. Because exact figures vary widely by region, by how much water escaped, by how long it ran before discovery, and by how far it spread, it is not useful to attach a single dollar amount, and any specific number would be invented. What is reliable is the direction and the magnitude: prevention is measured in tens of dollars, a serious burst is measured in thousands or more, and the ratio between them is enormous.

There are secondary costs too. An insurance claim for water damage, even when covered, carries a deductible, can raise future premiums, and takes time and paperwork to resolve. Claims are sometimes contested when an adjuster concludes the homeowner failed to take reasonable precautions, such as leaving the heat off during a freeze, which can shift costs back onto the homeowner. Policies vary in how they treat freeze damage, and a home left vacant or unheated can face a coverage dispute that a maintained, occupied home would not, so relying on insurance as a substitute for prevention is a weak plan. There is the disruption of living around a restoration project, the days without a usable kitchen or bathroom, and the stress of coordinating repairs. And there is the opportunity cost of the January plumber, who is booked solid during a cold snap and prices accordingly, versus the October plumber with an open schedule.

The time cost compounds the money cost during a regional freeze event. When a cold snap bursts pipes across a whole area at once, every plumber is overwhelmed, wait times stretch to days, and a homeowner with a flooded floor may be far down a long list. The restoration companies fill up the same way. A home that came through the freeze intact because its owner spent an afternoon in the fall skips that entire queue. Prevention buys not just the avoided repair cost but the avoided scramble to find help in a market where demand has spiked and everyone qualified is already busy.

Set against all of that, the fall routine is one of the highest-return maintenance tasks a homeowner can do. Few other jobs offer such a large reduction in potential cost for such a small investment of time and money. This is the prevention math that thin, reactive content skips: not just that you should winterize, but that the return on the effort is lopsided enough that skipping it is a genuinely poor bet. The broader logic of spending small now to avoid large repairs later runs through the whole [home maintenance calendar](/2027/12/01/home-maintenance-calendar/), and freeze prevention is one of its clearest cases.

## Winterizing a Vacant or Seasonal Home

A house that will sit empty through the cold, a seasonal cabin, a vacation property, a home whose owner travels for the winter, needs more than the standard routine, because there is no household heat and no one watching to catch a problem early. For a home that will be unoccupied and unheated, the safest approach is to winterize the plumbing fully by removing the water from the system rather than relying on heat to keep it from freezing.

Fully winterizing an empty home means shutting off the water at the main, then draining the entire plumbing system: opening every faucet, flushing the toilets to empty the tanks and bowls, draining the water heater, and clearing the supply lines. Because low points in the piping can hold water even after the faucets run dry, thorough winterizing often uses compressed air to blow the remaining water out of the lines, and adds non-toxic plumbing antifreeze, the kind made for potable systems, to the traps and toilets so the small amount of water that must remain in the drain traps does not freeze and crack the fixtures. This is a specific process, and getting it wrong, leaving water in a low spot or a trap, can still result in a crack, so many owners of seasonal properties have a plumber winterize and de-winterize the home each year, especially the first time.

A middle path, for a home that will be checked periodically or left with utilities on, is to keep the heat set to a temperature high enough to prevent freezing throughout the home, use freeze alarms or smart thermostats that alert you if the temperature drops, and consider a water-leak sensor and an automatic shutoff that cuts the water if a leak is detected. These devices are worth considering for any home left empty in winter, because they catch a failure early when no one is present to notice. Keeping the heat on works only if the power and the heating system stay reliable, so it carries more risk than fully draining the system; a power outage during a cold snap in an unheated, undrained home is a burst-pipe recipe.

The choice between draining fully and heating through depends on how long the home sits empty, how cold it gets, how reliable the power is, and whether anyone can check on it. A cabin closed for the whole winter in a cold region is a candidate for full draining. A home left for a two-week trip in a milder area may be fine with the heat set to hold, the standard day-of moves in place if a freeze is forecast, and a leak sensor as backup. When in doubt, draining is the more certain protection, and having a plumber handle the winterizing of a seasonal property removes the guesswork.

### Should you shut off the water when you leave for winter?

For a home left empty and unheated, yes, shut off the water at the main and drain the system so there is nothing to freeze. For a short trip with the heat left on, shutting the main and draining is optional but wise as backup, since it means a failure while you are away cannot flood the house.

## De-Winterizing: Reopening the System in Spring

Winterizing has a counterpart in spring, and reopening the system carelessly can undo a careful winter or reveal a crack you did not know was there. When the freeze risk has passed for the season, reversing the outdoor and drained-system steps deserves the same attention you gave to shutting them down.

Start with the outdoor faucets. Close the spigot you left open through winter, then reopen the interior shutoff valve feeding it, and go outside and turn the spigot on to check for normal flow and, importantly, for any leak. A hose bib that cracked during winter often shows no sign until the water is turned back on in spring and it leaks inside the wall, so run each spigot and watch and listen for water where it should not be, including checking the wall or ceiling below on the inside. Catching a spring leak from a cracked sillcock early prevents it from running unnoticed. Then reconnect hoses and restore any irrigation system, which for a blown-out system means turning the water back on gradually and checking the zones and heads for leaks or damage.

For a home that was fully drained and winterized, de-winterizing reverses the process: closing the faucets that were left open, refilling the water heater before restoring power or gas to it so the element or burner is not run dry, turning the water back on at the main slowly to let the system refill without a pressure surge, and then checking every fixture and connection for leaks as the system comes back up. If plumbing antifreeze was added to traps and toilets, running the fixtures clears it. Because a drained system can hide a crack that only shows when water and pressure return, watching carefully during the refill is the moment to catch a winter failure. For a seasonal property that a plumber winterized, having the same plumber de-winterize and pressure-test it in spring is a sensible pairing, especially the first year.

The habit worth building is to treat de-winterizing as a deliberate spring task on the same annual routine as the fall shutdown, not an afterthought. A quick spring check of the spigots, the exposed lines that made it through winter, and any equipment that was drained confirms the home came through the cold intact and catches any hidden damage while it is still small.

## Regional Reality: Winterizing in Cold Versus Mild Climates

The winterizing routine is the same everywhere in its logic, but how much of it you need and how urgently varies by climate, and the biggest mistake is assuming your region's usual weather is a reliable guide. Homes in consistently cold regions are generally built for it: water services buried below the frost line, pipes routed through heated interior space, insulated and often conditioned basements, and homeowners who winterize as second nature. In these regions the routine is habit, the vulnerable spots are known and addressed, and the failures that happen are usually from a heating outage or an overlooked exposed line rather than from unpreparedness.

Homes in mild regions are the opposite, and this is where the counterintuitive risk lives. When a house is built in a climate where freezes are rare, the plumbing is routed with little thought to the cold: pipes run through uninsulated attics and vented crawl spaces, exterior walls carry supply lines with thin insulation, outdoor spigots lack freeze-proof design, and the water service may sit at a shallow depth. The homeowners have often never winterized anything, because in most winters they never needed to. Then a rare polar outbreak reaches the region, temperatures crash into the teens or lower for a day or two, and thousands of unprepared homes burst at once. Plumbers are overwhelmed, hardware stores sell out of pipe insulation within hours, and the damage across a mild-climate region during a single hard freeze can exceed what a cold region sees in a normal winter. The homes that come through fine are the ones whose owners took the rare-freeze warning seriously and ran the routine.

The lesson for mild-climate homeowners is to take freeze forecasts seriously precisely because they are rare. When the unusual cold snap appears in the forecast, drip the vulnerable faucets, open the cabinets, hold the heat steady, and if you have not already, disconnect the hoses and cover the spigots, even if it feels like overkill for your area. It is not overkill; it is the exact preparation the cold-region homes do automatically, compressed into the few days a year your region needs it. The transitional regions, places that freeze hard some winters and barely at all in others, benefit from doing the fall preparation every year regardless, so a surprise freeze never catches the pipes undrained.

Home age and construction matter within any region too. Older homes, homes with additions or bump-outs where a wall was opened, homes with pier-and-beam foundations and open crawl spaces, and homes with plumbing run through unconditioned space all carry more exposed pipe than a tightly built modern home on a conditioned slab. Walk your own house with the vulnerable-spots lens rather than assuming your region's reputation protects you, because the pipe in your particular exterior wall does not know what the average winter looks like.

## Apartments, Condos, and Manufactured Homes

Not everyone winterizing a home owns a single-family house with a basement and a crawl space, and the routine shifts for other housing types, though the underlying goal never changes.

Renters in apartments and rented houses share responsibility with the landlord. The landlord owns the pipes and is generally responsible for the building's plumbing and insulation, but leases often require the tenant to take reasonable care, which includes keeping enough heat on to prevent freezing and reporting problems promptly. A tenant who turns the heat off entirely to save money while traveling, then returns to a burst pipe, may find themselves on the wrong side of the lease. The practical renter moves are the free ones: keep the heat set to a freeze-safe temperature even when away, open cabinet doors during a hard freeze, let a trickle run if a fixture is on an exterior wall, disconnect any hoses on a private spigot, and report drafts, poorly insulated pipes, or a fixture that slows during cold to the landlord before it becomes a burst. Know where the unit's shutoff is if there is one, and know how to reach the landlord or building maintenance fast if a pipe fails.

Condo and townhouse owners fall between the two, owning the interior and often the pipes within the unit while the association handles the building envelope and shared systems. Shared walls between units can carry water lines, and a vacant neighboring unit with the heat off can freeze a line in a shared wall, so it is worth knowing the building's winter policies and whether the association requires owners to maintain heat. The same interior moves apply within your unit.

Manufactured and mobile homes have a specific vulnerability: the water lines often run through the underbelly beneath the floor, exposed to outside air, and the skirting around the base is the main barrier against the cold. Intact, well-sealed skirting keeps the space beneath the home warmer and protects those underbelly lines, so checking and repairing skirting before winter is a key winterizing step for these homes. Heat tape on the exposed underbelly supply line, and on the water heater connections, is common in colder regions, and the underbelly insulation should be intact. Because the lines are harder to reach and the exposure is different, manufactured-home owners in cold regions often rely on heat tape and skirting together, and a rare-freeze warning in a milder area is a cue to check the skirting and the exposed connections.

Across all these housing types, the constants hold: keep heat reaching the pipes, protect the exposed lines, relieve pressure with a trickle during deep cold, and know where the water shuts off. Only the specific vulnerable spots and the division of responsibility change.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you winterize your plumbing?

Winterizing your plumbing is a fall routine plus a set of day-of moves for hard freezes. In fall, disconnect and drain garden hoses, shut off and drain outdoor faucets, insulate exposed pipes in unheated spaces with foam sleeves, seal drafts around the foundation and where pipes pass through exterior walls, and locate your main water shutoff to confirm it turns. When a hard freeze is forecast, open cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls so heat reaches the pipes, hold the thermostat steady day and night, and let a trickle run from the most vulnerable faucets. The fall work handles the exposed fixtures and still water; the day-of moves handle the pressure that freezing creates inside interior lines. Together they prevent the large majority of frozen-pipe damage for a small cost.

### Q: How do you prevent frozen pipes in winter?

Preventing frozen pipes comes down to keeping vulnerable lines above freezing and relieving the pressure that freezing builds. Insulate exposed pipes in basements, crawl spaces, garages, and attics, seal the drafts that let cold air reach them, and disconnect outdoor hoses so spigots can drain. During a hard freeze, keep household heat steady and a few degrees higher than usual, open cabinet doors so warm air reaches pipes in exterior walls, and let a trickle run from the faucets served by the most exposed lines. The trickle matters because an open faucet gives water an escape route, and it is trapped pressure between ice and a closed tap, not the ice itself, that usually bursts a pipe. Do the fall preparation before the first freeze and layer the day-of moves on top when deep cold arrives.

### Q: At what temperature do pipes freeze?

Water freezes at thirty-two degrees Fahrenheit, but a pipe carrying water inside a home does not freeze the instant the air hits that mark, because it takes time for the pipe to lose enough heat. The commonly cited threshold for taking precautions is around twenty degrees Fahrenheit for unprotected pipes in exterior walls or unheated spaces. That figure is a durable guideline rather than a hard rule. A poorly insulated pipe in a windy, exposed spot can freeze at higher temperatures, while a well-protected interior line can hold well below twenty degrees. Duration and wind matter as much as the raw number: sustained cold with wind freezes pipes faster than a brief overnight dip. Treat sustained cold in the low twenties, especially overnight, as your cue to run the freeze-day routine, and confirm your local forecast rather than relying on the number alone.

### Q: Does leaving a faucet running prevent frozen pipes?

Yes, letting a faucet run a small trickle during a hard freeze helps prevent frozen pipes in two ways. Moving water resists freezing better than still water, and more importantly, an open faucet relieves the pressure that builds between an ice blockage and a closed tap. That trapped pressure, not the ice itself, is what usually splits a pipe, so an open faucet gives the water somewhere to go. The trickle does not need to be heavy; a steady drip to a thin stream is enough. Run both the hot and cold sides, since both supply lines are at risk, and prioritize the faucets fed by pipes in exterior walls or unheated spaces. It is a temporary measure for the coldest nights, not a permanent habit, and the small water cost is trivial against the price of a burst pipe.

### Q: How do you safely thaw frozen pipes?

To thaw a frozen pipe safely, first open the faucet served by that line, both hot and cold sides on a mixing faucet, so melting water can flow and pressure cannot build behind the ice. Then apply gentle, gradual heat to the frozen section, working from the faucet end back toward the ice rather than starting in the middle. A hair dryer, a heat lamp, a space heater kept well clear of anything flammable, or towels soaked in warm water all work. Never use a blowtorch or any open flame, which can heat the pipe unevenly enough to burst it and is a serious fire risk in the framed, insulated spaces where frozen pipes live. If the pipe is inside a wall you cannot reach, turn up the house heat and be patient. If water begins spraying, the pipe has cracked; shut off the water and call a plumber.

### Q: What plumbing tasks should you do in fall to prepare for the season?

The fall plumbing routine has an outdoor phase and an indoor phase. Outside, disconnect and drain every garden hose, shut off and drain the outdoor faucets by closing the interior valve and opening the spigot, cover spigots that lack an interior shutoff, and winterize or blow out any in-ground irrigation system. Inside, insulate exposed pipes in unheated basements, crawl spaces, garages, and attics with foam sleeves, add heat tape to any line with a freezing history, and seal the drafts around the foundation, rim joist, and pipe penetrations that let cold air reach the lines. Also do the free knowledge work: locate your main water shutoff and the fixture shutoffs and confirm they all turn, so a seized valve is a calm October discovery rather than a freeze-day emergency. Complete this before the first hard freeze, not during one.

### Q: How do you winterize an outdoor faucet or hose bib?

Start by disconnecting and draining the garden hose, since a hose left attached traps water against the spigot and prevents it from draining, which is the main reason hose bibs crack. If the line feeding the spigot has an interior shutoff valve, usually just inside the wall in the basement or crawl space, close it, then go outside and open the spigot to drain the water sitting between the valve and the spigot. Leave the spigot open through winter so any residual water has room to expand. For frost-proof sillcocks, which shut off deep inside the heated wall, disconnecting the hose is still required for the drain-back design to work. For spigots with no interior shutoff, an insulated foam faucet cover adds protection, though draining is always better than covering alone.

### Q: Can frozen pipes burst and cause water damage?

Yes, and burst frozen pipes are among the most damaging things that happen to a home in winter. When ice forms in a closed line, it pushes water toward a closed faucet, and the pressure that builds splits the pipe, often at a spot away from the ice itself. Frequently the ice plugs the crack so no water escapes until the pipe thaws, which means the flood often arrives during the thaw rather than the freeze, when you may have stopped watching. A supply line under household pressure releases many gallons per minute, and inside a wall or ceiling the water soaks framing, insulation, and drywall before anyone notices. The repair is rarely just the pipe; it usually includes structural drying, replaced drywall and flooring, possible mold remediation, and repainting. The moment you find a burst, shut off the water first, then call a plumber.

### Q: What causes a pipe to burst when it freezes?

A pipe bursts not because of the cold directly but because of pressure. As water freezes it expands, and the growing ice inside a closed line drives a column of still-liquid water toward a closed faucet or fixture. With nowhere to go, that trapped water builds pressure until the weakest point in the pipe fails, which is why the split often appears in a section that was not even frozen solid. This mechanism is why keeping a pipe from freezing completely is only half of prevention; relieving the pressure is the other half. An open faucet during a freeze gives the water an escape route so pressure never reaches the bursting point, which is why letting a trickle run is such an effective and cheap protection alongside insulation and heat.

### Q: How long does it take for pipes to freeze in cold weather?

There is no single time, because it depends on how exposed the pipe is, how cold the air is, whether wind is stripping heat away, and how much insulation or heat surrounds the line. An unprotected pipe in a windy, exposed spot with air in the low twenties or teens can begin freezing within a few hours, especially overnight when temperatures bottom out. A well-insulated line in heated interior space may not freeze at all through the same night. Sustained cold is more dangerous than a brief dip, because it gives the pipe time to lose all its heat and the water time to freeze solid. This is why the overnight hours of a prolonged hard freeze are the critical window, and why the day-of moves focus on carrying vulnerable lines through those coldest hours.

### Q: Is pipe insulation alone enough to prevent freezing?

Insulation helps significantly but is not a complete solution on its own, because foam sleeves slow the loss of heat rather than adding any. An insulated pipe stays warm longer, but in a prolonged deep freeze with no heat source nearby, the water inside can still eventually reach freezing. Insulation works best combined with the other measures: sealing the drafts that let cold air reach the pipe, keeping some household heat circulating to the space, and running a trickle during the worst cold to relieve pressure. For a chronically freezing line in an especially exposed spot, heat tape, which actively warms the pipe, is stronger than insulation alone, and rerouting the pipe into heated space is the permanent fix. Treat insulation as the foundation of prevention, not the entirety of it.

### Q: How do you winterize a house that will sit empty all winter?

For a home left empty and unheated through winter, the safest approach is to remove the water from the system entirely rather than relying on heat. Shut off the water at the main, then drain the plumbing: open every faucet, flush the toilets to empty the tanks and bowls, drain the water heater, and clear the supply lines. Because low points and traps hold water even after the faucets run dry, thorough winterizing often blows the remaining water out of the lines with compressed air and adds non-toxic plumbing antifreeze to the drain traps and toilets so the small amount of unavoidable water cannot freeze and crack a fixture. Getting this wrong can still cause a crack, so many owners of seasonal properties have a plumber winterize and de-winterize the home each year, at least the first time.

### Q: Should you leave the heat on when you go away in winter?

If you are leaving for a short trip and not draining the system, yes, leave the heat on set high enough to prevent freezing, which is a lower temperature than comfort but not off. Turning the heat off entirely in an undrained home during cold weather invites frozen pipes. Setting the thermostat to hold a steady, freeze-safe temperature keeps the interior spaces and wall cavities warm enough to protect the lines. For extra safety, shut off the water at the main and drain the system as backup, so a heating failure while you are away cannot flood the house, and consider a freeze alarm or a leak sensor that alerts you if something goes wrong. Keeping the heat on protects only as long as the power and heating system stay reliable, so pairing it with a shutoff is the more certain approach.

### Q: Can PEX or plastic pipes freeze and burst?

Yes. No pipe material is freeze-proof, including PEX and other plastics. PEX is more forgiving than rigid copper or steel because the flexible tubing can expand somewhat as ice forms, so it sometimes survives a freeze that would split a metal pipe. Sometimes, not always: under a hard, prolonged freeze PEX can still burst, and its fittings and connections can fail even when a run of tubing holds. Rigid plastics like PVC and CPVC actually become brittle in the cold and can crack readily. The flexibility of PEX buys margin, not immunity, so treating any plastic line as safe to leave exposed is a mistake. Insulate, seal drafts, keep heat reaching the line, and trickle during deep cold for plastic pipes just as you would for copper.

### Q: How do you keep a well pump from freezing?

A well pump, its pressure tank, and the connecting pipes freeze when they sit in an unheated well house, pit, garage, or basement corner exposed to the cold. Protect them by insulating the well house or utility space and any exposed pipe, sealing the drafts, and adding a safe, thermostatically controlled heat source such as a heat lamp or a heater rated for the space and kept clear of anything flammable. Heat tape on the exposed pipe and tank connections helps in colder regions. The same fire cautions apply as anywhere else, doubly so in a small building full of equipment. For a well-system home left empty through winter, have the pump and tank winterized per the equipment's guidance, often with a professional, since a frozen pump takes down the whole water supply and is expensive to replace.

### Q: Why did my pipes freeze even though the heat was on?

Household heat protects pipes only where it can reach them, and several gaps let a pipe freeze despite a warm house. A supply line inside an exterior wall, in a vented crawl space, in an unheated garage, or in an attic sits outside the heated envelope, so the thermostat setting does not protect it. A nighttime setback that dropped the temperature during the coldest overnight hours can freeze a marginal line. Closed cabinet doors keep warm air from reaching under-sink pipes in exterior walls. A draft blowing cold air across a pipe can overwhelm the surrounding warmth. And insulation without draft sealing leaves a pipe exposed to moving cold air. The fix is to get heat to the exposed spots, hold the thermostat steady during a freeze, open cabinets, seal drafts, and trickle the vulnerable faucets.
