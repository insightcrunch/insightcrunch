## Egypt's Longest Argument: Nationalism and the Making of Egyptian Identity

What did it mean to be Egyptian when Egypt was not ruled by Egyptians? After 1882 the country's treasury, its army, its railways, and its village headmen all answered, directly or at arm's length, to British authority, while Egyptians remained the people doing the farming, the fighting, and the paying. Into that dissonance flowed the most sustained debate in the country's modern history: not merely how to end foreign rule, but what the Egypt that deserved independence actually was. This is where modern **Egyptian nationalism** stops being one idea and becomes a contest between Egyptian, Arab, and Islamic identities, a debate that asking what Egyptians were opens rather than settles. The pages that follow trace how that debate was born under occupation, in newspapers and party halls, in village punishments and lawyer's offices, before it split into rival visions of the nation.

## What Did It Mean to Be Egyptian Under Occupation?

The British occupation arrived in September 1882 as both a military fact and a psychological one. The bombardment of Alexandria in July and the battle of Tel el-Kebir in September crushed Ahmad Urabi's revolt in a matter of weeks, and what followed was what Lord Cromer's administrators called a "veiled" presence: British officials did not formally annex Egypt but exercised control through the khedive's government, through financial advisers, through the Department of Public Works, through British inspectors in every province. A farmer in the Delta might never see a British soldier in a given year, yet the cotton he was pressed to grow, the tax collector who visited his village, and the magistrate who heard his disputes all operated inside a system whose final authority spoke English and answered to London.

The memory of Urabi refused to die with his defeat. Exiled to Ceylon, the former officer became a symbol that British policy could not bury: the first native Egyptian to lead a mass movement against both the khedival court and foreign interference. As the historian Juan Cole has argued, the Urabi revolt represented a genuine national movement rooted in the Egyptian middle strata of army officers, landowners, and the ulama, not a palace quarrel or a debt crisis, and its crushing left behind a vocabulary of betrayal and redemption that later nationalists would draw on for decades. Villagers who had joined Urabi's cause remembered the red flags; schoolboys in the 1890s learned his name in whispers; and every subsequent challenge to the occupation inherited, willingly or not, the unfinished business of 1882.

It was precisely the veiled quality of British rule that made the identity question urgent. Direct colonial rule would at least have given Egyptians a single foreign enemy with a face. Instead they lived inside a political theater where Egypt remained nominally Ottoman, nominally sovereign under its khedive, and in practice British. Who was the "nation" supposed to liberate itself? The peasantry that bore the tax burden? The Turco-Circassian and Ottoman-Egyptian elite who dominated the court and much of the large landholding class? The Arabic-speaking townspeople whose numbers and literacy were growing? The Coptic minority whose stake in any national project had to be defined rather than assumed? Under occupation, these were not academic questions. They determined who could speak for Egypt, who counted as Egyptian, and what loyalty meant when the state's own rulers were compromised by foreign power.

## The Soil Nationalism Grew In

The first organized answer came from a young lawyer who turned French education into Egyptian political ammunition. Mustafa Kamil, born in 1874 to a modestly comfortable family and trained in law in Cairo and France, founded the National Party (al-Hizb al-Watani) and in 1900 launched the newspaper al-Liwa, The Standard, which became the most-read Arabic political paper of its generation. Kamil's program was simple to state and electric to hear: immediate British evacuation, restoration of Egyptian independence, and loyalty to the Ottoman caliph as the counterweight to British power. His oratory, his relentless lobbying in Paris and London, and al-Liwa's daily hammering at the occupation made him, by the time of his death in 1908 at only thirty-three, what many Egyptians then and later called the founder of modern Egyptian nationalism.

Kamil's nationalism carried a distinct Islamic and Ottoman coloring that later historians have debated at length. He wrapped his demands in devotion to the caliphate and presented Egypt's struggle as part of the defense of Islam against European encroachment, a framing that rallied the religiously inclined and won him admiration from Sultan Abd al-Hamid II. Contemporary critics and later scholars, have asked whether this Ottomanism diluted his Egyptianism; his defenders reply that in a world of empires, Ottoman loyalty was the only available geopolitical weapon against Britain. What cannot be disputed is that Kamil made mass political journalism a fact of Egyptian life and gave ordinary readers a language for grievance that the government could not confiscate.

That grievance acquired a martyr event in June 1906, at the village of Dinshaway in the Menufiya province. British officers shooting pigeons near the village quarreled with peasants over crop damage, one officer died of sunstroke fleeing the confrontation, and the British response was a special tribunal that hanged four villagers and flogged dozens of others before their families' eyes. The injustice was so stark that it reverberated far beyond Egypt: the journalist George Bernard Shaw in London called the tribunal's proceedings one of the empire's disgraces, and inside Egypt Dinshaway became what historians routinely describe as the galvanizing wound of the early nationalist movement. Recruitment to the National Party surged; al-Liwa's circulation soared; and the incident furnished nationalists with an image that needed no commentary, the spectacle of Egyptian bodies broken to uphold the prestige of the occupier.

### How did the Arabic press spread nationalist ideas under British rule?

Arabic newspapers carried nationalist ideas past censorship and into cafes, clubs, and village gatherings where papers were read aloud to illiterate listeners. Mustafa Kamil's al-Liwa, founded in 1900, combined anti-British editorials with European dispatches that educated readers about imperialism worldwide, turning subscribers into a network of politically awakened men across the country.

The press did its work in a landscape already shaped by a rival vision of the nation. Ahmad Lutfi al-Sayyid, a philosopher and journalist often called the teacher of the generation, founded the Umma Party (Hizb al-Umma) in 1907 as a deliberate counterweight to Kamil's fire. Where Kamil demanded immediate evacuation and leaned on the Ottoman caliphate, Lutfi al-Sayyid preached gradualism: constitutional reform, education, and the slow cultivation of an Egyptian character distinct from the Ottoman connection. His newspaper al-Jarida, founded the same year, became the voice of this Egyptianist tendency, arguing that Egypt's identity should rest on its own soil and its own people rather than on loyalty to Istanbul. This was the first clear articulation of what would later be called Pharaonism's seedbed, though Lutfi al-Sayyid himself framed it in liberal and civic terms rather than ancient glory: Egyptians were Egyptians because they lived in Egypt, worked its land, and shared its interests.

The rivalry between the National Party and the Umma Party was not merely personal, though personal it certainly was. It embodied the first fracture in modern Egyptian nationalism, between a vision that sought strength in the wider Islamic and Ottoman world and one that sought it in Egypt's separateness. Both operated under the same veiled occupation, both used the press as their weapon, and both drew on the same wound of Dinshaway. Yet they pointed the identity question in opposite directions, and their contest would outlive them both.

What held these early movements together, for all their differences, was the daily texture of [the British occupation of Egypt](/2015/01/01/british-occupation-of-egypt/): the schools where Egyptian children were taught that their capacities required European tutelage, the civil service where British advisers held the posts that mattered, the courts of mixed jurisdiction that reminded merchants daily of legal subordination. Cromer's administration kept Egypt solvent and built real infrastructure, facts that British apologists repeated endlessly, but solvency without self-rule could not answer the question the newspapers had taught Egyptians to ask. As the nineteenth century turned into the twentieth, a new generation of effendiyya, the Arabic-educated professional class of lawyers, teachers, journalists, and minor officials, found in nationalism not just a politics but an identity to inhabit, one that promised that being Egyptian could mean something more than being governed.

That promise split into three competing answers in the decades that followed, each claiming to name the true Egypt: an Egyptian territorial nationalism rooted in the land itself, an Arab nationalism that saw Egypt as the natural leader of the Arabic-speaking world, and an Islamic identity that placed Egypt's meaning inside a wider community of faith. The men and women who argued for these visions did not experience them as academic options but as the stakes of liberation itself, because the answer would determine the borders of the state, the language of the schools, the allies of the government, and the content of the soul.

## The Nahda's Egyptianism: Older Roots of the Argument

The contest between Egyptian, Arab, and Islamic answers to the question of who the Egyptians are did not begin with the press battles of the early twentieth century. It was already alive, in recognizable form, two generations earlier, in the classrooms, meeting rooms, and exile journals of the Nahda, the cultural and intellectual renaissance that swept the nineteenth-century Arabic-speaking Ottoman world. The argument that Egypt itself constituted a homeland deserving loyalty above tribe, dynasty, or empire has roots reaching back to Rifa'a al-Tahtawi's return from Paris in 1831 and running through Ali Mubarak's school reforms, the revolutionary mobilization of 1879 to 1882, Jamal al-Din al-Afghani's pan-Islamic agitation in Cairo, and the organizational work of Muhammad Farid after 1908. Tracing these roots matters because none of the three later strands, the territorial Egyptian answer, the Arab answer, or the Islamic answer, invented its vocabulary from nothing.

### Hubb al-Watan: Tahtawi Plants the Word Homeland

Rifa'a al-Tahtawi was born in 1801 in Tahta, in Upper Egypt, educated at al-Azhar, and then, in 1826, dispatched to France as the imam attached to a student mission sent by Muhammad Ali to study European arts and sciences. He spent five years in Paris, learned French, and returned with Takhlis al-Ibriz fi Talkhis Bariz, an account of his travels describing French institutions, French law, and, most consequentially, French patriotism. What struck him was not only the technology of the West but the idea that a citizen could love his country as a distinct moral object. He carried back a phrase and made it Egyptian: hubb al-watan, love of the homeland.

The significance of this phrase is easy to underestimate. In Tahtawi's Ottoman-Egyptian world, loyalty flowed upward to the ruler and outward to the Islamic umma or the Ottoman caliphate; a territorial Egypt that one could love as a homeland had no settled political meaning. Tahtawi gave it one. He argued that Egyptians were one people bound to one land, that their ancient history, their pharaonic past included, was a source of legitimate pride, and that loyalty to the watan did not contradict loyalty to Islam but completed it. He founded the School of Languages, the Madrasat al-Alsun, in 1835 and shaped a generation of translators, teachers, and bureaucrats who carried his vocabulary into the state apparatus.

A qualification is worth stating plainly. Tahtawi was an Ottoman Egyptian loyal to the dynasty that employed him, and his Egyptianism was inclusive rather than separatist: he imagined Egypt flourishing within the Ottoman Empire, not breaking from it. Some historians read him as a proto-nationalist, others as a reformer whose thought remained Ottomanist to the end. The fair reading is that he was both, and this ambiguity made his legacy usable by later partisans of all three answers to the identity question.

### Ali Mubarak: The Nation as a Construction Site

If Tahtawi supplied the idea of the homeland, Ali Mubarak supplied something just as necessary: the conviction that the homeland could be built. Born in 1823, educated in Egypt and in France, Mubarak was the architect of modern Egyptian public education and one of the most consequential urban planners of the nineteenth century. He drafted plans for the modern districts of Cairo, with their wide boulevards and ordered street grids, and, as minister of public works and education under the khedives, reorganized schooling on a scale Egypt had never seen. His great work, al-Khitat al-Tawfiqiyya, a twenty-volume topographical and historical survey of Egypt completed in the 1880s, was both a work of scholarship and an act of possession: a written claim that Egypt was knowable, mappable, and administratively one.

Mubarak matters for the identity argument because he treated Egypt as a project rather than merely a sentiment. Tahtawi had taught Egyptians to love the watan; Mubarak showed them the watan as something their own hands were remaking, in classrooms, in street grids, in irrigation plans. This was nationalism as pedagogy and engineering, and it planted the assumption, carried forward by every later Egyptianist writer, that identity could be deliberately cultivated through institutions. The schools and bureaucracies he built would, within a generation, produce the very class of educated Egyptians who turned against foreign control of the state.

### Afghani in Cairo: The Islamic Answer Takes Shape

The third root of the later argument grew in the same Cairo soil but pointed in a different direction. Jamal al-Din al-Afghani arrived in Egypt in 1871 and stayed, with interruptions, until his expulsion in 1879. He was the most famous pan-Islamic agitator of the nineteenth century, preaching that the Muslim world could resist European domination only by returning to a purified, rational Islam and uniting politically across borders. In Cairo he gathered a circle of young disciples that included the Azharite Muhammad Abduh, who would become grand mufti and one of the towering reformers of modern Islam. Afghani's Cairo lectures and journalism introduced a powerful counter-argument to Tahtawi's Egyptianism: that the true homeland was the umma, the community of Muslims, and that territorial nationalism was a European import designed to divide Muslims against one another.

After his expulsion, Afghani carried the argument into print. In 1884, from Paris, he and Abduh founded the newspaper al-Urwah al-Wuthqa, the Firmest Bond, which circulated secretly across the Muslim world. Its message was uncompromising: Muslims must unite against imperialism, reform their religion from within, and reject both blind imitation of the West and blind submission to corrupt rulers. The paper lasted less than a year before French pressure shut it down, but its ideas entered the bloodstream of Egyptian politics. Abduh's later career is covered elsewhere in this series; what matters here is that the Afghani-Abduh current established the Islamic answer to the identity question as a permanent rival to the Egyptian and Arab answers. From the 1870s onward, no Egyptian discussion of who Egyptians were could proceed without reckoning with the claim that Egyptianness was subordinate to Muslimness.

### The First Organization: The National Party of the Urabi Era

The Urabi revolution was the first mass movement in modern Egyptian history to organize explicitly around the name of the nation. The National Party, al-Hizb al-Watani, formed in 1879, gave institutional form to a coalition of army officers, large landowners, Azhar scholars, and journalists who opposed the Anglo-French financial control over Egypt and the Turco-Circassian officer elite that dominated the army. Its leader, Ahmed Urabi, an Egyptian-born officer, became the symbol of a demand that Egyptians govern Egypt. The movement's slogan, Egypt for the Egyptians, was the most direct political translation of Tahtawi's hubb al-watan that the nineteenth century produced.

The National Party of the Urabi era was not the same organization as the National Party that Mustafa Kamil would found in 1907, though the name was deliberately revived. The Urabi party was a loose coalition rather than a modern political party, and it contained genuine contradictions: its supporters included pan-Islamists who looked to the Ottoman caliph and Egyptianists who distrusted him, landowners who wanted constitutional limits on the khedive and officers who wanted pay and promotion. What united them was opposition to foreign domination and to a ruling order that treated native Egyptians as second-class subjects in their own country.

The revolution's defeat at Tel el-Kebir in 1882 and the beginning of the British occupation seemed to settle the question by force. The party was dissolved, Urabi was exiled, and Egypt entered a period of direct British control. But the defeat also proved that the Egyptianist idea had mass mobilizing power: an idea that could put an army in the field and a government in Cairo, however briefly, could not be unthought. When Kamil revived the National Party name a quarter century later, he was claiming an inheritance, and everyone understood the claim.

### 1904: The Entente Cordiale and the Closing of the Diplomatic Door

For the first decade of the twentieth century, Mustafa Kamil's strategy rested on a single diplomatic bet: that France, Britain's great rival, could be persuaded to press for the evacuation of Egypt. Kamil cultivated French opinion assiduously, wrote in French, and lectured in Paris. The Entente Cordiale of April 1904 destroyed that bet at a stroke: France recognized Britain's position in Egypt in exchange for British recognition of France's position in Morocco. France had abandoned the Egyptian cause in return for a free hand elsewhere.

The effect on Egyptian nationalism was profound and double-edged. It closed Kamil's diplomatic door: no great power remained willing to champion evacuation. But it also pushed the nationalists inward. If salvation would not come from Paris, it would have to come from Egypt itself, from Egyptian unity, organization, and a definition of the homeland that did not depend on foreign patrons. The Entente marks the moment when Egyptian nationalism, which had been in part a diplomatic campaign, became primarily a domestic one: building parties, newspapers, schools, and economic institutions at home. This inward turn intensified the identity debate, because a movement that had to mobilize Egyptians themselves needed to answer the question of who those Egyptians were, in terms that Egyptians found compelling. The Egyptian, Arab, and Islamic strands, which had coexisted uneasily in Kamil's broad coalition, now had to compete openly for the allegiance of the same domestic audience.

### Muhammad Farid: Stewardship After 1908

Mustafa Kamil died in February 1908 at the age of thirty-three, exhausted by his campaigning. The leadership of the National Party passed to Muhammad Farid, his closest associate, and Farid's stewardship from 1908 onward carried the older roots of the argument into the new century's second decade. Farid was in some ways a more systematic thinker than Kamil: where Kamil had been the orator and diplomat, Farid was the organizer and the ideologue, working to give the party a coherent doctrine. He kept the demand for immediate evacuation at the center of the party's program, but he also deepened its Islamic and Ottoman coloring, emphasizing loyalty to the Ottoman caliphate and the bonds of Islamic solidarity as the framework within which Egyptian independence should be sought.

This emphasis matters because it shows the Islamic strand gaining ground inside the nationalist movement itself, not merely outside it as a rival. Under Farid, the party's Egyptianism was increasingly articulated in Islamic terms: Egypt was a Muslim country whose independence was a religious as well as a national duty. Farid's long exile and his voluminous writings kept the party alive as an idea even as the British authorities harassed its organization. He also represents a bridge to the later contest: his insistence that Egyptian nationalism and Islamic solidarity were inseparable was an early formulation of the position that later Islamic movements would claim as their own, while his organizational model, a disciplined party with a press, a program, and an international presence, was the template that later Egyptianist and Arabist movements would borrow.

By the time the First World War broke over Egypt, with the British deposing the khedive, declaring a protectorate, and severing the Ottoman link in 1914, the root system was complete. Tahtawi had named the homeland and taught its love. Mubarak had built the schools and the city that made the homeland tangible. The Urabi movement had proved that the nation could be organized and could fight. Afghani and Abduh had established the Islamic answer as a permanent rival. The Entente Cordiale had forced the movement to look inward. And Farid had carried the party through the death of its founder and into the era of mass politics. None of this settled the argument. That was the point: the older roots did not produce a single answer; they produced the soil in which three answers could grow, each with nineteenth-century credentials, each able to claim that it was the authentic heir of the Nahda. When the writers and politicians of the 1920s and after began to argue, in earnest and at length, whether Egypt was fundamentally Egyptian, Arab, or Islamic, they were not inventing a debate. They were harvesting one.

## Three Answers to One Question

The occupation gave Egyptian nationalism its urgency, but it did not give it a single definition. Once the British settled into the machinery of the state, Egyptian thinkers faced a prior question that politics alone could not settle. What was the community they claimed to speak for? The answers they offered did not arrive one after another in tidy succession. They overlapped, argued with each other, and borrowed each other's language, and the argument itself became one of the most durable features of modern Egyptian life. Three broad answers dominated the conversation. One located the nation in the territory of the Nile Valley. One located it in the Arabic language and the kinship of Arab peoples. One located it in Islam and the community of believers. Each claimed to describe the same Egyptians, and each implied a different destiny.

This disagreement should not be mistaken for confusion. The thinkers behind these strands were serious readers of their own history, and each drew on real materials from Egyptian life. The territorialists pointed to the valley's geography, which had bound farmers to a single river system for millennia regardless of who ruled in Cairo or Constantinople. The Arabists pointed to the language spoken in every Egyptian market and mosque, and to the shared literary heritage that connected Cairo to Damascus and Baghdad. The Islamists pointed to the faith that ordered daily life, law, and worship for the overwhelming majority. All three descriptions were true in some sense, which is why none of them could fully defeat the others. Historians of the period often emphasize that ordinary Egyptians did not choose between these identities the way partisans choose platforms. A farmer in the Delta could feel Egyptian in his attachment to the land, Arab in his speech and poetry, and Muslim in his prayers without sensing any contradiction. The competition happened mostly among intellectuals and organizers, who needed sharper definitions because they were building institutions: parties, newspapers, schools, and movements that each required a story about who belonged and why.

### The Egyptian Strand: A Nation of the Nile Valley

The first and, for several decades, the most politically consequential answer was the territorial one. Egypt, in this telling, was a watan, a homeland with boundaries drawn by the river and the desert, inhabited by a people whose continuity ran deeper than any dynasty. The Arabic word misr carried both meanings at once, the country and its capital, which suited the strand's central claim that the land itself was the nation. Rulers had come and gone: Pharaohs, Persians, Greeks, Romans, Arab caliphs, Mamluks, Ottomans. The fellahin had stayed, planting and harvesting along the same river through every change of masters. This reading gave Egyptian nationalism a lineage that no empire could interrupt, because the nation was not the state or the throne. It was the valley and its people.

Ahmad Lutfi al-Sayyid, often described as the philosopher of this Egyptianism, gave the idea its most polished intellectual form in the first decades of the twentieth century. As a journalist, educator, and later rector of the Egyptian University, he argued that Egypt constituted a distinct nation with its own interests, character, and historical personality, separate from the Ottoman Empire and distinct even from the wider Arab world. His liberalism was inseparable from this territorial claim. If Egypt was a nation of its own, then it deserved the institutions of a nation: a constitution, a parliament, a free press, and schools that formed citizens rather than subjects. Lutfi al-Sayyid's famous emphasis on education as the foundation of national life followed directly. A people who knew themselves as a nation could govern themselves; a people who did not would be governed by others. Some scholars credit him with translating European liberal nationalism into an Egyptian idiom, while others stress how deeply his thought drew on indigenous materials, the Arabic press of the nahda, and the constitutional experiments of the late nineteenth century. Both readings capture something real about his project, which was synthesis rather than imitation.

The fellah stood at the moral center of this strand, and the choice was deliberate. Where Ottoman loyalists had looked to the sultan and pan-Islamists had looked to the caliphate, the Egyptianists looked downward to the peasant cultivator as the authentic Egyptian, the figure least touched by foreign courts and foreign tongues. This was partly romance. Most of the men who wrote about the fellah lived in cities, wrote in literary Arabic, and wore European suits. But the romance did political work. It allowed nationalists to claim that their movement expressed the nation from below rather than merely the grievances of an urban elite, and it gave the demand for independence a social depth that constitutional arguments alone could not supply. The fellah's labor on the land became a kind of title deed. Egypt belonged to those who worked it, not to the British officials who administered it or the Ottoman dynasty that claimed sovereignty over it. Painters, novelists, and later filmmakers would return to this figure again and again, making the peasant the visual shorthand of the nation for much of the twentieth century.

Territorial Egyptianism also shaped how nationalists read their own past. If the nation was the valley across time, then Egypt's history was one continuous story rather than a series of foreign occupations interrupted by brief native interludes. This did not mean ignoring the Arab conquest or the Islamic centuries, which the Egyptianists folded into the national story as chapters rather than ruptures. It did mean resisting any account that made Egypt a mere province of some larger whole, whether Ottoman, Arab, or Islamic. The boundaries of the watan mattered because they defined the community that had the right to decide Egypt's future. This was the strand that carried the Wafd to power after the First World War, and it supplied the language in which the demand for complete independence was most often framed. When nationalists spoke of Egypt for the Egyptians, they were speaking this strand's language, and its echo can be heard in debates about Egyptian distinctiveness long after the particular battles of the interwar years had faded. Scholars disagree about how widely this ideology penetrated beyond the educated classes, with some arguing that peasant communities experienced nationalism primarily through local grievances like taxation and conscription, but few dispute that territorial Egyptianism provided the dominant vocabulary of public politics between the two world wars.

### The Arab Strand: A Nation of Language and Kinship

The second answer located the nation not in a bounded territory but in a shared language and the kinship it implied. Arabic, in this account, was more than a medium of communication. It was the vessel of a common literature, a common historical memory, and a common destiny stretching from the Atlantic to the Gulf. An Egyptian who read the pre-Islamic poets, the Quran, and the classical historians belonged to a community far larger than the Nile Valley, and the political implications followed. If Arabs were one nation, then their fragmentation into separate states under Ottoman and then European rule was an unnatural condition to be overcome. This was the core of Arabism as Egyptian intellectuals encountered it, and it offered a powerful alternative to the territorial story.

The early currents of this thinking reached Egypt from the Syrian and Levantine writers who had made Cairo their refuge and their printing press. Abd al-Rahman al-Kawakibi, a Syrian Arab thinker whose writings circulated widely in Egypt around the turn of the century, argued that the Arabs possessed a natural capacity for self-rule that despotism had stifled, and he linked Arab revival to a reformed Islam led by Arabs rather than Turks. His work gave Arabist ideas a moral and religious charge they might otherwise have lacked. Rashid Rida, the Syrian-born Islamic reformer who settled in Cairo and edited the influential journal al-Manar, carried related ideas further by sketching visions of an Arab caliphate that would restore leadership of the Muslim world to the Arabs. Rida's thought straddled the Arab and Islamic strands, which is one reason his legacy remains contested. Some historians read him as primarily an Islamic reformer for whom Arabism was instrumental, a means of reviving the faith. Others see a genuine Arab nationalist whose religious language clothed an ethnic and linguistic claim. The dispute itself reveals how porous the boundaries between these strands could be in practice.

Yet Arabism remained, for several decades, the secondary current in Egyptian political life, and the reasons are instructive. The territorial strand had first claim on the energies of the independence struggle because the immediate enemy was concrete and local: the British administration in Cairo, the British army in the barracks, the British adviser in every ministry. A nationalism defined by the valley named this enemy precisely and promised a definite prize, an independent Egyptian state. Arabism named a larger and more distant prize, the unity of peoples still under various forms of foreign rule, and it asked Egyptians to subordinate their particular struggle to a collective one whose leadership and program remained vague. Many Egyptian nationalists, including some who admired Arab culture deeply, suspected that Arab unity would dilute Egypt's hard-won sense of itself or entangle it in conflicts not of its choosing. The Wafd, the dominant vehicle of Egyptian nationalism, kept its focus on Egypt's independence and treated Arab causes with sympathy but at arm's length.

This did not mean Arabism lacked a constituency. It mattered greatly to intellectuals, journalists, and students who moved in the Arabic press's cosmopolitan world, and it found institutional expression in cultural societies, literary circles, and eventually in political organizations that looked beyond Egypt's borders. The tension with Egyptian particularism was productive as well as divisive. Arabist writers forced the territorialists to explain why the Arabic language and the shared heritage did not define the nation, and territorialists forced the Arabists to explain what unity would mean in practice for a country with Egypt's distinct history and institutions. The argument kept both sides sharper than either would have been alone. It was only from mid-century onward, with the rise of new regimes and the transformation of regional politics, that Arabism would move from the intellectual margins to the center of Egyptian state ideology. Before that shift, its role was that of a persistent minority report: always present in the conversation, never quite commanding it, and for that reason a useful measure of what the dominant territorial story could not fully absorb. The story of how [the Urabi Revolt](/2015/03/01/urabi-revolt-british-invasion/) was remembered illustrates the point, since rival commemorations cast Urabi as an Egyptian patriot, an Arab hero, or a Muslim defender depending on which strand was doing the remembering.

### The Islamic Strand: A Nation of Faith

The third answer began where the other two could not reach: in the conviction that the deepest bond among Egyptians was not territory or language but shared submission to God. For the overwhelmingly Muslim population, Islam ordered the day from the call to prayer to the evening meal, governed marriage and inheritance, and supplied the vocabulary of right and wrong in which political claims were most readily understood. Reformers who worked this strand did not need to invent a community. They needed to awaken one, to convince Muslims that their faith was not a private consolation but a public program capable of answering the challenges of occupation, modernity, and decline.

Muhammad Abduh, the Azhar-trained scholar who became Egypt's Grand Mufti, gave reformist Islam its foundational modern statement in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Abduh argued that Islam, properly understood, was compatible with reason, science, and constitutional government, and that Muslim decline resulted not from the faith itself but from centuries of rigid imitation and corrupt rule. His project was double-edged. Against conservative ulama, he insisted that independent reasoning could revive the tradition. Against secularizing nationalists, he insisted that the revival must remain Islamic in its foundations rather than borrowing wholesale from Europe. Abduh's influence ran through the schools, the courts, and the press, and his disciples carried his method into fields he never touched. Historians debate how far his reformism represented a genuine renewal of the tradition and how far it conceded the terms of debate to European categories, but his centrality to the period is not seriously disputed. He made it possible for educated Egyptians to be both modern and Muslim without apology, and that possibility structured religious politics for generations.

Rashid Rida inherited Abduh's mantle and bent it in a more activist direction. Where Abduh had worked within the institutions of the khedival state, Rida built an independent platform in his journal and cultivated networks of followers across the Arab world. His thought moved steadily toward the conviction that Islam required political expression and that the caliphate, restored to Arab hands, should anchor Muslim life. This trajectory made him a bridge figure, claimed by later Islamists as a forerunner and studied by scholars of Arabism as a formative voice. The ambiguity was not a flaw in his thinking so much as a reflection of the period, when the categories of religious reform and national revival had not yet hardened into rival camps.

### How did Hassan al-Banna's Muslim Brotherhood organize around Islamic identity after 1928?

Banna built the Society of Muslim Brothers as a mass movement blending mosque preaching, welfare services, and disciplined branches, presenting Islam as a complete program for personal life, charity, education, and politics. Cells spread through Egypt's towns and cities, recruiting workers, students, and professionals around loyalty to faith and obedience to leadership.

What distinguished Banna's project from the reformers who preceded him was organization. Abduh had written and taught; Rida had published and corresponded. Banna built. The Society's genius lay in treating Islamic identity not as a doctrine to be debated but as a way of life to be practiced collectively, through night schools, clinics, sports clubs, and scout troops alongside study circles and sermons. This dense associational life gave members a community more immediate than the abstract nation of the territorialists or the distant Arab homeland of the Arabists. A young man who joined a Brotherhood branch found comrades, purpose, and a moral code in one package, and the movement's hierarchical discipline, with its emphasis on obedience and stages of membership, turned sympathy into commitment. Scholars who have studied the Society's early records note how rapidly it scaled: founded among workers in Ismailia in 1928, it had spread across the country within a decade, with estimates of membership and sympathizers varying widely enough that historians prefer ranges in the hundreds of thousands by the late 1940s rather than single figures. The imprecision reflects both the movement's cellular structure and the political stakes of counting it.

The Brotherhood's relationship to the other strands was one of rivalry and selective borrowing. It accepted the territorial nation as a fact but subordinated it to the umma, the worldwide community of believers, which it presented as the more fundamental community. It honored the Arabic language as the tongue of revelation but rejected Arabism as a sufficient basis for solidarity when it excluded non-Arab Muslims or elevated ethnicity over faith. And it drew freely on the reformist language of Abduh and Rida while accusing the religious establishment of quietism and the secular nationalists of importing foreign gods. This positioning allowed the movement to present itself as the authentic voice of the Egyptian Muslim majority against elites of every description, British officials, palace politicians, and Westernized intellectuals alike. Its social services filled gaps the state left open, which earned it a loyalty that polemics alone could never have purchased. Whether the Brotherhood is best understood as primarily a religious movement that entered politics or a political movement that used religious language remains a live scholarly debate, and the movement's own statements across different periods support both readings. What is settled is that after 1928, Islamic identity in Egypt possessed something it had never possessed before: a mass organization capable of mobilizing it independently of the state, the parties, and the mosque hierarchy.

The three strands never resolved their argument, and in a sense the argument was the point. Each named something real about Egyptian life, and each left something out that the others supplied. The territorialists gave the independence movement its state and its citizen but struggled to explain why Egypt's culture overflowed its borders. The Arabists gave Egyptians a vast linguistic and historical family but struggled to convert kinship into institutions. The Islamists gave the majority a comprehensive way of life and, in the Brotherhood, a formidable organization, but struggled to accommodate those Egyptians, above all the Copts, for whom the umma could never be the whole story. Out of this unresolved competition came the political vocabulary in which Egypt would debate itself for the rest of the century.

One strand of that vocabulary, however, reached further back than any of the others for its authority. If the territorialists claimed the valley across the centuries, some thinkers began to ask what the valley's most distant centuries might contribute: the age of the Pharaohs, whose monuments still dominated the landscape and whose deciphered inscriptions were revealing a civilization of startling depth. The ancient mirror was about to be held up to the modern nation, and the reflection would transform the argument once again.

The previous three strands, Egyptian, Arab, and Islamic, each claimed to answer a single question: what made Egypt itself. Pharaonism offered a fourth answer, older than all the rest, and for a generation of nationalists it carried a force the others could not match. It told Egyptians that their truest self was not made in the seventh century or the twentieth, but in the thirty centuries before either, in the temples, tombs, and river fields of the Pharaohs.

## Pharaonism: The Ancient Mirror

The rediscovery of ancient Egypt in the nineteenth century was largely a European project. The French scholars who accompanied Napoleon's expedition produced the Description de l'Égypte, a multivolume survey that catalogued monuments with a thoroughness no Egyptian institution had attempted. Champollion deciphered hieroglyphs in 1822. Museums in London, Paris, Turin, and Berlin filled with obelisks, coffins, and statues, while Egyptians themselves had little part in interpreting the heritage buried beneath their feet. This imbalance did not go unnoticed. As [the nahda cultural awakening](/2014/11/01/egypt-nahda-cultural-awakening/) drew Egyptian intellectuals into debates about language, education, and national character, some began to ask why the ancient past belonged to foreign scholars rather than to the people who farmed the same valley the Pharaohs had ruled.

Pharaonism was the answer they built. In its strongest form it claimed that modern Egyptians were the direct heirs of the Pharaohs, that the fellah working the Nile mud in 1920 stood in unbroken continuity with the laborer who raised Karnak, and that the Arab conquest of 641 had been a passing chapter in a history whose real subject was the land of Egypt itself. It was a territorial identity with an ancient pedigree, and it offered something no other strand could: a past of unquestioned greatness that belonged to Egypt alone, not shared with the Arab world, not borrowed from Europe, and not dependent on Islam.

No figure carried the Pharaonist argument further than Taha Hussein. In The Future of Culture in Egypt, published in 1938, he argued that Egypt's mental structure had always been closer to the Mediterranean world than to the East. Egypt, in his account, had absorbed Greek philosophy and Roman law, had entered the circle of European civilization, and should orient its schools, its law, and its culture toward that Mediterranean inheritance rather than toward an Arab or Islamic East. The Pharaonic past was his anchor. If Egypt had been a great civilization before the Arabs arrived, then modernization on a Western model was not imitation of foreigners but a return to Egypt's own deepest character. The book provoked storms of criticism precisely because the argument was so sweeping. Arabists read it as an attempt to cut Egypt off from the Arab world. Islamists read it as an attempt to subordinate thirteen centuries of Islamic civilization to a pagan antiquity. Both understood what was at stake: Hussein was not merely praising old stones but proposing a program for the nation's future, one in which the Pharaohs mattered more than the Caliphs.

The emotional fuel for Pharaonism arrived a decade and a half earlier, in November 1922, when Howard Carter, funded by Lord Carnarvon, broke through the sealed doorway of Tutankhamun's tomb in the Valley of the Kings. The tomb was nearly intact, packed with gold, chariots, furniture, and the young king's nested coffins. The discovery electrified the world, and it electrified Egypt most of all.

### How was the discovery of Tutankhamun's tomb received in Egypt in 1922?

Egyptians greeted the discovery with national pride and some resentment. The press celebrated a pharaoh's gold as proof of Egypt's glory, while nationalists objected that Lord Carnarvon had sold exclusive coverage to The Times and that Europeans controlled the find. The tomb became a symbol of both heritage and colonial grievance.

The pride was genuine and widespread. Newspapers carried the story in banner headlines, and ordinary Cairenes who had never thought of themselves as heirs of kings suddenly saw the boy king's mask reproduced everywhere as an emblem of what Egypt had been. Yet the resentment was equally real, and it sharpened the political meaning of the find. Carnarvon's exclusive contract with The Times meant Egyptian papers had to buy their news of their own country's treasure from a London daily, an arrangement that struck nationalists as a perfect emblem of the occupation. The Service des Antiquités remained under French direction, and although the Egyptian government tightened antiquities regulations in the wake of the discovery, the perception endured that foreigners dug, foreigners published, and foreigners profited while Egyptians watched. The opening of the burial chamber in February 1924, attended by Egyptian officials alongside foreign dignitaries, was staged partly as a national ceremony for exactly this reason. Tutankhamun gave Pharaonism its most potent image: the golden mask as a face Egyptians could claim as their own, and the controversy around the dig as proof that claiming it required political struggle.

That struggle moved into art. Mahmoud Mukhtar, the sculptor who became the movement's visual prophet, unveiled Nahdat Misr, rendered in English as Egypt's Awakening, in 1928. The monument showed a sphinx rising on its forepaws beside a fellaha, a peasant woman, who lifts her veil with one hand and rests the other on the sphinx's head. The symbolism was deliberately layered. The sphinx stood for the ancient past stirring back to life; the woman stood for the modern nation, rural and authentic, unveiling itself as it awakened. Erected in what was then Bab al-Hadid Square beside the Cairo railway station, later Ramses Square, the monument put Pharaonism in the most public space imaginable, where travelers arriving in the capital could not miss it. Mukhtar worked in a neo-Pharaonic idiom, carving granite with a severity learned from ancient models, and his success encouraged a generation of painters and sculptors to reach for Pharaonic motifs. For supporters, the monument proved that the ancient past could speak a modern political language. For critics, it risked turning nationalism into ancestor worship, a cult of stones.

Literature carried the same message in prose. Tawfiq al-Hakim's novel Awdat al-Ruh, The Return of the Spirit, published in 1933, told of a young Egyptian whose consciousness awakens to a deep, timeless Egyptian soul rooted in the Nile valley and its Pharaonic past. The novel treated Egyptian identity as something older than and separate from Arabness, a spirit that slept through centuries of foreign rule and could be roused again. Al-Hakim, who had studied in Paris and returned steeped in both French letters and Pharaonist enthusiasm, made the ancient past into a kind of national unconscious. The book's influence was considerable among educated youth in the 1930s, who found in its pages a flattering explanation of their distinctness: Egypt was not merely another Arab province but a civilization unto itself, entitled to its own destiny.

The appeal of this vision was not evenly distributed, and the pattern of who embraced it reveals much about what was at stake. Copts found Pharaonism congenial for reasons both historical and practical. As Christians whose church traced itself to the apostolic age, Copts could claim the pre-Islamic past without embarrassment, and Pharaonism offered them a nationalism in which they were full members by birth rather than tolerated minorities in an Islamic or Arab framework. Territorial nationalists of the Wafd generation found it equally useful. If Egypt's identity rested on the land and its ancient continuity, then the nation could stand apart from the Arab world, resist pan-Arab schemes that might dilute Egyptian interests, and claim a pedigree older than any rival's. For both groups, Pharaonism solved a problem of belonging: it made Egyptianness primary and everything else secondary.

Islamists and Arabists saw the same doctrine from the opposite side and found it threatening. For Islamists, the celebration of a pagan antiquity sat uneasily with an identity grounded in thirteen centuries of Islamic civilization. Some went further and treated Pharaonism as a kind of idolatry, a worship of pre-Islamic kings that the Quran itself presented as enemies of the faith, with Pharaoh as the archetype of the tyrant. For Arabists, the danger was political. Pharaonism's insistence on Egypt's separateness undercut the case for Arab unity, and Taha Hussein's Mediterranean turn looked like an intellectual secession from the Arab nation. The quarrel was not only about the past. Arabists in the 1930s and 1940s were building movements, journals, and eventually political programs that required Egypt to see itself as the leading Arab state; a doctrine that told Egyptians they were Pharaohs first made that project harder.

What kept Pharaonism alive through these attacks was its flexibility. It could be a cultural program in Hussein's hands, a sculptural idiom in Mukhtar's, a mystical nationalism in al-Hakim's, and a Coptic reassurance or a Wafdist slogan as occasion required. It never became the official doctrine of any government, and after 1952 its fortunes would shift dramatically, but in the interwar decades it supplied Egyptian nationalism with its most distinctive image of itself: a nation as old as civilization, waking from a long sleep, lifting its own veil.

None of these strands won its argument cleanly in the interwar years, and the contest moved into the places where Egyptians argued in public. The press became the arena where Taha Hussein's Mediterranean Egypt collided with Arabist and Islamist rebuttals, week after week, in essays and counter essays that ordinary readers followed like a serial. Art carried the quarrel into stone and canvas, as painters debated whether a modern Egyptian art should look to Paris, to Baghdad, or to Karnak. Politics gave the debate its sharpest edge, because each identity implied a different foreign policy, a different school curriculum, and a different answer to the question of who counted as fully Egyptian.

## The Classroom, the Museum, and the Academy

The identity strands compared above did not live only in speeches and pamphlets. Each of them built institutions that taught, displayed, or guarded its version of Egypt, and each claimed the authority to say what Egypt was. The Ministry of Public Instruction decided which past schoolchildren memorized. The Egyptian Museum decided which past deserved glass cases, though for decades foreigners made those decisions. An Egyptian excavator at Giza, a Coptic notable in Old Cairo, and the king's academicians of Arabic each raised a rival flag. The result was not a single national story but a competition among institutions, each confident it spoke for the nation. The struggle mattered because the winner would decide what counted as Egyptian: a pharaoh's cartouche, a Coptic icon, or an Arabic root.

### The 1920s Battle over the Schoolbook

After the declaration of independence in 1922, Egyptians took charge of the Ministry of Public Instruction for the first time in decades, and education became a nation-building project argued out in parliament instead of settled in an adviser's office. The historian Misako Ikeda, studying this period, shows that the post-1919 years produced tuition-free education and a standardized primary school curriculum, reforms that emerged from parliamentary debate attuned to public interest instead of from decree.

The sharpest fights concerned who wrote the books and in what language they were written. Nationalists had long demanded Arabic as the language of instruction, and in the 1920s the ministry commissioned Egyptian scholars to replace foreign-authored textbooks, turning authorship into a political prize. History was the most contested subject of all, because every committee had to apportion the book among the Pharaohs, the Arabs, and Islam, and every apportionment pleased one strand of opinion while angering another. A concrete glimpse of the classroom survives in a 1929 public primary timetable recorded by the educator Boktor in 1936: Religion received six percent of weekly periods, set beside Arabic, English, Arithmetic and Geometry, Science and Hygiene, Geography and History, Drawing, and Physical Training. The past was taught, but it was rationed, and the rationing itself was a political act. Meanwhile Arabic pressed back into classrooms from which it had been pushed, and every new primer carried the fingerprints of the committee that approved it.

At the university level, the groundwork had been laid even before independence. The Egyptian University's Antiquities Committee produced a report in 1920 that made the study of archaeology and heritage part of the university's business. Its recommendations were precise: the study of hieroglyphs was not to be separated from archaeology, foreign languages were to be used in archaeological training, and a preparatory year was proposed, though it was never carried out. When the Egyptian University became a state institution in 1925, the government gained direct control of higher learning and of the scholars who would staff the museums.

The temperature of these debates showed in 1926, when Taha Hussein's book on pre-Islamic poetry questioned the authenticity of the old Arabic verse and provoked an uproar in parliament and at al-Azhar. The scandal concerned literature, but it revealed how explosive the curriculum of the past had become. Abdel Rahman al-Raf'i's 1929 history of the national movement gave teachers a Wafdist narrative to carry into their classrooms. Salama Musa, looking back on the schools of the occupation era, complained that the inherited curriculum had slighted ancient Egyptian history; the reformers of the 1920s set out to correct the slight, and their opponents watched every page.

### A Museum Run by Foreigners in the Name of Egypt

The Egyptian Museum was founded to display the Pharaonic strand of identity, yet for nearly eighty years foreigners controlled it. Muhammad Ali decreed the protection of antiquities in 1835. Auguste Mariette founded the Antiquities Service in 1858, the Bulaq museum opened in 1863, the collection moved to Giza in 1891, and the purpose-built Tahrir building, designed by the French architect Marcel Dourgnon, opened on November 15, 1902 under Khedive Abbas Hilmi II. Al-Ahram announced the opening ceremony for that afternoon, with the khedive, his ministers, foreign diplomats, and Maspero himself in attendance: the antiquities of Egypt displayed under the patronage of its rulers and the direction of a Frenchman.

Direction stayed French through the whole period. Gaston Maspero, Eugene Grebaut, Jacques de Morgan, and Victor Loret each held the directorship, and then came Pierre Lacau, director-general of Antiquities from 1914 to 1936, followed by Etienne Drioton from 1936 to 1952. Lacau used the office to tighten state control over finds: excavation concessions would go only to public institutions, and the museum would keep every unique object. These measures asserted Egyptian ownership even as a Frenchman wielded them. In 1924 John D. Rockefeller Jr. offered ten million dollars for a grand new museum building, but the gift came with thirty years of control by an international commission, and the government rejected it as an infringement of sovereignty, as the historian John Wilson recounts.

Egyptianization came slowly and from below. The Egyptian University began training Egyptologists in the 1920s, and its graduates, among them Mahmoud Hamza, Sami Gabra, and Selim Hassan, entered the Antiquities Service as inspectors and curators. The museum's own directors' register names Mahmoud Ali Hamza, trained in Cairo, Liverpool, and Paris, as the first Egyptian to direct the museum, holding the post from 1941 to 1950; the outgoing foreign director stayed on as his technical adviser, a telling detail. Only after 1952 did the Antiquities Service itself pass fully into Egyptian hands. The museum thus embodied the Pharaonic strand while dramatizing its contradiction: the nation's most potent symbol of itself, administered for decades by men who were not its citizens.

### Selim Hassan Digs at Giza

Selim Hassan turned the Egyptian claim on the Pharaonic past into a matter of scientific practice. Born in 1886 in the Delta village of Mit Nagi and trained as a teacher, he joined the Egyptian Museum as an assistant keeper in 1921, studied in Paris from 1923 to 1927, earned a doctorate at Vienna in 1935, and began teaching Egyptology at Cairo University in 1928.

In the winter of 1928 to 1929 he opened excavations at Giza on behalf of the Egyptian University: a major concession held not by a foreign mission but by an Egyptian university, and directed by an Egyptian, working the central field alongside long-established American and German expeditions. He remained at Giza until 1939, clearing mastabas and rock-cut tombs east and southeast of the Second Pyramid, the pyramid's causeway, boat graves, and the valley temple area. In 1936 he took over the Sphinx excavations from the French engineer Baraize. His finds included the tomb complex of Queen Khentkawes. He published the results with Oxford University Press as Excavations at Giza, excavation reports that carried an Egyptian name on the title page, and later produced a multi-volume Arabic encyclopedia of ancient Egypt.

The symbolism was unmistakable: an Egyptian, employed by an Egyptian university, digging at the pyramids under Egyptian direction and publishing in the idiom of international science. In 1936 Hassan became the university's first Egyptian professor of Egyptology and deputy director of the Antiquities Service, the highest post an Egyptian had yet held in the antiquities administration. He had moved from the classroom to the field to the director's office, and the discipline of Egyptology moved with him.

### The Coptic Museum and a Community's Claim

In 1908 Marcus Simaika Pasha, a Coptic civil servant and amateur historian, founded a museum for Coptic art in Old Cairo, beside the Babylon fortress, on a plot originally intended for a church, with the approval of Patriarch Cyril V. He raised the money by public subscription, and the Coptic community answered with donations of vestments, frescoes, icons, and architectural fragments from old churches. The museum opened on March 14, 1910.

Simaika acted at a moment when European dealers and collectors were buying up Coptic pieces and carrying them abroad. His museum was a rescue operation, an assertion that Coptic heritage belonged in Egypt and deserved its own house instead of a corner of the Egyptian Museum, where Maspero had first gathered Coptic rooms. The new institution presented Coptic art as the bridge between the Pharaonic, Greco-Roman, and Islamic eras, a continuous Egyptian story told in stone, wood, ivory, and textile.

The state absorbed the project within a generation. In 1931 the museum became a state museum under the Department of Antiquities, and in 1939 the department transferred all the Christian antiquities held by the Egyptian Museum to it, a new wing following in 1944. A private communal initiative had become a national institution, and the Coptic strand of identity had won permanent gallery space. Yet the absorption also meant that the state now decided how that strand was displayed, a pattern repeated across each of these institutions.

### The Academy Takes Charge of Arabic

The Academy of the Arabic Language, the Majma al-Lughah al-Arabiyah, was proposed in parliament in the 1928 to 1929 session and established by royal decree from Abdeen Palace in December 1932 under King Fuad I, with the minister of education Muhammad Hilmi Isa among its sponsors. Its constitution was modeled on the Academie Francaise. Its charge was to confront the urgent problems of the Arabic language and adapt it to the needs of the twentieth century: coining terms for science and modern life, correcting usage, and compiling a historical dictionary of Arabic. The historical dictionary was never completed, and between 1938 and 1968 the Academy weighed more than two hundred proposals for reforming the Arabic script, modernizers and traditionalists arguing inside the fortress of the language itself.

The council first met on January 30, 1934: twenty members, half of them Egyptian, under its first president Mohammed Tawfik Rifaat, with Mansour Fahmy as secretary. A second cohort of ten Egyptian members joined in 1940, including Ahmad Lutfi al-Sayyid, Taha Hussein, Abbas Mahmud al-Aqqad, Muhammad Husayn Haykal, and Ahmad Amin. The presence of foreign orientalists among the members provoked objections from those who held that Egyptians and Arabs alone should decide the future of the language, a dispute over authority that mirrored the museum's slow transfer of power.

The Academy gave the Arab strand of identity its own fortress, parallel to the museum's Pharaonic one. Where the museum guarded objects, the Academy guarded words; where Selim Hassan dug at Giza, the academicians dug through lexicons. Both claimed to be recovering the authentic Egypt, and each implied that the other was incomplete.

### Rival Institutions, Rival Egypts

No single body won the contest. The schools taught all the strands at once and satisfied none of their partisans fully. The Egyptian Museum kept the Pharaonic past while slowly becoming Egyptian in staff. The Coptic Museum held the Christian centuries that the national museum had once absorbed. The Academy policed the language in which every strand had to speak. Each institution had been founded to settle the question of what Egypt was, and each ended by proving the question was still open. When the debates moved into the press, the parliament, and the studios of artists, they carried these institutional loyalties with them. The argument was no closer to an end, but it now had addresses: a ministry, two museums, a dig house at Giza, and an academy.

## The Debate in Art, Press, and Politics

The identity argument did not remain in books and salons. It spilled into newsprint, onto the stage, and finally into the street, where each strand of the debate had to prove it could move ordinary Egyptians rather than merely persuade intellectuals. The table of rival identities becomes a living quarrel only when it is followed into these arenas, where the abstractions acquired editors, audiences, and martyrs.

In the first decade of the twentieth century, Cairo's newspapers functioned as party headquarters in print. Mustafa Kamil's al-Liwa, founded in 1900, preached Egyptian patriotism while appealing to the Ottoman sultan as a counterweight to Britain, a pairing that showed how territorial and pan-Islamic loyalties could still share a single page. Ali Yusuf's al-Muayyad leaned toward the palace and toward Islamic solidarity, while Rashid Rida's al-Manar, founded in 1898, carried the reformist pan-Islamic case that Egypt's dignity lay in the wider Muslim community and its caliph rather than in a separate nationalism. Against them stood Ahmad Lutfi al-Sayyid's al-Jarida, founded in 1907 as the voice of the Ummah Party, which argued that Egypt was a watan, a homeland whose members were Egyptians first, Muslim or Copt, and that religion was a private matter on which the nation should not be built. The press wars made the identity question unavoidable because they attached it to every controversy of the day. The Dinshaway incident of 1906 gave Kamil a national cause to prosecute in print week after week, turning a village clash into a referendum on British justice. The khedivial succession, the British advisers planted in the ministries, and the language of instruction in the schools each became another front in the same war. Readers chose a paper the way later generations would choose a party, and in choosing they chose an answer to the question of who the Egyptian was. Literacy was still limited, but the papers were read aloud in cafes and workshops, so the quarrel traveled far beyond the subscriber lists. British prosecutions of editors only confirmed the papers' importance: a press the occupier bothered to jail was a press that mattered.

Language itself became a front in the war. Lutfi al-Sayyid and his circle argued that Arabic, the tongue of the street and the mosque, should be the language of administration and higher learning, against an elite that conducted business in French and took its orders in English. The campaign for Arabic was never only about efficiency; it was an identity claim, an insistence that the nation speak with one voice and that the voice be its own. The pan-Islamists agreed on Arabic but for different reasons, as the language of the Quran and the bond of the umma, which shows how the same cause could serve two definitions of Egypt at once.

Kamil's death in 1908 did not end the paper wars; it sharpened them. His successor Muhammad Farid kept al-Liwa and the National Party on their Ottoman-leaning course, while Lutfi al-Sayyid's Ummah Party answered with a program that made Egyptianness a matter of shared territory and shared interests rather than shared creed. The rival party programs of 1907 read like competing definitions of the nation set side by side, and their newspapers spent the following decade annotating the difference. The press wars survived the coming of partial independence and restaged the old quarrel inside the nationalist camp itself. The Wafd's al-Balagh, founded in 1923, carried a populist territorial nationalism against the Liberal Constitutionalists' al-Siyasa, which spoke for a more elitist and legalistic patriotism, while palace papers cultivated an Islamic idiom of kingship. King Fuad, who took the title of king rather than sultan in 1922, was said by contemporaries to covet the caliphate after its abolition in 1924, a rumor that shows how the Islamic strand of identity still tempted even a territorial monarch. All parties now claimed the watan, but they filled the word with different contents, and the cartoons, feuilletons, and parliamentary reports carried the argument to a widening readership.

Poetry and the stage carried the same argument to people who never read editorials. Ahmad Shawqi, crowned the Prince of Poets, wrote verse dramas that dressed the present in the costumes of the past. Shawqi himself embodied the entanglement: exiled to Spain in 1914 after the British deposed Abbas II, he returned after the war to a country rehearsing its identity, and his turn to verse drama in the 1920s gave each strand its tragedies. His plays restaged pharaonic queens, including Cleopatra in the drama of 1929 that bore her name, alongside heroes of the Islamic era, letting audiences applaud a deep Egyptian antiquity and an Arab-Islamic glory on the same boards. Nationalist songs moved through cafes and demonstrations, and composers such as Sayed Darwish set patriotic verse to melodies that crowds could sing, including tunes that later generations would adopt as the national anthem. The theater troupes of Cairo and Alexandria turned historical melodrama into civic instruction, while school textbooks began folding ancient Egypt into the story children learned about themselves. The new Egyptian University, founded in 1908 as a nationalist cultural project, gave the debate lecture halls to go with its newsprint. Shawqi's patriotic qasidas were memorized in schools and recited at public ceremonies, so that children learned the watan as rhythm before they learned it as argument. The popular stage added its own register: comic troupes and musical revues smuggled nationalist jokes past the censor, proving that the identity debate could laugh as well as declaim. The arts gave each identity strand a wardrobe and a soundtrack: pharaonic motifs for the territorialists, the Arabic-Islamic past for the pan-Islamists, and songs of the watan for the mass politics then taking shape. Sculpture joined the chorus when Mahmoud Mukhtar unveiled Nahdat Misr, Egypt's Awakening, in 1928: a fellaha lifting her veil beside a sphinx, the peasant present leaning on the pharaonic past, set in stone at one of Cairo's great squares. No single image stated the territorial answer more plainly.

### How did Coptic-Muslim symbolism work during the 1919 Revolution?

The 1919 crowds fused Islamic and Coptic symbols into a single national emblem: marchers carried the crescent alongside the cross, priests and imams shared platforms, and the slogan held that religion belongs to God while the nation belongs to everyone. The pairing announced that Egyptian unity outranked sect, and it denied Britain a wedge.

All of this rehearsal became performance in [the 1919 Revolution](/2015/05/01/1919-revolution-in-egypt/), when the identity debate stepped out of the papers and into the streets. The uprising's most deliberate invention was its symbolism of unity. Coptic priests marched beside Muslim imams, demonstrators carried the crescent entwined with the cross, and the slogan, al-din li-llah wa-l-watan li-l-jami, held that religion is for God and the homeland is for all. The slogan passed from mouth to mouth and was claimed by many speakers; its exact authorship is disputed, which itself suggests it belonged to the crowd rather than to any party. The pairing was a political argument in visual form. It told the British that Egypt could not be split into a Muslim majority to be managed and a Coptic minority to be protected, and it told Egyptians that the nation, not the sect, was the unit that counted. British officials and the London press played up every sectarian incident they could find, and the nationalists answered each one with a fresh display of the fused symbols, turning rebuttal into ritual. The Wafd gave the street's symbolism a diplomatic twin. Its case at Paris rested on the Wilsonian language of self-determination, translating the territorial claim into the idiom of the postwar settlement: Egypt was a nation, nations deserved states, and the crescent-and-cross crowds were the evidence. The argument failed in 1919, but the vocabulary stuck, and Egyptian nationalism would speak it for a generation. Coptic figures stood in the Wafd's front ranks, Makram Ebeid among them, so the unity on the banners had names and faces behind it. Women's demonstrations, led by figures such as Huda Sha'arawi, carried the same fused symbols, extending the claim that the nation included everyone who marched under it. The symbolism worked because it was simple enough for a crowd to carry and serious enough for diplomacy to cite, and it gave territorial nationalism its most memorable image, one that later demonstrations would reach for again whenever the sectarian card was played.

Territorial nationalism found its mass vehicle in [Saad Zaghloul and the Wafd](/2015/06/01/saad-zaghloul-and-the-wafd/). The Wafd's demand for independence translated the editors' watan into a program: an Egypt defined by its territory and its people, negotiating as a nation among nations. Under Zaghloul, the Wafd built a coalition that crossed class and sect, and its victories in the elections of the 1920s, beginning with the landslide of 1924, made it the unmistakable voice of the nationalist mainstream. The Wafd called itself the nation, and for a decade the claim was close enough to true to govern by. Its success proved that the territorial answer to the identity question could fill streets and ballot boxes, even while its rivals kept their presses running and their own answers in circulation. The palace under King Fuad and the British residency each tried to build counterweights, and each discovered that the Wafd had made Egyptian territorial nationalism the default language of politics, the idiom every other actor had to speak even to oppose it. The Wafd's dominance was never uncontested. The landslide of 1924 was followed by a bruising cycle of palace interventions, dissolutions of parliament, and manipulated contests, as King Fuad and the residency tried to manufacture alternatives to the party that claimed to be the nation. Each confrontation restaged the identity question in institutional form: was sovereignty located in the elected nation, the crowned king, or the occupying power that reserved the right to intervene? The mausoleum raised for Zaghloul after his death in 1927 became a shrine of that language, a pharaonic-inflected monument where the faithful of the watan paid their respects.

Britain's unilateral declaration of February 1922 ended the protectorate in name while reserving four points: the security of imperial communications in Egypt, the defense of Egypt against foreign attack, the protection of foreign interests and minorities, and the future of the Sudan. The formula produced a kingdom without full sovereignty, and it reframed the identity debate rather than resolving it. Independence was declared for a territorial Egypt, which vindicated the nationalists' definition of the country, yet the reserved points kept British troops, advisers, and courts in place, which kept the question of who actually governed painfully alive. The Sudan reservation cut deepest into the Wafd's own map, since the party claimed the unity of the Nile Valley and counted the Sudan as Egyptian; a nationalism defined by territory could not easily surrender territory. The constitution of 1923, promulgated that April, built the new order's legal shell: a constitutional monarchy with a bicameral parliament, ministerial responsibility, and citizenship as the basis of Egyptianness, alongside the declaration that Islam was the religion of the state. The drafting committee argued over the king's prerogatives and over the place of religion, and the finished text carried both answers at once. The electoral arrangements sharpened the point. The Wafd opposed separate electorates for Copts, insisting that a territorial nation voted as one body, and Coptic candidates won seats on the Wafd's lists rather than as sectarian delegates. The arrangement worked as long as the Wafd's umbrella held; it left unanswered what would protect the principle if the umbrella ever folded. Egyptians were citizens of a territorial nation, but the state's religion was named. Copts were full members of the nation, ran on Wafd lists, and sat in parliament, yet the constitution inscribed a religious identity on the state itself. Every later quarrel over sect, citizenship, and the character of the state would work inside the frame that 1922 and 1923 had built without finishing.

Even the flag told the story. The green banner adopted in 1923 bore a white crescent with three stars, commonly read as Egypt, Nubia, and the Sudan: the territorial claim, including the contested south, stitched into cloth. A flag that counted the Sudan as Egyptian made the reserved point on the Sudan a daily visual contradiction, and every flag-raising restated the map the Wafd refused to surrender.

## Where the Contest Led

By the middle of the twentieth century, the contest had produced a clear winner in politics and no winner at all in the deeper argument. The state was territorial: a kingdom with fixed borders, a flag, a parliament, and an army taking shape, governing in the name of a citizenry defined by geography rather than creed. The Wafd's definition of Egypt had become the operating system of public life. But the other strands had not vanished; they had changed address.

Pharaonism conquered the museum, the schoolbook, and the postage stamp while losing the street. The discovery of Tutankhamun's tomb in 1922 electrified popular pride in the ancient past and gave the territorial state a pedigree of five thousand years, endlessly reproduced in official imagery, public monuments, and the treasures of the Egyptian Museum. As a mass political identity, however, historians generally judge it to have remained thin: few cultivators saw themselves as heirs of the pharaohs in any politically active sense, and by the 1930s its intellectual energy was fading into cultural decoration, a style of official art rather than a program. On the political fringe, movements such as Young Egypt in the 1930s dressed territorial nationalism in paramilitary style and borrowed pharaonic imagery for a politics of national rebirth; historians read this as Pharaonism's last political gasp rather than its revival. The Tutankhamun craze had a scholarly underside as well. Egyptian Egyptologists began claiming the ancient past as a national discipline rather than a European monopoly, and the Egyptian Museum, long a foreign-run institution, became a site of quiet nationalization. Pharaonism thus survived as expertise and as spectacle even where it failed as politics.

The debate even reached for the Mediterranean. Taha Hussein's The Future of Culture in Egypt, published in 1938, argued that Egypt belonged culturally to the Mediterranean world as much as to the Arab east, drawing on its Pharaonic and Greco-Roman layers. The book provoked furious replies from Arabist and Islamist critics, which proved the point its author was making: every attempt to fix Egypt's identity in one direction summoned defenders of the others. Literary monthlies such as al-Risala, founded in 1933, kept the quarrel in print through the following decade, hosting Pharaonists, Arabists, and Islamists in the same pages and proving that the press wars had become a permanent institution.

Arabism traveled the opposite road from Pharaonism. Marginal among Egyptian intellectuals in 1900, it gained ground through the 1930s and 1940s as the Palestine question, regional journalism, and the Second World War tied Egypt's fate to the Arab east. The founding of the Arab League in 1945, with its headquarters in Cairo, gave Arabism an institutional home in the Egyptian capital itself, a paradox the territorialists of 1919 could hardly have imagined. By the late 1940s, Egypt's Arab identity was asserted in diplomacy and in the press with a confidence that would have surprised the generation of 1919, for whom the watan had been a sufficient answer.

Islamism persisted as the moral vocabulary of much of society. The mosque sermon and the ulema, with al-Azhar at their center, carried an identity in which the Egyptian was first a Muslim and the nation a chapter in a larger story. After 1928 the Muslim Brotherhood gave that identity a mass organization with schools, clinics, and branches across the country. It did not capture the state in this period, yet it shaped how millions understood themselves all the same, and its growth showed that the territorial answer had never satisfied everyone. Al-Azhar anchored the strand institutionally. As the oldest seat of Sunni learning in the country, it lent the Islamic definition of Egypt a weight no newspaper could match, and its scholars policed the boundary between acceptable nationalism and unbelief whenever the debate grew heated.

Ottomanism simply died. The abolition of the caliphate in 1924 removed the institutional anchor of pan-Islamic loyalty to Istanbul, and the generation that had once appealed to the sultan against Britain aged out of politics. Of all the strands, it was the one the century clearly closed.

The Coptic position illustrated the unfinished business better than any abstract argument. The community that had marched under the fused banners of 1919 and supplied the Wafd with leaders found its anxieties growing as the decades wore on: the constitution's naming of Islam, the Brotherhood's rise, and the palace's flirtation with Islamic legitimation each raised the question of what citizenship meant in practice. Makram Ebeid's bitter break with the Wafd in the early 1940s, sealed by his famous Black Book denouncing the party's corruption, showed the strain inside the nationalist coalition itself. The unity symbolism of 1919 had been real, but it had been a beginning rather than a settlement.

When army officers seized power in 1952, they inherited the territorial state the Wafd had defined and the full repertoire of identities along with it. The new regime would reach for different strands at different moments, which is precisely what a repertoire is for.

The question stayed open because each surviving strand held a piece of undeniable truth. Egypt was geographically distinct, with a coherence the Nile gave it; it was the heir of a famous antiquity; it was Arabic-speaking; it was overwhelmingly Muslim. No single formula absorbed all four facts at once, and the political settlements of the century kept tripping over the remainder. The reserved points made sovereignty itself partial, which kept nationalism permanently mobilized and permanently unsatisfied, and the Anglo-Egyptian treaty of 1936 eased but did not end the British presence, so the argument retained its fuel. The 1923 constitution's pairing of citizenship with an official state religion left the Coptic question to be renegotiated in every generation, since the same document promised equality and named a state religion. And the regional pressures of the 1940s rewarded whichever identity best fit the crisis of the moment. The contest became a repertoire rather than a debate with an ending: Egyptians learned to speak several languages of identity and to reach for whichever one the occasion demanded, which is why the argument never quite died and never quite resolved.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did Egyptian nationalism grow?

Egyptian nationalism grew in layers rather than in a single founding moment. Muhammad Ali's nineteenth-century state-building gave Egypt a distinct administration and army, and the press and schools of the later 1800s created a reading public that could imagine the nation. The National Party of Mustafa Kamil turned that sentiment into organized politics after 1907, demanding British evacuation through the newspaper al-Liwa and appeals to European opinion. The Dinshaway incident of 1906 widened the movement's social base by turning peasant grievances into a national cause. The upheaval of 1919 then fused elite politics with mass protest, producing a nationalism that claimed to speak for all Egyptians. Over the following decades this territorial nationalism competed with Pharaonist, Arab, and Islamic currents, each offering a different answer to what Egypt was.

### Q: How did Egyptians define their identity?

Egyptians in the modern era defined their identity through several overlapping answers rather than one agreed formula. One answer was territorial: Egypt was a distinct nation with its own history, borders, and interests, a view rooted in the long continuity of life along the Nile. A second answer was Arab: Egypt shared language, literature, and much of its recent history with the Arabic-speaking world. A third was Islamic: for the Muslim majority, faith shaped law, morals, and community, while Copts held their own Christian tradition as an equally Egyptian inheritance. A fourth, Pharaonism, located the nation's deepest roots in its pre-Islamic past. Which answer came first depended on who was speaking and when, and much of modern Egyptian politics was a contest over how these strands should rank.

### Q: Did Egyptians see themselves as Arab or Egyptian?

Most Egyptians in the modern period were both, and the question usually produced a ranking rather than an either-or choice. Arabic was the language of daily life, literature, and religion, and Egypt stood at the center of the Arab world's press and thought from the Nahda onward, so an Arab identity was natural and widely felt. Yet Egyptians also saw their country as distinct: its geography, its ancient past, and its strong centralized state set it apart from other Arab lands. Territorial nationalists from Mustafa Kamil to the Pharaonists insisted that Egypt came first, while Arab nationalists argued that Egypt's destiny lay with the wider Arab world. Ordinary people moved between these loyalties without feeling contradiction, identifying as Egyptian in the nation and Arab in language and culture.

### Q: How did ancient Egypt inspire modern nationalism?

The rediscovery of ancient Egypt gave modern nationalists a usable past. As archaeologists uncovered temples and tombs and scholars read the hieroglyphs, the pharaonic age stopped being a vague legend and became evidence that Egyptians had built one of the world's great civilizations long before the occupation began. Nationalists used this record to answer the claim that Egypt needed foreign tutelage: a nation with such a past, they argued, was capable of ruling itself. Ancient symbols appeared in political art, schoolbooks, and the new national iconography, while the 1922 discovery of Tutankhamun's tomb turned pharaonic pride into a popular sensation. For Pharaonists, the lesson went further: Egypt's identity was rooted in the Nile valley itself, older than any later conquest or creed.

### Q: What was Pharaonism?

Pharaonism was a current of Egyptian nationalism that located the nation's identity in its pre-Islamic, pharaonic past. Its supporters, including the writer Taha Hussein and the sculptor Mahmoud Mukhtar, argued that Egypt's distinctiveness came from the civilization of the Nile valley, which set Egyptians apart from other Arabs and Muslims. Pharaonism flourished in the 1920s and 1930s, boosted by the discovery of Tutankhamun's tomb and expressed in literature, art, and calls for a Mediterranean orientation. It never became official doctrine, and critics charged that it cut Egypt off from its Arab and Islamic heritage. Its importance lies in what it reveals: modern Egyptian identity was contested, and one serious answer to "what is Egypt" reached back seven thousand years rather than across the Arab world.

### Q: How did nationalism resist the British?

Egyptian nationalists resisted the British occupation with politics, words, and organized noncooperation rather than armies. Mustafa Kamil's National Party, founded in 1907, built its campaign on the Arabic press, especially the newspaper al-Liwa, on petitions, and on appeals to French and British public opinion, demanding evacuation through argument and publicity. Students, lawyers, and journalists turned boycotts of British goods and institutions into weapons of moral pressure. The Legislative Council and the press became forums where Egyptians challenged the occupation's legality. After the First World War this repertoire expanded into the mass demonstrations and strikes that forced Britain to negotiate, but the core method of the nationalist movement was already established: make the occupation politically costly while denying it any Egyptian legitimacy.

### Q: What role did religion play in Egyptian identity?

Religion was both a pillar of Egyptian identity and a question nationalists had to manage. Islam was the faith of the great majority, and al-Azhar gave Egypt religious authority across the Sunni world; Islamic language shaped how Egyptians talked about justice, community, and resistance. The Coptic Church preserved a Christian tradition older than the Arab conquest, and nationalists insisted Copts were Egyptians first, making Muslim-Coptic unity a test of the movement's sincerity. At the same time, the leading nationalist organizations presented themselves as territorial rather than religious, arguing that citizenship, not creed, defined the Egyptian. This balance was never settled. Secular nationalists, Islamists, and church leaders offered rival visions of how much public life religion should govern, and the argument ran through the whole identity debate.

### Q: How did Egyptian nationalism change over time?

Egyptian nationalism changed its center of gravity several times. In the late nineteenth century it meant loyalty to a reforming Egypt within the Ottoman order; after the British occupation of 1882 it became an anti-colonial movement demanding evacuation. The 1920s added Pharaonism, which rooted the nation in its ancient past and looked toward the Mediterranean. From the 1930s onward, Arab nationalism gained ground, arguing that Egypt's future lay with the Arab world, a view that triumphed after the 1952 Revolution under Nasser's leadership. Meanwhile Islamic currents, from the Muslim Brotherhood founded in 1928 onward, offered an identity built on faith rather than territory. Each shift reflected new circumstances: occupation, independence, regional war, and the search for an idea big enough to hold a changing country together.

### Q: Who was Mustafa Kamil and what did the National Party stand for?

Mustafa Kamil (1874-1908) was the founder of organized Egyptian nationalism and of the National Party, which he launched in 1907. A French-educated lawyer and brilliant orator, he built his movement on the newspaper al-Liwa, which he founded in 1900, using it to denounce the British occupation and to demand immediate evacuation. His slogan, often rendered as "Egypt for the Egyptians," framed independence as a national right rather than a favor to be granted. Kamil sought French diplomatic support and appealed to British liberal opinion, arguing that the occupation contradicted Britain's own principles. He died young, at thirty-three, but his party and his press gave the nationalist movement its first permanent organization, and his demand for evacuation became the movement's fixed goal.

### Q: How did the Dinshaway incident of 1906 strengthen Egyptian nationalism?

In June 1906 a group of British officers shooting pigeons near the village of Dinshaway clashed with local farmers, and in the ensuing confrontation one officer died, apparently of heatstroke. The British response was a special tribunal that sentenced four villagers to death and others to flogging and imprisonment. The harshness of the punishments shocked Egyptian opinion across classes: peasants saw their own vulnerability, while urban nationalists saw proof that the occupation served British interests rather than justice. Mustafa Kamil seized on the case in al-Liwa and in his speeches abroad, turning Dinshaway into an international symbol of colonial injustice. Historians generally agree the incident widened the nationalist movement's social base and hastened Lord Cromer's departure in 1907.

### Q: Who was Ahmad Lutfi al-Sayyid, and why was he called the teacher of the nation?

Ahmad Lutfi al-Sayyid (1872-1963) was a philosopher, journalist, and educator whom admirers called "the teacher of the nation." As editor of the newspaper al-Jarida and first director of the Egyptian University when it opened in 1908, he argued that Egypt needed liberal education and constitutional government more than slogans. He taught that Egyptians should think for themselves, value reason and individual judgment, and build a modern nation through schools rather than through blind imitation of either East or West. His students and readers carried these ideas into politics, journalism, and the university itself. In a nationalist movement often defined by protest, Lutfi al-Sayyid represented its constructive wing: the belief that independence had to be earned by creating an educated citizenry.

### Q: What did Taha Hussein argue in The Future of Culture in Egypt?

In The Future of Culture in Egypt, published in 1938, Taha Hussein argued that Egypt belonged culturally to the Mediterranean and European world rather than to the East. He claimed that Egypt's pharaonic heritage, its long contact with Greece and Rome, and its modern reforms under Muhammad Ali had made it part of Western civilization, and he urged Egypt to embrace European-style education, science, and democratic institutions without apology. The book was a high point of Pharaonist thinking: it treated the ancient past as a living inheritance that entitled Egypt to full modernity. Critics, especially Islamists and Arab nationalists, accused him of cutting Egypt off from its Arab and Islamic roots, and the controversy showed how sharply the identity question divided even the country's leading minds.

### Q: What did Mahmoud Mukhtar's sculpture 'Egypt's Awakening' symbolize?

Mahmoud Mukhtar's granite sculpture "Egypt's Awakening" (Nahdat Misr), unveiled in 1928, shows a sphinx rising beside a peasant woman who lifts her veil. The sphinx stands for ancient Egypt stirring after a long sleep, and the fellaha for the modern nation casting off backwardness and claiming its future. Commissioned by public subscription and first placed before Cairo's main railway station, it was the first monumental work by an Egyptian sculptor in a modern national style. Nationalists read it as a visual manifesto: the pharaonic past and the Egyptian people, joined in a single awakening. The statue became the best-known image of Pharaonism, proof that the identity debate was fought in art as well as in politics.

### Q: Why was the Arabic language central to Egyptian nationalist identity?

The Arabic language was central to Egyptian nationalist identity because it was the medium in which the nation learned to speak to itself. The nineteenth-century Nahda modernized Arabic prose and gave Egypt the newspapers, novels, and textbooks through which nationalist ideas circulated. Arabic linked Egypt to the wider Arab world, yet Egyptian writers also used it to mark their country's distinctiveness, developing a literary style rooted in Egyptian speech, as in Muhammad Husayn Haykal's novel Zaynab. Sermons, songs, and school lessons carried the national message to people who never read a newspaper. For nationalists, defending and modernizing Arabic was therefore a patriotic act: a nation that controlled its language, they believed, controlled the terms of its own identity.

### Q: How did Coptic intellectuals support Egyptian nationalism?

Coptic intellectuals played a visible part in Egyptian nationalism, and their participation proved the movement's claim to represent all Egyptians. Coptic journalists, lawyers, and politicians joined the nationalist press and parties, arguing that Copts were Egyptians by history and homeland, not a separate community to be bargained over. Figures such as Makram Ebeid, who rose to the top of nationalist politics, embodied this integration at the highest level. Coptic support mattered because the British sometimes posed as protectors of Christian minorities, and Coptic nationalists denied them that pretext. Their presence in the movement turned Muslim-Coptic unity from a slogan into a political fact, and it anchored the territorial idea of Egypt: a nation defined by the land its people shared.

### Q: Did Pharaonism conflict with Arab or Islamic identity?

Whether Pharaonism conflicted with Arab or Islamic identity was one of the sharpest disputes in the identity debate, and the answer depended on who was asked. Pharaonists such as Taha Hussein argued that celebrating the pre-Islamic past enriched Egyptian identity without replacing Islam or the Arabic language; in their view, a nation could honor seven thousand years of history at once. Islamist critics replied that Pharaonism elevated pagan antiquity over the Islamic heritage that had shaped Egypt for thirteen centuries, while Arab nationalists charged that it isolated Egypt from the Arab world. Most Egyptians lived with both loyalties untroubled, but among intellectuals the clash was real: it asked whether Egypt's deepest self was the Nile valley or the wider community of Arabs and Muslims.

### Q: How did Egyptian cinema spread nationalist ideas?

Egyptian cinema carried nationalist ideas to audiences far beyond the readers of newspapers. From the 1920s onward, Egyptian films used colloquial Arabic, popular songs, and familiar settings to tell stories that flattered national pride and mocked foreign arrogance. The founding of Studio Misr in 1935 by the industrialist Talaat Harb gave the industry a deliberate national mission: to build an Egyptian cinema free of foreign control. Historical films revived pharaonic and Islamic glories, while comedies and melodramas showed ordinary Egyptians outwitting the powerful. Stars became national figures, and the shared experience of the movie house helped create a common cultural vocabulary. Cinema thus did for identity what the press had done a generation earlier, making the nation visible to itself.

### Q: How did Muhammad Abduh's reformism shape Egyptian identity?

Muhammad Abduh (1849-1905), Grand Mufti of Egypt from 1899, shaped Egyptian identity through a reformism that tried to reconcile Islam with modern life. He argued that reason and revelation agreed, that Muslims should reopen independent judgment (ijtihad) rather than blindly follow tradition, and that education, not superstition, would strengthen both faith and nation. His reforms at al-Azhar and his writings gave Egyptian nationalists an Islamic vocabulary for modernity: one could be fully Muslim and fully modern at once. His students, including Ahmad Lutfi al-Sayyid and Qasim Amin, carried his rationalism into liberal nationalism and social reform. Abduh's legacy was therefore double: he renewed religious thought and supplied the nationalist movement with proof that authenticity and progress need not conflict.
