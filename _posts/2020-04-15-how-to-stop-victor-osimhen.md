The plan is lateral compression plus one named defender whose only job is to arrive at the flight of the ball already moving forward, and the price of that plan is that the opponent gets to switch the ball across your block, unopposed, for ninety minutes. That is the compressed answer to how to stop Victor Osimhen, and everything below is the argument for why the two instinctive alternatives fail. Drop the block deep and you invite the crosses that feed his best quality. Hold a high line and you hand him the running start he wants. Neither is wrong because it is timid or because it is brave. Each is wrong because it solves one half of a two-part problem and pays for the solution by worsening the other half.

Most opposition meetings about a forward like this go wrong in the first two minutes, when somebody says the word pace and somebody else says the word aerial, and the room silently accepts that these are two separate problems requiring two separate answers. They are not. A centre-forward who accelerates into the space between full-back and centre-back and who also attacks a delivery with momentum is exploiting the same structural feature twice: the horizontal gap that opens when a defensive line orients toward the ball. Close that gap and both threats lose their supply. Fail to close it and no amount of individual bravery in either duel will save you, because the duel will keep arriving under conditions the forward chose.

This article is the counter blueprint for that specific profile. It sets the shape and the line height, names the defender type who fits the individual assignment, defines the collective triggers, sequences the plan phase by phase, adds the transition and dead-ball layers, gives the fallbacks for when the first plan fails, states plainly what you are choosing to concede, and then rebuilds the whole thing for a side without organized, rehearsed compactness. If you want the wider category frame first, the way this forward type sits alongside the other elite nines is handled in the [complete guide to stopping elite strikers](/2020/01/01/stopping-elite-strikers-complete-guide/), which owns the general version of the question and routes down to each single-subject plan.

## Who You Are Actually Planning Against

Victor Osimhen is a Nigerian centre-forward whose game was built across Wolfsburg, Charleroi, and Lille before Napoli, and who has been an established international for his country through that arc. The profile pairs genuine acceleration into wide-central space with a real aerial threat and a direct, physical style of attacking the box. That combination is uncommon. Plenty of forwards run in behind. Plenty of forwards win headers. The number who force a defensive line to answer both questions in the same phase, with the same personnel, is small, and that is why the standard answers break.

One detail matters more than any other for planning purposes, and most opposition dossiers get it wrong. He attacks the space between the full-back and the centre-back far more than he attacks the space directly behind the centre-back. Those are different runs with different defeat conditions, and treating them as interchangeable is the single most common preparation error against this profile. A forward who runs directly behind a centre-back is beaten by line height and by the centre-back's turning speed. A forward who runs into the gap outside the centre-back and inside the full-back is beaten by geometry, by starting angles, and by who is willing to travel wide. You can hold a perfect line and still lose the second run every time, because the second run does not care where your line is. It cares where your full-back's shoulders are pointing.

### What is the single adjustment that stops Victor Osimhen?

Compress laterally rather than dropping deep, angle the ball-side full-back so the gap outside the centre-back is closed before the pass is struck, and designate one defender as the first-contact player on every long delivery. Accept the switch of play. Refuse the diagonal into the channel and refuse the standing-start header.

That is the plan in four clauses, and each clause has a mechanism behind it that a coach has to understand before he can sell it to players. Players do not execute instructions they think are arbitrary. They execute instructions they can feel the logic of at three in the afternoon with their lungs burning. So the mechanism comes first.

## The Mechanism: Why Two Threats Share One Defeat Condition

### The channel run is a geometry problem, not a speed problem

Start with the honest version of the duel. If a fast centre-forward gets a five-metre head start into open grass with the ball played into his path, no defender in the world catches him, and the plan is already dead. The counter-plan therefore cannot be a plan to win that footrace. It has to be a plan to prevent the footrace from ever starting under those conditions. That means the intervention happens two passes earlier and fifteen metres away from where the goal eventually gets scored.

Here is what actually creates the gap. When the ball goes wide to an opposition winger or an advancing full-back, your own full-back turns his hips and shoulders toward the ball. That is not a mistake; it is what he is coached to do, because he has to be able to press, to jockey, and to block the cross. But the instant his shoulders square to the touchline, the corridor behind his inside shoulder opens, and it opens diagonally, running from the outside of your centre-back's position toward your own six-yard box. That corridor is the runway. Osimhen does not need it to be enormous. He needs it to exist for about a second and a half, which is the time between the wide player lifting his head and the ball leaving his foot.

The forward's job in that second and a half is to be in a starting position from which he can enter the corridor with momentum while your two defenders are stationary or moving in the wrong direction. He achieves that by holding a position on the blind side of the centre-back, roughly on the centre-back's outside shoulder, far enough away that the defender loses him in peripheral vision when the defender's eyes go to the ball. The run then arrives from a place the centre-back is not looking, at an angle the full-back cannot cover because his weight is committed forward. Nobody has been beaten for pace at that point. Two players have been beaten for orientation, and the pace only shows up afterward.

The defeat condition follows directly. If the corridor is closed before the ball is struck, the run has nowhere to go and the forward has to stop, check, and receive with his back to goal instead, which is a completely different and far more manageable event. Closing the corridor is not a heroic act by one defender. It is a starting-angle decision by the full-back and a willingness question for the centre-back, and both decisions are made before the wide player has the ball under control.

### The aerial threat is a first-contact problem, not a height problem

Now the second half. The standard preparation instinct against a strong header of the ball is to find your tallest defender and tell him to mark the forward. That instinct misreads what is actually happening in the duel.

An aerial duel between two players standing still is largely decided by height, reach, and standing leap. An aerial duel where one player arrives running and the other has to jump from a static position is decided by momentum, and momentum beats a six-centimetre height advantage almost every time. A forward moving toward the flight of the ball converts horizontal speed into vertical lift and, more importantly, into contact. He gets his body across the defender's front, gets his shoulder and forearm into the defender's chest at the moment of takeoff, and the defender's jump collapses before it starts. The header is then won at a height neither player would reach from a standing start, because one of them is rising through the contact and the other is absorbing it.

That is where the article's central claim comes from, and it deserves a name that a coaching staff can use in a meeting.

**The first-contact rule: against a forward who wins the ball in the air on the move, the defensive objective is not to out-jump him but to be the player moving forward at the moment of contact. That is not an aerial-ability decision. It is a starting-position decision made two seconds earlier.**

The rule reframes the whole selection question. You are no longer looking for the tallest available defender. You are looking for the defender who is most reliably in front of the forward when the ball is in the air, which is a function of how early he reads the delivery, how comfortable he is defending with his back to his own goal, and whether he has the courage to attack a ball that is dropping over his own shoulder. Height helps at the margin. Starting position decides it.

### And here is why the two threats collapse into one answer

The channel run needs a horizontal gap in the defensive line. The aerial threat needs the defender to be moving backward, or stationary, when the ball arrives. Both conditions are produced by the same defensive behaviour: a line that reacts to the ball by shifting late and dropping, rather than by sliding early and staying flat.

A block that drops deep as the ball goes wide gives the forward exactly what he wants in the air, because every defender is now retreating toward his own goal and the forward is the only player on the pitch travelling forward. A block that stays high but slides slowly gives him the corridor. A block that slides early, stays flat, and compresses the width of its own back line so the gaps between defenders shrink to two or three metres does neither. The forward has no corridor to enter, and if a delivery comes anyway, the ball-side defender is stepping toward it rather than backing away from it.

Lateral compression is the single answer to both questions. Everything else in this blueprint is detail about how to execute it without falling apart somewhere else.

## The Shape and the Line Height

### Why the block is medium, not low and not high

Set the defensive line so it holds roughly on the edge of your own defensive third when the ball is in the opponent's build-up, and no deeper than eighteen to twenty metres from your own goal when the ball reaches the final third in wide areas. That is a medium block, and it is chosen for one reason: it is the only line height that lets you keep the distance behind you short enough to survive a run and the distance in front of you short enough that crosses have to be struck from further out.

The low block fails against this profile for a specific and often misunderstood reason. Sitting deep does reduce the space behind, which feels like it addresses the pace. But it also concedes the entire middle third, which means the opposition full-backs and wide midfielders receive the ball facing your goal, unpressured, twenty-five metres out, and can pick the moment and the height of their delivery. Against a forward whose aerial threat rises with the quality of the service, giving the service unlimited time is a poor trade. You removed one weapon and armed the other.

The high line fails for the obvious reason, but it is worth being precise about it. A high line does not lose because it is high; it loses because a high line requires the distance between your last defender and your goalkeeper's starting position to be defended by somebody, and against a forward who accelerates into that space diagonally rather than straight, your goalkeeper's coverage arc is much smaller than the coaching manual suggests. A sweeper-keeper covers the vertical ball over the top well. He covers the diagonal into the channel poorly, because the ball is travelling away from him laterally and he has to commit to a line he cannot correct.

The medium block, held with genuine lateral discipline, keeps the running lanes short enough that the forward's acceleration advantage has less pitch to express itself on, and keeps the delivery point far enough out that the flight time gives your first-contact defender time to get moving.

### The horizontal distances that actually matter

Most coaching conversations about compactness are vertical: the distance between the defensive line and the midfield line. Against this profile the vertical number is secondary. The number that decides the match is the horizontal spacing inside the back four.

The working target is that no two defenders in the back line are ever more than eight to ten metres apart while the ball is in a wide area on their side, and that the ball-side full-back is never more than twelve metres from his nearest centre-back. That sounds tight because it is. It means the far-side full-back has effectively abandoned his winger and tucked inside to become a third centre-back, and it means the whole unit has slid so far toward the ball that a switch of play is available at almost any moment.

Conceding that switch is not an accident of the plan. It is the plan. A cross-field pass takes between one and a half and two and a half seconds to travel, which is enough time for a well-drilled back line to slide across and re-establish the same compression on the other side. What it costs you is a lot of running, which is why this plan is a fitness decision as much as a tactical one. What it buys you is the permanent absence of the corridor, and the corridor is the whole game.

Against a back three, the same principle applies with a different mechanism. The wide centre-back in a back three is the natural answer to a channel forward, because he starts in the corridor rather than having to travel into it, and because the wing-back ahead of him can press the wide player without opening anything behind. Sides with the personnel for a back five defending phase should strongly consider it against this profile, and the reason is not that three centre-backs are better than two in a general sense. It is that the specific gap being attacked is occupied by a defender at kick-off instead of being covered by a decision.

### What the midfield line is for

The midfield line has one job in this plan and it is not screening shots. It is denying the diagonal.

The passes that hurt you are not the ones played from the middle of the pitch. They are the ones played from a half-space by a midfielder who has been given time to lift his head, and the ones played early from deep by a centre-back who has spotted the run before your line has reacted. Both are diagonal balls, and both are killed by pressure on the passer's outside foot at the moment he shapes to strike.

So the ball-side central midfielder in this plan does not drop into the back line to add a body. He steps toward the passer at an angle that closes the outside of his standing foot, forcing the pass inside or backward. The far-side central midfielder does not stay wide with his own winger; he slides across to occupy the inside lane the full-back vacated when he tucked in. That second job is the one that gets forgotten, and its absence is what turns a compressed block into a block with a hole in the middle of it.

## The Individual Assignment and the Defender Profile

### What kind of centre-back should take this assignment?

The front-foot centre-back who defends forward and travels wide without panicking: quick over the first ten metres, comfortable turning and running, and willing to attack a ball dropping over his shoulder. Pure size without mobility loses the channel. Pure recovery pace without aggression loses the aerial duel. The profile that survives is the aggressive reader.

Unpack that, because the selection call is where most plans are won or lost before kick-off.

The first profile that fails is the big, dominant, old-fashioned stopper. He is often the instinctive pick, because the forward is physical and the reasoning goes that physicality answers physicality. What actually happens is that the stopper is superb in the two or three duels that occur in his own zone, and hopeless in the six or seven that occur ten metres outside it. The forward simply stops attacking the middle and attacks the corridor instead, and the stopper either refuses to travel wide, which leaves the run unaccompanied, or travels wide and gets turned in space, which is worse.

The second profile that fails is the pure recovery athlete: the centre-back whose game is built on being fast enough to correct mistakes. He is comfortable defending a big pitch, which sounds ideal, but his instinct is to give himself a metre of insurance, and that metre is exactly the head start the forward needs to arrive at a delivery with momentum. He wins the footrace and loses the header, every week, and after four or five weeks somebody in the crowd starts talking about his aerial ability when the actual problem is his starting depth.

The profile that works is the front-foot reader. He starts tighter than is comfortable, backs himself to travel wide, and, above all, moves toward the ball early. He does not need to be the tallest player in the squad. He needs to be the one who commits first, because under the first-contact rule the defender who commits first is the defender who wins.

For the full-back, the requirement is different and narrower. The ball-side full-back in this plan is not primarily a one-on-one defender against a winger. He is the man who closes the corridor. That means his defensive starting position is angled: inside shoulder dropped, hips half-open so he can see both the ball and the run without turning his head, and his depth set relative to his centre-back rather than relative to his winger. A full-back who tracks his winger to the touchline and then presses the ball with his shoulders square has just built the runway himself, and no instruction to the centre-backs will repair it.

### The designated first-contact defender

The second piece of the individual assignment is the one most sides never make explicit, and making it explicit is the highest-value five minutes in the whole opposition meeting.

Name one player, before kick-off, whose responsibility on every long or lofted delivery is to arrive at the drop zone moving forward. Not to mark the forward. Not to shadow him. To attack the ball. In most set-ups this is the ball-near centre-back, but it can be the full-back on a wide delivery from deep and it should be the goalkeeper on anything inside the six-yard box, and the point of naming it is that the alternative is two defenders both watching the forward and neither watching the flight.

The instruction to that player is specific. He starts one metre in front of the forward, on the ball side, not level and not behind. He watches the striker of the ball, not the runner. On the strike, he moves toward the flight immediately, before he knows exactly where it is going, and he adjusts on the way. He accepts that he will occasionally get it wrong and be beaten by a ball dropping behind him, and he accepts that because the alternative failure, standing flat-footed while a forward runs onto the ball across his front, is worse and more frequent.

Everyone else's instruction is the mirror of that one. The second defender does not also attack the ball. He drops two metres and takes the knockdown zone, because a contested header goes somewhere and the somewhere is usually eight to twelve metres behind the point of contact.

### The one-on-one moment that will happen anyway

No plan removes every duel. When the forward does receive with a start on your defender, running at the last line with the goalkeeper behind, the instruction is to show him toward the touchline and to delay rather than to tackle. The reason is not caution. It is that a forward whose first instinct is to attack the box directly is uncomfortable being pushed onto a wider angle, because his finishing strength is arriving into the six-yard box rather than manufacturing a chance from a narrow angle at speed.

Delay also buys the covering centre-back time to arrive, and against this profile the second defender arriving is worth more than the first defender lunging. The tackle from behind in that zone is where you concede the penalty, and the discipline question is dealt with in its own section below because it is the part of the plan most likely to be misread by players as an invitation to get physical.

## The Full-Back's Angle: The Detail That Decides the Corridor

Everything in this blueprint rests on one player getting one body position right, repeatedly, while tired. It is worth spending a section on the technical detail, because "stand at an angle" is not a coaching point, it is a slogan, and players cannot execute slogans.

The starting position is measured against the centre-back, not against the winger. The full-back's default is roughly two to three metres in advance of his centre-back and eight to ten metres inside the touchline when the ball is in the middle third on his side. He is not level with the touchline defender he is nominally marking, and he is not level with his own back line either. He sits in a diagonal relationship with both, which is what allows him to move to either without turning.

The feet matter more than the body. The outside foot is forward and the inside foot is dropped, which orients his hips at roughly forty-five degrees to the touchline. From that stance he can push off the outside foot to close the ball or off the inside foot to run the corridor, and neither movement requires a preparatory turn. A player standing square has to turn before he can run, and the turn costs him about half a second, which at this level is four or five metres.

The eyes are the piece that gets coached last and matters most. His head should be on a two-beat cycle: ball, inside shoulder, ball, inside shoulder. The check over the inside shoulder is not about locating the forward precisely; it is about confirming that the forward has not moved into a launching position. Full-backs who watch the ball continuously are not lazy or unaware. They are doing what pressing coaching taught them, and the fix is to give them a specific cue for when to look, which is every time the ball changes possession or changes direction.

The failure mode to drill against is the one that feels most professional: sprinting to close the ball. A full-back who breaks toward the ball in a straight line has committed his weight forward and has opened the gap behind his inside shoulder by his own momentum. The corrective is the curved approach, arriving from the inside on a bent path, which arrives slightly later and arrives with his body already between the ball and the danger. Slower and correct beats faster and beaten.

## The Collective Triggers and the Cover Behaviour

A shape without triggers is a diagram. The triggers are what turn it into a defensive performance, and against this profile there are four that matter, each with a single named cue and a single named response.

The first trigger is the opposition centre-back lifting his head with the ball at his feet and no immediate pressure. That is the diagonal cue. The response is that the back line slides toward the far side of the pitch by two or three metres before the pass is struck, closing the corridor pre-emptively rather than reacting to the ball's flight. Sliding after the pass is struck is too late; the ball travels faster than the defensive line does.

The second trigger is the opposition full-back receiving on the touchline with his body open. That is the cross cue, and it activates the first-contact designation. The named defender steps in front of the forward, the second centre-back drops into the knockdown zone, and the far full-back tucks to the back post. The whole rotation takes about a second and a half and has to be automatic, which means it has to be rehearsed, which is the reason the [session design for containing a superstar](/2020/10/15/training-to-contain-a-superstar/) exists as its own article rather than being crammed into this one.

The third trigger is the forward himself dropping toward the ball. That is the good cue, and the response is counterintuitive: nobody follows him far. When a channel forward comes short, the temptation is for a centre-back to travel with him, which is precisely what he wants, because a centre-back who has travelled twelve metres up the pitch has vacated the corridor for somebody else and has surrendered his own starting position for the next delivery. The correct response is that the holding midfielder picks him up from behind, the centre-back holds his line, and the reception is allowed to happen with the forward facing his own goal. A forward receiving to feet thirty metres out with his back to goal is not the problem you are trying to solve.

The fourth trigger is the turnover, and it is the most dangerous of the four because it is the one moment when lateral compression does not exist. The response is covered in the transition section, because it needs more than a paragraph.

### Rest defense: the shape you hold while you have the ball

Half of the counter-plan happens while your own team is in possession, and sides that ignore this lose the match in the four or five seconds after they give the ball away.

The rest-defense requirement against this profile is that at least two of your back line stay central and level with each other at all times, regardless of where the ball is, and that the deepest midfielder positions himself on the inside of the corridor rather than behind the ball. That means your attacking full-backs cannot both advance. It means the holding midfielder does not join the final-third overload. And it means you accept a slightly slower, narrower attack in exchange for never presenting the forward with a five-metre head start against one defender.

If that sounds like a heavy price, consider what the alternative buys the opponent. A forward with this profile does his most efficient work in the eight seconds after a turnover, when the defensive structure is stretched and there is grass in every direction. Removing that phase from the match is worth more than the extra body in your own attack, and it is the cheapest structural decision available to you.

## Defending Him Out of Different Opponent Structures

The plan above is written as though the opposition shape were fixed. It is not, and the supply lines change enough between structures that a coaching staff should prepare two or three versions rather than one.

### Against a 4-3-3 with high, wide wingers

This is the structure the base plan assumes. The wide players hold width, the full-backs overlap, and the delivery into the box comes from the touchline or from the cut-back angle just inside it. The forward's runs are launched from a central starting position and break toward whichever side the ball is on.

The base plan holds without modification, with one emphasis: because the winger holds width, your full-back is genuinely occupied and cannot tuck in early. That means the ball-side centre-back's willingness to travel wide becomes load-bearing rather than optional, and the far-side full-back's tuck has to be deeper and faster. If your centre-backs will not travel, this structure will beat you down the sides all afternoon regardless of what else you install.

### Against a 4-2-3-1 with a creator behind the striker

Here the danger changes character. The wide players come inside, the full-backs provide the width, and the killer pass is frequently played from the half-space by the number ten rather than from the touchline by a winger.

Two adjustments follow. The first is that your holding midfielders take on the primary job of closing the passing angle from the half-space, which means one of them is effectively man-oriented on the creator whenever the ball is within his range. The second is that your full-backs get more freedom to protect the inside, because the width is being provided by an opposition full-back arriving from deep, and a late-arriving full-back is a slower, more readable threat than a winger who starts wide. That freedom is precisely what closes the gap the forward attacks, so this is the structure your plan handles most comfortably.

### Against a back three with wing-backs

A 3-5-2 or 3-4-3 opponent creates the diagonal from a different origin: the wide centre-back, who has time and space and a clear sightline down the outside channel. That is the most dangerous delivery point of all against this profile, because it is unpressed by default and the angle is perfect.

The adjustment is that one of your forwards must be given a defensive job he will not enjoy: he presses the wide centre-back on the outside shoulder to force the ball infield, every time, without being asked to win it. He is not pressing to regain possession; he is pressing to change the angle. Sides that leave the wide centre-back alone against a channel forward are handing over a free diagonal every ninety seconds, and no back line survives that volume.

### Against a direct, long-ball opponent

Some sides simplify the whole question and go over the top, repeatedly, with a long kick from the goalkeeper or a diagonal from the centre-back into the channel. It looks crude and it is extremely effective against a back line that is not prepared for it.

The adjustment is the first-contact rule applied at range, plus a change in your own line's behaviour: on the back-lift, your line steps forward five metres rather than dropping. Stepping into a long ball feels wrong and it is correct, because it converts a ball dropping behind your defenders into a ball dropping in front of them, and a ball dropping in front of a defender who is moving forward is a defensive win. The risk is the pass played early and flat into the space you just vacated, which is why the step is on the back-lift and not on any earlier cue.

### Against a front two rather than a lone forward

Everything above assumes he is the single reference point. When he plays alongside a partner, the arithmetic changes in a way that punishes the base plan if it is applied unmodified.

The problem is that lateral compression relies on your far-side defenders being free to tuck inside, and a second forward removes that freedom by occupying one of them. The gap the plan is built to close reopens, not because anybody made an error, but because you have one fewer spare body than the plan assumes.

There are two workable responses. The first is to accept a numerical match and defend with three central defenders, which restores the spare man and lets the wide centre-back own the channel directly. This is the cleaner answer and it costs you a body higher up the pitch.

The second is to keep four at the back and buy the spare man from midfield, dropping the deepest midfielder between the centre-backs whenever the ball is in a wide area. This preserves your attacking shape and asks a great deal of one player, who has to read the moment to drop, arrive early enough to matter, and get back out again when the ball is recycled. It works with the right individual and it collapses without him.

The choice between the two is a squad question rather than a tactical one. If your best defensive midfielder is a positional reader, buy the spare man from midfield. If he is a ball-winner who wants to press, use three at the back and let him press, because asking a front-foot midfielder to hold a covering position for ninety minutes produces the worst version of both jobs.

One further detail applies regardless of which response you choose. With a front two, the partner's movement is frequently designed to move your first-contact defender rather than to receive the ball, and if that designation is being dragged around by a decoy run, the delivery arrives with nobody attacking it. The instruction has to be explicit: the first-contact player is contact-first and man-second, and if a decoy takes him away from the drop zone, the job transfers immediately to the nearest defender rather than travelling with him.

## Cutting the Supply: Plan Against the Service, Not Just the Striker

Most opposition plans against a famous forward spend ninety percent of the meeting on the forward and ten percent on the players who feed him. That ratio should be closer to even, because the deliveries that hurt you come from a small number of identifiable players making a small number of identifiable passes.

### The three suppliers to identify

The first is the deep progressive passer, usually a left-sided centre-back or a deep-lying midfielder with the range to hit a fifty-metre diagonal. The whole threat of the channel run depends on somebody being able to see it and execute it, and against many sides only one or two players actually can. Denying that player's outside foot for ninety minutes is a cheaper intervention than defending the run afterward, and it is the single highest-leverage individual instruction in the plan.

The second is the wide crosser, and the detail that matters is not how good he is but where he delivers from. A player who habitually reaches the byline produces cut-backs and low deliveries, and your withdrawn midfielder becomes the key man. A player who habitually delivers early from deep produces the lofted balls that trigger the aerial contest, and your first-contact designation becomes the key instruction. Watch two matches and you will know which type you are facing.

The third is the runner beyond, the winger or attacking midfielder who makes a run past the forward to drag your covering defender out of the picture. This is the most underappreciated part of the supply chain: the forward's best chances often arrive because a second runner has occupied the defender who was supposed to cover the corridor. Your midfield must be told to track that second run, and the moment the tracking stops is the moment the plan opens.

### Choosing which supply to cut

You cannot cut all three, so choose by cost. Denying the deep passer is a positional job that costs one forward some pressing energy and nothing else, which makes it the first choice almost every time. Denying the crosser costs a full-back his attacking output. Denying the second runner costs a midfielder his position in your own counter-attack.

Rank them in that order, install the first, install the second if you have the legs, and treat the third as a coaching point rather than a structural commitment. A plan with three defensive commitments and no attacking outlet loses matches by a different route, and the point of a counter-plan is to win, not to concede fewer goals while never scoring.

## How to Stop Victor Osimhen Phase by Phase

### Phase one: the opposition build-up

While the ball is with the goalkeeper and centre-backs, you are not pressing high, because pressing high against this profile means playing a high line, and a high line means the corridor is behind you with acres to run into.

Instead, the front two or three screen the central lanes and shepherd the ball wide, deliberately. You want the build-up to go down the touchline, because the touchline is a defender you do not have to pay. The forward line's job here is angle, not intensity: cut the pass into the holding midfielder, allow the pass to the full-back, and then arrive as the ball arrives so the full-back's first touch has to be backward or square.

The one exception is the goalkeeper distribution moment. If the opposition goalkeeper is a long-kick outlet who launches into the channel toward this forward, the front line steps up on the goalkeeper's back-lift specifically to force the kick early and inaccurate, and the back line steps forward five metres at the same moment so that the flight arrives in front of your defenders rather than behind them. That is the first-contact rule applied at seventy metres instead of twenty.

### Phase two: the ball reaches the wide areas

This is the phase the whole plan exists for. The moment the ball goes to a wide player in your defensive half, three things happen simultaneously.

The full-back on that side closes to a distance of about three metres, close enough to prevent an unhurried look up, and crucially he closes on a curved path from the inside, so his body already sits between the ball and the corridor. He is not sprinting straight at the ball. He is arriving from the inside, which means his momentum is carrying him across the runway rather than away from it.

The ball-side centre-back travels wide with him, holding a position roughly on the outside of the penalty area's width, and this is the instruction that gets refused most often in practice. Centre-backs are coached their whole careers to protect the middle, and asking one to leave the middle feels like an error. The reason it is not an error here is that the far centre-back and the tucked-in far full-back have already re-formed a pair in the middle, so the middle is not empty; it is occupied by different people. Selling that to a back four takes a full session on its own, and it will not happen from a whiteboard.

The ball-side central midfielder drops into the space between the retreating full-back and the near centre-back, filling the seam at midfield height so that the cut-back and the short pass inside are both covered.

### Phase three: the delivery

When the cross comes, the first-contact designation resolves everything. The named defender attacks the near-post zone moving forward. The second centre-back takes the knockdown zone eight to ten metres behind. The far full-back takes the back post. The holding midfielder does not enter the box; he holds the penalty-spot line, because the cut-back is the most under-defended pass in amateur and professional football alike and the player who covers it has to be a midfielder, since every defender is occupied.

The goalkeeper's rule is the simplest in the plan and the hardest to hold: everything inside the six-yard box is his and he takes it with a decisive claim, and everything outside it belongs to the defender, and he does not start a movement he cannot finish. A goalkeeper who comes for a ball he cannot reach against a forward who arrives at speed does not merely lose the ball; he removes himself from the follow-up.

### Phase four: the second phase

Most goals against a compressed block do not come from the first delivery. They come from the second ball after a partial clearance, when the block has collapsed inward and nobody has re-established the line.

The rule is that on any clearance, the back line steps out together to the edge of the box within two seconds, and the nearest midfielder attacks the drop zone rather than marking a man. Stepping out is not about winning an offside decision. It is about restoring the compression before the next delivery, because a second cross into a broken block is where a forward with this profile does his damage.

### Phase five: game state

If you are leading with twenty minutes left, the temptation is to drop the line and defend the edge of your own box. Against this profile that is the single worst adjustment available, because it converts the match into a sequence of crosses into a crowded box with the forward attacking them with a running start.

The correct late-game adjustment is to keep the line where it is and change the personnel: a fresh centre-back or a fresh full-back on the ball side, because the plan is physically expensive and the failure mode is a tired full-back losing his starting angle by half a metre. Change the legs, not the height of the line. The wider argument about when a defensive structure should change and when it should hold is developed further in the [head-to-head on pace versus positioning](/2024/02/15/pace-or-positioning-beating-defenders/), which takes up the general question of which defensive quality actually decides these duels.

## Three Sequences, Walked Through Second by Second

Coaches install plans through scenarios more reliably than through principles, because a scenario tells a player what he will see. Here are the three situations that decide most matches against this profile, narrated from the cue to the outcome.

### Sequence one: the diagonal from the opposite half-space

The opposition left-sided centre-back receives with his head up, thirty-five metres from your goal, and no pressure within eight metres. Your right-sided full-back is fifteen metres from him, watching the winger in front of him. The forward is standing on the outside shoulder of your right centre-back, half a metre behind the defender's eyeline.

At the moment the passer's head comes up, your back line slides two to three metres toward that side, which sounds trivial and is the entire intervention. Your right full-back drops his inside shoulder and gives up two metres of ground to the winger. Your right centre-back steps half a metre wider and half a metre forward. The forward, who was preparing to enter a five-metre gap, is now looking at a two-metre gap with a defender's body already angled across it.

What happens next is that he does not make the run. Forwards of this quality do not run into closed doors; they check, they come short, or they drift to the other side. That is the outcome you wanted. You have not made a tackle, won a header, or made a single spectacular defensive action. You have removed the phase.

If the diagonal is played anyway, it now has to be threaded rather than lofted into space, which raises the difficulty of the pass by a large margin and gives your centre-back an interception angle he did not have three seconds earlier.

### Sequence two: the early cross from deep

The opposition right-back receives on the touchline, forty metres from goal, and immediately looks to deliver rather than to advance. Your left-back is closing but will not reach him. The forward begins his movement from the penalty spot toward the near post.

Your designated first-contact defender, in this case the left centre-back, does one thing: he moves to a position one metre in front of the forward on the ball side, and he watches the crosser's standing foot. On the strike, he goes. He does not wait to see where the ball is going. He commits toward the near-post zone and adjusts his path in flight.

The forward, arriving from behind him, now has a body in his running lane at the point of contact. He can still get his head to the ball, and sometimes he will, but he is now heading across a defender rather than through empty air, and the contact he wanted to initiate has already been initiated against him.

Meanwhile your right centre-back has dropped eight metres and is standing in the zone where a flicked or partially cleared ball lands. Your holding midfielder is on the penalty spot facing the ball, not marking anyone. Your right-back is at the back post. The box has four jobs and four people doing them, none of whom is trying to out-jump anybody.

### Sequence three: the turnover in your attacking third

Your full-back has advanced, your holding midfielder has drifted forward to support the attack, and the ball is lost thirty metres from the opposition goal. Two of their players are already facing forward. The forward has started his run before the ball is even under control.

There is no shape to save you here, so the plan has to have been made in advance. Your two centre-backs are level and eight metres apart because that is the standing rest-defense rule, which means the corridor between them is not available. Your holding midfielder, who was the designated delay man, is not sprinting toward the ball; he is running an angled line on the inside of the carrier, forcing the pass outward. Your recovering full-back is not chasing the ball either; he is running toward the space the forward is heading for, aiming to arrive at the corridor rather than at the player.

The pass, when it comes, is wide rather than forward. The forward receives it moving away from goal rather than toward it. Your centre-back shows him toward the touchline and delays. Four seconds later there are six defenders back and the phase is over.

Every part of that sequence was decided while your team had the ball. That is the uncomfortable truth about defending counter-attacks: the defending happens in the attacking phase, and any side that treats rest defense as an afterthought will lose to this profile no matter how well the block is organized.

## The Transition Addendum

### What kills this plan in transition?

Chasing the runner instead of denying the first pass. The counter-attack against a channel forward is a two-touch event: turnover, then one forward pass into the corridor. Rest defense must already have a player set to press the passer within a second, and two central defenders level, before possession is lost.

Transition is where this plan is most vulnerable, and being honest about that is more useful than pretending the compression covers everything. When your side loses the ball in the opposition half, the corridor is not eight metres wide; it is the whole pitch. There is no structural answer to that in the moment. The answer has to have been installed before the turnover.

Three habits carry the load. The first is the counter-press decision, which should be binary and pre-agreed: either the two nearest players press immediately and everybody else steps up behind them, or nobody presses and the whole side retreats to a line thirty metres from goal. The disaster is the half-press, where one player chases, the line does not step, and a single pass eliminates six players.

The second habit is the delay assignment. One named player, usually the holding midfielder, has the job of fouling nothing and stopping nothing, only of running back on the inside of the ball carrier's path to force the pass wide rather than forward. Buying two seconds is worth more than an attempted interception, because two seconds is the difference between the forward receiving in stride and the forward receiving with a defender goalside.

The third habit is the centre-backs' starting relationship. In transition they must be level and no more than ten metres apart. A staggered pair is comfortable in possession and fatal on the break, because the deeper of the two invites the run across his front and the higher of the two has no cover.

### The specific counter-attack pattern to prepare for

The pattern to rehearse against is the turnover in the middle third followed by an immediate diagonal into the channel on the side where your full-back is highest. Your defenders should recognize it as a shape, not as an event, and their reaction should be automatic: the high full-back does not chase the ball, he runs a recovery line to the inside of the forward's path, aiming to arrive at the corridor rather than at the player, while the ball-side centre-back turns and runs early rather than holding position and hoping the pass is overhit.

Turning early feels like conceding territory and it is, and it is still correct, because a centre-back who has already turned and is running can compete for the ball, while a centre-back who is caught square is running the first three metres of a footrace facing the wrong way. Against a forward with this acceleration profile those three metres are the match.

## The Set-Piece and Dead-Ball Addendum

Dead balls deserve their own treatment because a compressed medium block is a foul-generating structure. You will concede free kicks in wide areas, and the plan has to survive them.

### Defending the wide free kick

The marking scheme against this forward should be zonal at the near post, with one man-marking assignment on him, and the man-marker's instruction is the first-contact rule again rather than a shirt-tracking instruction. He starts one metre in front, on the side the delivery is coming from, and his objective is to be the player moving toward the ball. He is not trying to occupy the forward or to grapple with him; he is trying to make the forward attack the ball from behind a body already in motion.

The zonal element covers the near post and the six-yard line, because a delivery that beats the first zone is the delivery that finds a runner arriving. Sides that go fully man-oriented against a forward with a strong run-up are giving away the one advantage the defending team has, which is that zonal players can start moving on the strike while man-markers start moving on the run.

### Corners

The same logic, with one addition: the second-ball zone at the top of the box needs two players, not one. A partial clearance against a side with a physical presence in the box is a re-loaded attack, and the most common goal conceded is not the header from the corner but the header from the cross that follows the recycled corner ninety seconds later.

### Your own attacking corners

This is where sides forget the plan entirely. When you attack a corner, the forward you have spent all week planning against is standing on the halfway line with one defender near him, waiting for exactly the turnover you are about to give him.

Leave two players back, not one, and position them level with each other rather than one high and one deep. One of them should be your best recovery runner rather than your best defender, because the job is not to win a duel; it is to run alongside a forward for forty metres and force him wide of the shooting angle. This is the cheapest goal you will ever prevent and the one most often ignored.

## The Physicality Question, Answered Properly

### Where on the pitch is contact worth the risk?

Contact is legitimate and useful in the specific windows where it prevents a running start: the shoulder contact as the run begins, the body position that forces a check, the arm across the front on a cross. It is a losing tactic everywhere else, especially in your own box, where it converts a defensive success into a penalty.

The community position this section answers is the one that says a physical forward should be met with physicality, that you fight fire with fire, and that a defender who backs down has already lost the duel. That position contains a real insight buried inside a bad conclusion.

The real insight is that a forward with this profile relies on contact to complete his actions. He initiates the shoulder-to-shoulder as he enters the corridor, and he uses the contact to hold his line and unbalance the defender. A defender who accepts being physically manipulated will be manipulated. Meeting force with a settled, legal, well-timed body position genuinely changes the duel, and defenders who are technically strong in contact make this forward's runs meaningfully less productive.

The bad conclusion is that this makes physicality a general strategy. Consider where the contact actually occurs. In the corridor, thirty metres from goal, a shoulder-to-shoulder that stops a run and concedes a free kick is a good outcome, because a free kick from that position is not a scoring chance and the run has been eliminated. Inside your own penalty area, the identical action is a penalty. The forward attacks the box directly, which means a high proportion of the total contact between him and your defenders will occur inside the eighteen-yard line, and that is precisely where the physical approach carries a catastrophic price.

So the instruction is zonal rather than temperamental. Be physical in the corridor and in midfield, where a foul costs you nothing and prevents everything. Be technically clean inside the box, where the objective is body position and first contact with the ball, never contact with the man. A defender who cannot make that distinction under pressure is the wrong defender for this assignment, regardless of how good he is in a duel.

There is also a fatigue dimension worth stating. A defensive plan built on physical duels asks your centre-backs to fight for ninety minutes, and the duel a tired defender loses is the seventy-eighth-minute one. A defensive plan built on starting position and first contact asks them to think for ninety minutes and fight for about twelve, which is a far more sustainable load, and it is one reason this plan holds up better late than the physical alternative does.

## The Fallbacks: When the First Plan Fails

Plans fail in specific ways, and the value of preparing fallbacks is that a coaching staff can recognize the failure mode from the touchline in five minutes rather than at half time.

### Failure mode one: the corridor keeps opening

The signal is that the forward is receiving in stride in wide-central areas two or three times in the opening twenty minutes, without anybody having made an obvious individual error. That means the full-back's starting angle is wrong, or the centre-back is refusing to travel.

The first adjustment is not a substitution. It is to drop the ball-side central midfielder into the back line permanently while the ball is wide, converting to a temporary back five in the defensive phase. This costs you a midfielder in the counter and it closes the corridor immediately, because the gap the forward attacks is now occupied rather than covered.

If that does not hold, change shape properly to a back three with wing-backs. The wide centre-back starts in the corridor, which removes the geometry problem at its root, and the wing-back can press the wide player without consequence. The cost is that you have given up territory and one attacking body, and against a side that is comfortable with the ball you may not see it again for twenty minutes.

### Failure mode two: you are losing the first contact in the air

The signal is knockdowns falling to opposition players in the box rather than headers being conceded. If your designated defender is arriving late, the problem is almost always that he is watching the runner rather than the striker of the ball.

The adjustment is to move the designation. If the near centre-back is losing the flight, give the first-contact job to the full-back on crosses from deep, and let the centre-back take the knockdown zone instead, where his reading is an asset and his late start is not fatal. Swapping the two roles is a smaller and faster change than swapping the two players.

### Failure mode three: the switch is hurting you

You accepted the switch of play, and the opponent's far winger is now the one beating you. That is the plan working as designed and the concession costing more than it should, which usually means your far-side full-back is tucking in too far or too early.

The adjustment is to narrow the acceptance: the far full-back tucks in only when the ball is in the wide third, and stays honest when the ball is central. You have re-opened the corridor slightly in exchange for reaching the switch in time. This is a genuine trade with no free side, and the decision depends on which opponent is hurting you more. If the winger is a serious threat one-on-one, the trade is worth it. If he is not, hold the compression and let him cross from a distance where your first-contact defender is comfortable.

### Failure mode four: you are conceding free kicks in dangerous areas

The signal is three or four fouls inside thirty metres of goal in a half. That is a discipline problem produced by structure, not by attitude: defenders foul when they are late, and they are late when the compression is a step behind.

The adjustment is to accept a slightly deeper line for a ten-minute period to restore the timing, then push back up. That is the one circumstance in which dropping the block is correct against this profile, and it is a temporary repair, not a change of plan.

## Broken States: Chasing, Leading, and Playing With Ten

A counter-plan that only works at nil-nil with eleven players is a training exercise. Matches spend most of their time in states the plan was not designed for, and against this profile the broken states are where the goals arrive.

### Running the plan when you need a goal

The moment your side has to chase the match, lateral compression becomes unaffordable, because compression requires both full-backs to be defensive and requires the holding midfielder to stay home, and you have just asked all three to contribute to the attack. Pretending otherwise leads to the worst of both: an attack without numbers and a block without integrity.

The honest adjustment is to change what you are protecting rather than to protect everything badly. Give up the compression and keep two things: the two centre-backs level and within ten metres of each other, and the delay assignment on turnovers. Everything else can be spent on the attack.

That means you will concede the corridor when the ball is turned over, and you have to accept it, which is why the substitution planning matters. If you are chasing from the seventieth minute, the correct substitution is often a second holding midfielder rather than a second forward, because the extra body in front of the back line is what makes an attacking full-back affordable. Sides that empty the midfield to chase a goal against a counter-attacking forward frequently lose by two, and the second goal is almost always the same goal: turnover, diagonal, corridor, finish.

The other lever is the restart. When you are behind and pushing, more of your possession ends in wide areas and dead balls, and each of your own corners is a counter-attacking opportunity for the opponent. Keeping two players back at every attacking set piece costs you almost nothing in attacking terms and removes the most efficient counter-attack in football.

### Defending a lead

The instinct is to drop, and this article has already argued that dropping is the wrong axis. When protecting a lead, keep the line where it is and change three things instead.

Change the personnel on the ball side, because the plan degrades through the full-back's fatigue before it degrades anywhere else. Change the pressing height of your forwards, asking them to hold their positions rather than to press, so that the block does not get stretched by isolated pressing. And change the restart behaviour, using every legitimate opportunity to slow the game, because the plan's cost is running and the recovery time between phases is worth more than territory at that stage of a match.

The one thing that must not change is the first-contact designation. It is common for a side protecting a lead to flood the box with defenders, which sounds safe and is not, because a crowded box with eight players and no designated ball-attacker is a box where the forward will meet the delivery unopposed.

### Defending with ten players

If you lose a player, the temptation is to go to a back five and defend the edge of the box. Against this profile that is close to surrender, because it maximizes crossing volume against a reduced number of aerial defenders.

The better answer is to keep four defenders, drop one forward into midfield, and keep the compression while narrowing the pitch you are trying to defend. You give up the far touchline entirely and you defend a corridor of about forty metres in width, sliding as a unit, and you accept that the opponent's far-side player is free all match. That is a real cost and it is smaller than the cost of conceding a stream of crosses into a static box.

The exception is the last ten minutes with a lead and a player down, where the calculation genuinely changes, because the number of phases remaining is small enough that survival mathematics beat structural mathematics. Even then, hold the first-contact designation.

## The Return Fixture: Planning for the Adaptation

Any plan that works once will be studied, and the second time you face the same opponent the supply patterns will have changed. The two adaptations to expect are specific enough to prepare for in advance.

The first adaptation is that the forward's starting positions move wider. If his central starting position is being closed, the natural response is to begin from a wider origin and attack the gap from outside in rather than inside out. That is harder for your full-back, because the forward is now starting on the wrong side of him, and the answer is that the full-back stops treating the winger as his primary reference entirely and passes him off to the midfielder in front, becoming a second centre-back in the defensive phase.

The second adaptation is the deliberate second runner. Having found the corridor closed, good coaching staffs will send a midfielder or winger into it first, specifically to occupy the defender who was supposed to cover it, with the forward arriving second into the space that opens behind. This is the hardest version of the problem, and the answer is the one your midfield will not like: the covering midfielder tracks the first run all the way, even when it looks harmless, because the run is not the threat and it is not meant to be.

Preparing both answers before the first fixture, and having a session in the bank for each, is what separates a plan from a performance. The plan is the thing that survives the opponent's response.


## What You Concede Deliberately

A counter-plan that claims to close everything is a plan nobody can execute. This one concedes four specific things, and naming them at the start of the week means nobody panics when they happen.

You concede the switch of play. The opponent will move the ball across your block freely, and your players will spend the match running sideways. This is the fuel bill for the plan.

You concede the cross from distance. When the wide player is not pressed to the byline, he will deliver from twenty-five metres, and some of those deliveries will find their target. You accept them because a cross struck from further out has a longer flight time, which is exactly what your first-contact defender needs.

You concede possession in the middle third. You are not pressing high, so the opponent will have the ball, and the possession statistic will look grim. That number has no bearing on the plan's success and coaching staff should say so out loud before the match, because players who feel they are being overrun start pressing individually, and one player pressing individually against this structure opens the corridor for everybody.

You concede the shot from outside the box. The withdrawn midfielder is covering the cut-back rather than closing the shooter, and that is the correct priority. A twenty-metre shot with bodies in front of it is a far better outcome than a six-yard header, and defenders should be told to live with it rather than lunging out.

What you do not concede, ever, is the ball played into the corridor with the forward already moving, and the delivery met by a defender jumping from a standing start. Those two events are the plan's only real failure conditions, and every instruction above exists to eliminate them.

## Scaling Down: The Plan for a Side Without Elite Organization

Everything above assumes a squad that can rehearse, a back line that has played together, and midfielders who will run sideways for ninety minutes without complaint. Most coaches reading this do not have that. So here is the version that survives at grassroots, school, and amateur level, where you may get two sessions a week and half your defenders arrive ten minutes before kick-off.

### The two rules that survive without rehearsed compactness

Reduce the whole plan to two instructions, and drill only those.

The first is for the full-backs: never defend with your shoulders square to the touchline when the opposition forward is on your inside. Stand at an angle, see both, and if you have to choose, protect the inside. Concede the cross. This one instruction closes most of the corridor at amateur level, because amateur wide players rarely have the technique to punish a defender who is deliberately showing them outside.

The second is for the centre-backs: when the ball is in the air, move toward it. Do not wait, do not jump from a standstill, do not watch the forward. Attack the flight. At amateur level this instruction alone flips a large share of aerial duels, because the standard behaviour is to stand and wait, and the standard behaviour loses.

Two rules is what a side with limited training time can actually retain. Adding a third dilutes the first two.

### The formation shortcut

If you cannot drill sliding compactness, buy it with personnel: play a back five in the defensive phase. It is a blunter, more expensive answer, and it works, because it puts a body in the corridor instead of asking two players to coordinate a decision under fatigue. A team that cannot rehearse coordination should not build a plan that depends on coordination.

The trade is that you will have fewer players in your own attack and your midfield may be outnumbered, and both of those are survivable in a way that a repeatedly opened corridor is not.

### The youth-football version

For a coach working with young players, the framing has to change, because the objective is development rather than a result. Teach the starting angle as a habit rather than as an anti-Osimhen measure, since the half-open body position that closes the inside gap is a foundational full-back skill that pays off against every forward. Teach attacking the flight of a ball as a general aerial principle. Both are transferable, both are teachable at a young age, and neither requires the tactical maturity that lateral compression demands.

What you should not do with a young side is install a low block to survive a physically superior forward. It works on the day and teaches nothing, and the players learn that the answer to a good opponent is to retreat, which is the opposite of the lesson. When you want the session structure that installs these habits progressively, that work belongs to the training article rather than to this one, and it is worth building the week around the behaviour rather than around the opponent.

## The Goalkeeper's Job

The goalkeeper in this plan has three responsibilities and one prohibition, and getting them explicit is worth more than any amount of shot-stopping preparation.

The first responsibility is starting position. Against a forward who accelerates diagonally into the channel, the goalkeeper's default position is higher than usual but shaded to the ball side, because the diagonal ball arrives closer to the near post than the vertical ball does. Standing on the middle of his line against this profile leaves him equidistant from two situations and quick to neither.

The second responsibility is the six-yard-box claim. Everything inside that area is his, taken decisively and with a punch or a catch depending on the traffic, and the defenders must know it well enough that they clear his path rather than competing with him.

The third responsibility is communication on the drop. The goalkeeper is the only player who can see the whole picture, and the two instructions worth shouting are the line height on the diagonal cue and the identity of the first-contact defender when a delivery is coming from an unexpected angle. Those two calls are worth more than any distribution work.

The prohibition is on the half-commitment. A goalkeeper who advances three metres and stops has removed himself from his line and reached nothing. Against a forward who arrives at speed and gets his head on almost anything in the air, the half-commitment is a goal. Go, or hold. The middle is where the goals come from.

## What the Popular Debate Gets Wrong

Five positions come up in every discussion about defending this profile, and each is worth answering with mechanism rather than assertion, because a coach who cannot answer them will find his own players raising them at half time.

The first is that you simply need a big centre-back. Height helps in a static duel and is close to irrelevant in a duel decided by momentum. A tall defender who jumps from a standing start loses to a shorter one arriving on the run, which is why the first-contact rule is a starting-position rule rather than a selection rule.

The second is that pace beats power, and that a quick centre-back neutralizes the threat. Pace answers the run and does nothing about the delivery, and, worse, a defender who trusts his pace defends deeper, which makes the aerial half of the problem harder. The two halves have to be answered together, which is the entire argument of this blueprint.

The third is that a deep block handles a physical striker. A deep block reduces the space to run into and multiplies the crosses into the box, and against a forward whose box presence is a genuine weapon that is a trade that loses. Depth is the wrong axis. Width is the right one.

The fourth is that a direct forward can be waited out, that if you frustrate him for an hour the threat fades. Planning around an opponent's mood rather than his mechanism is not a plan, and it fails against any professional. Structure that depends on the opponent losing interest is structure that concedes in the eightieth minute.

The fifth is that the first touch is the obvious flaw to target. It is worth treating carefully, because it contains a partial truth wrapped around a tactical error, and it gets its own section below.

### Why is targeting his first touch only a secondary plan?

Ball control at speed is not the strongest part of this profile, and pressing the touch from behind when he receives facing his own goal is a genuine opportunity. It stays secondary, because a forward who is never forced to receive to feet simply will not receive to feet.

The reason it is a poor primary plan is that you do not get to choose which technical action he performs. A forward whose value comes from running and from attacking deliveries can play an entire match without taking a difficult touch in a tight area, and a plan built around a weakness that only appears when the opponent chooses to expose it is a plan waiting on charity.

The right use of the observation is as a secondary preference. When you have the option of forcing a reception to feet rather than a run in behind, take it. Show him the ball into feet, press from behind and from the ball side so that his first touch has to be taken under contact and facing his own goal, and let the holding midfielder collect the loose ball. That is a genuine and repeatable win. It is a way of choosing which duel you fight, and choosing the duel is what the whole blueprint is about. It is not a substitute for closing the corridor.

## What the Analyst Should Track

If you are preparing this plan from video rather than from memory, the viewing order matters, and the whole job takes less time than most analysts spend on it.

Start by counting run types across two matches, and count only two categories: runs into the space between full-back and centre-back, and runs directly behind the centre-back. The ratio between those two numbers tells you whether you are facing a channel forward or a depth runner, and it changes the plan more than anything else you will observe. If the ratio leans heavily toward the channel, the compression plan above is correct. If it leans toward depth, the answer is closer to the one developed in the [counter blueprint for a depth-running nine](/2020/01/15/how-to-stop-erling-haaland/), where the defeat conditions sit with line height and the timing of the drop rather than with lateral spacing.

Second, track the starting position before each run rather than the run itself. Where is he standing when the delivering player looks up? On the centre-back's outside shoulder, or central, or drifting wide? That starting position is the thing your full-back has to be told about, and it is the observation that never appears in a highlights compilation.

Third, on every aerial duel, note who was moving forward at contact. Not who won it. Who was moving. Two matches of that produces a clearer picture of the first-contact dynamic than any aerial-duel percentage, and it converts directly into an instruction.

Fourth, watch the goals conceded by teams who defended him well, not just the goals he scored. The instructive footage is the match where nothing happened, because that is where the successful mechanism is visible. Nobody uploads those matches, which is exactly why the analyst who watches them has an advantage.

## Judging Whether the Plan Worked

The result will not tell you, and neither will the number of chances he had. A plan of this type can be executed perfectly and still lose to one deflected finish, and it can be executed poorly and survive because a delivery was overhit twice. Coaching staffs who judge the plan by the scoreline learn nothing and change the wrong things on Monday.

Three observable markers give a truer reading. The first is the count of receptions in stride in wide-central areas. If that number is close to zero, the compression held, regardless of what happened elsewhere. If it is three or more, the plan failed even in a match you won, and the full-back's starting angle is the first place to look.

The second is the direction of movement on aerial contests. Go back through every contested delivery and ask only who was travelling forward at the moment of contact. A side that wins that count and still concedes a header has been unlucky. A side that loses it has a coaching problem that will recur next week against a different opponent.

The third is where the fouls happened. Fouls in the middle third are evidence of a plan working, because they are the price of contact in the zone where contact is cheap. Fouls inside your own penalty area, or repeated fouls within twenty-five metres of goal, mean the block was consistently a step late and the physical instruction was being applied in the wrong zone.

Reviewing those three markers takes about twenty minutes with the match footage, and it is worth more to the next preparation cycle than any amount of discussion about effort or concentration. The plan is a set of mechanisms, and mechanisms either operated or did not.

## Why This Profile Is Harder to Plan For Than a Pure Depth Runner

It is worth stating directly, because coaches who have prepared against elite forwards of other types will arrive expecting the same difficulty and find a different one.

A pure depth runner sets you a single question with a single variable: how high do you dare hold your line? Answer it well and the rest follows, because line height, the timing of the drop, and the goalkeeper's starting position are all expressions of one decision. You can get that decision wrong and still be coherent, and a coherent side that loses one duel a match will usually survive. The mechanics of that problem, and the specific line-height calculations behind it, belong to the [counter blueprint against a depth-running nine](/2020/01/15/how-to-stop-erling-haaland/), which owns that question.

The forward this article addresses sets you two questions whose obvious answers contradict each other, which is a genuinely different class of problem. You cannot solve it by choosing well on one axis. You have to find the third answer, the one that satisfies both constraints at once, and that answer costs more physically than either of the simple ones. A side can be brave, well drilled, and defensively excellent in every conventional sense and still lose repeatedly, because conventional excellence tends to mean vertical discipline and this problem is horizontal.

There is a personnel consequence too. Against a depth runner you need one quality in your back line: recovery pace, or a system that removes the need for it. Against this profile you need two qualities that rarely sit in the same defender, which is why the plan distributes them across roles rather than demanding them from one man. The travelling centre-back supplies mobility. The designated first-contact player supplies aggression toward the ball. The full-back supplies the angle. No single defender has to be complete, and that distribution is the reason the plan is available to ordinary squads at all.

The broader question of which defensive quality matters most, and whether recruitment should chase pace or reading, is taken up properly in the [comparison of pace and positioning as defensive assets](/2024/02/15/pace-or-positioning-beating-defenders/), which reaches a verdict this article deliberately leaves alone.

## The Recruitment Note: Building a Squad That Handles This Problem

If you are a coach or sporting director who faces this forward type more than once a season, the plan has a personnel implication worth acting on in the transfer market rather than on the training pitch.

The first recruitment priority is not a centre-back at all. It is a full-back who defends the inside gap by instinct. This is a scarce and undervalued profile, because full-backs are scouted on attacking output and on one-on-one defending against wingers, and the quality that matters here shows up in neither metric. The way to identify it is to watch a full-back in the phase where the ball is on the far side of the pitch and see where he stands. Does he tuck in level with his centre-backs, or does he hold width with his winger? The one who tucks is the one you want, and he is usually available cheaply because his heat map looks unadventurous.

The second priority is a centre-back who attacks the flight of the ball rather than the opponent. Aerial duel percentages will not find him, because those numbers count contested headers and reward the defender who competes for balls he should have attacked earlier. The observable marker is the direction he is moving at the moment of contact, and two matches of deliberate viewing will tell you more than a season of aggregate numbers.

The third priority is a holding midfielder who is comfortable defending in a straight line behind the ball rather than pressing. Most modern midfield recruitment favours ball-winners who step forward, and against this profile the more valuable trait is the discipline to hold a position on the penalty spot while everything happens around him. Coaches often describe that player as limited. He is the reason the cut-back does not become a goal.

The pattern across all three is the same and it is the reason this section exists: the qualities that beat this forward are the ones conventional scouting undervalues, which means they are affordable. A club with a modest budget can build a defensive unit specifically resistant to the most expensive attacking profile in football, and that arbitrage is available precisely because the market pays for jumping and sprinting rather than for standing in the right place two seconds earlier.

## Closing Verdict

The forward this article is about presents a problem that looks like two problems and is one. The channel run and the aerial threat are both produced by horizontal gaps in a defensive line that reacts to the ball late, and both are eliminated by lateral compression combined with a single named defender whose job is to arrive at the ball already moving. That is the plan, it is cheap to state and expensive to execute, and the expense is measured in running rather than in courage.

The alternatives fail predictably. The deep block loses to the delivery. The high line loses to the run. The physical approach loses to the referee inside the box and to fatigue outside it. The big centre-back loses to momentum. The fast centre-back loses to his own instinct to buy insurance. Each of these is a real idea that answers half the question, and half an answer against this profile is a goal.

The honest limitation of the blueprint is that it is fitness-dependent and rehearsal-dependent, which means the plan a professional side can run is not the plan a Sunday side can run. That is why the scaled version exists, and why it is two rules rather than nine. Angle the full-back so the inside gap is closed. Move toward the ball in the air. A side that does only those two things will concede fewer goals to this profile than a side that does everything else and neither of those.

And the transferable idea, the one worth carrying beyond this single opponent, is the first-contact rule. Aerial defending is taught as a physical contest and it is mostly a positional one. The defender who decides two seconds early wins the duel that looks, on the replay, like it was won by a jump.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you stop Victor Osimhen?

Compress the defensive line laterally rather than dropping it deep, so the gap between full-back and centre-back never exceeds eight to ten metres, and designate one defender whose sole job on every long or lofted delivery is to arrive at the flight of the ball already moving forward. Accept the switch of play across your block as the price. The two obvious alternatives fail in opposite directions: a deep block feeds his box presence by allowing unlimited crossing time, while a high line hands him the running start he wants into the channel. Lateral compression removes the supply for both threats at once, because both depend on the same horizontal gap.

### Q: What is Osimhen's biggest weakness?

Relative to elite peers, his output depends heavily on the structure and service around him. He is at his most dangerous when the ball is delivered early into the space he attacks or lofted into the box for him to attack on the run, and considerably less so when he is forced to receive to feet in a congested area facing his own goal. Ball control at speed in tight spaces is not the strongest part of the profile. A defensive plan that consistently denies the early diagonal and forces receptions with his back to goal reduces his threat more than any individual duel ever will.

### Q: How do you defend Osimhen's runs into the channels?

The run is defeated before it starts, by the full-back's starting angle. He must defend with his hips half-open rather than square to the touchline, keeping both the ball and the forward's position in view, and he must protect the inside gap as his first priority even at the cost of conceding a cross. Simultaneously the ball-side centre-back has to be willing to travel wide toward the outside of the penalty area, which is uncomfortable but safe, because the far centre-back and the tucked-in far full-back have already re-formed the central pair behind him.

### Q: Can you win an aerial duel against Osimhen?

Yes, and the way to do it has almost nothing to do with height. The duel is decided by which player is moving toward the ball at the moment of contact, not by who jumps highest from a standstill. A defender who starts one metre in front of the forward on the ball side, watches the striker of the ball rather than the runner, and commits toward the flight the instant the ball is struck will win a large share of these contests regardless of a height disadvantage. A taller defender who waits, watches the man, and jumps from a static position will lose most of them.

### Q: Does a high line work against Osimhen?

No, and the reason is more specific than the usual warning about pace. A high line depends on the goalkeeper covering the space behind, and a sweeper-keeper covers a vertical ball over the top far better than a diagonal ball into the channel, because the diagonal travels laterally away from him and forces him to commit to a line he cannot correct mid-run. Against a forward who attacks the space between full-back and centre-back rather than the space directly behind the centre-back, a high line converts his best run into his easiest one.

### Q: How do you stop Osimhen on the break?

Deny the first pass rather than chasing the runner, because once the ball is travelling into the corridor with him already moving, the phase is lost. That requires rest defense installed before the turnover: two central defenders level and within ten metres of each other at all times, the holding midfielder positioned on the inside of the counter lane rather than behind the ball, and one named player whose job on any turnover is to run a recovery line that forces the pass wide instead of forward. Buying two seconds is worth more than attempting an interception.

### Q: Should defenders be physical with Osimhen?

Contact is useful in a narrow zone and dangerous everywhere else. In the channel and in midfield, a settled, legal body position that forces him to check or stops the run is a good outcome, because a free kick thirty metres out costs nothing and eliminates the phase. Inside your own penalty area the identical action is a penalty, and since he attacks the box directly, a high share of total contact happens exactly there. So the instruction is zonal rather than temperamental: physical outside the box, technically clean inside it, with the objective being first contact on the ball and never on the man.

### Q: How do you defend crosses when Osimhen attacks the box?

Assign roles by zone rather than by man. One named defender attacks the near-post zone moving forward and takes the first contact, a second centre-back drops eight to ten metres behind him to own the knockdown, the far full-back covers the back post, and the withdrawn central midfielder holds the penalty-spot line to cover the cut-back rather than entering the box. The goalkeeper claims everything inside the six-yard box decisively. The error that concedes goals is two defenders both tracking the forward while nobody reads the flight of the delivery.

### Q: Is Osimhen's first touch a vulnerability?

It is a real observation and a poor primary plan. Ball control at speed in congested areas is not the strongest element of the profile, and pressing the touch from behind when he receives facing his own goal is a genuine opportunity. The problem is that a forward built around running and attacking deliveries can play a full match without ever taking a difficult touch in a tight area. Use it as a preference rather than a strategy: when you can force a reception to feet rather than a run in behind, take that option and press it hard from the ball side.

### Q: What midfield protection do you need against Osimhen?

Two specific jobs, neither of which is screening shots. The ball-side central midfielder steps toward the passer at an angle that closes his outside foot, so the diagonal into the channel cannot be struck cleanly. The far-side central midfielder slides across to occupy the inside lane vacated when the far full-back tucks in, which is the job most often forgotten and the absence that turns a compressed block into a block with a hole through the middle. On crosses, one midfielder holds the penalty-spot line for the cut-back rather than dropping into the crowded box.

### Q: How does a goalkeeper deal with Osimhen's speed to the ball?

By starting higher than usual but shaded to the ball side, since the diagonal delivery arrives nearer the near post than a vertical ball does, and by treating the six-yard box as an absolute claim zone taken decisively. The prohibition matters more than the permissions: never half-commit. A goalkeeper who advances three metres and stops has left his line and reached nothing, and against a forward who gets contact on almost anything in the air, that hesitation is a goal. Go fully or hold the line, and communicate the first-contact designation loudly on unusual delivery angles.

### Q: Which defender profile copes with Osimhen best?

The front-foot reader: a centre-back who is quick over the first ten metres, comfortable turning and running, willing to travel wide of the penalty area, and instinctively inclined to attack a ball dropping over his shoulder rather than retreat under it. The two profiles that fail are the large positional stopper, who is excellent in his own zone and refuses the corridor, and the pure recovery athlete, who buys himself an insurance metre of depth and thereby hands over the aerial half of the problem. Aggression of starting position beats both size and raw speed here.

### Q: Does a deep block reduce Osimhen's threat?

It reduces one threat and amplifies the other, which is why it fails overall. Sitting deep does shorten the running space behind, but it concedes the entire middle third, letting opposition wide players and full-backs receive facing your goal, unpressured, and choose the exact moment and height of every delivery. Against a forward whose box presence is a real weapon, giving the service unlimited time is a losing trade. The correct axis of adjustment is width rather than depth: compress laterally, hold a medium line, and force deliveries from further out.

### Q: How should an amateur side defend a forward with pace and power like Osimhen?

Reduce the plan to two instructions and drill only those. First, full-backs never defend with shoulders square to the touchline when the forward is on their inside; stand half-open, protect the inside gap, and concede the cross. Second, centre-backs move toward the ball whenever it is in the air rather than waiting and jumping from a standstill. If coordination cannot be rehearsed, buy the same protection with personnel by defending in a back five, which puts a body in the gap instead of asking two players to coordinate a decision while fatigued.

### Q: What do analysts track when scouting Osimhen?

Count run types across two matches, splitting them into runs between full-back and centre-back versus runs directly behind the centre-back, because that ratio determines whether the compression plan or a line-height plan applies. Then record his starting position at the moment each delivering player looks up, since that is the detail your full-back needs and the detail highlight compilations never show. On aerial duels, log who was moving forward at contact rather than who won it. Finally, study matches where he was contained, because successful mechanisms are only visible when nothing happens.

### Q: How does Osimhen react to very tight marking?

Tactically, a forward with this profile responds to close man-orientation by widening his starting positions and dropping toward the ball more often, both of which are attempts to pull the marking defender out of the line and create the gap for a teammate to attack. That reaction is the reason a purely man-oriented scheme tends to backfire: it converts one problem into a different one, and the vacated space is often more damaging than the original run. The better answer is zonal ownership of the corridor with a first-contact designation, which does not require anyone to follow him anywhere.
