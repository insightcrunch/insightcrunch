The stage directions of Romeo and Juliet are the least read and most revealing words in the early texts. Readers skim them on the way to the speeches; editors print many of them in brackets, as if apologising for their presence; and yet the few hundred short prose instructions scattered through the first quarto of 1597, the second quarto of 1599, and the First Folio of 1623 are the nearest thing the play possesses to a record of its own first life in the theatre. They say who entered and with what in hand, who stood above and who below, where a body fell and behind what curtain it disappeared. No other stratum of the text speaks so directly about performance, and none has been asked to carry so much argumentative weight. Bibliographers have treated the directions as fingerprints of lost manuscripts. Performance historians have treated them as photographs of lost stagings. Both parties claim more than the documents hold, and the task of a census is to say, direction by direction and stratum by stratum, what this evidence can certify and what it cannot.

The verdict argued through this article can be stated at the outset. The stage directions of the early texts certify staging practice: the moves, groupings, properties, and vertical arrangements that the early modern stage used and understood when it played this tragedy. They do not certify genealogy: no direction, and no pattern of directions, proves how the copy behind a printed text was assembled, whether by an author writing, an actor remembering, or a bookkeeper annotating. The first quarto's directions are fuller, more descriptive, and more theatrical than the second quarto's because the two books were made from different kinds of paper prepared for different purposes, and the Folio's directions are tidier than either because a further generation of print shop and playhouse habit had passed over them. Read under that discipline, the directions teach a great deal: how Juliet was placed at a window and never on a balcony, how Mercutio received his wound in a piece of choreography precise enough to reconstruct, how an actor's own name could stand where a character's name should be. Read without that discipline, they teach mirages, and the history of the play's editing is littered with both.

## What Do the Stage Directions of Romeo and Juliet Actually Record?

A stage direction is an instruction or a notation, and the early texts of this play contain both kinds without distinguishing them. Some directions command: a door is to open, a torch is to be carried, a fight is to begin. Others describe: they narrate an action in the manner of a chronicle, as though reporting something already witnessed rather than prescribing something still to come. The distinction matters because the two kinds carry different evidentiary force. A command belongs to the paperwork of production, the stratum of the text that someone, author or bookkeeper or scribe, prepared so that a performance could happen. A description may belong to that same paperwork, or it may be the trace of a performance remembered, or the invention of a writer imagining the scene for a reader. When the first quarto writes its fullest directions in a narrative present tense, narrating business as it unfolds, the prose itself raises the question that the whole census must keep open: whether the page is prescribing the staging or remembering it.

Alan C. Dessen, whose work on the vocabulary of early modern staging remains the standard map of this territory, insisted that stage directions constitute a theatrical language with its own conventions, signals, and shorthand, a language shared between the professionals who wrote the directions and the professionals who executed them. A direction was not a novel in miniature. It was a signal in a code, addressed to practitioners who needed very few words because the conventions supplied the rest. That insight disciplines the census in both directions. Against the sceptic, it means that a sparse direction is not an empty one: two or three words could specify a complex and standard piece of staging to the company that received them. Against the enthusiast, it means that a full direction is not a transparent window onto a unique historical performance: the fullness may be conventional fullness, the standard expansion of a standard signal, rather than a report of one afternoon at the Theatre or the Curtain.

### What kinds of stage directions do the early texts contain?

The early texts contain entrances and exits, directions for grouping and placement, calls for properties, cues for music, dance, and fighting, directions for the upper and inner spaces, and a small residue of narrative descriptions of action already under way. Each kind answers to a different stage convention and carries its own standard of proof.

Within those kinds, the census can sort the evidence by function. Entrance directions are the commonest stratum in all three early texts, and the most formulaic: a name, sometimes a companion or two, sometimes a property. Exit directions are markedly less common, and their scarcity is itself evidence, since a text that marks entrances carefully and exits carelessly was prepared by someone who trusted the actors and the bookkeeper to clear the stage by convention. Property directions cluster at moments of high practical consequence: torches for the night scenes and the tomb, weapons for the fights, a rope ladder that the dialogue promises and the staging must somehow honour, the vial, the dagger, the lantern and spade and crow of iron that equip the final scene. Music and dance directions concentrate in the feast of the first act and the musicians' scene of the fourth. Directions for the upper level concentrate, with a symmetry the census will return to, in the two scenes that made the play's visual reputation: the orchard scene of the second act and the parting at dawn in the third.

The second quarto, the text from which nearly all modern editions descend, is the sparest of the three witnesses in this stratum, and its sparseness has a particular flavour. Its directions frequently read as though written for insiders who needed only to be reminded, not instructed: the famous entrance for the feast brings on Romeo, Mercutio, and Benvolio "with five or six Maskers, Torch-bearers, and others," a formulation that cannot decide its own number and does not trouble to. A direction that offers a company five or six of anything is not a prescription; it is a permission, and W. W. Greg gave such formulations their enduring name when he called them permissive stage directions, instructions that license a range of stagings rather than commanding one. The permissive direction is the fingerprint of a draft written before production decisions had hardened, or of papers in which the author left the company its customary freedom. Either way, its presence in the second quarto and its relative absence from the first is one of the cleanest contrasts the census affords.

The first quarto's directions, by contrast, repeatedly cross the line from prescription into narration. Where the second quarto is content to start a fight and let the dialogue carry it, the first quarto at the crisis of the duel narrates the wounding itself, specifying the very mechanics of the blow. Where the second quarto ends Juliet's potion scene with the briefest of notations, the first quarto follows her onto the bed and behind the curtains. Where the second quarto names a character in an entrance, the first quarto at one celebrated point names the actor instead, printing "Will Kempe" where the fiction requires Peter the servingman. Each of these cases receives its own examination below. Together they establish the character of the stratum: the first quarto's directions behave like the record of a staging, whether or not any particular theory of the book's origin can be built on that behaviour.

The Folio's directions form the third stratum, and their character is regularity. The Folio text of the play descends from the later quartos rather than from either of the first two directly, and its directions show a text being tidied in transmission: entrances completed, exits supplied where the quarto copy had left the stage to clear itself, groupings spelled out, and the whole articulated by the act and scene headings that the quartos lack. Some Folio directions are fuller than their quarto counterparts, and that fullness has occasionally been mistaken for independent theatrical authority. The census position taken here is that Folio fullness is editorial and compositorial fullness, the natural product of a prestigious collected edition preparing a play for readers as much as for players, and that it must be weighed as such. A Folio direction can preserve a genuine staging detail transmitted through its copy, but it cannot be quoted as though the Folio were a fourth eyewitness standing beside the two quartos.

One further preliminary concerns the directions that are not there. The dialogue of the play is dense with implicit directions, actions encoded in speech: offers of hands, drawings of swords, kneelings, embracings, the whole choreography that a line can prescribe without any marginal instruction at all. Those implicit cues belong to the speeches, and the census counts only the explicit stratum, the directions printed as directions. But the boundary matters for the verdict. A scene poor in explicit directions may be rich in encoded action, and the second quarto's sparseness is partly an illusion created by a text that trusted its dialogue to do the directing. The early texts direct the play twice over, once in the prose stratum and once inside the verse, and the census of the prose stratum is a census of the paperwork, not of the performance entire.

## The First Quarto's Descriptive Directions: Business Written Down

The single most quoted stage direction in the play's early texts occupies nine words of prose at the hinge of the tragedy. In the second quarto, the duel of the third act is set in motion and the dialogue is left to carry the catastrophe: Mercutio's "I am hurt" (3.1) announces the wound after the fact, and Romeo's reproaches gesture at the mechanics without specifying them. The first quarto specifies. Its direction for the moment reads, in the spelling of the original, that Tybalt, under Mercutio's arm, stabs him, and flies with his followers. The sentence is a piece of choreography complete in itself. It places Tybalt's body in relation to Mercutio's, explains how a thrust could land on a man whose friend stood between the combatants, accounts for Romeo's guilt in the precise physical terms the following speeches require, and clears Tybalt from the stage in the same breath. Generations of fight directors have staged the moment exactly so, most of them without knowing that the arrangement descends from a prose clause printed in 1597.

The evidentiary character of that direction deserves slow attention, because the whole debate about the first quarto's directions can be conducted over this one example. The direction is descriptive rather than prescriptive in form: it narrates an action in a compact present tense, as a report narrates. It is also theatrically exact in a way that invention rarely is. A writer composing directions at a desk, imagining how the wound might plausibly have been given, could have written it; the sentence requires no eyewitness. But the sentence solves a staging problem that the dialogue creates and does not solve, the problem of how Mercutio, separated from Tybalt by Romeo's intervening body, nevertheless receives a mortal thrust. Whoever wrote the direction had thought about the bodies in the space, or had watched bodies in the space, and the two possibilities are not equally easy to separate. The census records the fact and its weight: this direction certifies that the staging it describes was a staging the early stage used or could use, coherent with the dialogue's own account of the wound. It does not, by itself, certify the process by which the sentence reached print.

### What does the direction for Mercutio's wounding actually record?

It records a piece of fight choreography: Tybalt striking under Mercutio's raised arm as Romeo stands between the two men, which explains the wound, Romeo's share of blame, and Tybalt's immediate flight. The direction matches the dialogue's account of the injury point for point, so it certifies a staging the early theatre practised or could practise.

The potion scene offers the second celebrated case. Juliet's soliloquy in the fourth act ends with the drinking of the vial, and the texts divide over what the audience then sees. The second quarto's notation is minimal. The first quarto's direction follows the action through: Juliet drinks, and falls upon the bed within the curtains. The addition of the curtains converts a piece of information into a piece of staging. A bed within the curtains is a bed in the discovery space, the recessed area at the back of the early modern stage that curtains could close off, and the direction therefore settles a practical question that the second quarto leaves floating: where the apparently dead Juliet lies during the interval before the lamentations begin, and how the stage manages the transition from her solitary resolution to the household's discovery of her body. The direction also carries a consequence for the shape of the scene. Because Juliet does not exit, the scene has no conventional close; the body remains present, the space remains hers, and the discovery in the following scene is a reopening of a space the audience has already seen closed. Editors and directors who care about that continuity take the direction seriously, and its presence in the first quarto alone is a standard exhibit whenever the harvesting of first quarto directions into second quarto texts is defended.

A third class of first quarto fullness concerns sound. The feast in the first act and the musicians in the fourth are moments where the play's staging depends on music arriving at the right instant and in the right spirit, and the first quarto's directions attend to the dances and the playing with a care the second quarto does not match. Such directions are the working marks of a text that has lived in a playhouse, where the difference between a dance that begins on its cue and one that begins late is the difference between a scene and an embarrassment. They are also, taken singly, weak evidence of anything beyond competence: any competent preparation of the text for performance, at any date, would tend to accumulate exactly such marks. The census therefore grades the first quarto's directions in descending order of probative force. The wounding choreography stands highest, because it solves a dialogue problem with unique precision. The bed within the curtains stands next, because it fixes the use of a specific stage resource. The music cues stand lowest, because they are the common small change of theatrical paperwork. A responsible argument builds from the top of that grading and declines to rest weight on the bottom.

Against this fullness, the silences of the first quarto must also be entered in the record. The short text is short everywhere, in its directions as in its speeches: whole passages of business that the second quarto's dialogue implies pass without notation, and scenes that a performance must somehow have staged are left as bare in the short text as in the long one. The first quarto is not a promptbook, and no one has ever handled it as one. Its descriptive directions are islands of narrative in a text that elsewhere trusts convention as completely as its rival does. That unevenness is itself a datum, and it cuts against every simple theory. A bookkeeper's annotated copy would be expected to mark business systematically, at the pressure points of production; the first quarto marks it episodically, at the moments of highest theatrical salience, which is the pattern of memory or of selective attention rather than of office routine. What that pattern proves is the question reserved for the adjudication below; what it describes is not in dispute.

The fullest account of the first quarto as a printed object, its printer, its copy, and the label that has clung to it since the early bibliographers, belongs to the companion study of [The First Quarto of 1597: The 'Bad' Quarto](/2026/03/27/first-quarto-1597-bad-quarto/), and the directions census takes that account as its foundation: the directions are one stratum of a short, strange, theatrically saturated book, and they must be read as part of its whole character rather than quarried as isolated proof.

## An Actor's Name in the Text: Will Kempe and the Fourth Act

In the fourth act of the first quarto, at the point where the musicians are to be summoned and dismissed in the household's grief, the text prints an entrance for Will Kempe. The character required by the fiction at that moment is Peter, the Capulet servingman who argues with the musicians in prose that the second quarto gives to an unnamed servant and a named Peter in its speech headings. The first quarto's direction reaches past the character to the performer: the company's clown, the most famous comic actor in England, is summoned by his own name. No other direction in the early texts of the play does anything quite like this, and the small anomaly has attracted a weight of commentary out of proportion to its nine letters.

The fact itself is uncontested, and its minimum meaning is plain. Whoever prepared the copy from which the first quarto was printed was thinking of the performer in the role, or copying a document in which the performer had been thought of. Casting lived in that document. The direction therefore certifies something the census values highly and the dialogue nowhere states: the identity of the actor who played Peter in the early performances, and with it a piece of the company's personnel history. Kemp's presence in the role also colours the scene retrospectively. The exchange with the musicians, a prose interlude of punning grief-management that many readings treat as expendable, was the clown's set piece, written or adapted to the measure of a star performer whose jigging entrances were famous enough to be marketed. A direction that names him explains why the scene exists at its length.

The maximum meaning claimed for the direction is another matter. In the classic account of the first quarto's origin, the naming of Kempe is a symptom of memorial transmission: a text assembled from the recollections of performers would naturally preserve, at the joints of the action, the names of the performers themselves, surfacing where the fiction's names had not yet been substituted or had slipped. That reading is possible, and the direction is consistent with it. But the direction is equally consistent with at least two other histories: a company document prepared for use, in which casting notes belonged by right, and an authorial or scribal paper in which the writer, composing for a known company, thought in actors as readily as in characters. Playwrights wrote for sharers they knew; a draft that summoned Kempe by name at his entrance would be a draft written inside the profession's ordinary habits. The census therefore records the Kempe direction as decisive evidence about casting and indecisive evidence about transmission, and it treats every argument that loads the second conclusion onto the first as an overdraft on the document.

The same discipline applies to the first quarto's other actorly traces, its habit of naming entrances by function and occasion rather than by the fiction's tidy labels, and its occasional merging of a performer with his part at the level of speech headings. These features make the short text feel close to the playhouse, and the feeling is genuine evidence of one thing: the copy behind the first quarto passed through hands for which the play was a performance before it was a literature. By 1597 the play could hardly have been anything else. The census insists only that closeness to the playhouse is a property the first quarto shares with every theatrical document of the decade, including the papers behind the second quarto, whose permissive directions and unresolved doublet betray a study origin of a different kind. The contrast is between kinds of paperwork, and the kinds are the subject of the next section.

## The Second Quarto's Sparse Directions and the Papers Behind Them

The second quarto of 1599, the "good" quarto whose text modern editions print, announces on its title page that it has been newly corrected, augmented, and amended, and in its speeches the claim is justified. In its directions, the book is a study in omission. Entrances are given, often with their properties carefully itemised; exits are repeatedly left to be inferred; business that the dialogue unmistakably requires passes unmarked; and the great set pieces of staging, the fight and the potion and the tomb, are set in motion with a brevity that trusts the performers entirely. The tomb scene may stand as the type. The second quarto equips its final movement with a quartermaster's precision about objects: Romeo and Balthasar enter "with a torch, a mattock, and a crow of iron," and the Friar follows "with a lantern, crow, and spade." The inventory is exact, and the action is absent. Who digs, who forces the monument, how the bodies are disposed as the scene accumulates its dead: of this the direction says nothing, and the dialogue must carry the staging on its back. A text that itemises the tools and omits the work was written for readers of the words, or for performers who needed no telling, and scholarship has generally concluded that it was written by the author in his papers, before the playhouse had annotated them into a working document.

The permissive direction belongs to this stratum and illuminates its character. When the second quarto brings on the maskers in a number it declines to fix, five or six, it writes as a dramatist writes who knows that the company will field as many dancers as the afternoon affords and that the fiction is indifferent to the count. Greg's term for such directions, permissive, captures their grammar: they permit a family of stagings rather than prescribing one. The permissive direction is precious evidence precisely because it is useless as instruction. No bookkeeper preparing a performance text leaves the size of a dance troupe open; no reporter remembering a performance reports an alternative he did not see. The openness is the mark of a stage of the text prior to production fixing, and its presence in the second quarto is one strand of the larger case, built also on the duplicated dawn lines and the variable speech headings, that the copy behind the second quarto was the author's own working draft.

### What is a permissive stage direction, and why does the second quarto rely on them?

A permissive stage direction licenses a range of stagings instead of commanding one: the maskers enter in a number left open at five or six. The second quarto relies on such directions because its copy preserves a draft stage, written before production fixed the numbers and groupings a playhouse text would have had to settle.

The sparseness of the second quarto is not uniform, and its distribution is informative. Where a property is unusual, the direction is full: the crow of iron is not standard equipment, and the text takes care over it. Where an action is conventional, the direction thins to nothing: fights begin, dances happen, letters change hands, and the page records the fact of the matter in the fewest possible words or leaves the dialogue to record it alone. This is the distribution of an author's papers as the editorial tradition has reconstructed them, a manuscript in which the writer noted what struck him as notable and trusted the theatre for the rest. It is also, the census must add, the distribution that a careful scribe copying such papers would preserve, and the distinction between the author's own notation and a transcript's fidelity to it is one the directions alone cannot draw. The systematic comparison of the two quartos as wholes, their agreements and divergences across every category of evidence, is conducted in the companion study [Q1 vs Q2: What the Differences Between the Quartos Reveal](/2026/04/10/q1-vs-q2-romeo-and-juliet-differences/); the directions are one witness within that larger collation, and not the loudest.

Two of the second quarto's directional habits deserve separate entries in the census, because each has been asked to bear more than it can hold. The first is its treatment of the heroine's appearances in the upper space, examined in the following sections: the second quarto is, at the crucial moment, more specific than its reputation for sparseness predicts, and its word is window. The second is its handling of asides and soliloquy, which is to say its silence. The early texts possess no settled convention for marking an aside, and the play's most famous speech, Juliet's "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo" (2.2), depends for its entire effect on an arrangement the directions never state: that the speaker believes herself unheard while the audience and her lover hear every word. The staging fact on which the scene turns is carried wholly by the dramatic situation and the performers' placement, and the texts' silence about it is the census's standing reminder that the explicit stratum is a fraction of the play's direction in any sense that matters. Whoever argues from these documents argues from a partial record, and the partiality must be priced into every conclusion.

## "Enter Juliet at a Window": The Balcony No Early Text Names

The most famous piece of architecture in English drama does not exist in the play's early texts. No direction in the first quarto, the second quarto, or the Folio mentions a balcony; the word balcony appears nowhere in the dialogue; and the woman for whom the structure was supposedly built is introduced by the second quarto in a direction of studied plainness: "Enter Juliet at a window." The window is what the texts offer. The balcony is what the centuries supplied, through stage tradition, illustration, and the gravitational pull of a romantic image that proved stronger than the documents. The census of the directions begins its most consequential entry here, because the case shows, in the clearest available form, both what a direction can certify and how completely a staging tradition can overwrite its own evidence.

What the window direction certifies is a vertical arrangement and an opening. Juliet appears above Romeo, in or at an opening in the structure that fronts the orchard, and the dialogue corroborates the arrangement at every point: Romeo looks up, speaks of the light that breaks through the window, imagines her at the casement, and the lovers' whole negotiation is conducted across a distance that the architecture enforces. "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2) fixes the opening as a source of light before it fixes it as a place, and the scene's erotics of separation, close enough to hear and too far to touch, depend on the height being real but modest, a first floor's remove rather than a tower's. The early modern stage possessed exactly the resource the direction requires: an upper playing area above the tiring house facade, reached from within and fronted by openings that could serve as windows. The direction, the dialogue, and the building agree. Nothing in the three witnesses requires, or is improved by, a projecting balcony with a balustrade, and everything in the witnesses is consistent with the simpler and better evidenced arrangement of an upper window.

The balcony's conquest of the scene is a chapter of reception history rather than of textual history. Restorations and eighteenth century stagings dressed the window with the architectural vocabulary of their own theatres; nineteenth century pictorial production, with its appetite for buildable scenery, gave the lovers a structure that could be climbed toward, leaned from, and engraved; and the illustrated editions and schoolroom posters of the following century fixed the structure in the imagination of readers who had never seen the play staged at all. By the time the direction's plain window needed defending, the defence sounded like pedantry against the evidence of everyone's memory. Editors of the scholarly tradition have mostly held the line of the documents, printing the window and noting the tradition, while the popular text of the scene continues to be staged, filmed, and merchandised on a balcony that no early witness knows. The dedicated staging study, [There Is No Balcony: Staging Act 2 Scene 2](/2027/12/31/no-balcony-staging-act-2-scene-2/), conducts the full inquiry into the scene's placement; the census enters the headline finding and its moral. The moral is that a direction's authority is documentary, not democratic: it certifies what the paperwork said, and the paperwork can be outvoted by four centuries of scenery without being refuted.

Richard Hosley's classic investigations of the early modern upper stage gave the window its scholarly architecture, arguing from the playhouse evidence that the upper level served precisely such appearances and that the texts ask for nothing more elaborate. Andrew Gurr's reconstruction of the Shakespearean stage reached consonant conclusions from the company's practice: the playing places were conventional, the signals economical, and the window at which Juliet stands belongs to the standard vocabulary of the house. Against that scholarly settlement, the persistence of the balcony demonstrates a law of theatrical transmission that the whole census illustrates: staging traditions, once pictorially established, acquire an authority independent of the texts they claim to serve, and editors who print the window find themselves annotating against an image rather than against an argument. The direction is three words long. The industry built upon its replacement is three centuries wide.

## Aloft, Above, Within: The Vertical and Hidden Geography of the Directions

The orchard scene does not exhaust the directions' use of the upper stage. At dawn in the fifth scene of the third act, the lovers part in the same vertical arrangement that joined them, and the early texts bring them on aloft for the aubade, the lark and nightingale debate, and the descent. Juliet's line as the parting completes itself, "Then, window, let day in, and let life out" (3.5), addresses the opening itself, and the staging fact beneath the poetry is that the scene has been played at height, in the chamber space the upper level conventionally represented, with Romeo's descent to the orchard floor enacted or mimed below her. The symmetry is a piece of pure stagecraft that the directions alone preserve in outline: the play's two great scenes of union are staged on the same vertical axis, the woman above and the man below ascending in hope and descending in exile, and the axis is broken only when the tomb replaces height with depth in the final movement. A reader who attends only to the speeches receives the poetry of that design; the directions supply its geometry.

### What does the word aloft certify in the parting scene?

The word aloft certifies placement and nothing more: the lovers begin the dawn scene in the chamber above the main stage, from which Romeo descends as the parting ends. The scene was staged vertically, Juliet remaining at the height from which she watches him go; the word names no structure, prescribes no balcony, and records no particular performance.

The horizontal geography deserves an entry beside the vertical. The early texts mark a small vocabulary of inwardness: bodies fall within curtains, figures are discovered, and the discovery space at the rear of the stage does the work that later theatres would assign to built interiors. Juliet's bed within the curtains is the play's fullest use of the resource, but the logic of the curtained space runs through the staging of the tomb as well, where the monument must open, receive its bodies in sequence, and disclose them to each new arrival in the scene's dreadful arithmetic. The directions for the tomb are, as the census has noted, inventories of tools rather than narratives of action, and the management of the monument's interior is left to the dialogue and to convention: Paris enters and is laid within, Romeo enters and dies within, Juliet wakes within and dies within, and the direction stratum records the entrances at the threshold while the verse performs the penetrations. The pattern is the same one the census found in the duel. Where the paperwork is fullest, it is full about objects and thresholds; the interiors belong to the poetry.

Between the upper space and the discovery space, the main platform carries the play's public world, and its directions are the most conventional of all: entrances in companies, trains, attendants, the civic choreography of Verona's streets. The census notes the contrast because it explains the stratum's uneven reputation. The directions seem thin where the staging was standard and thicken exactly where a scene's arrangement was unusual enough to need recording: a wound given under a raised arm, a girl asleep behind curtains, a heroine at a window, a clown summoned by his own name. On that reading, the fullness of a direction is an index of the unusual, and the argument from fullness to origin must pass through that index before it can begin. A text that records the unusual and omits the standard behaves like every theatrical document of the period, and the first quarto's directions, for all their narrative colour, conform to the period's documentary habits more closely than the rhetoric of the debate has usually allowed.

## The Folio's Additions: Regularity as an Editorial Act

The First Folio of 1623 printed the play from copy derived from the third and fourth quartos, and its directions show what a generation of transmission does to a stratum of theatrical paperwork. The Folio supplies the act headings the quartos lack, though imperfectly and without scene numbers; it completes entrances that the quarto copy had left ragged; it adds exits at points where the earlier texts had trusted the stage to clear itself; it spells out attendants and groupings; and it occasionally expands a direction into a fuller form than any quarto offers, the expansion smoothing the path of a reader who must stage the scene in imagination. The cumulative effect is of a text being prepared for the study as carefully as for the stage, and that doubleness is the Folio's character in every stratum, not in the directions alone.

Two cautions govern the census's use of this stratum. The first concerns descent: a Folio direction that agrees with the second quarto may do so by inheritance through the intermediate quartos, and agreement by inheritance is no corroboration at all, since a copy transmits its source's readings without adding a witness's authority. The second concerns the expansions. Where the Folio's direction is fuller than the second quarto's, the fullness may descend from annotation in its copy, the working marks of a playhouse or a careful reader somewhere in the chain, or it may be the Folio's own compositors and editors tidying as they set. The chain cannot now be reconstructed link by link, and the honest census therefore treats Folio expansions as readings of unknown pedigree: possibly precious, possibly compositorial, and in either case unable to outvote the quartos on a point of staging where the quartos speak clearly. Where the quartos are silent and the Folio speaks, an editor may gratefully print the Folio's direction, marked as the Folio's. Where all three witnesses agree, the census rests.

### What did the Folio add to the directions besides its act headings?

Besides the act headings, the Folio added completion and clarity: exits where the quartos had left the stage to clear itself, entrances spelled out with attendants and groupings, and expansions turning bare notations into a reader's guidance. The additions regularise the stratum for a collected edition, transmitting genuine detail and manufacturing tidiness that cannot now be separated from it.

## What a Stage Direction Can Prove, and What It Cannot

The classic theory of the first quarto was built substantially on its directions, and the theory must now be tried on them. Harry R. Hoppe's monograph of 1948, The Bad Quarto of Romeo and Juliet, gave the memorial reconstruction hypothesis its fullest application to this play: the short text, on his account, was assembled from the recollections of actors who had performed in it, and its features were the symptoms of that origin. The directions fitted the diagnosis invitingly. Descriptive directions read like the prose of men remembering business they had themselves performed; the narrative present tense sounded like dictation from the stage; the naming of Kempe looked like a performer's name surfacing where memory had kept the man rather than the part. W. W. Greg, the dominant bibliographer of the generation, lent the framework his authority, and for decades the first quarto's directions were cited in editions and classrooms as the visible traces of a reported text, the fingerprints on the weapon.

Laurie E. Maguire's Shakespearean Suspect Texts, published in 1996, subjected the whole diagnostic method to the test it had never faced. Maguire assembled the features that the memorial theory treated as symptoms, the descriptive directions among them, and checked their distribution across the full population of early play texts, suspect and sound alike. The features failed the test. Directions of the supposedly memorial kind occur in texts whose transmission is authorial and uncontaminated; the diagnostics do not discriminate, and a test that cannot discriminate cannot convict. Her conclusion was methodological before it was historical: memorial reconstruction had become an explanation that absorbed every feature and was endangered by none, and the directions, specifically, could not certify the origin of any text that contained them. On the question of genealogy, the census finds Maguire's audit decisive. The descriptive direction is a period habit, not a pathology, and the first quarto's fullness in this stratum is evidence about the kind of document its copy was, not about the mental processes of hypothetical reporters.

### Can a stage direction prove how a scene was staged on a particular occasion?

No single direction can prove the staging of a particular occasion, because a direction may prescribe an intention, record a habit, or copy an earlier document, and the page never says which. A direction proves that its staging belonged to the period's practice and was coherent with the dialogue; the occasion itself stays beyond the paperwork's reach.

Tiffany Stern's work on the documents of performance supplies the framework within which the surviving value of the directions can be stated exactly. Stern demonstrated that the materials of early modern production were more various than the old contrast between authorial draft and promptbook allowed: parts, plots, bills, and annotated copies circulated, and a printed text's directions might descend from any of them, or from the author's study, or from several in succession. The consequence for the census is bracing and liberating at once. Bracing, because descent can rarely be proved for any single direction. Liberating, because the directions are released from the genealogy trial and can be valued for what survives the trial intact: their testimony to the conventions of the stage that played the play. Lukas Erne's advocacy for the first quarto as a performance shaped text, and Jill L. Levenson's editorial practice in the Oxford edition, converge on the same resting place from different directions. The short text's directions record the play as a thing done by bodies in a building, and that record has an authority about staging that no theory of the book's origin can confer or remove.

The adjudication can now be stated in its final form. On genealogy, Maguire defeats Hoppe and Greg: the directions cannot certify memorial reconstruction, and every argument that begins "this direction shows the actors remembered" exceeds its evidence. On staging, Erne and the performance historians defeat the scepticism that Maguire's method might seem to licence: the directions remain the best surviving evidence of how the early stage arranged this play's bodies, levels, and properties, and their testimony is strongest exactly where it is most specific, in the wound, the bed, the window. On method, Stern and Dessen govern the peace: a direction is a signal in a professional code, certifying a staging possibility within period convention, and the critic's task is to read the code, not to psychoanalyse the copyist. The three verdicts are compatible, and together they describe the settlement under which modern editing proceeds.

## How Editors Harvest the First Quarto's Directions

Modern editions of the play are built on the second quarto's words and staged, to a remarkable degree, out of the first quarto's directions. The practice is so established that readers rarely notice it: the wound choreographed as the short text describes, the bed within the curtains, the exits and groupings silently completed from the fuller witness, all printed in the familiar text as though they had always stood there. Brian Gibbons, in the Arden edition of 1980, regularised the practice with an editor's candour, taking the second quarto as copy text for the words while admitting first quarto directions where the copy text was silent or baffled. G. Blakemore Evans, in the New Cambridge edition, harvested more liberally still, and his textual notes defend the harvest direction by direction. Levenson's Oxford text pressed the logic furthest, on the argued ground that the first quarto's theatrical stratum deserves systematic respect. René Weis, editing the play for the Arden third series, inherited the settlement and adjusted its margins, printing a text whose words descend from 1599 and whose staging descends, at the marked points, from 1597.

The settlement has a rationale that the census endorses and a cost that the census insists be acknowledged. The rationale is the adjudication above: since the first quarto's directions certify staging practice rather than textual authority, an editor may take them for the staging while refusing them for the words, without contradiction. The words and the directions are different kinds of evidence with different pedigrees, and an edition that treats them differently is not being eclectic but exact. The cost is legibility. An edition that imports directions owes its reader a visible account of the imports, and the conventions for giving that account, brackets, textual notes, and appendices, vary enough between editions that the same scene can present three different documentary faces in three standard texts. A seminar leader laying the rival directions side by side for a class, assembling the quarto and Folio evidence into a single deck with the [ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), performs the comparison that the editions disperse, and the exercise usually ends with the class understanding the stratum better than any one edition permits. The directions reward juxtaposition because juxtaposition is their native condition: three witnesses, three paperwork traditions, one stage.

Two editorial temptations must be refused, and the census refuses them on the record. The first is silent regularisation, the Folio's own temptation inherited by its successors: completing every entrance, supplying every exit, and smoothing the stratum into a consistency none of the witnesses possesses. A regularised text of the directions is a fiction about the paperwork, and it destroys exactly the evidence, the contrast between draft openness and playhouse fullness, that makes the stratum worth studying.

The harvest has one further refinement that the best editions observe and the census records with approval. An imported direction is admitted for the staging it certifies and for nothing else: it may place a body, time an entrance, or equip a hand, but it may not be used to gloss a word, date a revision, or characterise a speaker, since the direction's pedigree supports the first family of uses and not the second. Gibbons's notes observe the boundary instinctively, supplying the staging from the short text while arguing the readings from the long one, and Evans makes the boundary explicit at the duel, where the first quarto's choreography is printed and discussed as staging while the second quarto's speeches are edited as text. The refinement sounds small and is not. It is the adjudication of the preceding section translated into editorial mechanics, the point at which a theory of evidence becomes a practice on the page, and editions that observe it can be trusted with the stratum exactly as far as the stratum deserves. The second temptation is antiquarian refusal, the printing of the second quarto's directions alone on the ground that imports contaminate. That purity is purchased by suppressing the best evidence of the play's early staging, and it presents the reader with a text less stageable and less informative than the documents jointly allow. Between the two temptations stands the Gibbons and Evans practice, importation with disclosure, and the census records it as the editorial verdict that matches the evidentiary one.

## The Rosaline Direction and the Discipline of the Single Case

One entry in the census is deliberately curtailed. The early texts contain a notorious directional tangle involving the entrance associated with Rosaline, the unseen beloved of the first act, in which the witnesses' handling of names and entrances has generated a small literature of its own. The case is reserved for its own study, [The Rosaline Confusion in the Stage Directions](/2026/08/21/rosaline-confusion-stage-directions/), and the census notes it here for one methodological reason only. A single confusing direction, however fascinating, is a sample of one, and the history of this play's bibliography shows how readily a sample of one becomes the foundation of a general theory. The census has been built on the stratum as a whole, its census character insisted on from the first section, precisely so that no single exhibit, not the wound, not the window, not the Rosaline tangle, is asked to prove what only the population can show. The tangle will repay its own examination; its lesson for the present inquiry is the discipline of leaving it alone until then.

## How a Direction Reaches Print: Copy, Compositor, and the Printed Page

Between the staging and the scholar stands the printing house, and the census must account for the stratum's passage through type before it argues from the stratum's forms. A direction in a printed play of the 1590s had survived a chain of handlings: the papers from which the compositor worked, the compositor's own reading and setting of those papers, the conventions of the shop for displaying such matter, and the economics of the page, in which every notation cost type, space, and time. Each link in the chain could lose, compress, or regularise a direction, and the losses are not random. Compositors setting from difficult copy economised where the sense seemed recoverable, and a direction whose content the dialogue also carried was precisely the kind of matter a pressed workman could shorten or drop with a clear conscience. The census therefore treats absence with systematic suspicion: a direction missing from a quarto may have been missing from its copy, or may have died in the shop, and the two histories leave identical pages.

The display conventions of the early books also shape what a modern eye takes the stratum to be. The quartos print their directions in the italic letter reserved for matter outside the dialogue, set within the measure of the page, and the visual subordination of the stratum, smaller in presence than the verse it serves, began in the print shop rather than in the theatre. The Folio's grander page dignifies the directions it prints, spacing and centring them with a collected edition's ceremony, and that dignity has quietly inflated the Folio stratum's perceived authority ever since: the same notation looks like a record in a folio and like a jotting in a quarto, and arguments have been swayed by the costume. A census that counts from transcriptions, indifferent to typography, is protected against that particular seduction, and the protection is one more reason the article has proceeded by census rather than by impression.

The chain of transmission also explains a feature of the stratum that purely theatrical accounts handle awkwardly: its occasional nonsense. Directions in early play texts sometimes arrive garbled, misplaced, or attached to the wrong moment, the debris of misread copy and hurried composition, and the play's witnesses have their small share of such debris at the stratum's margins. An editor confronting a direction that cannot be staged as printed must decide whether the nonsense is a corruption of sense or a sense whose conventions have been lost, and the decision is a wager either way. Dessen's dictionary work shifted the odds in such wagers across the whole field, recovering period meanings for formulas that Victorian editors had emended as errors, and the census adopts the resulting presumption: a direction is presumed intelligible in its period language until the period language has been honestly tried. Even under that presumption, some notations remain debris, and the census enters them as such, since a stratum without admitted noise would be a stratum described by faith rather than by counting.

## Counting the Stratum: What a Census of the Directions Shows

A census differs from an anthology, and the difference is the burden of this article's method. An anthology collects the famous directions, the wound, the window, the curtains, and argues from their brilliance. A census counts everything, the brilliant and the routine together, and argues from the distribution. The distribution, once attended to, disciplines every claim made elsewhere in this inquiry, because the stratum's character is visible only in aggregate: hundreds of short notations, overwhelmingly entrances, a minority of exits, a scatter of properties, a handful of descriptions, and a very small residue of anomalies. Any theory of the directions that cannot accommodate the routine nine tenths of the stratum is a theory of the residue dressed as a theory of the whole.

Counting, however, is itself an interpretive act, and the census must declare its conventions. Whether the second quarto's stage contains a given direction at all can depend on decisions a counter makes in advance: a speech heading that names a character not previously entered may imply an entrance the text never prints; a direction divided between two lines of type may be one notation or two; the Folio's regularisations multiply notations that its copy expressed once. Honest counters of the early texts have therefore produced differing totals for the play's directions, and the census declines to rest any argument on a spurious precision. What survives every reasonable convention of counting is the shape of the distribution, and the shape is stable. Entrances dominate everywhere. Exits lag everywhere, and lag worst in the second quarto. Properties cluster at the scenes of highest practical risk, the night work, the fights, the tomb. Descriptions, the narrative directions that have attracted the scholarship, form a small minority of the whole stratum in every witness, including the first quarto whose reputation rests on them. The first quarto's distinctiveness is real but must be stated as a proportion: within a stratum of largely conventional notations, its descriptive minority is larger, richer, and more theatrically literate than the corresponding minority in the second quarto, and that difference of proportion, not a difference of kind, is what the evidence supports.

The distribution across the play's five acts tells a further story. The first act, with its brawl, its feast, its dance, and its masked entrances, is the most directionally furnished act in every witness, because its staging is the most populous and the least reducible to two figures in a space. The second act thins as the play contracts to the lovers and their go betweens, and its directional record is correspondingly sparse, a handful of entrances and the one immortal window. The third act's directions gather around the fights and the parting, action and altitude. The fourth act's record concentrates on the potion, the bed, the discovery, and the musicians, the machinery of the feigned death. The fifth act equips the tomb with its inventories and then falls almost silent, as though the paperwork, like the characters, had exhausted its instructions and could only watch. This arc, from civic fullness to funereal silence, is visible in all three witnesses with variations of degree, and it reflects the play's own shape rather than any copyist's programme: directions multiply where bodies multiply, and the tragedy progressively empties its stage.

Set against the population, the celebrated anomalies resume their proper scale. The naming of an actor, the permissive troupe of maskers, the narrative wound: each is a single notation among hundreds, and each has generated commentary exceeding the commentary on all the routine notations combined. The census does not begrudge the attention, since the anomalies are genuinely the most informative single entries in the record. But the census insists on the inferential price. An anomaly can show what the stratum could do at its most expressive; it cannot show what the stratum characteristically was, and theories of origin built on the anomalies alone commit the statistician's oldest error, generalising from the tail of the distribution. The routine entrances, unglamorous and unanimous, are the stratum's centre of gravity, and they testify to a shared professional paperwork across all three witnesses, beneath every difference that the debate has magnified.

## Fighting, Dancing, Music: Directions for the Play in Motion

The directions for organised movement form a class of their own, because they coordinate not individuals but ensembles in time. A fight direction must start the combat, and in a text that trusts convention it need do no more: the combatants were professionals, the weapons were standard, and the outcome was written in the dialogue that followed. The first quarto's expansion of the duel direction is therefore all the more striking against the class's norms, since the class invited brevity and the short text chose narration. The choice suggests that the moment's choreography was felt, by whoever shaped that copy, to be non standard, a piece of business whose exact arrangement carried meaning that the standard signal would have lost. The census reads the expansion as a marker of salience within the production's own memory of itself: the wound under the arm was the company's signature difficulty in the scene, the pass that had to be got right, and the paperwork remembered it.

Dance directions obey the opposite pressure. The feast's dance is a set piece whose steps belonged to the company's stock of accomplishments, and the texts mark its occurrence and leave its content to the dancers, as a modern score marks a cadenza. The permissive number of the maskers belongs to this class's logic: a dance fields the bodies available, and the direction's openness is the professional's shrug, not the amateur's confusion. Only when the argument turns to the copy's origin does the permissive direction acquire its second life as evidence of a draft, and the census has already recorded that inference as sound but bounded: the direction certifies that the number was unfixed in the copy, not that it was unfixed in the author's mind at every stage, nor that the performance itself varied. Companies fix in rehearsal what papers leave open, and the fixing is exactly the event the paperwork cannot show.

Music directions complete the class, and they carry a quiet evidentiary bonus. A direction for music is among the hardest of all notations to invent retrospectively, because music's function in these scenes is structural rather than decorative: the dance must cover an entrance's preparation, the musicians' playing and refusal to play articulate the fourth act's passage from feigned death to household management, and the timing of sound against speech is a production fact of a kind that literary composition does not spontaneously generate. The first quarto's attentiveness to this class is therefore meaningful even under the census's strict grading, not because any single music cue proves a provenance, but because the pattern of attention is the pattern of a document shaped by use. Set beside the second quarto's comparative indifference to the class, the contrast restates the article's central distinction in miniature: one witness's paperwork leans toward the event, the other's toward the composition, and the directions register the lean in every class the census examines.

The class also exposes the stratum's dependence on skills the modern reader no longer supplies automatically. A direction to fight, in a playhouse whose sharers fenced as a condition of employment, specified an art; a direction to dance specified another; and the modern editor's expansions of such directions, however well meant, risk translating a professional signal into a lay description and thereby falsifying its register. Dessen's dictionary of stage directions exists largely to prevent that mistranslation, by recovering the period senses of the stratum's verbs and formulas. The census adopts its discipline as a rule of reading: before a direction is used as evidence, its words must be construed in the theatrical language of the 1590s, where a term's modern sense is a hypothesis to be tested and never a gift to be assumed.

## Fault Lines: Missing Exits, Divided Entrances, and the Border of the Speech Heading

The stratum's defects are as instructive as its riches, and the census closes its survey with the fault lines along which the three witnesses characteristically fail. The commonest fault is the missing exit. Characters remain on stages they should have left, in the paperwork if not in the performance, and the second quarto is the worst offender, repeatedly bringing on its persons with care and abandoning them without ceremony. The fault's distribution argues its own explanation: entrances matter to the paperwork because an entrance must be prepared, a body summoned, a property readied, while an exit prepares nothing and can be left to the performer's instinct and the bookkeeper's eye. A text prepared for the study rather than the tiring house would show exactly this asymmetry, and the second quarto shows it in the highest degree, which is one more strand in the case for its copy's origin in draft papers.

The divided and merged entrance is the second fault. The early texts sometimes distribute a group's arrival across more than one notation, and sometimes gather into a single entrance persons whom the dialogue then treats as arriving in sequence, and the compositor's page cannot always be made to say which pattern the copy intended. Editors resolve such cases silently at their peril, since the resolution determines who hears what in scenes built on overhearing and partial knowledge, the orchard scene above all, where the whole effect depends on the precise distribution of bodies between the wall, the window, and the dark. The census records the class because it marks the boundary of the stratum's competence: at the level of sequence within a scene, the directions are frequently not evidence at all but problems, and the honest editor's note confesses the reconstruction rather than concealing it in a confident entrance line.

The third fault line runs along the border between direction and speech heading, the narrow margin where the stratum meets the dialogue. The second quarto's speech headings fluctuate in ways that the editorial tradition has long read as signs of draft copy: names abbreviated, expanded, and varied as a writer varies them when writing fast for readers who know the persons, the Nurse's husbandry of labels, the Friar's shifting styles, the servants' generic designations. The first quarto's headings show the opposite tendency, regularised toward the fiction's names with the celebrated exception of the actor who displaces his character outright. Neither tendency is a direction, strictly speaking, and yet the two strata illuminate each other, because both are paperwork, and paperwork has habits that cross its own categories. A copy casual in its headings is likely to be casual in its exits; a copy theatrical in its headings is likely to be narrative in its descriptions. The census's three witnesses keep their characters consistently across the border, and the consistency strengthens the article's central claim that the witnesses embody different kinds of document, prepared at different distances from the stage, rather than different degrees of care applied to the same kind.

What the fault lines cannot do is convict any witness of worthlessness, and the census resists the polemical use of defects as firmly as the polemical use of riches. The second quarto's missing exits coexist with its matchless speeches; the first quarto's theatrical fullness coexists with a text of the dialogue that no editor would print; the Folio's tidiness coexists with derivative authority. Each witness fails in the direction of its origin, and the pattern of failure is among the most reliable guides to that origin the documents provide, more reliable, in the census's judgement, than the pattern of their splendours, because splendours can be borrowed and faults are fingerprints.

## In the Rehearsal Room and the Classroom: The Directions at Work

The stratum has a working life beyond the bibliography that studies it, and the census ends its survey where the directions still earn their keep. Directors and fight arrangers consult the first quarto's wound choreography because it solves a problem their craft recognises, and the solution's survival across four centuries of restaging is a practical corroboration that no theoretical argument could supply: a piece of business that companies keep rediscovering is a piece of business the early stage is likely to have possessed. Actors preparing the potion scene work with the curtains direction because it fixes the scene's physical destination, and the discovery that follows inherits the staging whether the production knows its source or not. Teachers use the window direction because three words of prose can dismantle, in a single lesson, the most durable misconception their students bring to the play, and the balcony's absence from the documents teaches the difference between a text and its afterlife more efficiently than any general lecture on the theme.

That working life imposes a final evidentiary consideration. A direction that performers find actable, that solves the scene's physical problem in the period's own terms, possesses a kind of verification that library evidence cannot confer, the verification of the body in the space. The census is careful with this consideration, since actability is not ancestry: a staging can be excellent, faithful to the dialogue, and entirely modern in origin. But where a documented direction and a rediscovered staging coincide, as at the wound and the window, the coincidence closes a circle that pure bibliography leaves open, joining the paperwork to the practice it was written to serve. The directions were made for use. Their survival in use, tested nightly in rooms the bibliographers never enter, is the stratum's last and least refutable testimony.

## The Playhouse Inside the Paperwork: Architecture Presupposed by the Directions

Every direction presupposes a building, and the census can read the building out of the paperwork with unusual confidence because the directions were written for a known kind of house. The play belonged first to the public amphitheatres of the 1590s, the Theatre and the Curtain in Shoreditch, and its stratum assumes their common anatomy: a platform thrust into a yard, a tiring house facade behind it pierced by doors, an upper level above the facade, a curtained space at the platform's rear, and a stage large enough to field the civic crowds of the first act and empty enough, an hour later, to hold two figures and a friar. The directions never describe this anatomy, because the men who wrote and read them were standing inside it. They presuppose it at every line, and the presupposition is among the strongest evidence the stratum offers, since a document's unexamined assumptions are harder to counterfeit than its assertions.

The doors carry the play's traffic, and the directions' handling of entrances assumes the facade's openings without counting them. Scholarship on the early modern stage, from Hosley's studies of the upper level to Gurr's reconstruction of the company's playing conditions, has debated the exact door arrangement of the first houses, two doors or three, and the place of the discovery space between or behind them. The play's paperwork contributes its own testimony by what it requires: simultaneous arrivals from opposed directions in the street scenes, the management of the feast's service through the same facade that admits the guests, and the discovery of the bed within the curtains, which requires a recess that curtains can close at the platform's centre or side. A text whose directions can be executed only in a house of that general anatomy certifies the anatomy as surely as it certifies any single piece of business, and the certification runs in the useful direction: the paperwork corroborates the architectural reconstruction at the points where the reconstruction is built from other evidence entirely, the surviving contracts, the drawings, the archaeology of the Rose and the Globe.

The upper level receives the census's closest architectural attention because the play uses it with such economy. Two scenes require it, the orchard and the parting, and both place the heroine there in a chamber that the convention reads as hers. The level's height in the first houses was a single storey above the platform, near enough for a whispered negotiation to carry to the yard and far enough to make the descent a visible event, and the directions' calm assumption of that distance matches the archaeology precisely. No direction calls for flying machinery, for descents from the heavens that roofed part of the stage, or for the trap work that other plays of the decade exploit for ghosts and devils, and the absences are as architectural as the presences: the tragedy's wonders are poison, sleep, and timing, and its building is asked for nothing more spectacular than a window, a curtain, and a monument that opens. The monument itself is the stratum's one large unwritten presupposition. The tomb must stand somewhere, admit a succession of bodies, and dominate the final scene's stage picture, yet no direction builds it; the dialogue's references to the Capels' monument and the paperwork's property inventories together imply a practicable structure or a designated area whose identity the company settled in rehearsal and the documents never needed to fix. The census records the tomb as the stratum's largest silence, the point where paperwork, convention, and carpentry divided their labour and only two of the three left records.

Light belongs to the architecture of an afternoon stage, and the directions register it obliquely through properties. The torches that equip the maskers, the tomb breakers, and the watch are the standard early modern notation for night, carried into scenes played in daylight, and the paperwork's consistent distribution of them is a small system of its own: where the fiction is nocturnal, the directions provide flames, and where the flames appear, the audience read darkness. The lanterns and tapers of the final movement extend the system to its limit, a whole act of the fiction transacted before dawn and lit, in the paperwork, by an accumulating inventory of carried fire. A modern reader in a darkened theatre receives darkness as the house's gift; the first audiences received it as a property's meaning, and the directions are the documents that performed the translation.

## From the Quartos to the Modern Page: The Stratum Transmitted

The directions a modern reader meets have survived three distinct transmissions, and the census closes its documentary survey by separating them, because conflating the three is the last and commonest source of error in arguments from this evidence. The first transmission carried the stratum from the papers of the 1590s into the printed quartos and the Folio, with the losses, regularisations, and compositorial interventions that the preceding sections have described. The second carried it through the editions of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, whose editors treated directions as part of the play's furniture to be polished: Nicholas Rowe's edition of 1709, the first to attend systematically to the stratum, supplied scene locations and completed entrances in the spirit of the Folio's own tidying, and his successors regularised spelling, punctuation, and placement until the stratum on the page looked like a single composed system rather than the braided record it is. The third transmission is the scholarly reaction, the twentieth century's return to the early witnesses, which restored the quartos' directions to view, quarrelled over their origins, and equipped modern editions with the apparatus that lets a reader distinguish, at least in principle, a direction of 1597 from a direction of 1709.

The typography of the modern page belongs to this history and shapes the evidence more than readers suspect. A direction printed in italics, centred, and bracketed announces itself as an editorial layer; the same direction printed in the text's ordinary type, as the early books print it, stands as part of the document. Editions differ in their signalling, and the differences are arguments: the edition that brackets every import from the first quarto teaches its reader to distrust the stratum's unity, while the edition that prints a seamless text teaches a confidence the documents do not warrant. The brackets themselves have a history within the play's editing, tightening across the twentieth century as the bibliographical conscience sharpened, and the direction stratum of a modern text is therefore a small museum of editorial ethics, in which the Gibbons and Evans practice of importation with disclosure appears, materially, as a forest of square brackets in the feast, the duel, and the tomb.

One consequence of the triple transmission deserves emphasis, because it governs every quotation in this article. When a modern text prints a direction without brackets, the direction may descend from any witness or from Rowe, and only the edition's textual notes can say which; when it prints one with brackets, the notes can say from where. Arguments from the directions must therefore be conducted, as this census has conducted them, from the early witnesses themselves or from editions whose apparatus is consulted rather than assumed. The point is not pedantry. The balcony error, the stratum's most famous corruption, entered the tradition precisely through the second transmission's habit of improving the paperwork, and it survived the third transmission's corrections because the improved version had become the culture's picture of the play. The census's whole method, counting from the witnesses, grading by kind, and refusing to let any single notation outvote the population, is the discipline that the transmission history prescribes.

## Three Directions Re-Examined Under the Standard

The adjudication reached above can be tested by returning its standard to the three exhibits on which the debate was built, and the census performs the test before it rests. The wound direction first. Under the standard, the direction certifies a choreography: Tybalt's thrust under Mercutio's arm, delivered in the instant of Romeo's intervention, belongs to the period's staging practice, agrees with the dialogue's distribution of blame, and has been corroborated by centuries of professional restaging. Under the standard, the same direction does not certify the mental history of the copyist, and the memorial theory's use of it as a symptom is disallowed, not because the theory is impossible but because the exhibit is equally consistent with desk composition by a writer who understood the scene's physical problem. The exhibit's strength on practice and its neutrality on provenance are the standard's whole content in one case.

The window direction second. Under the standard, the direction certifies a placement: the heroine in an upper opening, the lover below, the scene transacted across a modest and enforceable height. It certifies nothing about carpentry, and the tradition that furnished the opening with a balcony is therefore classified as reception, the culture's property, carrying no documentary vote. The exhibit also demonstrates the standard's asymmetry in the useful direction: a direction's silence about the balcony is, in this instance, strong evidence against the balcony, because the direction speaks to the very matter in dispute, the heroine's position, and a projecting structure would have belonged to the position's description in any paperwork tradition that possessed one. Silence is evidence where the document undertakes to cover the ground, and noise elsewhere; the census has applied that rule consistently, crediting the second quarto's silence about exits with little weight, since exits were never its undertaking, and crediting its testimony about the window with full weight, since placement was.

The Kempe direction third. Under the standard, the direction certifies a casting: the company's clown played Peter, and the copy from which the first quarto was printed moved in a world where performer and part were thought together. It certifies nothing about the copy's assembly, and the three histories that explain it, authorial familiarity, company annotation, and memorial surfacing, stand exactly where the evidence leaves them, as possibilities ranked by arguments drawn from other strata of the book. The exhibit's career in the scholarship is the standard's cautionary tale in miniature: a fact of the first quality, casting, was spent for a century as though it were a fact of another kind, transmission, and the exchange rate between the two was never examined. The census's refusal of that exchange, applied impartially to every exhibit, is the difference between a documentary history of the directions and another anthology of their wonders.

## The Verdict: What the Early Stage Directions Teach

The directions teach, first, that the play's first theatre worked with an economy and a precision that the speeches alone would never reveal. The paperwork trusts convention for the standard matter and spends its words on the unusual: the thrust under the raised arm, the bed within the curtains, the heroine at her window, the clown at his entrance. That distribution is the signature of a professional culture that knew what needed writing down, and it allows the modern reader to reconstruct the staging's logic with a confidence that the dialogue, for all its richness, could not supply on its own. The vertical design of the love scenes, the discovery space's management of the feigned death, the property discipline of the tomb: these are facts about the play in performance that survive because a few dozen prose clauses were printed with the verse, and they survive nowhere else.

The directions teach, second, a lesson in the grading of evidence that extends well beyond this play. The stratum's career in scholarship runs from fingerprint to photograph to code: treated first as the symptom of a text's origin, then as the transparent record of its staging, and finally, in the settlement the census endorses, as a professional notation whose testimony is real, conventional, and bounded. Each stage of that career corrected the previous one's characteristic error, credulity about genealogy, credulity about transparency, and the final position is not a compromise but a specification: directions certify practice, not provenance; possibilities within a convention, not occasions in a diary. The balcony controversy is the popular form of the same lesson. The window stands in the documents, the balcony stands in the culture, and the reader who has learned to ask what kind of evidence a direction is will never again confuse the two authorities.

The clique of received ideas about this play prefers its lovers framed in picturesque architecture and its text descended in a clean line from a single manuscript. The directions disturb both comforts. They replace the balcony with a window and the clean line with a braided transmission, authorial papers annotated by use, playhouse habits annotated by print, each witness preserving a different mixture of the study and the stage. Restoring the directions to the centre of the play's textual story, which is this series' standing argument conducted on fresh evidence, restores with them the working theatre that made the tragedy: a company that knew how a wound is given under a friend's arm, where a sleeping girl is laid, and how high a window needs to be for a lover to worship at it and climb down from it into exile. The speeches are immortal. The directions are the record of the hands that first held them up to the light.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch directions catalogue, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch directions catalogue is the table in this article that gathers the play's most revealing early stage directions, witness by witness, and states for each one what it certifies and what it cannot prove. Its pattern is double. The directions are fullest exactly where the staging was least conventional: a wound given under a raised arm, a bed behind curtains, a heroine at a window, an actor named in place of his character. And their certainty is graded in the same order, strongest on choreography and placement, weakest on every question of how the copy was made. The catalogue exposes a stratum that certifies performance practice with real authority while refusing, exhibit by exhibit, to certify the genealogy that generations of bibliography tried to build on it. That pairing of strength and limit is the pattern the whole census confirms.

### Q: What is a stage direction in an early modern play text?

A stage direction in an early modern play text is a short prose instruction or notation, printed apart from the dialogue, that governs or records the physical life of the performance: who enters and leaves, where a figure stands, what properties are carried, when music, dance, or fighting begins. The stratum was a professional shorthand, written by dramatists, bookkeepers, scribes, or printers for practitioners who shared its conventions and needed few words to execute complex business. Because several kinds of hand could contribute to the stratum at several stages of a text's life, a direction is evidence of mixed pedigree. It may prescribe a staging not yet performed, record a staging already given, or copy an earlier document's instruction, and the printed page rarely declares which. That undecided pedigree is the starting point of every responsible argument from the directions.

### Q: Which early text of the play prints the fullest stage directions?

The first quarto of 1597 prints the fullest stage directions, and the difference lies in character as much as in quantity. Where the second quarto of 1599 notates entrances and properties and trusts the performers for the action, the first quarto repeatedly narrates the action itself: the mechanics of Mercutio's wound, Juliet's fall upon the bed within the curtains, the business of the feast and the musicians. Its directions behave descriptively, as though reporting staging, while the second quarto's behave prescriptively, as though licensing it. The Folio's stratum is fuller than the second quarto's in places but derivative, a regularisation of inherited copy rather than an independent record. Editors therefore treat the first quarto as the richest witness for staging and the second quarto as the authority for the words, a division of trust that the whole editorial tradition since Gibbons and Evans has practised.

### Q: What does the first quarto's direction for Mercutio's wounding describe that the second quarto leaves unstated?

The first quarto's direction describes the wound's exact mechanics: Tybalt, reaching under Mercutio's arm as Romeo stands between the combatants, stabs him, and then flees with his followers. The second quarto leaves all of that unstated, setting the fight in motion and letting Mercutio's "I am hurt" (3.1) and Romeo's reproaches carry the information after the event. The short text's clause explains what the long text's dialogue assumes: how a man separated from his opponent by his friend's body nevertheless receives a mortal thrust, and why Romeo's share of the blame is physical rather than merely moral. Fight directors continue to stage the pass as the direction describes it, which gives the notation a practical corroboration rare among textual evidence: the choreography works in the space exactly as written, and it solves the scene's staging problem completely.

### Q: Why does the second quarto leave so many of its exits unmarked?

The second quarto leaves exits unmarked because its copy was prepared at a stage where entrances needed planning and exits did not. An entrance must be summoned, costumed, and equipped, so the paperwork attends to it; an exit requires nothing to be prepared and can be trusted to the performer's instinct and the bookkeeper's supervision. The asymmetry is characteristic of authorial draft papers, in which the writer notes what composition requires and leaves production routine to the professionals who will stage the piece. The pattern supports the larger case, built also on the permissive maskers direction and the duplicated dawn lines, that the copy behind the second quarto stood close to the author's working manuscript. It does not make the text unperformable or unperformed: a company could stage the play from such papers by supplying the routine the papers omit, which is precisely the division of labour the early modern theatre practised.

### Q: What does the direction "Enter Juliet at a window" leave undecided?

The direction "Enter Juliet at a window" decides the essential fact, that Juliet appears above in an opening of the tiring house facade, and leaves nearly everything else undecided. It does not specify the opening's height beyond the upper level's single storey, its width, its furnishing, or whether Juliet stands within the opening, leans from it, or is framed by it like a portrait. It names no balcony and no balustrade, and it gives Romeo no answering direction for his position below beyond the orchard the dialogue establishes. Those undecided matters are exactly the ones later staging traditions settled by invention, dressing the window with the architecture of successive centuries until the invented balcony displaced the documented window in the culture's picture of the scene. The direction's economy is professional: the company needed the placement, not the carpentry, and the page records the placement alone.

### Q: What is the difference between a descriptive direction and a permissive one?

A descriptive direction narrates an action as though reporting it, in the manner of the first quarto's account of Tybalt stabbing Mercutio under his arm and flying with his followers. A permissive direction, in the term W. W. Greg gave the class, licenses a range of stagings instead of commanding one, in the manner of the second quarto's entrance of five or six maskers, a troupe whose number the text declines to fix. The two kinds point toward different documentary origins. Description leans toward a record shaped by performance or by the imagination of performance; permission leans toward a draft written before production had settled its quantities. Neither lean amounts to proof, since a descriptive direction can be composed at a desk and a permissive one can survive into a working text. The census grades them as indications of the copy's character, strong in aggregate and weak in isolation.

### Q: Why does the first quarto send Juliet to a bed within the curtains?

The first quarto sends Juliet to a bed within the curtains because the staging requires her body to remain present, concealed, and discoverable in the same space. The curtained recess at the rear of the early modern stage, the discovery space, allowed a figure to be hidden at a scene's end and revealed at the next scene's need without any exit or entrance. Juliet drinks the potion, falls on the bed, and is closed within the curtains; the household's lamentations then play before a space the audience knows to be occupied, and the discovery of the body is a reopening rather than a fresh entrance. The direction also fixes the scene's refusal of a conventional close: the heroine never exits, the stage picture persists, and the feigned death acquires a physical continuity that the second quarto's briefer notation leaves to inference.

### Q: How does the Folio change the directions it inherits from the quartos?

The Folio regularises the directions it inherits. It supplies the act headings the quartos lack, completes entrances that its copy had left ragged, adds exits at points where the earlier texts trusted the stage to clear itself, spells out attendants and groupings, and occasionally expands a bare notation into a fuller form that guides a reader staging the scene in imagination. The changes descend through the third and fourth quartos from the second quarto's line, with the Folio's own compositors and editors contributing the final tidying. The result is a stratum smoother and more consistent than any quarto's, and correspondingly less trustworthy as a record of any single document. A Folio expansion may preserve a genuine detail transmitted through its copy, or it may be an early seventeenth century tidiness mistaken by later readers for authority, and the two can no longer be separated link by link.

### Q: What part did Will Kempe play, according to the first quarto's direction?

According to the first quarto's direction, Will Kempe played Peter, the Capulet servingman whose prose exchange with the musicians follows the discovery of the apparently dead Juliet in the fourth act. The direction summons the performer by his own name where the fiction requires the character, the only such naming in the play's early texts. Kempe was the Lord Chamberlain's Men's celebrated clown, a sharer in the company and a draw in his own right, famous for his jigs and his improvisational energy. His presence in the role explains the scene's proportions: the musicians' interlude, often treated as expendable comic relief, is a star performer's set piece positioned at the tragedy's darkest threshold. The direction certifies the casting beyond argument. What it cannot certify is the process by which the actor's name came to stand in the copy, since authorial familiarity, company paperwork, and memorial transmission would each produce the same nine letters.

### Q: What is an implicit stage direction, and where does the play hide them?

An implicit stage direction is an action encoded inside a line of dialogue rather than stated in the prose stratum: an offer, a refusal, a drawing of steel, a kneeling, announced by the words themselves. The play hides them everywhere, most consequentially where the explicit stratum is most silent. Juliet's balcony soliloquy depends entirely on an arrangement no direction states, that she believes herself unheard while Romeo hears her from below; Romeo's speeches at the window encode his gaze upward and his stillness; the tomb scene's penetrations and disposals of bodies are narrated in verse while the directions inventory only the tools. The census counts the explicit stratum, but the implicit stratum directs the performance at least as powerfully, and its existence disciplines every conclusion drawn from the paperwork's sparseness. A scene poor in printed directions may be rich in encoded action, and usually is.

### Q: Why are the directions for the tomb scene mostly lists of properties?

The directions for the tomb scene are mostly lists of properties because properties were what the paperwork needed to secure. Romeo and Balthasar enter with a torch, a mattock, and a crow of iron; the Friar follows with a lantern, crow, and spade. Each object is unusual, must be found, carried, and used, and its absence would stall the performance, so the copy specifies the inventory with a quartermaster's care. The actions performed with the tools, forcing the monument, fighting Paris, disposing the bodies, belonged to the performers' craft and the dialogue's narration, and the directions leave them unwritten. The distribution restates the stratum's governing logic across the whole play: the paperwork spends its words where preparation is required and falls silent where convention can be trusted. An inventory direction is therefore not a failure of description but a success of the paperwork's actual function, which was to make the performance possible rather than to commemorate it.

### Q: How do the early directions handle the lovers' parting at dawn?

The early directions handle the parting at dawn by placing the lovers aloft, in the upper space that served as Juliet's chamber, and letting the vertical arrangement carry the scene. The aubade, the debate of the lark and the nightingale, plays at height; Romeo then descends to the orchard floor, and Juliet remains above, watching him go, in the disposition her line seals: "Then, window, let day in, and let life out" (3.5). The arrangement mirrors the orchard scene's geometry with its meaning inverted, the same axis of height and distance that joined the lovers in hope now separating them in exile. No direction furnishes the chamber or specifies the manner of the descent, whether by ladder, stair, or the rope the dialogue elsewhere promises. The stratum certifies the placement and the descent, and trusts the company for the carpentry, in its habitual division of labour.

### Q: What can a direction written in the narrative present tense suggest about its copy?

A direction written in the narrative present tense, narrating business as it unfolds rather than commanding it in advance, suggests that its copy was shaped by performance or by close knowledge of performance. The first quarto's wound direction is the type: it reads like a report of an action witnessed, and the classic memorial reconstruction theory built heavily on that flavour, arguing that performers dictating or assembling a text would naturally describe the business they had enacted. The suggestion is real but bounded. Laurie Maguire's audit of the suspect texts showed that descriptive directions occur in texts of uncontaminated authorial transmission, so the flavour cannot diagnose an origin. A dramatist composing at a desk could write descriptively, imagining the scene in performance as every dramatist must. The tense therefore indicates the copy's imaginative or documentary closeness to the stage, a property the first quarto possesses in high degree, without identifying the hands that gave it that property.

### Q: Why do music and dance directions cluster in the feast and the musicians' scene?

Music and dance directions cluster in the feast and the musicians' scene because those are the play's two set pieces of performed sound, and sound requires coordination that the paperwork must fix. The feast's dance covers entrances, displays the household's magnificence, and creates the conditions in which Romeo sees Juliet and Tybalt sees Romeo, so its occurrence and placement are structural facts of the plot. The musicians' scene turns on playing and its refusal: the men are summoned to play at a wedding become a funeral, debate their instruments and their payment in prose, and are dismissed, and the timing of music against grief is the scene's whole meaning. Directions attend to these moments because a late or missing musical cue damages the action rather than merely decorating it wrongly. The first quarto's fuller attention to the class is the attentiveness of paperwork shaped by performance, where such cues are the difference between a scene and an embarrassment.

### Q: How should a student cite a stage direction that survives in only one early text?

A student should cite a stage direction that survives in only one early text by naming the witness, not merely the edition in hand. The honest form identifies the direction as the first quarto's, the second quarto's, or the Folio's, gives its act and scene in the modern numbering, and notes where the modern edition being used has imported, bracketed, or omitted it. The discipline matters because the witnesses disagree in kind: the first quarto's direction for Mercutio's wound and the second quarto's silence about it are different kinds of evidence, and a citation that merges them into an undifferentiated "the text" misrepresents both. Where an argument depends on the direction, the student should also state its class, descriptive, permissive, or inventory, since the class governs the weight the direction can bear. Directions are documents, and documents are cited by their provenance.

### Q: What is the strongest claim the early directions can support?

The strongest claim the early directions can support is a claim about staging practice: that the early stage arranged this play's bodies, levels, and properties in the ways the directions describe, at the wound, the window, the curtained bed, and the tomb. Such claims are corroborated from three directions at once, by the dialogue's agreement with the notation, by the architecture of the houses the company used, and by the directions' conformity to the period's theatrical vocabulary as the dictionaries of stage practice have recovered it. Where all three corroborations hold, the census treats the staging as established. The corresponding weak claim, by contrast, is any claim about the mental or mechanical process that produced a given copy: memory, dictation, annotation, composition. The directions bear on practice with authority and on provenance only by suggestion, and scholarship goes wrong when it spends the first kind of authority on the second kind of question.

### Q: Why did eighteenth century editors add directions and locations of their own?

Eighteenth century editors added directions and locations of their own because their editions served readers in studies and actors in theatres very different from the play's first houses, and both constituencies needed guidance the early texts did not supply. Nicholas Rowe's edition of 1709 began the work systematically, assigning each scene a location, completing entrances, and regularising the stratum in the spirit of the Folio's own tidying, and his successors continued through the century. The additions were acts of hospitality that became acts of authority: printed without consistent marking, they merged into the received text, so that generations of readers met a stratum part documentary and part Georgian without any map of the join. The balcony's conquest of the orchard scene belongs to this transmission, the window dressed by stages into architecture. The scholarly return to the early witnesses in the twentieth century was, in large part, an attempt to redraw the map.

### Q: How do fight directors use the first quarto's directions in modern productions?

Fight directors use the first quarto's direction for the duel as a choreography brief, because it solves the scene's standing problem in a form that bodies can execute. The problem is fixed by the dialogue: Mercutio must be mortally wounded by Tybalt while Romeo stands between them attempting peace, and Romeo must bear a physical share of the blame. The direction's arrangement, Tybalt striking under Mercutio's raised arm in the instant of intervention, satisfies every constraint at once, and production after production has adopted it, often through editions and handbooks rather than from the quarto itself. The adoption functions as a practical test that bibliography cannot perform: a piece of business repeatedly found actable, in the period's own terms, by professionals solving the scene afresh. Such corroboration does not prove the direction records a particular early performance, but it strongly supports the census's verdict that the direction certifies the early stage's practice.

### Q: What is lost when an edition silently regularises the early directions?

When an edition silently regularises the early directions, the evidence of the stratum's contrasts disappears, and with it the reader's ability to reason about the documents at all. The second quarto's permissive openness, its unmarked exits, its inventories without actions, are the marks of one kind of copy; the first quarto's narrative fullness is the mark of another; the Folio's tidiness is the mark of transmission itself. A regularised text irons the three into a single composed system and presents the result as the play's paperwork, which no historical document ever was. The loss is methodological before it is aesthetic: arguments about origin, staging, and authority all depend on the contrasts, and the reader of a smoothed text inherits conclusions without the means to test them. The editorial practice the census endorses, importation with disclosure, brackets and notes that keep the witnesses distinguishable, preserves the evidence inside the convenience of a single readable page.
