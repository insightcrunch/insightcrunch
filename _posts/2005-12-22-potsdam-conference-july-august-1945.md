On the morning of July 16, 1945, in the desert near Alamogordo, New Mexico, a tower holding a plutonium device roughly six meters tall vanished in a fireball that witnesses described as a second sunrise. The Trinity test worked. Its yield, estimated at roughly twenty-one kilotons, exceeded the expectations of the scientists who had built it. The next morning, Harry Truman boarded his aircraft for the flight to the Potsdam Conference, carrying a secret that no wartime leader had ever carried to a negotiating table: the certain knowledge that his country possessed a weapon capable of destroying a city in a single blow. The conference, which opened on July 17 in the Cecilienhof Palace on the outskirts of ruined Berlin, was therefore the first summit in history conducted under the shadow of the atomic age, and the last summit of the wartime Big Three.

The conference that followed ran for seventeen days, consumed thirteen plenary sessions, and employed some four thousand support personnel. It produced the administrative architecture of occupied Germany, the Oder-Neisse line that became Poland's western border, the Council of Foreign Ministers, the charter framework for the Nuremberg trials, and the Potsdam Declaration that set the terms for Japan's surrender. It also witnessed something no summit had ever witnessed: the British delegation changed prime ministers in the middle of the proceedings, when Winston Churchill lost the general election and Clement Attlee arrived to take his seat. The namable claim: Potsdam was the Allied committee system operating at maximum institutional intensity, absorbing two leadership transitions in a single fortnight without breaking stride, and proving that the coalition's machinery had become larger than any of the men who sat at its head.

## The World That Waited in the Ruin of Berlin

The decision to hold the third Big Three summit at Potsdam was itself a measure of how completely the war in Europe had been decided. Berlin lay in ruins, divided by agreement into sectors but occupied in fact by the Red Army, which had taken the city in May at a cost of tens of thousands of Soviet dead. Potsdam, a suburb some twenty-five kilometers southwest of the capital, sat inside the Soviet occupation zone, which meant that the entire conference took place on territory controlled by one of the three negotiating parties. Stalin did not need to argue for this arrangement the way he had argued for Soviet venues at the two previous summits. The Red Army's position across Eastern Europe, from the Baltic to the Balkans, had settled the question of venue before any diplomat raised it.

Germany's unconditional surrender on May 8 had created the administrative vacuum that the conference was convened to fill. Four occupation zones had been drawn on paper by the European Advisory Commission during the war, and the Allied Control Council existed as a legal concept, but the machinery of joint occupation had barely begun to function. Denazification policy was a set of principles awaiting enforcement. The German economy, the largest in continental Europe, lay shattered, and the question of how much industrial capacity the victors would strip as reparations divided the coalition along lines that would soon become permanent. The protocol signed at the conference's end would run to thousands of words of administrative detail precisely because the victors were governing a country, not merely dividing spoils.

The Pacific war supplied the conference's other clock. American forces had taken Okinawa in June at staggering cost, and planning for the invasion of the Japanese home islands projected casualties on a scale that appalled the American chiefs. Stalin had promised at Yalta to enter the war against Japan within ninety days of Germany's defeat, which placed the Soviet deadline in early August, and the American delegation arrived at Potsdam expecting that promise to be kept. The atomic bomb, whose test had succeeded the day before the conference opened, hung over every calculation without ever being placed formally on the agenda. Truman's delegation understood that Soviet entry into the Pacific war and the new weapon were the two instruments that might end the war without invasion, and the conference became the place where the timing of both was quietly coordinated.

The Germany the victors gathered to administer was barely governable. The Reich's administrative apparatus had collapsed with the regime, the transport network was shattered, the cities were piles of rubble housing millions of homeless civilians, and the food situation was approaching catastrophe in the western zones. The Supreme Headquarters Allied Expeditionary Force had established military government in the areas its armies occupied, and General Dwight Eisenhower, as the American military governor, was already confronting the administrative chaos that the conference's elegant protocol language would soon claim to organize. The Allied Control Council, the joint authority Potsdam would formalize, held its first meeting on July 30, while the conference was still in session, a reminder that the machinery was being built while the architects were still arguing over the blueprints. Millions of displaced persons, liberated forced laborers, and refugees were moving across the wreckage in every direction, and the occupation authorities' first task was simply keeping people alive through the coming winter.

## A President of Ninety-Five Days

Harry Truman had been President of the United States for roughly ninety-five days when the Potsdam Conference opened, and the brevity of his tenure shaped everything about the American performance there. Franklin Roosevelt's death on April 12 had elevated a vice president who had been deliberately excluded from the inner councils of wartime diplomacy. Truman had not attended the great summits, had not been briefed on the Manhattan Project until after taking office, and had never met Stalin or, before the conference, Churchill in person. The institutional question hanging over the American delegation was whether a new president could operate the most complex diplomatic machinery in American history without having built any of it.

The answer, as the conference demonstrated, was that the machinery operated him as much as he operated it. Truman arrived at Potsdam accompanied by a State Department briefing apparatus that had been preparing the American position for months. Secretary of State James Byrnes, who had his own presidential ambitions and his own theories about negotiating with the Soviets, managed the American delegation's day-to-day tactics. Charles Bohlen, who had served as Roosevelt's interpreter at the earlier summits, carried the institutional memory of what had been promised at Tehran and Yalta and what those promises had meant in practice. The Joint Chiefs and their British counterparts, the Combined Chiefs of Staff, continued the military coordination that had run the coalition's campaigns. Truman's inexperience was real, and his diary entries during the conference record a man consciously learning the craft of summitry in public, but the decisions that emerged from the American side bore the unmistakable imprint of institutions that predated his presidency and would outlast it. The succession from Roosevelt to Truman, explored in the series' account of [Roosevelt's death and the transfer of wartime power](/2006/01/07/fdr-dies-april-12-1945/), became at Potsdam a live demonstration of how much of grand strategy was carried by staffs, briefing books, and standing committees rather than by the singular genius of the man at the head of the table.

Truman's relationship with his advisers also revealed the committee character of the American position. Byrnes favored a transactional style, trading concessions on reparations for Soviet cooperation elsewhere, and frequently negotiated language with Soviet Foreign Minister Vyacheslav Molotov outside the plenary sessions. Stimson, the Secretary of War, served as the channel for atomic information, delivering the Trinity results to Truman at Potsdam in the guarded memoranda that the War Department had prepared. Admiral William Leahy, the President's chief of staff, provided the military skepticism that balanced Byrnes's deal-making. The American delegation did not speak with one voice because no large delegation ever does, and the conference record shows Truman managing these internal differences in the open, sometimes reversing positions that Byrnes had advanced, sometimes endorsing them. Truman's education in foreign policy between April and July was intensive and largely invisible to the public. He inherited Roosevelt's advisers along with Roosevelt's office, and he used them systematically: daily briefings from the State Department, long sessions with Stimson on the military situation, consultations with former Secretary of State Cordell Hull's successors on the treaty pipeline, and a careful reading of the Yalta papers that taught him what the United States had promised and what the Soviet Union had delivered. His correspondence with Churchill during these months, voluminous and increasingly candid, gave him a private channel to the one Allied leader with continuous summit experience. By the time he boarded the aircraft for Potsdam, Truman had converted himself from a domestic politician with no foreign policy record into a president who could preside over a seventeen-day multilateral negotiation. The conversion was the work of the institutions around him, and it is the clearest small-scale demonstration of the article's thesis: the committee did not merely support the leader, it substantially created his competence.

This was not weakness. It was the Allied committee method applied to its own internal workings, the same method that had produced the coalition's wartime strategy, now producing its peace.

## Cecilienhof: The Machinery of the Last Summit

Cecilienhof Palace, the former residence of Crown Prince Wilhelm in the Neuer Garten park at Potsdam, had been chosen for reasons that mixed symbolism with logistics. The palace was large enough to house the plenary sessions around a single great round table, and its grounds could accommodate the security perimeters that three delegations required. The building itself carried the weight of the German imperial past, a deliberate reminder, in the Soviet staging, of the regime whose destruction had made the conference necessary. The delegations were housed separately: Truman in a villa on Kaiserstrasse that the Americans dubbed the Little White House, Churchill and later Attlee in nearby residences, Stalin in his own secured compound. Each leader's living quarters functioned as a secondary negotiating venue, hosting the bilateral conversations and informal dinners where much of the conference's real business was transacted.

The formal structure was formidable. Thirteen plenary sessions convened between July 17 and August 1, each attended by the three leaders with their foreign ministers and interpreters, each supported by committees of experts who met separately to draft language on reparations, boundaries, economic policy, and the dozen lesser questions on the agenda. Beneath the plenaries ran the foreign ministers' meetings, the drafting subcommittees, and the military staff conversations, a layered architecture inherited from the earlier summits and refined by practice. Some four thousand personnel supported the three delegations across the Potsdam area, handling communications, security, transport, translation, and the preparation of briefing papers. The scale was not pageantry. It was the physical expression of the house thesis: the Allied coalition fought and negotiated by committee, and the committee had grown so elaborate by 1945 that it could absorb shocks that would have shattered a command-centered system.

The conference's most extraordinary shock arrived from London. The British general election had been held on July 5, but the counting of overseas military ballots delayed the results, and Churchill attended the opening sessions of the conference not knowing whether he would remain prime minister. On July 26 the results were announced: a Labour landslide, 393 seats to the Conservatives' 213, the most decisive electoral repudiation of a wartime leader in British history. Churchill resigned the next day. The conference recessed. On July 28 Clement Attlee took Churchill's seat at the round table, accompanied by Ernest Bevin as the new Foreign Secretary, and the proceedings resumed as though the British delegation had merely changed its neckties. Attlee, who had served as deputy prime minister in the wartime coalition and knew the files intimately, and Bevin, the formidable former trade union leader, continued the British negotiating positions without visible discontinuity. The machinery absorbed the transition so completely that the conference's substantive output shows almost no trace of the change. No Axis equivalent existed or could have existed: no three-power Axis summit had ever convened, and no institutional framework stood ready to continue policy across a change of German, Italian, or Japanese leadership, because the Axis coalition had built no such framework.

## The Road to the Table: April to July 1945

The conference that opened on July 17 was not summoned in a vacuum. The three months between Germany's collapse and the Cecilienhof plenaries were filled with the incidents that set the negotiating positions, and the positions hardened as the incidents accumulated. Understanding Potsdam requires understanding the spring of 1945, because the leaders arrived at the round table carrying the grievances of April, May, and June.

The American-Soviet relationship had deteriorated sharply in the war's final weeks. On April 23, eleven days after Roosevelt's death, Truman received Molotov in the White House and delivered a blunt message: the United States expected the Soviet Union to carry out its Yalta agreements on Poland. The published accounts of the meeting, including Truman's memoirs and the memoranda of conversation, record a president speaking with a directness that startled the Soviet foreign minister. Molotov protested that he had never been talked to in such a manner. Truman replied that the agreements should be carried out. The exchange set the tone of the new presidency's Soviet policy: less personal, less patient, and more insistent on textual compliance than Roosevelt's approach had been. It did not change Soviet behavior in Poland, but it established that the American seat at Potsdam would be occupied by a man who treated Yalta's language as binding commitment rather than as diplomatic aspiration.

The Polish crisis of the spring supplied the substance behind the tone. The Soviet-backed Lublin government was consolidating its control, the London Poles were being marginalized, and in March the Soviet security services arrested sixteen leaders of the Polish underground, luring them to negotiations and then seizing them. Their trial in Moscow in June, the so-called Trial of the Sixteen, produced sentences that outraged Western opinion and convinced many in Washington and London that the Yalta electoral promises were being systematically voided. Truman dispatched Harry Hopkins, Roosevelt's closest adviser, to Moscow in late May for direct conversations with Stalin. The Hopkins mission, running from May 26 to June 6, salvaged enough of the Polish negotiation to permit the formation of the Provisional Government of National Unity later in June, and it secured Stalin's confirmation that the Soviet Union would attend the San Francisco conference's closing stages. But the mission's success was procedural rather than substantive: it kept the diplomatic process alive without altering the distribution of power in Poland.

Churchill, meanwhile, was sounding alarms that Truman was not yet ready to act upon. On May 12, four days after the German surrender, Churchill cabled Truman warning that an iron curtain was descending across Europe, that the Soviets were establishing puppet regimes, and that the Western armies should not withdraw from their advanced positions until the political questions were settled. The telegram was the first draft of the argument Churchill would make famous the following year, but in May 1945 it found a president still organizing his administration and still hoping that personal negotiation might succeed where cables had failed. Truman's reply was cautious. The American armies did withdraw, in early July, from the parts of Saxony, Thuringia, and Mecklenburg that lay within the agreed Soviet zone, fulfilling the zonal bargain in a gesture of good faith. The withdrawal removed the one piece of military leverage the Western powers held over the Soviet position in Eastern Europe, and it did so before the conference that was supposed to settle Eastern Europe's future.

The San Francisco conference, which ran from April 25 to June 26 and produced the United Nations Charter, overlapped this deterioration and partially masked it. The Charter's signing on June 26 was the Yalta institutional project reaching completion, and the Big Three could still cooperate on the design of the new organization even as they diverged on the fate of the countries it was meant to protect. By the time the delegations traveled to Potsdam, the pattern was set: cooperation on machinery, deadlock on substance. The conference would reproduce that pattern across seventeen days.

## The Trinity Test Enters the Conference Room

The atomic bomb was the most important subject never placed on the Potsdam agenda, and its invisible presence shaped the conference more powerfully than most of the items that were formally discussed. The sequence of information is worth reconstructing precisely, because it explains both what Truman believed he possessed and what Stalin already knew. The Trinity device detonated at 5:29 a.m. Mountain Time on July 16. Reports traveled through War Department channels to Stimson, who was already at Potsdam, and Stimson carried the news to Truman in a series of guarded conversations on July 16, 17, and 18. The final technical assessment, delivered in a memorandum that scholars of the war's end have examined with exceptional care, reported that the test had exceeded expectations, with an estimated yield of roughly twenty-one kilotons. Truman's diary records his reaction in the plain language of a man grasping a new category of power: the weapon was, in his private assessment, the most terrible instrument ever devised, and it might now end the war.

Truman's handling of the information at the conference table was deliberate to the point of theatricality. On July 24, after a plenary session, he approached Stalin during the informal milling that followed and mentioned, in a studiedly casual tone, that the United States had developed a new weapon of unusual destructive force. He did not say the word atomic. He did not describe the Trinity test. He offered no technical detail. Stalin's response was equally studied: he expressed the hope that the Americans would make good use of the weapon against Japan, and the conversation moved on. Truman and Churchill, who had been briefed in advance about the approach, interpreted Stalin's casualness as evidence that he had not grasped the weapon's significance. They were wrong, and the error illuminates the asymmetry of the conference's intelligence picture. Soviet intelligence had been reporting on the Manhattan Project since 1943, with the physicist Klaus Fuchs among the sources passing detailed technical information from inside the American program. Stalin's casual response was not ignorance but discipline: he gave away nothing, confirmed nothing, and returned to Moscow's existing policy, which was to accelerate the Soviet Union's own atomic program while revealing no anxiety to the Americans.

The bomb's effect on the negotiations was real but indirect, operating through Truman's confidence rather than through explicit bargaining. Before Trinity, the American position on several issues, particularly Soviet entry into the Pacific war, had been shaped by the assumption that Soviet help might be necessary to end the war without catastrophic American casualties. After Trinity, Truman's need for Soviet entry diminished, though it did not vanish: the Joint Chiefs still valued a Soviet attack on the Kwantung Army in Manchuria as a means of pinning Japanese forces, and the political value of Soviet belligerency in legitimizing the postwar settlement in Asia remained significant. The shift was one of degree rather than reversal, and the conference record shows Truman pressing Stalin to confirm the August entry date even after the Trinity news arrived. What changed was the American willingness to pay a high diplomatic price for Soviet cooperation. Issues on which the Americans might previously have conceded ground to secure Soviet goodwill, notably the terms of the Pacific settlement and the handling of Eastern Europe, were now negotiated from a position of reduced dependence. Stalin, for his part, understood that the weapon gave the Americans a postwar advantage that no conference language could neutralize, and his urgency in entering the Pacific war before Japan's surrender reflected his determination to secure the territorial gains promised at Yalta while the window remained open.

### What did Truman know about the atomic bomb when Potsdam opened?

When the conference opened on July 17, Truman knew from Stimson's guarded briefings that the Trinity test had succeeded the previous day, with an estimated yield of roughly twenty-one kilotons. He understood that combat weapons were nearing readiness. He had not yet received the final technical assessment, which arrived during the conference's first days.

The historiographical debate over Truman's atomic diplomacy at Potsdam remains one of the conference's most contested questions, and it requires named positions. David Reynolds, in his study of summit diplomacy, treats the bomb as a background condition that stiffened Truman's posture without becoming a bargaining instrument, emphasizing that the weapon was never formally introduced into the negotiations. Charles Mee's narrative account stresses the psychological dimension: Truman's awareness of the weapon gave a new president, insecure in Stalin's company, a private source of confidence that steadied his performance at the table. Michael Beschloss, examining the German settlement debates, argues that the bomb's principal effect was to reduce American interest in extracting Soviet cooperation through concessions, particularly on reparations and Eastern Europe. The revisionist position, associated with historians who argue that Truman wielded the bomb as diplomatic leverage against Moscow, finds limited support in the conference record itself: the plenary minutes show no instance of Truman linking the weapon to a negotiating demand. The adjudication favors Reynolds and Beschloss. The bomb altered the American delegation's reservation price across the agenda, which is a real and significant effect, but it did not function as a chip placed on the table, because Truman never placed it there.

## Germany Carved: Zones, Reparations, and Denazification

The German settlement was the conference's largest single block of business, and its outputs became the legal foundation of the occupation. The four zones, American, British, Soviet, and French, were confirmed along the boundaries drawn by the European Advisory Commission, with Berlin divided into four sectors despite lying deep inside the Soviet zone. The Allied Control Council was established as the joint governing authority, with the principle that Germany would be treated as a single economic unit even as it was administered in zones. France, which had not been invited to Potsdam and whose leader Charles de Gaulle nursed the exclusion as a grievance, nevertheless received its zone, carved from territory originally allocated to Britain and America, and a seat on the Control Council. The arrangement reflected Churchill's insistence that France be restored as a great power, and it demonstrated the committee's capacity to integrate a fourth occupying authority into machinery designed by three.

Denazification and the political principles of the occupation were set out in language that combined moral clarity with administrative vagueness. The protocol committed the occupiers to the destruction of the Nazi Party and its affiliated organizations, the repeal of Nazi legislation, the removal of active Nazis from public office, and the punishment of war criminals. The economic principles were more specific and more contentious: German industry was to be decentralized, the production of arms and munitions prohibited, and the German economy restructured toward agriculture and peaceful domestic industry. The famous Level of Industry negotiations, which would fix the specific ceilings on German industrial capacity, were deferred to subsequent Allied negotiation, and the deferral was itself a decision. By agreeing on principles while postponing numbers, the conference created a framework that each occupying power would interpret according to its own interests, a pattern that would characterize the entire occupation.

Reparations produced the conference's hardest bargaining and its most consequential compromise. The Soviet Union, whose territory had suffered destruction on a scale the Western Allies could barely comprehend, demanded massive transfers of German industrial equipment and production. The Western powers, mindful of the economic collapse that had followed the reparations regime after the previous war, resisted any fixed total. The formula that Byrnes negotiated with Molotov became the conference's signature economic achievement: the Soviet Union would take reparations from its own occupation zone, plus ten percent of the industrial capital equipment in the Western zones without compensation, plus an additional fifteen percent in exchange for deliveries of food, coal, and other commodities from the Soviet zone to the west. The twenty-five percent figure from the Western zones, half of it paid for in goods, was designed to keep Germany functioning as an economic unit while satisfying Soviet claims. In practice, the arrangement broke down almost immediately. The Soviets stripped their zone far more aggressively than the protocol's language contemplated, the Western powers grew reluctant to deliver equipment as the political climate chilled, and the food-for-machinery exchange never operated as designed. The reparations compromise illustrates the conference's characteristic pattern: the committee could produce language that all parties could sign, but the language papered over incompatible economic philosophies that implementation would expose.

### Why did Potsdam give the Soviet Union twenty-five percent of Western reparations?

The twenty-five percent figure bought Soviet assent to the principle that each power would take reparations primarily from its own zone. Ten percent of Western-zone industrial equipment would transfer without compensation; fifteen percent in exchange for Soviet food and coal. The Western delegations accepted the transfer to secure Soviet agreement on Germany's treatment as a single economic unit.

The Level of Industry question, deferred at Potsdam to subsequent negotiation, deserves attention because the deferral shaped the occupation's economic history. The protocol committed the occupiers to restructuring German industry toward peaceful production and to eliminating war-making capacity, but the specific ceilings on steel production, machine tools, chemicals, and other categories were left for the Allied Control Council to determine. The subsequent negotiations produced the Level of Industry Plan of March 1946, which set German steel capacity at roughly a quarter of its prewar level and scheduled the dismantling of hundreds of plants for reparations. The plan proved unworkable: it condemned the Western zones to economic stagnation, created a running dispute with the French over the Saar and the Ruhr, and was progressively revised upward as the Cold War made West German economic recovery a strategic necessity. Potsdam's decision to agree on principles and postpone numbers thus bought a signature at the cost of a decade of industrial controversy.

The German question also encompassed the war crimes framework that would become the Nuremberg trials. The conference approved the principle of an international military tribunal and agreed that the first list of major war criminals would be published, committing the four powers to a judicial process rather than to summary punishment. The London Charter establishing the International Military Tribunal was signed on August 8, days after the conference closed, and the trials opened at Nuremberg in November, with verdicts delivered the following October. The decision to try rather than simply execute the surviving Nazi leadership was not a foregone conclusion in the summer of 1945; powerful voices in all three governments had favored swifter and less legalistic retribution. Potsdam's endorsement of the tribunal framework, building on the Moscow Declaration of 1943 on war crimes, locked the coalition into the judicial path and created the institutional precedent for international criminal law. The trials' subsequent proceedings, and the body of legal principle they generated, trace their authority directly to the language agreed at Cecilienhof.

## The Oder-Neisse Line and the Twelve Million

The Polish-German border decision was the conference's most fateful territorial act, and its consequences are still legible on the map of Europe. The question before the leaders was where to draw Poland's western frontier along the rivers Oder and Neisse, and the choice between the eastern and western branches of the Neisse determined the fate of millions of people. The Soviet position, which Poland's communist-led government supported, favored the western Neisse, the more westerly of the river's branches, which would transfer to Poland the whole of Silesia and a substantial belt of territory east of the Oder. The British and American delegations had reservations: Churchill in particular worried about the scale of the population transfer that the western line would require, and about the moral and practical implications of moving millions of Germans westward. But the Soviet position prevailed in practice. The protocol's language placed the territories east of the Oder and the western Neisse under Polish administration pending the final peace settlement, and the qualifier about final delimitation did not alter the facts created on the ground.

The population transfer that followed was among the largest forced migrations in recorded history. Approximately twelve million Germans were expelled from the territories east of the Oder-Neisse line, from Czechoslovakia, and from other parts of Eastern Europe between 1945 and 1949, in movements that the protocol described with the phrase "orderly and humane" transfer. The reality bore little resemblance to the phrase. The expulsions began in the chaotic months before the conference, as Red Army advances and the collapse of German administration drove millions westward in panic, and continued after Potsdam under the authority of the new Polish and Czechoslovak governments. The death toll remains contested among historians: the West German government's documentation of the 1950s placed it above two million, while Rüdiger Overmans's 1994 archival study estimated roughly half a million deaths. The precise figure is unlikely ever to be settled, because the records of the chaotic first months are fragmentary and because the political stakes of the number have distorted its study. What is not disputed is that the transfer involved mass suffering on an enormous scale, including deaths from exposure, starvation, disease, and violence during the treks westward, and that the conference's language authorizing the transfer made the three powers complicit in its execution.

The border decision also settled the ethnic composition of postwar Poland in a single stroke. The Poland that emerged from Potsdam had lost its eastern territories to the Soviet Union, as agreed at the earlier summits, and gained the German lands to the west, producing a state that was more ethnically homogeneous than any Poland in modern history. Millions of Poles were themselves resettled from the lost eastern regions into the newly acquired western territories, in a vast exchange of populations that the conference's territorial decisions set in motion. The Oder-Neisse line, drawn as a provisional administrative boundary pending a peace treaty that would not come for decades, became an exceptionally durable border in Europe. The Two Plus Four settlement of 1990, which finally provided the formal peace-settlement confirmation the Potsdam language had deferred, came a decade and a half before this article's publication date and ratified a frontier that had been functioning as permanent for forty-five years.

### Why did the western Neisse rather than the eastern Neisse become the border?

The western Neisse prevailed because the Soviet delegation insisted upon it and the Western delegations could not impose an easterly line. The western branch compensated Poland for eastern territories annexed by Moscow and created a Polish state dependent on Soviet support to hold the lands. With Polish authorities already administering the territories, the conference ratified the facts on the ground.

## Eastern Europe: The Agreement That Was Not Made

On the political future of Eastern Europe, Potsdam produced language rather than settlement, and the language revealed the limits of what the committee could accomplish. The conference reaffirmed the Yalta Declaration on Liberated Europe, with its promises of broadly representative governments and free elections, and recognized the Polish Provisional Government of National Unity that had been formed in June. The Western delegations secured the reaffirmation of the electoral principle; the Soviet delegation secured the recognition of governments that were already, in Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, and Hungary, under communist control or moving decisively in that direction. Both sides could claim that the protocol supported their position, which was precisely the problem. The language was designed to be signed, not to be enforced.

The Council of Foreign Ministers was the conference's institutional answer to the problem of unfinished business. Composed of the foreign ministers of the five principal Allied powers, the United States, Britain, the Soviet Union, France, and China, the Council was charged with preparing the peace treaties for Italy, Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary, and Finland, and with addressing the other territorial questions the plenary sessions could not resolve. Its first meeting convened in London that September. The creation of the Council reflected the committee method's characteristic response to deadlock: when the principals could not agree, they created a standing body to continue the negotiation. The method had served the coalition well during the war, when the Combined Chiefs of Staff had converted strategic disagreements into staff studies and then into plans. Applied to the political settlement of Europe, it produced years of foreign ministers' meetings that registered the deepening division of the continent without arresting it. The Council's existence testified to the coalition's institutional reflex; its record testified to the limits of institutions when the underlying distribution of power no longer supported agreement.

The treatment of the smaller Axis satellites illustrated the same pattern. Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary, and Finland were to conclude armistices and eventually peace treaties, with the Council of Foreign Ministers preparing the terms. Italy, which had changed sides in 1943 and declared war on Japan that July in a bid for better treatment, was promised a peace treaty that would recognize its contribution to the Allied cause, though the terms would be hard. The Italian colonies in Africa, Libya, Eritrea, and Somaliland, were left for the Council to dispose of, a deferral that reflected the victors' competing imperial interests as much as their indecision. In each case the conference produced a procedure rather than a result, and the procedures would operate in a political climate that grew steadily colder.

Austria received more favorable treatment, and the contrast with Germany is instructive. The Moscow Declaration of October 1943 had pronounced Austria the first victim of Nazi aggression rather than a defeated enemy, and Potsdam confirmed the four-zone occupation of Austria with Vienna divided into sectors, alongside the commitment to Austrian independence. The Austrian settlement worked. The four powers cooperated in Austria more successfully than in Germany, the occupation ended with the State Treaty of 1955, and Austria emerged as a neutral independent state. The difference was not in the conference's language, which was similar in both cases, but in the strategic stakes: Austria mattered less to Soviet security planning than Germany did, and the lower stakes permitted the cooperation that the German settlement could not sustain. The comparison demonstrates that the committee's machinery functioned best where the underlying interests of the powers were compatible, and broke down where they were not, a finding that qualifies the house thesis without refuting it.

The mechanics of the Polish recognition at Potsdam illustrate how the committee converted Soviet facts into Allied language. The Provisional Government of National Unity, formed in Warsaw in late June with a handful of non-communist figures added to the Lublin core, was recognized by the conference as Poland's government, and the London government-in-exile, which had fought alongside the Western Allies for six years, was informed that its useful life was over. The Western delegations secured the reaffirmation of the Yalta pledge of free elections, and the Polish representatives at Potsdam repeated the promise. The promise was kept in form and voided in substance: the January 1947 election was conducted under conditions of intimidation, fraud, and the suppression of the opposition, producing the communist monopoly the Yalta language had been written to prevent. The conference's Polish paragraphs thus became the template for the Eastern European pattern, Western language and Soviet reality, signed together at the same table.

## The Pacific Endgame and the Potsdam Declaration

The Pacific war occupied a smaller share of the plenary agenda than the German settlement, but the conference's Pacific decisions shaped the war's final weeks. Stalin reconfirmed the Soviet commitment to enter the war against Japan, and the military staffs coordinated the timing of the Soviet offensive against the Japanese Kwantung Army in Manchuria with the broader Allied campaign. The Soviet declaration of war came on August 8, with operations beginning the next day, fulfilling the Yalta timetable and delivering the strategic shock that, combined with the atomic bombings, broke the deadlock in Tokyo. The military coordination of the Soviet entry was handled through the Combined Chiefs of Staff channels running alongside the plenaries. The Soviet General Staff's planning for the Manchurian offensive, an operation involving over a million and a half men, three fronts, and the Trans-Siberian logistical pipeline, was already far advanced when the conference opened, and the staff conversations at Potsdam synchronized its timing with the American Pacific campaign. The American chiefs wanted the Soviet attack to coincide with the intensifying air and naval campaign against the home islands, pinning the Kwantung Army in Manchuria and preventing any transfer of Japanese forces. Stalin confirmed the early-August timetable, and the declaration of war on August 8, with operations beginning the next day, fulfilled the commitment with the precision of a staff plan executed on schedule. The coordination was the committee's military machinery functioning exactly as designed, even as the political machinery around it was beginning to divide.

The conference also addressed the disposition of former Japanese territories: Korea was to be divided at the thirty-eighth parallel into Soviet and American zones of responsibility, Taiwan was confirmed for return to China in accordance with the Cairo Declaration, and the arrangements for Indochina and Malaya were settled among the Western powers. These decisions, taken in a few sessions amid the press of European business, drew the lines that would define postwar Asia.

The Potsdam Declaration of July 26 was the conference's public act on the Pacific war, and its drafting history reveals the committee method operating under acute time pressure. The declaration was issued in the names of Truman, Churchill, and Chiang Kai-shek, the Chinese leader signing remotely, and it was pointedly not signed by the Soviet Union, which was not yet at war with Japan. Its terms called for the unconditional surrender of the Japanese armed forces, with the warning that Japan faced "prompt and utter destruction" if it refused. The declaration's most carefully negotiated sentence concerned the Japanese Emperor: the document did not explicitly guarantee the imperial institution, but it provided that the ultimate form of Japan's government would be established by the freely expressed will of the Japanese people, language that the American drafters understood as leaving room for the Emperor's retention under Allied authority. Whether the Japanese leadership understood it the same way became one of the war's most consequential questions of interpretation.

The Japanese response arrived through the tangled channels of Tokyo's divided government. Prime Minister Kantaro Suzuki, responding to press questions about the declaration, used the word mokusatsu, a term whose meaning ranges across "ignore," "treat with silent contempt," and "withhold comment." The Allied press and intelligence services rendered it as rejection, and the rejection reading hardened into the operative interpretation in Washington. Foreign Minister Shigenori Togo's subsequent cables suggest that the Japanese leadership intended something closer to deliberate noncommittal pending further consideration, but the distinction was lost in the transmission. The mokusatsu episode has generated its own scholarly literature, with historians debating whether a more precise translation might have altered the sequence of August. The weight of the evidence suggests that the translation question, while genuinely interesting, did not determine the outcome: the Japanese Supreme Council remained deadlocked between the peace faction and the military hardliners regardless of the declaration's wording, and the impasse was broken only by the twin shocks of the atomic bombings and Soviet entry. The declaration's terms, and Japan's response to them, belong to the series' account of [the atomic bombings that followed within days of the conference's close](/2005/12/16/hiroshima-nagasaki-august-1945/), which examines the missions and their consequences in full.

### What did the Potsdam Declaration actually demand of Japan?

The declaration demanded the unconditional surrender of the Japanese armed forces, the elimination of militarist authority, Allied occupation of designated points, and a peacefully inclined government chosen by the freely expressed will of the Japanese people. It threatened prompt and utter destruction upon refusal. The Emperor's fate was left open for Allied determination.

The declaration's drafting also illustrated the conference's handling of absent parties. Chiang Kai-shek's remote signature was secured by cable, a procedural improvisation that reflected China's nominal great-power status and its actual marginalization from the conference's proceedings. The Soviet Union's absence from the declaration was a calculated omission: Stalin did not wish to provoke Japan before the Red Army was ready to strike, and Truman did not wish to complicate the declaration's issuance by waiting for Soviet belligerency. The result was a document that spoke for the coalition's Pacific war effort while excluding the power whose entry would prove decisive. The irony was not lost on the participants, and it foreshadowed the postwar pattern in which the coalition's Asian and European policies would diverge.

## The Personalities at the Round Table

The conference's institutional machinery was formidable, but the men operating it were not interchangeable parts, and their individual styles left marks on the proceedings. Truman's style at the table was the style of a presiding officer rather than a protagonist. He opened the plenaries, recognized speakers, summarized agreements, and intervened decisively when the drafting deadlocked, but he rarely engaged in the extended argumentative exchanges that had characterized Roosevelt's performances. Participants described a president who listened carefully, consulted his advisers openly, and decided quickly once the positions were clear. The openness was itself a method: where Roosevelt had sometimes concealed his intentions from his own delegation, Truman's visible reliance on Byrnes, Leahy, and Bohlen reassured the American staff that their work would be used. Stalin, accustomed to Roosevelt's more theatrical diplomacy, found the new president harder to read precisely because there was less performance to read.

Stalin's style had changed across the three summits, and the change measured his shifting position. At Tehran he had been the petitioner on the second front, pressing his allies with a bluntness born of military need. At Yalta he had been the confident arbiter, conceding language on Poland because his armies made the language irrelevant. At Potsdam he was the patient consolidator, defending positions already won and yielding only where the concession cost him nothing. His interventions were short, concrete, and final: he stated the Soviet position, listened to the objections, and restated the position. The technique was enormously effective against opponents who needed agreement more than he did. His one visible miscalculation, if it can be called that, was the assumption that Attlee's arrival would soften the British position; Bevin's firmness on the German economic questions corrected the impression within days.

Churchill's truncated participation gave the conference a study in contrasts. In the nine days he attended, he performed the role he had perfected across the war: the eloquent advocate, the historical memory of the coalition, the leader who could frame a boundary dispute as a question of civilization. His exchanges with Stalin over the Polish border were the plenaries' most vivid passages, and his warnings about the scale of the population transfer were the conference's most prescient. Then he was gone, and the contrast with his successors was instructive. Attlee brought no rhetoric to the table and needed none: his authority derived from the electoral mandate and from his mastery of the briefs, and he conducted the British side with a quiet efficiency that the plenary minutes record without color but also without error. Bevin, the trade unionist turned Foreign Secretary, surprised the Soviet delegation with the toughness of his positions and the speed of his command of the files. The British transition thus demonstrated something about democratic leadership that the conference's institutional story required: the system produced competent successors, not merely replacements.

The supporting cast shaped the outcomes as much as the principals. Byrnes, managing the American delegation's tactics, favored the transactional style that produced the reparations formula: identify each side's price, construct the package, close the deal. His bilateral sessions with Molotov were the conference's real negotiating engine, and his willingness to trade across issues dismayed those in the American delegation who preferred a more principled stand on Eastern Europe. Molotov, Stalin's indispensable instrument, combined granite stubbornness on instructed positions with flexibility on the margins where Moscow permitted it; his late-night sessions with Byrnes were contests of endurance that both men understood as the serious business beneath the plenary theater. Bohlen and Pavlov, the interpreters, were the conference's memory: Bohlen's notes preserve the American record of every critical exchange, and the triangulation of the two interpreters' accounts remains the historian's best tool for reconstructing what was actually said. The personalities mattered, but they mattered inside the machinery, not above it.

## Three Summits, One Vanishing Table

Potsdam was the third of the Big Three summits, and its character can only be understood against the two that preceded it. The Tehran Conference of November and December 1943 had been the committee's strategic workshop: the agenda was dominated by the second front, the leaders were planning how to win the war, and the personal dynamics reflected Roosevelt's courtship of Stalin and his deliberate distancing from Churchill. The account of [the Tehran summit and its second-front bargain](/2006/03/25/tehran-conference-november-1943-second-front/) shows a conference where military strategy crowded out political settlement, and where the committee's machinery was still being built. Yalta, in February 1945, was the committee's political summit: the war was nearly won, the agenda was dominated by the shape of the peace, and the personal dynamics reflected Roosevelt's declining health, Churchill's diminished leverage, and Stalin's maximum strength. The series' reconstruction of [the Yalta negotiations and their postwar consequences](/2006/01/29/yalta-conference-february-1945-postwar-division/) shows a conference where the committee produced its most ambitious institutional architecture, the United Nations and the German zone system, alongside its most contested political compromises.

Potsdam differed from both predecessors in the composition of the table itself. At Tehran and Yalta, the three seats had been occupied by the same three men, Franklin Roosevelt, Winston Churchill, and Joseph Stalin, and the continuity of personalities had permitted a continuity of personal diplomacy. Roosevelt's death removed the American seat's occupant and replaced him with a man who had never practiced summitry. The British election removed the British seat's occupant in the middle of the proceedings and replaced him with a man whose entire political identity was bound up in domestic reconstruction rather than imperial strategy. Only Stalin remained, the single constant across all three summits, negotiating in 1945 with the accumulated knowledge of how both his counterparts' predecessors had bargained. The personal-dynamics shift was not merely biographical color. It altered the substance of the negotiations, because Truman's inexperience made him more dependent on his briefing apparatus than Roosevelt had been, and because Attlee's arrival removed from the British delegation the one figure whose personal authority had occasionally checked Stalin.

The institutional continuity across the three summits is the deeper story, and it is where the house thesis finds its strongest confirmation. The same foreign ministries prepared the briefing books. The same Combined Chiefs of Staff managed the military coordination. The same interpreters, Bohlen for the Americans and Pavlov for the Soviets, sat behind the same leaders' chairs, providing the continuity of record-keeping that made the plenary minutes a coherent archive across three conferences. The British Foreign Office briefs that Churchill carried into the opening sessions were the briefs that Attlee and Bevin carried out of the closing sessions, with only the names on the covering memoranda changed. The Soviet Ministry of Foreign Affairs operated without any discontinuity at all. The committee had become, by Potsdam, a transnational institution in its own right, capable of conducting the business of the coalition across the replacement of two of its three principals. The Axis powers, which had never built an equivalent institution, could not have survived such a transition, because there was nothing to survive it with. Germany's decision-making died with Hitler's authority, Italy's with Mussolini's, Japan's with the imperial system's paralysis. The Allied coalition's decision-making outlived Roosevelt and survived Churchill's electoral defeat, and that difference was not incidental to the war's outcome. It was among its principal causes.

## The Potsdam Conference in Session: Seventeen Days of Plenaries

The conference's day-to-day record shows the committee method operating at full extension, and the details of the proceedings illuminate how the outputs were actually produced. The first plenary convened on the afternoon of July 17, and its opening moments set a tone of practiced collegiality. Stalin proposed that Truman preside over the sessions, a courtesy that acknowledged the new president's status while costing the Soviet leader nothing. The leaders then approved the agenda, which ran from the political and economic principles for Germany through Poland, reparations, the Pacific war, and the long list of secondary questions. The agenda's order was itself a negotiation: the German settlement came first because all three powers needed the occupation machinery functioning, and the contentious items were placed where the drafting committees would have time to work on them between sessions.

The early plenaries moved through the German political principles with relative speed. Denazification, demilitarization, the repeal of Nazi law, and the democratization of German political life commanded broad agreement in principle, and the drafting committees converted the agreement into protocol language without major crisis. The economic principles proved harder. The American delegation pressed for the treatment of Germany as a single economic unit, with common policies on industry, agriculture, and trade across the zones. The Soviet delegation resisted provisions that would limit its freedom of action in the eastern zone. The compromise language papered the difference: the protocol proclaimed economic unity while leaving each zonal commander substantial discretion, a formula that permitted signature and guaranteed future dispute.

Reparations deadlocked the conference in its middle days and produced the bilateral negotiations that became the proceedings' real engine. The plenary sessions could not bridge the gap between the Soviet demand for massive transfers and the Western resistance to any fixed total, so the issue moved to private meetings between Byrnes and Molotov. These bilaterals, conducted outside the formal record but reported back to the plenaries, were where the ten-plus-fifteen formula was hammered out in the sessions of July 23 and 24. The pattern was characteristic of the summit's working method: the plenaries ratified what the bilaterals negotiated, and the bilaterals succeeded because the two foreign ministers could trade concessions across issues in ways the full table could not. The reparations bargain was linked, in the give and take, to understandings on Poland and on the Pacific, though the precise linkages were never written down.

The conference's social dimension should not be discounted as mere pageantry. The leaders dined together, toured the ruined capital under escort, and conducted the informal conversations in which positions were tested before being committed to the record. Churchill, in the sessions he attended, deployed the full range of his conversational powers, engaging Stalin in long exchanges about the Polish border and the German future that the minutes record only in summary. Truman hosted a dinner for the principals at the Little White House. These occasions were working sessions disguised as hospitality, and the participants understood them as such. The committee's business was transacted as much in the intervals as in the plenaries.

July 24 brought the atomic aside that has overshadowed much of the conference's substantive record. After the plenary adjourned, Truman delivered his casual remark to Stalin about the new weapon, received the casual reply, and returned to the German and Polish questions as though nothing of consequence had passed between them. The disproportion between the remark's historical weight and its procedural insignificance is itself instructive: the most consequential military secret of the war entered the diplomatic record as small talk, because the conference had no procedure for handling it and neither leader wanted to create one.

The last plenary Churchill attended convened on July 25. The leaders reviewed the state of the drafting, noted the unresolved items, and agreed to recess for two days. None of the published records indicates that Churchill expected the recess to end his participation; the British delegation's papers for the resumed sessions were prepared on the assumption of his return. The recess announcement was therefore issued as a routine adjournment, and its transformation into a change of government was accomplished in London while the delegations waited.

The resumed conference opened on July 28 with Attlee in Churchill's chair and Bevin beside him. The Soviet delegation tested the new British team promptly, probing for softness on the Polish border and reparations, and found none. Bevin's interventions were, if anything, firmer than Churchill's had been, particularly on the economic treatment of Germany, and the plenary minutes show the new Foreign Secretary mastering the files with impressive speed. The remaining days were consumed by the final drafting: the precise language of the protocol's German sections, the Polish border paragraphs, the war crimes commitment, and the secondary agenda. The protocol was signed on August 1. The communiqué, the public document summarizing the conference's work, was issued on August 2. The delegations dispersed, and the most intensively staffed diplomatic gathering in history to that date dissolved into the files. The protocol's formal title, the Protocol of the Proceedings of the Berlin Conference, reflected the fiction that the meeting had been a Berlin conference rather than a Potsdam one, a small diplomatic courtesy to the ruined capital. Its public reception was muted: the communiqué's dense administrative language offered little drama for the press, and the atomic news from Hiroshima four days later erased Potsdam from the front pages almost entirely. The conference that had settled the occupation of Germany, the Polish border, and the Pacific endgame was thus overshadowed within a week by the weapon whose shadow had hung over its proceedings.

## The Secondary Agenda: Fleets, Colonies, and Unfinished Business

Beyond the great questions of Germany, Poland, and the Pacific, the protocol settled a long inventory of lesser matters, and the inventory reveals the conference's reach. The disposition of the surviving German surface fleet was divided among the three naval powers, with the remaining warships allocated by agreement and the U-boat arm ordered destroyed; the German merchant marine was similarly divided, its vessels distributed to the victors as partial compensation for wartime shipping losses. Northern East Prussia, with the city of Königsberg, was transferred to the Soviet Union pending the final peace treaty, a territorial award that recognized the scale of the Soviet war effort and gave Moscow its long-sought ice-free Baltic outlet. German external assets in the neutral countries, the holdings in Switzerland, Sweden, Spain, Portugal, and Argentina that had financed the Reich's wartime trade, were claimed for the reparations account, committing the victors to years of negotiation with the neutrals.

Italy received the conference's most carefully calibrated secondary treatment. The protocol promised that the Council of Foreign Ministers would prepare a peace treaty recognizing Italy's break with Germany and its contribution to the Allied cause, a formula that acknowledged the 1943 armistice without granting Italy full allied status. Italy had declared war on Japan on July 15, two days before the conference opened, in a transparent bid to qualify for the United Nations and for better treaty terms. The Italian colonies in Africa were deferred to the Council's disposition, a postponement that reflected the competing imperial interests of Britain and France as much as indecision about the territories themselves. Tangier was restored to international administration. Spain was addressed in a protocol statement that withheld support for the Franco regime's admission to the United Nations, a moral gesture that cost the victors nothing and gave the Spanish opposition a measure of encouragement.

The Ruhr, the industrial heartland whose coal and steel had powered two German war efforts, was the subject of proposals for international control that the conference discussed without resolving. The British delegation favored some form of internationalization; the Americans were sympathetic; the Soviets were uninterested in arrangements that would limit their reparations claims. The question was deferred, and its deferral foreshadowed the postwar struggle over the Ruhr's status that would run through the late 1940s. Korea's future was treated in the same provisional spirit. The conference endorsed the principle of an eventual independent and unified Korea, and the practical division of the peninsula at the thirty-eighth parallel into Soviet and American zones of responsibility was worked out by American planners in Washington in the days after the conference closed, when two young officers, Dean Rusk and Charles Bonesteel, drew the line on a National Geographic map in the middle of the night. The line was meant as a temporary military convenience. Like so many of Potsdam's provisional arrangements, it became permanent.

The war criminals commitment deserves emphasis among the secondary items because its consequences outlived all the others. The protocol approved the establishment of an international military tribunal and provided that the first list of major war criminals would be published, locking the four powers into the judicial path. The London Charter, signed on August 8, created the International Military Tribunal; the trials opened at Nuremberg in November; the verdicts came the following October. The decision to try the surviving Nazi leadership rather than subjecting them to summary execution was contested within all three governments during the summer of 1945, and Potsdam's endorsement settled the argument in favor of law. The tribunal's proceedings generated the body of legal principle, crimes against humanity, the rejection of superior orders as a defense, individual responsibility for aggressive war, that became the foundation of international criminal law. Of all the conference's outputs, the Nuremberg framework is the one whose authority has grown most steadily in the decades since.

## The Complication: Committee Success, Human Cost

The house thesis celebrates the Allied committee system, and Potsdam is its showcase, but the showcase has a shadow that no honest account can avoid. The committee's success in maintaining coalition unity and producing a comprehensive settlement came at costs that were counted in millions of displaced persons and in the political subjugation of Eastern Europe. The complication is not a footnote to the Potsdam story. It is central to it, because the same institutional machinery that absorbed two leadership transitions also ratified arrangements whose human consequences were catastrophic.

The expulsions stand first in any honest accounting. The conference's authorization of the "orderly and humane" transfer of Germans from Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Hungary provided the legal cover for one of the largest forced population movements in European history. The qualifier "orderly and humane" was a fiction that the drafters must have known to be a fiction: the transfers were already underway in conditions of chaos and violence, and no language agreed at Cecilienhof could have made them otherwise. The Western leaders who signed the protocol understood, at least in general terms, what the transfer would entail. Churchill had warned explicitly about the scale of the movement. The American delegation had reports of the conditions in the eastern territories. They signed anyway, because the alternative was to reopen the border question that the Soviet Union considered settled, and because the coalition's unity was judged to require the concession. The committee method, which excelled at converting disagreement into signable language, here converted a foreseeable humanitarian catastrophe into an administrative procedure. The language was the committee's product. The suffering was the language's consequence.

Eastern Europe presents the harder and more contested case. The Potsdam arrangements for Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary, and the other states of the Soviet sphere substantially departed from the principles of the Yalta Declaration on Liberated Europe, and the departure was visible to the participants. The defenders of the Potsdam record, a position developed most fully in the post-Cold War scholarship, argue that the arrangements were an accommodation to military reality rather than a betrayal of principle. The Red Army occupied Eastern Europe. No Western army stood in a position to contest the occupation. No Western government was prepared to threaten war against the Soviet Union in the summer of 1945 to enforce electoral promises in Warsaw or Bucharest. On this reading, Potsdam did not cause the communist takeover of Eastern Europe; it registered a takeover that force had already accomplished, and the language of the protocol was the most that diplomacy could extract from the situation.

The critics answer that registration is itself a choice, and that the choice had consequences. By recognizing the Lublin government in Poland, by accepting the Soviet-sponsored regimes in Romania and Bulgaria, and by deferring the hard questions to the Council of Foreign Ministers, the conference gave Soviet policy in Eastern Europe a legitimacy that naked occupation would not have possessed. The critics do not argue that the Western powers could have liberated Eastern Europe by force; they argue that the West could have withheld the legitimizing language, maintained the political claim even where the military position was lost, and thereby preserved a moral and diplomatic position for the confrontation to come. The debate is genuine, the evidence supports elements of both positions, and the adjudication must be qualified: Potsdam's Eastern European arrangements were substantially unavoidable in their military dimension and substantially chosen in their diplomatic dimension, and the choice to legitimize what force had accomplished was the conference's most fateful political act.

The Cold War consequences followed from both dimensions. The division of Germany into zones hardened into the division of Europe into blocs, the Oder-Neisse line became the frontier of the Soviet sphere, and the Council of Foreign Ministers became the forum where the coalition's disagreements were performed rather than resolved. The atomic dimension compounded the division: Stalin's knowledge that the Americans possessed the bomb, and the Americans' knowledge that Stalin knew, introduced into the postwar relationship a strategic asymmetry that no conference language addressed. Potsdam did not cause the Cold War in any simple sense; the structural conditions of Soviet-American rivalry would have existed regardless of what was agreed at Cecilienhof. But the conference's specific arrangements, the zones, the border, the reparations formula, the legitimized governments, became the concrete terrain on which the Cold War was fought. The committee's success in producing a settlement was also the committee's success in producing the settlement's consequences, and the two cannot be separated.

The expellees' subsequent history adds a final layer to the complication. The roughly twelve million Germans driven westward were absorbed, over the following decade, primarily into the Western occupation zones, where they arrived destitute into a ruined economy and were initially received with resentment by the local populations. Their integration became one of West Germany's great postwar achievements and one of its great unspoken traumas: the expellee organizations became a political force, the question of the lost eastern territories shaped West German foreign policy for a generation, and the silence surrounding the suffering of the treks distorted the country's reckoning with its own wartime guilt. The conference that authorized the transfer did not plan for the absorption, because the protocol treated the population movement as a logistical problem rather than as a human one. The committee produced language about orderly transfer; the societies that received the transferred produced the actual history, in all its difficulty.

## Verdict: The Committee Absorbs Two Successions

The verdict on Potsdam begins from the institutional fact that dominates every other: the conference worked. It convened on schedule, it conducted seventeen days of complex multilateral negotiation, it produced a comprehensive protocol covering the occupation of Germany, the Polish border, reparations, war crimes, the Pacific war, and a dozen lesser questions, and it did so while absorbing the replacement of the American president before the conference and the replacement of the British prime minister during it. No other diplomatic gathering in history has sustained that level of institutional shock without collapse. The committee method, which the house thesis identifies as the Allied coalition's decisive advantage, passed at Potsdam the most severe test it would ever face, and it passed because the machinery had become independent of the men.

The succession test deserves emphasis because it is the finding that no other account of the war's diplomacy has placed at the center. Roosevelt's death in April removed the architect of the American summitry system, the one Western leader who had practiced personal diplomacy with Stalin across two conferences. Truman's substitution could have produced drift, inconsistency, or exploitation by the more experienced Soviet negotiators. It produced none of these, because the State Department, the Joint Chiefs, and the summit staffs carried the American position across the transition without visible seam. Churchill's electoral defeat in July removed the British delegation's dominant personality in the middle of the proceedings, a shock without precedent in summit diplomacy. Attlee's substitution could have produced discontinuity, renegotiation, or Soviet exploitation of British confusion. It produced none of these, because the Foreign Office briefs and the Cabinet's agreed positions carried the British stance across the transition without visible seam. The two successions together demonstrate that by 1945 the Allied coalition possessed something the Axis never built: a decision-making institution that could outlive its leaders.

The verdict must also register what the institution could not do. It could not negotiate away the Red Army's position in Eastern Europe. It could not make the population transfer humane by describing it as humane. It could not convert Soviet security imperatives into liberal democratic outcomes by writing electoral principles into a protocol. The committee's power was the power of procedure: it could keep the coalition functioning, it could produce language that all parties could sign, and it could create standing bodies to continue unresolved business. It could not alter the underlying distribution of force, and where force and language diverged, force prevailed. This is the precise limit of the house thesis, and Potsdam defines it more sharply than any other case. Committee architecture outperformed command architecture in every dimension where institutions mattered, and it was irrelevant in the dimension where only armies mattered.

The final assessment, then, is double-edged by design. Potsdam was the Allied committee system's greatest administrative achievement and its most morally compromised political act, and the two descriptions refer to the same conference, the same protocol, the same seventeen days. The machinery that divided Germany into zones also divided Europe into blocs. The language that authorized the population transfer also authorized the suffering it entailed. The recognition that preserved coalition unity also legitimized the subjugation of Eastern Europe. The verdict does not resolve these tensions; it states them as the conference's essential character. The committee fought better than the command, and the committee's victories, like all victories, had costs that the victors chose to pay with other people's lives.

The verdict on the atomic dimension requires the same double vision. Potsdam was the hinge between the Manhattan Project and Hiroshima: the conference was where the weapon's existence entered the diplomacy of the war's end, and where the decision to use it, taken elsewhere and examined in the series' account of the atomic decision, acquired its international context. The Trinity test gave Truman a private certainty that transformed his public negotiating posture, and the July 24 aside gave Stalin a public fact that transformed his private strategic calculations. Neither leader placed the weapon on the agenda, and the conference's protocol contains no atomic paragraph, yet the weapon shaped the reparations bargain, the Pacific timetable, and the confidence with which the Americans faced the Soviet delegation. The committee's greatest unspoken subject was also its most consequential, and the silence was itself a decision: the victors chose to end the war with the new weapon without ever submitting its use to the coalition's deliberative machinery.

The conference's place in the war-ending sequence completes the verdict. Potsdam was the last act of the Grand Alliance as a functioning institution. After August 2, the alliance's machinery, the Council of Foreign Ministers, the Control Council, the military liaison channels, continued to operate, but it operated in a new political universe defined by the atomic bomb, the Soviet entry into the Pacific war, and the impending collapse of Japan. The institutions that Potsdam created outlived the coalition that created them, and their subsequent history, the Control Council's deadlock, the Council of Foreign Ministers' transformation into a Cold War forum, the Nuremberg tribunal's growth into international law, is the history of wartime machinery repurposed for peacetime rivalry. The committee fought the war by committee and won; it then discovered that the committee's products belonged to the peace, where different rules applied. Potsdam's verdict is therefore not only about the seventeen days. It is about what happens when the most successful decision-making system in military history completes its task and finds that its task has changed.

## Study: Reading the Potsdam Record

The documentary record of Potsdam is the richest of the three Big Three summits, and it rewards a disciplined approach. The starting point is the published Foreign Relations of the United States volume on the Berlin conference, which prints the American minutes of the plenary sessions, the memoranda of conversation, and the staff papers that supported the American positions. Bohlen's interpreter notes for the American delegation and Pavlov's records for the Soviet side allow the critical exchanges to be triangulated, and the comparison is especially valuable at Potsdam because the mid-conference change of British leadership makes the continuity of the record itself an object of study. Truman's diary entries from the conference, published in his memoirs and in the documentary editions, must be read as the private reflections of a new president learning summitry in real time; the famous July entries on Stalin record first impressions, not settled judgments, and they harden into something more consequential only in retrospect. Churchill's account in Triumph and Tragedy covers only the sessions he attended, and its abrupt ending is itself a historical document, the memoir of a man whose war ended at the ballot box in the middle of a conference.

For the historiography, the sequence of reading matters. Begin with Charles Mee's narrative account, which established the conference's dramatic structure for a generation of readers, then move to David Reynolds's study of summit diplomacy, which places Potsdam in the longer pattern of great-power meetings and shows what was normal and what was unprecedented about the Cecilienhof proceedings. Michael Beschloss's examination of the German settlement debates illuminates the reparations and occupation questions that the plenary minutes treat most elliptically. Two important studies appeared after this article's publication date and are cited here strictly as research apparatus under the series' documented post-publication-date exception: Wilson Miscamble's 2007 study of the transition from Roosevelt to Truman, which reconstructs the institutional briefing that carried American policy across the presidential succession, and Michael Neiberg's 2015 comprehensive history of the conference, which remains the fullest single treatment of the seventeen days. Their findings are used here only to frame the research questions, not as narrative evidence, and the article's historical claims rest on sources available at the publication date. Read with the minutes in one hand and the historiography in the other, Potsdam reveals its double character: the best-documented summit of the war and the one whose documents most require reading against themselves.

Students of wartime decision-making may also find value in examining how the medical services supporting the delegations managed the extraordinary demands of the summit, a subject explored through the lens of battlefield medicine at [the ReportMedic WWII battlefield medicine tool](https://reportmedic.org/tools/wwii-battlefield-medicine.html), which illustrates the logistical and medical infrastructure that underpinned large-scale wartime operations and conferences alike.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why was Potsdam chosen as the conference site?

Potsdam was chosen because it lay inside the Soviet occupation zone, on the outskirts of Berlin, and the military facts of mid-1945 made any other choice impractical. Stalin would not travel outside Soviet-controlled territory, a position he had maintained at both previous summits, and the Red Army's occupation of eastern Germany meant that a Berlin-area venue was inevitably a Soviet venue. Cecilienhof Palace offered the necessary scale: a great hall for the plenary sessions around a single round table, grounds large enough for three security perimeters, and surviving infrastructure in a city that had been fought over street by street two months earlier. The choice also carried deliberate symbolism. Holding the victors' conference in the ruined capital of the defeated Reich, in a former Hohenzollern palace, advertised the completeness of Germany's defeat. The Western delegations accepted the venue without serious contest, which itself measured the distribution of power: the side that controlled the ground chose the building.

### How long did the Potsdam Conference last and how many plenary sessions were held?

The conference ran from July 17 to August 2, 1945, seventeen days in total, though the proceedings were interrupted by a two-day recess on July 26 and 27 while the British election results were digested and the delegation changed. Thirteen plenary sessions convened during the working days, each attended by the three leaders with their foreign ministers and interpreters. Beneath the plenaries ran a layered structure of foreign ministers' meetings, drafting subcommittees, and military staff conversations that did much of the detailed negotiation. Some four thousand personnel supported the three delegations across the Potsdam area, handling communications, security, transport, translation, and briefing papers. The scale dwarfed the earlier summits: Tehran had operated with far smaller staffs, and even Yalta's roughly seven hundred support personnel were a fraction of the Potsdam establishment. The seventeen days made Potsdam the longest of the three Big Three conferences and the most administratively intensive.

### What was the Little White House at Potsdam?

The Little White House was the villa at 2 Kaiserstrasse in Babelsberg, near Potsdam, that served as President Truman's residence during the conference. The Americans gave it the nickname to signal that the full apparatus of the American presidency had been transplanted to Germany for the duration of the proceedings. The villa functioned as far more than lodgings: it hosted Truman's morning briefings with Secretary of State Byrnes and Admiral Leahy, received the guarded War Department memoranda reporting the Trinity test results, and served as the venue for bilateral conversations that supplemented the formal plenaries. Stimson's atomic briefings were delivered there, away from the conference table. The building's role illustrates how the summit's real business flowed through multiple channels: the plenary sessions at Cecilienhof provided the formal record, but the leaders' residences were where positions were tested, intelligence was absorbed, and the next day's tactics were set.

### Why did Churchill leave the Potsdam Conference halfway through?

Churchill left because he lost the British general election. The election had been held on July 5, but the counting of overseas military ballots delayed the results, so Churchill opened the conference not knowing his political fate. On July 26 the results were announced: a Labour landslide of 393 seats to the Conservatives' 213, among the most decisive electoral defeats ever inflicted on a sitting wartime leader. Churchill resigned as prime minister the following day. The conference recessed for July 26 and 27, and on July 28 Clement Attlee, the Labour leader, took Churchill's seat at the round table. The departure was not a diplomatic maneuver or a protest; it was the straightforward operation of parliamentary democracy, applied without pause to a man in the middle of negotiating the peace. Churchill's final plenary on July 25 was thus his last act as a Big Three leader, a fact that none of the participants had anticipated when the conference was scheduled.

### Who replaced Churchill at Potsdam and did British policy change?

Clement Attlee replaced Churchill as prime minister on July 28, accompanied by Ernest Bevin as the new Foreign Secretary, and British policy did not visibly change. The continuity was the conference's most remarkable institutional fact. Attlee had served as deputy prime minister throughout the wartime coalition and knew the government's foreign policy files intimately. Bevin, the former general secretary of the Transport and General Workers' Union, brought formidable negotiating weight and a firmly anti-communist outlook that surprised Soviet expectations of a socialist ally. The Foreign Office briefs prepared for Churchill became the briefs that Attlee and Bevin executed, and the plenary record shows no discontinuity in the British positions on Germany, Poland, reparations, or the Pacific. The seamless transition demonstrated that British foreign policy at Potsdam was carried by institutions, the Foreign Office, the Cabinet's agreed positions, the professional diplomatic staff, rather than by Churchill's personality. Stalin, who had expected the change to weaken the British delegation, found instead that the machinery continued without him.

### How did the British election results reach Potsdam?

The results traveled by the ordinary channels of wartime government communication, but their arrival detonated inside the conference like a political bombshell. The British delegation had been receiving constituency returns through July 25, and the trend was unmistakable well before the formal announcement: Labour was winning by margins that stunned even its own leadership. Churchill received the confirmed national result on the evening of July 26 in his Potsdam residence. His private secretary recorded a leader who had expected defeat in principle but was shocked by its scale. The conference's British staff spent the recess managing the transition: briefing Attlee and Bevin, who flew in from London, on the state of every negotiation, transferring the delegation's papers, and preparing the new prime minister for his first plenary. The two-day recess, officially unexplained at the time, was in substance a change-of-government interval inserted into a multilateral summit. No diplomatic protocol covered the situation, because no summit in history had ever experienced it.

### What did Truman tell Stalin about the atomic bomb on July 24?

After a plenary session on July 24, Truman approached Stalin during the informal conversation that followed and told him, in a deliberately casual manner, that the United States had developed a new weapon of unusual destructive force. The phrasing was chosen with care: Truman said nothing about atomic energy, nothing about the Trinity test eight days earlier, and nothing about the weapon's intended use against Japan. The remark was designed to inform without briefing, to place the fact of the weapon on the record between the two leaders while revealing no technical detail. Churchill, who had been told in advance about Truman's plan, watched the exchange closely. Truman's diary records his belief that Stalin had not grasped the significance of what he had been told. The July 24 conversation was the only direct communication between the two leaders about the weapon during the conference, and its studied casualness on both sides has made it a heavily analyzed minute of small talk in diplomatic history.

### Why did Stalin react so calmly when Truman mentioned the new weapon?

Stalin's calm was discipline, not ignorance. Soviet intelligence had been reporting on the American atomic program since 1943, with detailed technical material reaching Moscow from sources inside the Manhattan Project, including the physicist Klaus Fuchs. By July 1945 Stalin knew the weapon's approximate stage of development, understood its strategic significance, and had already authorized the acceleration of the Soviet Union's own atomic program. His casual response, expressing the hope that the Americans would make good use of the weapon against Japan, was therefore a performance: it revealed nothing about Soviet knowledge, confirmed nothing about Soviet intentions, and denied Truman any read on whether the information was new. The episode illustrates the conference's intelligence asymmetry. Truman believed he was introducing a new factor into the relationship; Stalin was managing a factor he had been tracking for two years. The Soviet leader's composure at the moment is best understood as the product of preparation, not of incomprehension.

### How did the Trinity test change Truman's negotiating position at Potsdam?

The Trinity test, which succeeded on July 16 with a yield of roughly twenty-one kilotons, changed Truman's reservation price across the agenda without changing any specific American demand. Before the test, the American delegation had operated on the assumption that Soviet entry into the Pacific war might be necessary to end the war without catastrophic casualties, which gave Stalin leverage to extract concessions. After the test, Truman's dependence on Soviet military cooperation diminished: the weapon offered an alternative instrument for ending the Pacific war, which reduced the price the Americans were willing to pay for Soviet goodwill. The shift showed most clearly in the American approach to Eastern Europe and reparations, where the delegation negotiated from a position of reduced need rather than making explicit threats. Historian David Reynolds's assessment captures the mechanism: the bomb stiffened Truman's posture without becoming a bargaining chip, because Truman never placed it on the table. The effect was real but indirect, operating through confidence rather than coercion.

### How did Potsdam turn the occupation zones from paper into administration?

The zones had been drawn on paper by the European Advisory Commission during the war, but Potsdam converted the drawing into a governing system. The protocol confirmed the four zones, American, British, Soviet, and French, along the Commission's boundaries, established Berlin's four sectors deep inside the Soviet zone, and created the Allied Control Council as the joint authority for Germany as a whole. It set out the political principles of the occupation, denazification, demilitarization, and the repeal of Nazi law, and the economic principles that were supposed to govern all four zones uniformly, including the treatment of Germany as a single economic unit. The French zone, carved from territory originally allocated to Britain and America, was integrated into machinery designed by three powers. Where Yalta had ratified the zone map as a principle, Potsdam built the administrative architecture on top of it: the Council, the Kommandatura for Berlin, the agreed procedures for legislation and enforcement. The machinery functioned until the Cold War's political logic overwhelmed it.

### How did de Gaulle react to France's exclusion from Potsdam?

Charles de Gaulle regarded France's exclusion from the Potsdam Conference as a humiliation and said so with characteristic bluntness. France had not been invited to the summit, despite receiving an occupation zone in Germany and a seat on the Allied Control Council, and de Gaulle treated the omission as evidence that the Big Three intended to settle Europe's future without French participation. His government protested the exclusion through diplomatic channels and, more consequentially, used it to justify French obstructionism within the occupation machinery afterward. France delayed the establishment of centralized German administrations, resisted Control Council decisions that implied German economic unity, and pursued a separate policy in its zone, including the detachment of the Saar. The exclusion thus had practical consequences beyond wounded pride: it gave the French government a standing grievance that legitimized non-cooperation with four-power institutions. The episode illustrates the conference's blind spot regarding the powers it claimed to be restoring.

### What was the Allied Control Council and how did it work?

The Allied Control Council was the four-power body established at Potsdam as the supreme governing authority for occupied Germany. It consisted of the four military governors, American, British, Soviet, and French, acting jointly, with decisions requiring unanimity. Beneath the Council sat the Kommandatura, the four-power authority governing Berlin, and a structure of coordinating committees handling the occupation's functional areas: political affairs, economics, reparations, and the rest. In its first year the Council produced a substantial body of joint legislation, including the denazification directives and the laws dissolving Nazi organizations. Its unanimity rule, however, made it hostage to the political climate: as Soviet-Western relations deteriorated through 1946 and 1947, the Council deadlocked, and in March 1948 the Soviet representative walked out, ending four-power government in practice. The Council's rise and fall traces the arc of the Potsdam settlement itself, from comprehensive institutional design to Cold War paralysis.

### What did the Potsdam Protocol say about denazification?

The protocol committed the four occupying powers to the complete destruction of the Nazi Party and its affiliated organizations, the repeal of all Nazi legislation, the removal of active Nazis from public and semi-public office, and the punishment of war criminals. These political principles were stated as joint obligations binding on all four zones, and the Control Council subsequently issued directives translating the principles into administrative procedures, including the questionnaires and tribunals that classified Germans by degrees of Nazi involvement. In practice, denazification varied enormously by zone. The American zone pursued the most systematic program, the British zone a more pragmatic one, the French zone an inconsistent one, and the Soviet zone used denazification as an instrument of political transformation, removing not only Nazis but also social democrats and other non-communist opponents. The protocol's uniform language thus produced four different realities, and the divergence became one of the earliest visible fractures in the occupation.

### How did the reparations formula at Potsdam actually work?

The formula divided reparations into two streams. Each occupying power would satisfy its claims primarily from its own zone, through the removal of industrial equipment and the seizure of German external assets. In addition, the Soviet Union would receive ten percent of the industrial capital equipment in the Western zones without compensation, plus a further fifteen percent in exchange for deliveries of food, coal, and other commodities from the Soviet zone to the west. The twenty-five percent Western-zone transfer was the negotiated price of Soviet assent to the zonal principle and to Germany's treatment as a single economic unit. In implementation, the arrangement failed on both sides. Soviet removals from the eastern zone far exceeded what the protocol's drafters had contemplated, stripping entire factories in ways that crippled the zone's economy. Western deliveries of equipment stalled as the political climate chilled, and the food-for-machinery exchange never operated as designed. The formula that had bought agreement at the table became, within two years, one of the principal grievances dividing the occupiers.

### What is the Oder-Neisse line and why did it matter?

The Oder-Neisse line is the frontier along the Oder River and the western branch of the Neisse River that the Potsdam Conference established as Poland's western border, transferring to Polish administration the German territories of Silesia, most of Pomerania, and southern East Prussia. It mattered for three reasons. First, it moved Poland roughly two hundred kilometers westward, compensating the Poles for the eastern territories annexed by the Soviet Union and creating a state more ethnically homogeneous than any in Polish history. Second, it required the transfer westward of the German population of those territories, setting in motion the expulsion of some twelve million people. Third, drawn as a provisional boundary pending a final peace treaty, it functioned as a permanent frontier for forty-five years, becoming the western edge of the Soviet sphere in Europe and one of the Cold War's most durable borders. Its confirmation in the Two Plus Four settlement of 1990, a decade and a half before this article's publication date, closed a question Potsdam had left formally open.

### Why were twelve million Germans expelled after Potsdam?

The expulsions were the demographic consequence of the Oder-Neisse border decision combined with the Czechoslovak and Hungarian transfers the protocol also authorized. The conference's language provided that the transfer of German populations from Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Hungary should be effected in an orderly and humane manner, which gave the new governments of those countries international cover for removals that were already underway. The expulsions unfolded in two phases: the chaotic flight and forced removals of 1945, as the Red Army's advance and the collapse of German administration drove millions westward, and the more organized transfers of 1946 to 1949 under Allied supervision. The death toll remains disputed: West German government documentation of the 1950s estimated over two million dead, while Rüdiger Overmans's 1994 archival study placed the figure near half a million. The suffering is not disputed. The conference authorized the transfer knowing its scale, and the qualifier humane described an aspiration that the reality never approached.

### What was the Council of Foreign Ministers created at Potsdam?

The Council of Foreign Ministers was the five-power standing body established by the Potsdam Protocol to prepare the peace treaties for Italy, Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary, and Finland and to address the territorial questions the plenary sessions left unresolved. Its members were the foreign ministers of the United States, Britain, the Soviet Union, France, and China, and it first convened in London in September 1945. The Council embodied the committee method's characteristic response to deadlock: when the principals could not agree, they created an institution to continue the negotiation. During the war, that reflex had worked, as staff bodies converted strategic disagreements into plans. Applied to the political settlement, it produced years of foreign ministers' conferences that registered the division of Europe without arresting it. The Council did eventually produce the 1947 peace treaties for the minor Axis satellites, but on the great questions, Germany, Eastern Europe, Austria, it managed process rather than results. Its record is the institutional trace of the coalition's transition from wartime cooperation to Cold War confrontation.

### Why was the Soviet Union not a signatory of the Potsdam Declaration?

The Soviet Union was excluded from the Potsdam Declaration because it was not yet at war with Japan, and both Stalin and Truman had reasons to keep it that way until the timing suited them. Stalin did not want a public ultimatum to Japan issued in Moscow's name before the Red Army was ready to strike in Manchuria; a Soviet signature would have been a provocation inviting a Japanese preemptive response or at minimum complicating the final preparations. Truman did not want to delay the declaration's issuance until Soviet belligerency, and issuing it in the names of the United States, Britain, and China was sufficient for its purpose. The declaration was therefore signed by Truman, Churchill, and Chiang Kai-shek, the last by remote cable, with the Soviet Union conspicuously absent from a document demanding Japan's surrender. The omission was repaired within days: the Soviet declaration of war on August 8 made Moscow a belligerent, and the declaration's terms became, in effect, the coalition's collective position.

### What did mokusatsu mean and did the translation matter?

Mokusatsu was the word Prime Minister Kantaro Suzuki used on July 28 in response to press questions about the Potsdam Declaration, and its meaning spans a range from "ignore" or "treat with silent contempt" to the more neutral "withhold comment." The Allied press and intelligence services rendered it as outright rejection, and the rejection reading became the operative interpretation in Washington, hardening the American view that Japan would not surrender on the declaration's terms. Foreign Minister Shigenori Togo's subsequent cables suggest the Japanese leadership intended something closer to deliberate noncommittal, preserving freedom of maneuver while the Supreme Council's internal deadlock continued. Historians have debated whether a more precise translation might have altered the sequence of August, but the weight of the evidence argues against it. The Supreme Council remained split between the peace faction and the military hardliners regardless of the declaration's wording, and the impasse broke only under the twin shocks of the atomic bombings and Soviet entry. The translation question is genuinely interesting and ultimately secondary.

### Why is Potsdam called the last Big Three summit?

Potsdam is called the last Big Three summit because the wartime coalition's three-leader format never convened again. The personal and institutional conditions that had made the summits possible dissolved within weeks of the conference's close. Roosevelt, the architect of the summit system, was dead. Churchill, its most experienced practitioner, had been removed by his own electorate. The two new Western leaders, Truman and Attlee, never developed the summit habit: their subsequent meetings with Stalin were conducted through foreign ministers, correspondence, and the new institutions of the emerging Cold War rather than through personal conclaves. The coalition itself was transforming from a wartime alliance into a peacetime rivalry, and the committee machinery that had sustained the summits was repurposed for confrontation. The foreign ministers met, the Control Council deadlocked, the United Nations provided a new forum, but the round table at Cecilienhof, with three leaders and two interpreters deciding the fate of continents, belonged to the war. Potsdam closed the era it had been convened to complete.
