## The Man and the Paradox

![Gamal Abdel Nasser addressing a crowd, the Egyptian president who became the voice of the Arab world - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Gamal Abdel Nasser was the most famous Arab of his age, and the least like the kings and presidents who shared it. He came from nowhere that mattered in the Egypt of the old order: no great family, no fortune, no foreign education, no patron in the palace. What he carried instead was a conviction, formed early and never abandoned, that Egypt's shame was not poverty or backwardness but subjection, and that subjection could be answered with dignity spoken aloud until millions believed it. The claim at the heart of his story is this: Nasser's power rested on becoming the symbolic voice of Arab dignity and independence; his authority was as much emotional and pan-Arab as it was Egyptian. He did not inherit a nation. He gave a scattered people a voice, and in return they gave him a power no constitution had granted.

He was, by temperament, a plotter rather than an orator, a man who preferred the locked room to the public square, and here is the second paradox: the instrument of his power would be the spoken word, addressed not to ministers or generals but to millions he would never meet. He understood, earlier than his rivals and more completely, that in a country humiliated for seventy years, dignity was not a luxury of the strong but a hunger of the weak, and that the man who fed that hunger would be forgiven almost anything.

He collected humiliations the way other men collect honors, and he remembered every one. The schoolboy dragged from a demonstration, the cadet lectured by foreign instructors, the junior officer ordered to stand aside while his king was bullied: each became a deposit in an account he intended to settle. What made him dangerous was not the anger itself, which thousands of Egyptians shared, but the patience with which he organized it.

He was not, in those early years, an impressive figure to meet. Tall and thin, unsmiling, with a manner that strangers found cold, he gave little away and asked much. Those who served under him remembered the silences more than the speeches: long, calculating silences in which he weighed a man and found his use.

The third paradox is that the most Egyptian of modern leaders drew his deepest strength from beyond Egypt's borders. His cause, Arab dignity, belonged to no single flag, and his appeal reached Arabs who had never set foot in Egypt. Egypt in those years was a country governed by men who did not believe in it. The king collected palaces; the great landowners collected rents; the party leaders collected ministries, and all of them drew their salaries under a British protection that made real independence unnecessary and real reform impossible. The British, for their part, had convinced themselves that Egypt could not govern itself, and they governed it just badly enough to prove themselves right. Into this decayed order came a generation of young men educated just enough to see the rot and armed just enough to do something about it.

To understand how a postman's son came to stand at the center of Arab life, the story must begin not with the seizure of power but with the long education in powerlessness that preceded it.

## A Postman's Son

He was born on 15 January 1918, in Alexandria, the eldest son of Abdel Nasser Hussein, a clerk in the postal service, and his wife. The family belonged to that thin stratum of Egyptians who lived by a government salary and moved wherever the government sent them, which meant the boy's childhood was divided among three places that each left a mark. There was Alexandria, the cosmopolitan port city of his birth; there was Khatatba, his father's village in the Delta, where he spent long summers; and there was Cairo, where he lived for stretches in the house of a paternal uncle while attending school. The constant moving made him self-reliant; the boy learned to enter a new school, size up its hierarchies, and find his place within weeks. His uncle's house in Cairo was crowded and noisy, full of cousins, and the boy learned there to hold his own in argument and to keep his own counsel. His mother died in 1926, when he was eight, and the loss hardened something in him early. He was, by every account, a serious and solitary boy, a reader, watchful beyond his years, already conscious that the country around him was not its own master.

Those summers in Khatatba gave him the other half of his education. The village was poor in the way much of rural Egypt was poor, and the boy from the city saw at close range the fellahin who worked land they did not own for landlords they rarely saw. He never forgot it. Years afterward, men who knew him would say that his anger had two addresses: the foreigner who occupied the country and the rich Egyptian who profited from it. It was a simple moral map, and he never redrew it.

The Egypt of his childhood was a kingdom in name and a protectorate in fact. The British had declared their protectorate in 1914, granted a qualified independence in 1922, and kept their troops along the Canal and their hand on the king's shoulder ever after. For a bright schoolboy in Cairo, politics was not an abstraction. It was the soldiers in the streets, the headlines about the palace, the talk in the coffeehouses. Young Nasser absorbed it all, and he absorbed too the lesson his generation was learning: that Egypt's rulers, whether the king in his palaces or the ministers in their offices, governed only so far as London permitted. The lesson was reinforced daily: by the British officers who commanded Egyptian units, by the British advisers who sat in every ministry, by the knowledge, shared by every Egyptian schoolboy, that the most important decisions about Egypt were made in London.

### The Schoolboy Agitator

By his secondary school years, the watchful boy had become an active one. He threw himself into the student demonstrations that shook Cairo in the early 1930s, marching against the British presence and against a palace that collaborated with it. In 1930, at the age of twelve, he was arrested during one such demonstration; accounts differ on whether he spent a day or a night in detention, but the arrest itself became part of his story, proof offered afterward that his opposition to the old order had begun in childhood. He wrote letters to friends full of nationalist feeling, organized his schoolmates, and earned a reputation as a boy who could not be kept out of a protest. His teachers despaired of him; his friends remembered a boy who argued about politics with an intensity other boys reserved for football. The mid-1930s brought the student movement to a new pitch. The demonstrations of 1935 and 1936, demanding the restoration of the constitution and an end to the British presence, swept up the schools, and Nasser was in the thick of them, marching, shouting, and learning the mechanics of the street: how a crowd gathers, how it holds, how it breaks.

The ideas swirling around him were the great currents of Egyptian nationalism in that decade: the liberal constitutionalism of the Wafd, which had led the struggle against the British after the First World War; the Islamic revivalism of the Muslim Brotherhood, which spoke to the pious poor; the militant youth movements that promised action rather than petitions. Nasser moved among these currents without anchoring himself to any of them, taking from each what answered his own anger. The fuller history of those currents, and of how they shaped his generation's idea of what Egypt was and should become, belongs to the account of [Egyptian nationalism and identity](/2015/07/01/nationalism-and-egyptian-identity/). What matters here is that the schoolboy emerged from those years with a fixed hatred of the British occupation and a fixed contempt for the Egyptian politicians who administered it.

### From Law Books to the Barracks

In 1936 he enrolled in the Faculty of Law at Fuad University in Cairo, the expected path for an ambitious young Egyptian. He lasted only months. The Anglo-Egyptian Treaty of that year had opened the Royal Military Academy to a wider intake of Egyptians, and Nasser, who had long been drawn to the army as the one institution where a poor boy might rise by merit, applied. He was admitted in 1937 and commissioned as an officer in 1938. The choice defined him. The law would have made him an arguer of cases; the army made him a plotter of campaigns, first military and then political. The academy itself was a revelation and a disappointment. The training was serious and the discipline real, but the shadow of the British lay over everything: British instructors, British manuals, British assumptions about what Egyptian officers could and could not do. Nasser absorbed the training and rejected the assumptions, filing away both for future use.

His early service carried him across the map of Egypt's diminished empire. At Manqabad in Upper Egypt, in 1939, he met Anwar Sadat, a fellow junior officer of similar background and similar resentments, and the two formed a friendship that would shape both their lives. A posting to the Sudan, with the Egyptian battalions stationed there under the Anglo-Egyptian condominium, gave him his first sustained look at how the British ran an empire from the inside: the condescension of the officers' mess, the distance between the Egyptian rank and file and the British command. He returned to Egypt a young officer with a professional's eye for the army's weaknesses and a nationalist's anger at the uses to which those weaknesses were put.

## The Family Man

In 1944, while Egypt still lived under British occupation and the Second World War ground toward its close, Gamal Abdel Nasser married Tahia Kazim. He was twenty-six, a captain in the Egyptian army, with a salary to match and no fortune of his own. She was the daughter of a prosperous merchant family, raised in comfort and educated in wartime Cairo. The match was arranged in the customary fashion. The marriage would hold for the rest of Nasser's life, a span of twenty-six years, and it would remain free of scandal, a fact his propagandists later made much of and his enemies could never dent.

The children came in steady succession: two daughters, Hoda and Mona, and three sons, Khaled, Abdel Hamid, and Abdel Hakim. Photographs from the period show a solemn father posing with a growing brood in the stiff rows of the studio portrait, and it is hard to look at those images without sensing how ordinary the scene was. There were no tutors from Europe, no private schools of the kind the old aristocracy favored. The Nasser children attended ordinary Egyptian schools and grew up, as far as the press could ever report, without the markers of privilege that had defined the households of kings and pashas.

Domestic life settled into a pattern that would not change even when Nasser became the most powerful man in the Arab world. The family lived in a modest villa in Manshiet el-Bakri, a quiet suburb on the edge of Cairo, in a house that visitors remembered as plain to the point of austerity. The furniture was simple, the meals unremarkable, the entertaining nearly nonexistent. Nasser dressed with a soldier's disregard for fashion and showed no appetite for cars, yachts, or the country estates that had once signaled power in Egypt. His days began before dawn and ended long after dark, leaving the household a place where the father was more legend than presence.

Tahia played her part by withdrawing from public view. She made almost no appearances, gave no interviews, and cultivated a privacy so complete that foreign journalists struggled to describe her. In an age when first ladies were becoming public figures, her absence was itself a statement, and it served her husband well.

The political effect was considerable. Egyptians who had watched King Farouk parade through Cairo in motorcades while the countryside went hungry saw a president who lived like a senior civil servant. The small house, the plain suits, the wife who never appeared in the newspapers, all of it fed the image of the man of the people, the officer who had taken power from duty rather than for its pleasures. Whether that modesty was temperament or calculation remains a question for historians. Its value to his authority in the 1940s and 1950s is beyond dispute.

## The Forging of a Nationalist

Two experiences in the 1940s turned the angry young officer into a revolutionary: a humiliation inflicted on his king by the British, which he took as a humiliation of Egypt itself, and a war in Palestine, which taught him that the regime would sacrifice its own soldiers to protect its corruptions.

### Abdeen, 4 February 1942

On the night of 4 February 1942, British tanks ringed Abdeen Palace in Cairo and British troops took up positions around the capital. The British ambassador, Sir Miles Lampson, drove to the palace with an ultimatum for King Farouk: appoint Mustafa al-Nahhas, the Wafd leader, as prime minister, or sign the instrument of abdication Lampson had brought with him. Farouk, surrounded and alone, capitulated. The most powerful man in Egypt had been shown, before his own court, that he ruled only by British permission. Lampson had meant to secure a reliable government for the war effort; what he secured was the moral destruction of the monarchy in the eyes of its own army.

For Egyptian officers, the lesson was shattering. Here was the commander in chief of their army, the sovereign to whom they had sworn loyalty, forced to take orders at gunpoint from a foreign ambassador, while the army itself was ordered to stand aside. Nasser was a junior officer when the news of that night reached him, and by his own later account it marked him permanently. If the king could be humiliated so completely, what protection did the nation have? If the army could not defend the honor of its own sovereign, what was it for? In the officers' messes the talk turned bitter and then quiet, and men who had sworn loyalty to the throne began to speak of the throne as the enemy's instrument. Some officers wept; more went silent, and the silence was the dangerous part. A humiliated army does not forget, and from that night the question in the messes was no longer whether the monarchy deserved loyalty but how long it could survive without it. The Abdeen incident, as Egyptians called it, converted a generation of officers from grumblers into conspirators. The monarchy had been exposed as a British convenience, and men like Nasser drew the conclusion the British never intended: that Egypt would have to be saved from its king as well as from its occupiers.

### The Faluja Pocket

The second forging came in war. In May 1948, within days of the proclamation of the state of Israel, Egypt joined the other Arab states in the invasion of Palestine, and Nasser went with the expeditionary force as a staff officer. The campaign was a disaster of planning and supply, and its most famous episode, for Egyptians, was the siege of the Faluja pocket. In October 1948, Israeli forces encircled an Egyptian brigade, several thousand men, in the area of Faluja, cutting it off from the main Egyptian lines. For months the encircled troops held out under bombardment and dwindling supplies, officers and men sharing the same hunger, refusing Israeli demands to surrender, until the armistice of early 1949 released them. The men of the pocket came home to a hero's welcome and a bitter question: what had their sacrifice been for?

Nasser served on the staff of the besieged force through those months, and fellow officers remembered his steadiness under fire and his refusal to despair. The pocket became his legend within the army: the young officer who had shared the soldiers' danger while the generals and ministers in Cairo bickered. But Faluja taught him a darker lesson as well. The Egyptian army had gone into Palestine brave and come out beaten, and the officers were convinced they knew why. Corrupt procurement had sent them to war with defective weapons, and the scandal over the faulty arms became, in the mess halls and the staff colleges, the proof that the regime in Cairo was rotten to its core. In the pocket, Nasser had watched men die for lack of supplies that Cairo's warehouses held in plenty, and the contrast between the courage of the soldiers and the cowardice of the system that commanded them became the fixed moral center of his politics. The king's court, the great landowners, the party bosses had grown rich while soldiers died for want of working rifles. Nasser emerged from Palestine with his nationalism fully formed and his target selected. It was not only the British who had to go. It was the whole order that had armed Egypt with broken guns.

## The Free Officers

Nasser returned from Palestine convinced that Egypt's liberation would have to be the work of the army itself, and that it would have to be done in secret. In 1949 he began assembling what became the Free Officers Movement: a clandestine organization of junior and middle-ranking officers, bound by personal trust and a shared program. The method was the cell. Nasser recruited officers he knew and trusted, each of whom recruited others, so that no single member knew the whole network and no informer could destroy it at a stroke. The discipline was severe. Meetings shifted from place to place, nothing was written down that could hang a man, and Nasser carried the full roster of the organization in his head. He recruited the way he did everything, patiently and personally, testing a man over months before trusting him with the secret. At the center sat a small executive committee, with Nasser as its unquestioned organizer, and its aims were plain: the evacuation of the British from Egypt, the end of the palace's corrupt domination of politics, and a reckoning with the great landowners and profiteers who fed on the country. It was not, at first, a program for ruling Egypt. It was a program for seizing the power to rule it, and the distinction mattered: the Free Officers knew what they hated far more clearly than they knew what they would build.

The movement grew slowly through 1950 and 1951, feeling its way. Around it, Egypt was sliding toward open confrontation: Egyptian guerrillas were attacking British installations in the Canal zone, the British answered with force, and in Cairo governments rose and fell with the seasons. The Free Officers were captains and majors, men in their early thirties, too junior to command divisions but senior enough to command companies and battalions, the working parts of the army. They were also, many of them, veterans of Palestine, men who had watched the same defeats and drawn the same conclusions, and that shared experience gave the movement a cohesion no ideology could have supplied. They met in apartments and mess halls, passed messages by hand, and built their strength in the units around Cairo. Nasser's particular gift, visible already, was for winning the confidence of other strong men. Sadat, his friend since Manqabad, joined early. Abdel Hakim Amer, a fellow officer bound to Nasser by close personal friendship, became his right hand. And in a move of political shrewdness, Nasser drew in the senior officer Muhammad Naguib, a respected general and a hero of the Palestine war, to serve as the movement's public face. The young conspirators needed a general's rank to reassure the army; Naguib gave their discontents a name the army already honored. The choice also revealed Nasser's realism: he understood that Egypt would not follow an unknown junior officer into the dark, and he was willing to let an older man carry the flag while he held the organization.

The test of the movement's strength came, unexpectedly, in the officers' club. In the elections for the club's governing board, held in the winter of 1951 to 1952 (histories differ on the exact month), the Free Officers ran a slate of their own candidates against the palace's men, and won. The victory was modest in itself; the officers' club was a social institution, not a command. But its meaning was unmistakable. The king's candidates had been defeated on the army's own ground, by the army's own vote, and the palace understood exactly what it meant. Farouk's circle, already shaken by the burning of Cairo in January 1952 and the procession of governments that could not contain the unrest, saw the result for what it meant. The Free Officers, for their part, read the palace's alarm as a warning. If they waited, they would be discovered, arrested, and broken. Word had reached them that the palace was preparing arrests of its own, and the conspiracy struck before it could be broken. It had to become a coup, and soon.

Through the spring and early summer of 1952, Nasser and the executive committee prepared. They mapped the barracks, the ministries, the radio station, the bridges over the Nile. They assigned units and commanders, arranged signals, and rehearsed the order of seizure. The plan was Nasser's in its essentials: strike in the small hours, take the army's own headquarters first so that no loyalist general could rally a resistance, then the instruments of communication, then the symbols of the state. Secrecy was total. Wives were not told, families were not warned, and each officer knew only his own part, so that if one cell fell the rest would survive. On the night of 22 July 1952 the officers moved. By dawn on 23 July, the Free Officers held Cairo's key points, the army's command had been neutralized, and the king's government discovered that it no longer governed. The coup was swift and met almost no resistance. Across the city, Egyptians woke to martial music on the radio and the announcement that the army had seized power to cleanse the country, and the streets, to the astonishment of the conspirators themselves, filled with cheering crowds. The full story of the planning, the night itself, and the days of maneuver that followed is told in the account of the [1952 revolution](/2015/12/01/1952-revolution-free-officers/).

What followed in those last days of July belonged to the revolution's momentum. Farouk, abandoned by his ministers and his generals, accepted the inevitable. On 26 July 1952 he abdicated in favor of his infant son and sailed from Alexandria into exile, saluted in a final irony by the guns of a navy he no longer commanded. The Revolutionary Command Council, the Free Officers' ruling body, took power in Cairo. A thirty-four-year-old officer who had begun as a schoolboy arrested for demonstrating against the British now stood at the center of the state he had set out to remake, and the long road from the Alexandria flat to the seat of power was complete.


## The Republic Is Born

On June 18, 1953, less than a year after King Farouk had sailed out of Alexandria into exile, the officers who had deposed him abolished the monarchy and declared Egypt a republic. The infant king Fuad II was deposed along with the regency council that governed in his name, and for the first time in the modern era the head of the Egyptian state was an Egyptian rather than the heir of a foreign dynasty. The Free Officers who had seized power in July of the previous year now ruled in the open, no longer as a corrective movement behind a throne but as the government itself. The declaration was brief and ceremonial, but it marked a genuine rupture. The palace, the court, and the whole machinery of royal government were gone, and nothing yet existed to replace them except the will of fourteen army officers.

The man placed at the head of the new order was not the man who had made it. Major General Muhammad Naguib, the senior officer the young conspirators had recruited to lend their movement a respectable public face, became the first president of the republic, and he took the premiership as well. Behind him stood the Revolutionary Command Council, the body formed from the Free Officers' executive committee, with Naguib as its chairman and a 35-year-old lieutenant colonel named Gamal Abdel Nasser as its vice chairman and its undisputed driving force. The arrangement was deliberate. Naguib gave the revolution a fatherly public image, a hero of the Palestine war with a reputation for plain speaking and personal modesty, while Nasser and his colleagues worked the levers of power behind the curtain. Historians commonly treat this moment as the opening of the story of [modern Egypt since 1952](/2015/11/01/modern-egypt-since-1952/), the point at which the republic replaced the kingdom and the army replaced the palace as the center of political gravity, and there is no better date for it than that hot June day when the monarchy was formally dissolved.

Yet the republic was born divided, and the division ran through the very top of the regime. Naguib understood himself as a transitional figure. He believed the officers had intervened to cleanse a corrupt political system and should then return the country to parliamentary life, permit free elections, and retire to the barracks. He said as much in private and, on occasion, in public, and his sincerity on the point won him genuine affection among civilians who had wearied of military rule before it had fairly begun. Nasser understood the mission differently. He had come to believe that the old parties, the old parliament, and the old landed elite were rotten beyond repair, and that Egypt required a long period of guided revolutionary transformation before any return to civilian politics could be contemplated. Where Naguib saw a caretaker government, Nasser saw a revolution that had barely started. The disagreement was philosophical, but it was also personal, and it would consume the next eighteen months of Egyptian politics. Naguib's popularity with the public and with much of the officer corps made him dangerous to Nasser. Nasser's control of the council's inner circle and of the army's political machinery made him dangerous to Naguib. Both men knew it, and each began to maneuver against the other almost from the day the republic was declared.

The early measures of the new republic already carried the twin marks of the Nasser era: reform that reached the poorest Egyptians, and repression that reached anyone who challenged the regime. The political parties had been dissolved in January 1953, replaced by the Liberation Rally, a mass organization meant to gather the nation behind the revolution but in practice an instrument of the officers' control. The press was brought under supervision through new legislation, and the council founded its own newspaper to carry the official line. Workers who struck at the textile mills of Kafr al-Dawwar in the summer of 1952 had been met with military tribunals and executions, a warning that the revolution would tolerate no independent centers of power, not even on the left. The pattern was set early. The regime would speak the language of social justice and national dignity, and it would enforce its monopoly on that language without hesitation.

## Naguib and the Man Behind Him

The contest between the president and his vice chairman came to a head in the spring of 1954. In March of that year, amid disputes over the pace of the return to civilian rule and over Nasser's accumulation of authority, Naguib resigned from his offices. The resignation was a gamble. Naguib expected that the street and the army would rally to him and force the council to accept his terms. For a few days the gamble seemed to work. Crowds demonstrated in Cairo in his support, and the council, caught off guard, restored him to the presidency. But the restoration was a tactical retreat by Nasser's faction, not a defeat. On April 17, 1954, Nasser himself became prime minister, taking direct control of the government while leaving Naguib the hollow shell of the presidency. The real shift of power had occurred. From that point forward Naguib presided, and Nasser governed.

The final act was swift and, by the standards of the region, bloodless. On October 26, 1954, as Nasser addressed a crowd in Manshiya Square in Alexandria, a member of the Muslim Brotherhood named Mahmoud Abdel Latif fired several shots at him from close range. The shots missed. Nasser, famously, kept his composure and continued speaking, and the failed attempt became the founding legend of his personal courage. It also became his pretext. The regime moved against the Brotherhood with overwhelming force, arresting thousands of its members, dissolving the organization, and staging trials that ended in long prison sentences and executions. Whether Naguib had any connection to the attempt was never established, and historians remain divided on how much of the subsequent purge was driven by genuine fear and how much by calculation. What is not disputed is that Nasser used the crisis to break every remaining rival. In November 1954, the council removed Naguib from the presidency and placed him under house arrest in a Cairo suburb, where he would remain, largely forgotten by the public, for the rest of the decade and beyond.

### How did Nasser take power from Muhammad Naguib?

Naguib, Egypt's figurehead president from 1953, clashed with Nasser over restoring parliamentary rule. Nasser outmaneuvered him through the RCC and army support, exploited the October 1954 assassination attempt to purge the Muslim Brotherhood and Naguib's allies, and removed him from office in November 1954.

The careful chronology matters, because it is easy to compress. Nasser did not become president in the aftermath of the 1952 coup, nor directly upon Naguib's fall. The interim belonged to Naguib, and after November 1954 the council ruled collectively with Nasser as its strongest figure, prime minister and the revolution's public voice. Nasser's own presidency was formalized only in the mid-1950s, when a new constitution and a national plebiscite gave the regime the civilian legal form it had long lacked. The gap between the fall of the monarchy and the formal start of Nasser's presidency was nearly four years, years in which the character of the new Egypt was decided by men in uniform arguing behind closed doors.

## The 1954 Treaty

If the domestic struggle defined Nasser's rise, the negotiation with Britain defined his statecraft. The British military presence in the Suez Canal Zone was the oldest open wound in Egyptian politics, the visible proof that independence remained incomplete. Some 80,000 British troops were stationed around the canal base, the largest military installation of its kind in the world, and every Egyptian government since the war had demanded their departure. Nasser, as prime minister, took up the negotiation personally, and he conducted it with a patience and flexibility that surprised British diplomats who had expected a firebrand.

The Anglo-Egyptian agreement was signed on October 19, 1954. Its terms were a genuine compromise, and each side claimed victory. Britain agreed to withdraw all its forces from the Canal Zone within twenty months, ending more than seventy years of continuous military occupation. In return, Egypt permitted British technicians to maintain the base installations in peacetime, and granted Britain the right to reactivate the base in the event of an attack on Egypt, any Arab League state, or Turkey. The treaty also recognized Sudan's right to self-determination, closing another long chapter of the Anglo-Egyptian condominium. Nasser's critics at home, particularly among the Muslim Brotherhood and the old nationalist Wafd, denounced the concessions as a betrayal. Nasser's answer was that he had achieved what decades of agitation had not: a dated, binding British commitment to leave. When the last British soldier departed the Canal Zone in June 1956, the treaty stood vindicated on its own terms, and Nasser's reputation as the man who had ended the occupation was secured. The negotiation revealed the other Nasser, the one beneath the revolutionary rhetoric: a pragmatist willing to bargain, to wait, and to accept half a loaf when the whole was not on offer.

## Building the Authoritarian State

With Naguib gone and the British departure scheduled, Nasser turned to the permanent architecture of power. The revolution needed a legal form, and in 1956 it received one. A new constitution was drafted that concentrated authority in a powerful presidency, and on June 23, 1956, it was put to a national plebiscite along with Nasser's own candidacy for the presidency. The official figures claimed overwhelming approval, and while the precise margins must be treated with caution given the regime's control of the process, the political meaning of the vote was unambiguous. Egypt now had a republican constitution, a president, and an elected national assembly, all of them subordinate in practice to the man who had authored the system. The presidency Nasser assumed that summer was the formalization of a power he had already held for two years, but the plebiscite gave it the stamp of popular consent, and he would invoke that consent for the rest of his career.

The political system built around the presidency was authoritarian by design. The Liberation Rally, the mass organization founded in early 1953, had never developed an independent life, and in 1957 it was replaced by the National Union, a single political organization meant to encompass the entire nation. Membership was in practice a requirement for public employment and advancement, and the Union's congresses served as stage-managed demonstrations of unity rather than forums of debate. No other party was permitted to exist. The press was brought under tightening control through ownership changes, censorship, and the steady pressure of a state that owned the paper supply and the advertising that kept newspapers alive. The council's own daily, founded in the revolution's first year, set the tone: journalism as an arm of the state, its headlines written to the regime's requirements. The security services grew in parallel. A general intelligence directorate was established in 1954 under Zakaria Mohieddin, one of the original Free Officers, and military intelligence under Abdel Hakim Amer extended the army's watchfulness into every ministry, university, and newsroom. Arrests of communists, Brotherhood members, and suspected Naguib sympathizers continued through the decade, and the prison camps in the desert became a permanent feature of Egyptian political life.

None of this should obscure the fact that the authoritarian state was also, for millions of Egyptians, a state that delivered. This is the complication that any honest account of the period must keep alive. The same government that imprisoned its critics built schools and clinics in villages that had never seen either. The same security apparatus that watched the universities presided over an expansion of free education that carried the children of peasants into the professions for the first time in Egyptian history. Nasser's Egypt was not a simple tyranny imposed on a passive population. It was a bargain, and for much of the 1950s the majority of Egyptians appeared willing to accept it: dignity and development in exchange for obedience, national pride in exchange for political silence. The bargain would be tested in later years, but in the decade of its making it held.

## Land Reform: The First Revolution at Home

The reform that did more than any other to define Nasser's revolution in the eyes of the Egyptian peasantry was the land reform, and although its founding law predated the republic by nine months, its meaning belonged to the Nasser era. On September 9, 1952, the council had promulgated the agrarian reform law, setting a ceiling of 200 feddans on individual land ownership and providing for the redistribution of the excess to landless peasants in small plots, paid for over decades at low interest. The great estates of the old landed aristocracy, the families that had dominated Egyptian politics and rural life for a century, were broken up. Compensation was paid in long-term government bonds, which in practice meant the old landlords were expropriated at a fraction of their wealth's value.

Nasser was the reform's champion within the council, and he made it the moral centerpiece of the revolution's domestic program. The law was presented not as a technical adjustment but as the correction of a historic injustice, the return of Egypt's soil to those who worked it. September 9 was declared Peasants' Day and celebrated annually with official fanfare. For hundreds of thousands of fellahin families, the reform was the first tangible proof that the revolution meant something in their lives. A man who had worked another man's land as a tenant or a day laborer now held a title deed with his own name on it, and he voted, when he voted, with that deed in his memory.

The limits of the reform were real, and they deserve to be stated plainly. The ceiling was high enough that a substantial class of medium landlords survived intact. Much of the best land remained in relatively few hands, and the cooperatives through which the new smallholders were organized became instruments of state control over the countryside, dictating crops and prices as effectively as the old landlords had dictated rents. Corruption and favoritism marked the redistribution in many districts, and the rural poor who received nothing, the landless laborers rather than the tenants, saw little change in their condition. Yet the political effect was transformative regardless of the economic fine print. The old landed elite, the social foundation of the monarchy and the Wafd alike, was destroyed as a political force. In its place stood a peasantry bound to the regime by gratitude and by interest, a rural base that would sustain Nasser through every crisis of the coming years. The land reform was the first domestic revolution of the Nasser era, and in the countryside it was the revolution itself.

## The New Citizens

The Egypt that took shape in the second half of the 1950s was Nasser's deliberate creation, a new domestic order raised on the ruins of the old parliamentary regime. The constitution of 1956 granted women the vote for the first time in Egyptian history, a step that put Egypt ahead of many states in the region and gave the regime a modernizing credential it never tired of displaying. Women could stand for office as well as cast ballots, and the promise carried real symbolic weight in a country where half the population had been formally excluded from political life.

The elections of 1957, held under the banner of the National Union, Nasser's single political organization, put the promise to the test. Two women won seats in the National Assembly: Rawya Attia, a former army officer who had served in the war of 1956, and Amina Shoukry, a social worker from Alexandria. Their presence in parliament was unprecedented, and the regime celebrated it as proof that the revolution had opened public life to women. The National Union, however, was an instrument of control rather than representation. Candidates were vetted, opposition was forbidden, and the Assembly ratified decisions already taken. Citizenship was real, but it came with a price: loyalty to the leader and silence about his failures.

In 1960 the state absorbed the press, bringing the country's newspapers under government ownership and turning editors into civil servants. Al-Ahram, Al-Akhbar, and their rivals kept their mastheads, but their lines were set in the offices of the presidency. The move completed a bargain the regime had been striking since 1952: the Egyptian would be given dignity, schools, hospitals, and jobs, and in return he would surrender the right to dissent. Criticism of the government became criticism of the nation, and the newspapers said so daily.

Education was the most visible dividend. The regime poured money into schools and universities, and in 1961 it made a promise no Egyptian government had dared to make before: free education at every level. To this it added a guarantee that every university graduate would find a government job. For the sons and daughters of peasants in the Delta and Upper Egypt, the classroom became a ladder out of the village. Enrollment soared through the late 1950s, and when census takers fanned out across the country in 1960, they counted a population of some twenty-six million and a nation being rebuilt from the ground up. Land reform broke up the great estates, and the fellah who had bowed to the landlord could at last cast a ballot in the National Union.

It was, in sum, a trade. The Egyptians of the late 1950s received what the old order had denied them: the vote, the school, the clinic, the dignity of being counted. What they gave up was the freedom to question the man who had given it all.

## 1956: The Making of a Symbol

By the middle of the decade, Nasser's ambitions had outgrown Egypt's borders. The Bandung conference had made him a world figure, the Czech arms deal had broken the Western monopoly on Egypt's weapons, and his opposition to the Baghdad Pact had made him the hero of Arab nationalists from Morocco to Iraq. Long before 1956, he was already the most consequential Arab leader of his generation.

Then came the act that sealed his stardom. On July 26, 1956, a week after the last British soldier had left the Canal Zone under the terms of the 1954 treaty, Nasser stood before a vast crowd in Alexandria and announced that Egypt was nationalizing the Suez Canal Company. The full account of what followed belongs to [the Suez Crisis of 1956 explained](/2016/02/01/suez-crisis-of-1956-explained/); for this story, the decisive fact is that the speech made Nasser the most famous Arab in the world overnight. The cheering crowds that greeted the announcement from Algiers to Baghdad were not celebrating a legal transfer of shares. They were celebrating the spectacle of an Arab leader defying the old imperial powers on live radio and surviving, the proof that the age of deference was over.

The Nasser who emerged from the second half of the 1950s was therefore a double figure, and Egyptians experienced him as such. He was the reformer who had given land to the peasants, schools to their children, and dignity to a nation long accustomed to humiliation. He was also the autocrat who had destroyed his rivals, silenced the press, and built a security state that reached into every corner of public life. The two figures were not a contradiction to be resolved but a single historical fact to be understood. The republic Nasser took in the middle of the decade was the product of both: the popular revolution and the cold machinery of control, the cheering crowds and the quiet prisons. That duality would define everything that followed, through the triumphs and the catastrophes of the years ahead, and it is the essential truth of the man who made modern Egypt in his own image.


## The Idea Before the Program

Nasser was not a writer of systems. He left behind speeches, decrees, interviews, and one short book, The Philosophy of the Revolution, published in 1955, rather than anything resembling a doctrine with numbered principles. Yet anyone trying to understand what he stood for between the mid-1950s and the mid-1960s keeps returning to three commitments that shaped nearly every decision he made. The first was that the Arabs were a single nation and that Egypt was its natural center. The second was that the Egyptian state should own and direct the commanding heights of the economy, a program he called Arab socialism. The third was that Egypt should take what it could from both Cold War camps while joining neither, a stance the world learned to call non-alignment. None of the three was original to him. What was distinctive was the way he fused them into a public presence that carried them from Cairo across the region. The word the world used for the fusion was Nasserism, and the plainest definition is the one his policies suggest: pan-Arab unity preached daily, a state-run economy built visibly, and independence from the superpowers performed loudly.

Pan-Arabism was the oldest of the three commitments and the one closest to Nasser's own formation. The idea held that the Arabic-speaking peoples from the Atlantic coast of Morocco to the plains of Iraq formed one nation, al-umma al-arabiyya, whose division into separate states was an artificial product of European empire, above all of the wartime understandings by which Britain and France divided the Ottoman inheritance between them. For Nasser, who had come of age in the street politics of the 1930s and the shock of the 1948 defeat in Palestine, this was not an abstraction. He belonged to a generation that had watched Arab armies lose while Arab kings competed with one another for British favor, and he drew a plain lesson from it: the Arabs were weak because they were divided, and Egypt, the most populous Arab country, with Cairo's newspapers, film studios, and the ancient mosque-university of al-Azhar, was the natural place from which to end the division. The Philosophy of the Revolution gave the instinct a map. Egypt, he wrote, stood at the center of three circles, Arab, African, and Islamic, and its fate was tied to all three. In practice he ranked the Arab circle first. His campaign against the Baghdad Pact of 1955 showed how the creed worked in action. The pact linked Iraq to Turkey, Britain, Pakistan, and eventually the United States in a Western-sponsored alliance, and Nasser opposed it not out of affection for Moscow but because it split the Arabs into Western clients and independent states and because it elevated his rival, Iraq's Nuri al-Said, as Washington's chosen Arab statesman. He denounced the pact on the radio until Iraq stood isolated in Arab opinion and the pact's Arab content withered to a name. That was pan-Arabism as Nasser practiced it: not the slow committee diplomacy of the Arab League, founded in 1945 and already drifting, but a popular creed repeated until ordinary listeners believed that Cairo spoke for them.

The nationalization of the Suez Canal Company on July 26, 1956 gave the creed its defining drama. Nasser announced the decision in Alexandria, live on the radio, and within hours the speech was being replayed from Baghdad to Casablanca. When Britain, France, and Israel attacked Egypt that autumn, the invasion collapsed under American and Soviet pressure, and Nasser emerged with his prestige higher than it had ever been. The episode taught the region a lesson he never tired of repeating: a single Arab state, acting alone and defying the old empires, could win. Suez was an Egyptian decision about an Egyptian canal, but across the Arab world it was received as an Arab victory, and the distinction was the point. Pan-Arabism needed proof that defiance worked, and Nasser had supplied it.

The second commitment announced itself in law in July 1961. On three days of that month Nasser issued the Socialist Decrees, a set of laws that nationalized Egypt's banks, insurance companies, and heavy industry and transferred majority state ownership, most often fifty-one percent, to more than a hundred large companies in trade, transport, and manufacturing. Land reform, begun in 1952 with a ceiling of two hundred feddans per owner, was tightened to one hundred. Workers gained seats on company boards, a national minimum wage was established, and profit-sharing was promised by statute. The following year, in June 1962, the National Congress of Popular Forces ratified the National Charter, which declared a socialism adapted to Arab and Islamic conditions as the basis of the state, and the first five-year plan, covering the years 1960/61 to 1964/65, directed investment toward industry and the public sector on a scale Egypt had never attempted. In December 1962 the single party, the Arab Socialist Union, was created to carry the program into every workplace and village.

What Arab socialism was not mattered as much as what it was. It was not Marxism, and Nasser insisted on the distinction so often that the insistence itself became part of the doctrine. Private property was not abolished; small businesses, shops, workshops, and farms remained in private hands. There was no official dogma of class struggle; the regime spoke instead of dissolving class differences through state action. Religion was not attacked; the regime presented its socialism as consistent with Islam and kept al-Azhar close rather than closing it. The model that emerged was closer to state-directed capitalism, with the state as owner, investor, and employer of first resort, advised by planners in Cairo. The reasons were practical as much as ideological. Foreign capital, British, French, and Belgian, still owned large parts of the Egyptian economy years after the 1952 revolution, and nationalization was the fastest way to break that hold. The old landowning families that had dominated parliamentary life under the monarchy were political enemies as well as economic ones, and land reform was a weapon against them. And Nasser believed, as did most development economists of his generation, that only the state could industrialize a poor country quickly enough to matter. The July 1961 decrees were therefore read across the region as proof that Egypt was serious. Socialism in Nasser's Egypt meant decrees with numbers in them, factories with state-appointed managers, and a plan with a five-year horizon, not slogans.

Arab socialism was also meant to travel. The decrees of 1961 were Egyptian law, but the language in which Nasser defended them, dignity, independence, the end of exploitation, was addressed to every Arab capital where a small elite still owned the land and the banks. The Arab Socialist Union, founded in December 1962 as the country's sole political organization, was presented as a model other Arabs might copy: a mass party open to workers, peasants, soldiers, and intellectuals, organized to carry the revolution into daily life. Few Arab states copied the model in full, and the Syrian experience had already shown what happened when Cairo's blueprints were imposed on different soil. But as an idea, Arab socialism gave Nasser's Egypt something no other Arab regime possessed in those years: a domestic program that looked like a future rather than a past.

The third commitment was the most theatrical and, in the short run, the most successful. At Bandung in April 1955, where twenty-nine Asian and African states gathered in Indonesia, Nasser stood beside India's Jawaharlal Nehru, Yugoslavia's Josip Broz Tito, Indonesia's Sukarno, and China's Zhou Enlai and signed onto a set of principles, national sovereignty, non-aggression, non-interference, and the right of peoples to self-determination, that became the founding charter of non-alignment. In 1961 the stance hardened into an institution at Belgrade, where the Non-Aligned Movement was formally founded, and in October 1964 Cairo hosted the movement's second summit, a visible honor for its most famous Arab spokesman. The substance was simple and it never changed: Egypt would join no Western military pact, sign no Soviet treaty of alliance, and accept help from either side on its own terms. The posture let him condemn the Baghdad Pact as imperialism while taking Soviet arms, and condemn French colonialism in Algeria while hoping for American economic aid. It gave smaller states a language for refusing the Cold War's forced choice, and Nasser became that language's most fluent speaker.

The stance paid material dividends as well. Moscow supplied the arms, the technicians, and eventually the credits and engineers for the Aswan project that Washington had refused to finance. Washington, for its part, unwilling to surrender Egypt entirely to the Soviet camp, kept the door open with food aid and diplomatic contact, calculating that a Nasser who talked to both sides was preferable to a Nasser who talked only to Moscow. Nasser understood the calculation perfectly and exploited it without apology. He could denounce American imperialism in a speech on Monday and receive American grain on Tuesday, and his audiences at home heard the denunciation while his planners counted the grain. Non-alignment, in his hands, was not neutrality. It was leverage.

### How did Nasser play the superpowers against each other?

By refusing to join either bloc after Bandung in 1955, he bid American and Soviet offers against each other. When Washington refused him arms and financing for the Aswan project, he signed the 1955 Czechoslovak arms deal, forcing the West to court him and Moscow to keep supplying him.

In practice the game worked because each side feared losing Egypt to the other. When the United States and Britain would not sell him the weapons he wanted for the army, he turned to Czechoslovakia, acting as Moscow's broker, and the deal announced on September 27, 1955 broke the Western monopoly on arms sales to the Middle East. When Washington withdrew its offer to help finance the Aswan project in July 1956, a rebuff delivered publicly by Secretary of State John Foster Dulles, Nasser answered within days by nationalizing the Suez Canal Company, seizing the revenues to pay for the dam himself. The pattern repeated for a decade: Moscow supplied arms, technicians, and credits, while Washington, unwilling to push Egypt fully into Soviet hands, kept channels open and aid flowing in smaller amounts. Nasser's skill in this theater was to make dependence look like independence. He needed both sides and belonged to neither, and he said so, loudly, which made the arrangement popular at home even when the bills came due.

The Aswan High Dam belonged to this same domestic program, the largest project the state ever attempted, and the full account of its financing, construction, and politics can be found at /2016/03/01/nasser-aswan-dam-arab-socialism/. For this chapter it matters only as the visible proof, rising concrete on the Nile, that the state was building on a national scale.

## The Voice of the Arabs

If the ideology gave Nasser something to say, radio gave him someone to say it to. The Voice of the Arabs, Sawt al-Arab, began broadcasting in July 1953 from a powerful transmitter at Abbassia, on the edge of Cairo. Its signal carried across North Africa and the eastern Mediterranean, and within a few years it was the most listened-to station in the Arabic-speaking world. The programming mixed music, above all the songs of Umm Kulthum, religious broadcasts, news bulletins, and political commentary, but its core was Nasser himself. His speeches were carried live and repeated, his interviews were serialized, and his phrases were picked up by announcers and turned into slogans. He spoke in a colloquial Egyptian Arabic that sounded intimate rather than official, the language of the Cairo street rather than the palace communique, and listeners from Tunis to Baghdad felt addressed directly. The station's most famous broadcaster, Ahmed Said, turned the nightly news into a kind of theater, and in cafes across the region the radio became the furniture around which the evening gathered. As inexpensive transistor sets spread through the 1950s, the audience grew from the educated few who read newspapers to the millions who did not need to read at all.

### How did Nasser use radio to speak to the Arab world?

Through the Voice of the Arabs station, launched in 1953, which broadcast his speeches and commentary across the region in colloquial Egyptian Arabic. Cafes from Damascus to Algiers tuned in by transistor radio, and the station's nightly broadcasts made him a daily presence in Arab homes, turning Egypt's president into the Arab world's most familiar voice.

The distinction mattered because it explains the central fact of Nasser's regional power. His face on a Cairo wall was seen by Egyptians; his voice in a Damascus cafe was heard by Arabs. Posters required a state to print them and a wall to hold them. A voice required only a signal, and the signal crossed borders that armies could not. When Nasser denounced the Baghdad Pact, shopkeepers in Amman heard him the same evening. When he nationalized the Suez Canal, crowds gathered around radios in Beirut before the newspapers had set the type. British and American diplomats in the region filed alarmed reports about the station's influence, which is itself evidence of how seriously the great powers took a medium they could not jam and a speaker they could not answer. Other Arab leaders had palaces, armies, and oil revenues. Nasser had the one instrument that reached into every home, and he used it every day.

The airwaves became a battlefield of their own. Other governments answered the Voice of the Arabs with stations of their own, and the region's listeners grew accustomed to a nightly war of transmitters: Cairo denouncing the conservative monarchies as reactionary, the monarchies' broadcasters denouncing Nasser as a reckless adventurer dragging the Arabs toward ruin. Jordan's King Hussein, Saudi Arabia's rulers, and Tunisia's Habib Bourguiba each had reason to fear a voice that spoke over their heads directly to their subjects. Nasser welcomed the quarrel. Every denunciation from a palace broadcaster confirmed his claim to speak for the street against the throne, and every attempt to jam or answer his signal advertised its importance. The medium suited him perfectly: it required no permission, recognized no border, and flattered the listener by treating him as a citizen of a larger nation than the one on his passport.

The voice was amplified by a cultural empire that Cairo already possessed. Egyptian films played in every Arab cinema, Egyptian songs dominated every Arab radio dial, and Cairo's newspapers, above all Al-Ahram under the editorship of Mohamed Hassanein Heikal from 1957, set the agenda for the Arabic press. Umm Kulthum, the most beloved singer in the Arab world, performed monthly concerts broadcast live across the region, and Nasser made a point of attending, binding the country's cultural glory to his political project. A Damascus merchant might distrust Egyptian bureaucrats and still weep at an Egyptian song. Nasser understood that affection for Cairo's culture could be converted, broadcast by broadcast, into loyalty to Cairo's politics.

The high-water mark of what the voice could build came in February 1958, when Syria joined Egypt in the United Arab Republic. The initiative came from Damascus, where the ruling Baath Party, fearing a Communist takeover, sent a delegation to Cairo to propose union as the lesser danger. Nasser accepted on his own terms, and the terms were absolute: Syria's political parties would dissolve, a single National Union would replace them, the capital would be Cairo, and Nasser would be president of the whole. Syria became the Northern Region, Egypt the Southern Region, and for a brief season the pan-Arab dream had a flag, an anthem, and a territory stretching from the Nile to the Euphrates. Crowds in Damascus wept in the streets when the union was proclaimed. For a time in 1958, with North Yemen joined in a loose confederation under the name of the United Arab States, it seemed that the map itself was being redrawn.

It lasted less than four years. The union failed for reasons that were visible early to anyone in Damascus. Egyptian administrators arrived to run Syrian ministries, Egyptian officers took commands that Syrian officers considered theirs, and the single-party system that Nasser imposed felt in Syria like an occupation by bureaucracy. The July 1961 Socialist Decrees, drafted for Egypt, were extended to Syria, where they struck at the merchant and landowning classes that had supported the union and turned them into its enemies. On September 28, 1961, Syrian army units seized the Damascus radio station and announced Syria's secession, and Nasser, after a brief show of anger, let it go. Cairo kept the name United Arab Republic for years afterward, a flag flown over a country that no longer existed, but the lesson was plain and the region learned it. Unity decreed from Cairo was unity on Cairo's terms, and Cairo's terms were not negotiable. Pan-Arabism survived the collapse as a sentiment, but as a state-building project it never recovered.

Nasser adjusted by changing the instrument. If he could not unite the Arabs under one flag, he would gather their leaders under his chairmanship. In January 1964 he convened the first Arab summit in Cairo, bringing the kings and presidents of the Arab League states together for the first time. The agenda was Palestine: Israel's plans to divert the waters of the Jordan River had alarmed the Arab states, and the summit created a unified Arab military command and called for the organization of the Palestinian people. That call led directly to the founding of the Palestine Liberation Organization at the Palestine National Council in Jerusalem in May 1964. A second summit followed at Alexandria in September 1964, and a third at Casablanca in September 1965. The summits did not produce unity of action, and their resolutions often dissolved on contact with the rivalries they were meant to settle, but they confirmed Cairo as the place where the Arab agenda was set and Nasser as the man who set it. No Arab leader could afford to be absent, and none could afford to be seen opposing him in public.

The voice had limits, and the late 1950s exposed them. In July 1958 Iraq's monarchy was overthrown by Abd al-Karim Qasim, an event Nasser welcomed until Qasim made clear he would not join the United Arab Republic, preferring an Iraq-first nationalism to Cairo's pan-Arabism. In Lebanon that same summer, American marines landed to steady a government that feared Nasserist pressure, and the crisis ended in compromise rather than conversion. Jordan's young King Hussein, whom Cairo's broadcasts had marked for destruction, survived the decade. The pattern was consistent: Nasser's voice could inspire crowds and topple no government by itself, and it could persuade no ruler to surrender sovereignty. His admirers heard a liberator; the rulers who survived him heard a warning, and governed accordingly.

The voice also carried into the anti-colonial struggles that made Nasser a hero beyond the Arab world. His support for Algeria's National Liberation Front was the most consequential: Egypt supplied arms, training camps, and diplomatic backing through the war, France protested furiously, and when Algeria became independent in July 1962, Nasser's standing in Africa was secured. In September 1962, after the death of Imam Ahmad of Yemen and the coup that brought Abdullah al-Sallal to power, Nasser committed Egyptian troops to defend the new republican regime against royalist forces backed by Saudi Arabia. The Yemen war became the largest foreign deployment in Egypt's modern history; Egyptian and Western estimates put the peak force at between fifty thousand and seventy thousand troops, and the fighting continued through 1967. It drained the treasury, cost thousands of Egyptian lives, and turned Yemen into the battlefield of what journalists called the Arab Cold War between Nasser's Egypt and Faisal's Saudi Arabia. Nasser also backed liberation movements further afield, in the Congo and across sub-Saharan Africa, and Cairo became a haven for exiles and guerrilla leaders of a dozen nationalities. The policy was expensive and often reckless, but it was consistent: wherever empire was fighting to hold on, Nasser wanted Egypt's voice, and sometimes its soldiers, on the other side.

By the mid-1960s, then, the three commitments of the ideology had each acquired an institution. Pan-Arabism had the summits, however fractious. Arab socialism had the decrees, the charter, the plan, and the single party. Non-alignment had the movement and its Cairo summit. And above them all stood the voice, the daily presence in the cafe and the home that made the rest credible. The limits were already visible to anyone who looked: Syria was gone, Yemen was bleeding the army and the budget, and the economy strained under the weight of the state's ambitions. But in these years the project held, and the reason it held was the one this chapter has argued. Nasser's power in the Arab world never rested primarily on Egypt's armies, which were untested, or on its economy, which was poor. It rested on the fact that from Damascus to Algiers, millions of people who had never seen Cairo felt that they knew the man who spoke from it, and felt, when he spoke, that he was speaking for them.


## The Road to June 1967

The catastrophe of June 1967 did not arrive as a single thunderclap. It gathered over the two years that followed the collapse of the union with Syria, as the regional order Nasser had built began to shift beneath him. By 1965 his attention was split between the expensive and draining intervention in Yemen, where Egyptian troops remained bogged down in support of the republican government against Saudi backed royalists, and a rivalry with Syria that had grown sharper since Damascus broke away from the United Arab Republic. The Syrians, now ruled by an even more radical Baath leadership than Nasser's own, accused Cairo of insufficient militancy toward Israel. Nasser, stung by the charge, escalated his rhetoric rather than his readiness, a habit that had served him before and that would now betray him.

The crisis that ignited the war began in May 1967, when Egypt requested the withdrawal of the United Nations Emergency Force from Sinai and the Gaza Strip. The force had been stationed there since the Suez aftermath of 1956 as a buffer, and Nasser's decision to remove it was read across the region as a test of his seriousness. United Nations Secretary General U Thant complied, and the buffer vanished. Days later, on 22 May, Nasser closed the Straits of Tiran to Israeli shipping, cutting off Israel's access to the Gulf of Aqaba and its Red Sea port. In Cairo the move was cheered as the recovery of Arab honor. In Tel Aviv it was understood as a casus belli, since Israeli leaders had said plainly for years that closing the straits meant war.

What followed was a diplomatic countdown that Nasser seems to have believed he could manage. He counted on Soviet backing, on the Arab armies that were now nominally under a joint command, and on the conviction that Israel would not strike first. On 5 June 1967 Israel struck first anyway. In a preemptive air operation launched at dawn, the Israeli Air Force destroyed most of the Egyptian, Jordanian, and Syrian air forces on the ground within hours. What happened next was a rout. Israeli armor advanced across Sinai toward the Suez Canal, reached the Old City of Jerusalem, and pushed onto the Golan Heights. In six days the map of the Middle East was redrawn. Egypt lost the entire Sinai Peninsula. The defeat was not only military. It destroyed the myth of Arab military parity with Israel that Nasser's rhetoric had sustained for a decade, and it humiliated him before the Arab public that had made him its champion.

## Resignation and Return

On the evening of 9 June 1967, Nasser appeared on Egyptian television and radio and did what almost no modern Arab leader had ever done. He took responsibility for the defeat and announced his resignation from the presidency. He spoke of assuming full responsibility for the setback, as he called it, and he named Vice President Zakaria Mohieddin as his successor. The speech was dignified, unsparing, and brief. It acknowledged the catastrophe without fully explaining how it had happened, and it contained no hint that the resignation was a performance. Those close to Nasser later said he meant it, at least in the moment of delivery, and that he was physically and emotionally broken by the scale of the loss.

What happened next astonished observers then and still puzzles historians. Within hours of the broadcast, crowds poured into the streets of Cairo, Alexandria, and cities across the Arab world. Hundreds of thousands of Egyptians marched toward the presidential palace demanding that Nasser stay. The demonstrations were enormous, spontaneous in appearance, and sustained through the night. On 10 June, Nasser withdrew his resignation and returned to office, telling the nation he would remain to lead the removal of the effects of aggression, the phrase that became the official formula for recovering the lost territory.

The sincerity of the demonstrations has been debated ever since. Some historians argue that the crowds were genuine, the product of fifteen years in which Nasser had become the living embodiment of Arab dignity for millions who had never known such a figure. Others contend that the security apparatus helped organize the turnouts, and that the resignation itself may have been a calculated gamble to reconfirm his mandate at the moment of greatest weakness. The evidence supports a mixed reading. Foreign correspondents present in Cairo described crowds that looked authentically distraught, people weeping in the streets, while Egyptian officials later admitted that state institutions facilitated the mobilization. Both can be true. A leader can orchestrate a demonstration and still be loved by many of the demonstrators.

Why his standing survived the catastrophe is one of the central questions of the Nasser era, and the answer lies in what he had come to represent. The defeat of 1967 was his failure, and every Egyptian knew it. Yet no credible alternative existed. The army was discredited by its own collapse. The old parties had been abolished. The Muslim Brotherhood was shattered. The communists were imprisoned. Nasser alone possessed the legitimacy to speak for Egypt and for the Arabs, and the crowds of June 1967 were, among other things, a refusal to let that legitimacy be confiscated by a military defeat. He had also, paradoxically, been strengthened by his honesty. A ruler who admitted fault, in a region where rulers never did, earned a measure of forgiveness that a denier would not have received.

## Khartoum and the War of Attrition

Three months after the defeat, Nasser traveled to Khartoum for the Arab summit of August and September 1967. The gathering produced the famous three noes: no peace with Israel, no recognition of Israel, no negotiations with Israel. The resolution committed the Arab states to collective action and, crucially, secured financial pledges from Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and Libya to the frontline states. Egypt received the funds it needed to rebuild its shattered army. For Nasser, Khartoum was a qualified recovery. He accepted the financial lifeline from the conservative monarchies he had spent a decade denouncing, which some critics read as the end of his revolutionary phase. He also preserved his position at the head of the Arab coalition, which had been the real prize all along.

The final years of his rule were defined by the War of Attrition, the grinding conflict along the Suez Canal from 1968 until the ceasefire of August 1970. Egyptian artillery pounded Israeli positions on the eastern bank of the canal, Israeli commandos raided deep into Egypt, and the civilian population of the canal cities was evacuated as the zone became a battlefield. The war served several purposes. It kept the cause of recovering Sinai alive, it gave the rebuilt Egyptian army combat experience, and it allowed Nasser to tell his public that Egypt was still fighting. It also carried a heavy cost. Israeli deep penetration bombing raids struck targets in the Egyptian interior in early 1970, and only the arrival of Soviet air defense batteries and pilots stabilized the situation. Nasser had spent the last years of his life as dependent on Moscow as he had once been independent of every foreign power, a dependence he accepted because the alternative was strategic paralysis.

## The Fall of Amer

The Six-Day War of June 1967 destroyed more than the Egyptian air force on the ground at its Sinai bases. It destroyed the closest friendship of Nasser's life. Abdel Hakim Amer had been at Nasser's side since their days as young officers plotting against the monarchy. He had commanded the armed forces through the Suez crisis and the Yemen war, served as vice president, and built the officer corps into his personal fiefdom. When the Israeli strike of June 5, 1967, wiped out Egyptian air power in hours and the army collapsed in Sinai, each man blamed the other. Nasser held Amer responsible for an army rotten with favoritism. Amer held Nasser responsible for the decisions, above all the closure of the Straits of Tiran, that had made war unavoidable.

Nasser announced his resignation on June 9, 1967, and withdrew it the next day after crowds filled the streets demanding he stay. The reprieve did not extend to Amer. The president moved to break the military's grip on the state, ordering a purge of the officer corps that Amer treated as an attack on his own authority. In August 1967 Nasser placed his old friend under house arrest. On September 14, 1967, Amer was found dead, officially by his own hand. His family rejected the finding and maintained that he had been murdered, and historians have never settled the question. What is certain is that the death closed the most intimate political partnership of the revolution.

The reckoning continued into the new year. In February 1968, military courts handed down light sentences to the air force commanders blamed for the June disaster, and the verdicts detonated a wave of protest. Students at Cairo University marched, workers at the Helwan factories walked out, and for days that February and March the streets carried a demand the regime had not heard in fifteen years: accountability. The demonstrators were not calling for Nasser's fall. They were calling on him to punish the guilty and mean it. The president who had built his authority on the promise of dignity could not afford to be seen shielding the men who had squandered it.

On March 30, 1968, Nasser answered with a program that promised to rebuild the state from its foundations. The declaration pledged a new constitution, elected councils at every level, an end to arbitrary detention, the rule of law, and a national effort to reconstruct the armed forces for the battle to recover Sinai. It was the most liberal document Nasser had ever issued, and also the most desperate: an attempt to save the revolution by admitting, in the careful language of official Egypt, that it had failed its own people. Whether the program could have delivered what it promised remains an open question. Nasser had less than three years to live, and the wreckage of June 1967 would outlast him.

## The Authoritarian Ruler Beside the Hero

The charismatic champion of the Arabs was also the builder of one of the most thorough security states in modern Middle Eastern history, and no honest account of Nasser can separate the two figures. The system he constructed after 1952 rested on the General Investigations Directorate, the internal intelligence service commonly called the mukhabarat, directed for most of the era by Salah Nasr. Alongside it stood military intelligence and a succession of presidential security organs. Together they penetrated political parties, unions, universities, the press, and the officer corps. Informers were everywhere. The press was nationalized and turned into an instrument of the state. Opposition parties were abolished, and after the brief experiment with the Liberation Rally and the National Union, even the single party served more as a mobilization machine than a forum for dissent.

### How did the security apparatus under Nasser work?

The General Investigations Directorate under Salah Nasr ran detention, surveillance, and interrogation. Suspects were held without charge in prisons and security offices, where beatings and other abuses occurred. Historians cite prisoner memoirs and trial records as evidence. The apparatus served the regime by intimidating organized opposition, especially the Brotherhood, leftists, and communists.

The Muslim Brotherhood bore the heaviest weight of this system. The failed Manshiya shooting of October 1954 became Nasser's pretext to crush the movement: thousands arrested, the organization banned, its leaders tried by military courts. The crackdown established the pattern of his rule toward organized Islamist opposition: zero tolerance, enforced by the security courts.

A decade later the pattern repeated. In 1965 the regime announced the discovery of a new Brotherhood conspiracy to overthrow the government. Mass arrests followed, and the trials of 1965 and 1966 ended with the execution of the organization's leading ideologue, Sayyid Qutb, in August 1966. Historians debate the seriousness of the alleged plot. Some argue that the conspiracy was real and dangerous, pointing to the regime's intelligence files. Others contend that the threat was exaggerated or manufactured to justify a fresh purge and to distract from economic difficulties, pointing to the thinness of the evidence presented at trial. What the record clearly shows is that the trials were political, that confessions were extracted under duress, and that Qutb's execution transformed him into a martyr whose prison writings would inspire Islamist movements for generations. The regime's attempt to destroy the Brotherhood instead gave it a theology of resistance.

The security apparatus did not confine itself to Islamists. Communists were imprisoned in waves, particularly in the late 1950s, even as Nasser was adopting socialist economic policies. Leftist intellectuals, liberal critics, and military officers suspected of disloyalty all passed through the detention system. Torture was not an aberration but a routine instrument of interrogation, documented in the memoirs of former prisoners and acknowledged in the work of historians who have studied the period's court records. The scale of detention is disputed in its exact numbers, which range in different accounts from the tens of thousands to higher figures, but the existence of a vast system of political imprisonment is not in dispute.

This is the balance the record demands. The same Nasser who electrified millions with his speeches signed the orders that filled the prisons. The same regime that redistributed land and built the Aswan High Dam maintained cells in which men were beaten for their opinions. His defenders argue that the authoritarian measures were the price of the revolution's survival in a region of coups and foreign plots, and that Egypt in the 1950s and 1960s offered no stable democratic alternative. His critics answer that the security state was not a regrettable necessity but the core of the system, and that it deformed Egyptian political life for decades. The historian's task is not to choose between the hero and the jailer but to hold both in view, because the Egyptians who cheered him in the streets and the Egyptians who feared a knock on the door often lived in the same households.

The limits of his popularity sharpen this picture. Abroad, among Arabs from Morocco to Iraq, Nasser's prestige after 1967 remained extraordinary. He was the symbol of a cause that transcended borders, and the defeat, being shared, did not diminish the symbol. At home the picture was more complicated. Egyptians respected him, many loved him, but they also lived under the daily reality of his rule: the shortages, the censorship, the fear. The crowds of June 1967 were real, but so was the silence of Egyptians who had learned not to speak. His popularity was deepest where his power was least direct, a paradox that defined his final years.

## The Last Mediation and Death

In September 1970, Nasser was drawn into the crisis that would kill him. In Jordan, King Hussein's army was crushing the Palestinian guerrilla organizations in the fighting that became known as Black September. Nasser, as the elder statesman of the Arab world, convened an emergency Arab summit in Cairo to mediate between the king and the Palestinian leadership. The negotiations were grueling. Nasser worked through the night, shuttling between delegations, smoking heavily, his health already fragile from years of diabetes and heart disease. On 27 September he secured a ceasefire agreement. It was his last diplomatic act.

The next day, 28 September 1970, Nasser suffered a massive heart attack and died. He was fifty two years old. The news broke over Arab radio and television, and the reaction was unlike anything the region had seen. In Cairo, millions poured into the streets for the funeral procession. The crowds were so vast that the coffin was nearly overwhelmed, and foreign dignitaries watched in disbelief as the city seemed to dissolve in grief. Across the Arab world, from Beirut to Baghdad to Algiers, people wept openly. Schools and businesses closed. The outpouring testified to what Nasser had become: not merely a president of Egypt but the embodiment of an era, the voice that had told Arabs they mattered. His vice president, Anwar Sadat, succeeded him, inheriting a country exhausted by war, a shattered economy, and a legend no successor could fill.

## The Verdict

The thesis that Nasser was the voice of the Arabs survives the evidence, but only if the voice is understood as both a promise and a warning. He gave millions of Arabs a language of dignity at a moment when dignity was the scarcest commodity in the region. The nationalization of Suez, the defiance of the old empires, the insistence that Arabs were a single nation with a single destiny, these were not illusions. They were political facts that shaped a generation, and no accounting of his failures can erase the genuine liberation that many Arabs felt in his years. He took a country that had been a British protectorate in all but name and made it the center of world attention. That achievement was real, and it explains why his funeral drew millions who had every reason to know his faults.

Against this must be set the full weight of the authoritarian record. He built a state in which dissent was a crime, in which the prisons held thousands of his fellow citizens, in which torture was a tool of government. He crushed the Muslim Brotherhood twice, and in doing so helped create the martyr, Sayyid Qutb, whose ideas would haunt the region long after Nasser's death. He led Egypt into the catastrophe of 1967 through a combination of brinkmanship and miscalculation, losing Sinai and with it the strategic depth of the country. He left an economy strained by war and state control, and a political system with no mechanism for succession beyond the choice of a loyal deputy. These are not footnotes to the heroism. They are the other half of the ledger, and any verdict that minimizes them is hagiography.

The honest judgment is that Nasser was the most consequential Arab leader of the twentieth century and that his consequence was double edged. He proved that Arab dignity could be asserted against the great powers, and he proved that such assertion, unmoored from institutions, military realism, and political freedom, ends in defeat and repression. The voice of the Arabs was real. It spoke for the hopes of millions. But a voice is not a program, and when the voice fell silent on 28 September 1970, it left behind a region that had learned to dream in his cadences and had not learned to govern in his absence. That is his triumph and his indictment, inseparable, and history has no obligation to choose between them.

## Study and Revision

The arc to carry away runs from the miscalculations of May 1967 through the six-day defeat, the resignation of 9 June and its withdrawal amid the demonstrations, the Khartoum summit's three noes, and the grinding War of Attrition that filled his final years. Beside it stands the security state: the mukhabarat under Salah Nasr, the Manshiya shooting of 1954 and the crushing of the Brotherhood, the trials and the execution of Sayyid Qutb in 1966, and the prisons that held communists and critics alongside Islamists. Remember that his popularity survived the defeat because he embodied a cause larger than any battle, and that it ran deepest abroad, where his power was least felt. Remember the mediation in Amman and Cairo in September 1970, the ceasefire of 27 September, and the heart attack of 28 September that ended the era. The verdict to hold is the double one: the champion of Arab dignity and the authoritarian who jailed his opponents, the voice that inspired millions and the ruler whose miscalculations cost Egypt Sinai. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was President Nasser?

Gamal Abdel Nasser was the Egyptian officer who became the defining Arab leader of the twentieth century. Born in Alexandria in 1918, he joined the army, founded the secret Free Officers movement, and led the 1952 coup that ended the monarchy. After removing President Muhammad Naguib in 1954, he became the face of the new republic and served as president from the mid-1950s until his death in 1970. He championed pan-Arab nationalism, non-alignment in the Cold War, and a state-led economy. His speeches, broadcast across the Arab world, made him a hero to millions far beyond Egypt, even after the crushing defeat of 1967. His funeral in Cairo drew millions of mourners.

### Q: Why is Nasser Egypt's most important modern leader?

No Egyptian leader after Muhammad Ali reshaped the country and the region as deeply as Nasser. He ended the monarchy, expelled the British military presence, and gave Egypt a republican identity that still stands. He made Egypt the center of Arab politics, turning Cairo into the capital of pan-Arabism. His domestic program of land reform, nationalization, and public schooling changed Egyptian society at its roots. Even his failures were historic: the 1967 defeat with Israel redrew the map and the politics of the region. Because he combined Egyptian rule with a pan-Arab mission, his importance extends far beyond Egypt's borders, which is why historians place him alongside the great twentieth-century revolutionaries.

### Q: What were Nasser's core ideas?

Nasser's thought rested on three pillars. The first was pan-Arabism: the belief that Arabs formed one nation that should unite, with Egypt as its natural leader. The second was Arab socialism: the belief that the state should control the commanding heights of the economy, redistribute land and wealth, and guarantee every citizen education, health care, and employment. The third was non-alignment: the belief that Egypt should refuse to join either Cold War bloc and instead use rivalry between Washington and Moscow to Egypt's advantage. Together these ideas formed Nasserism, a program of dignity, independence, and social justice that won him mass devotion across the Arab world.

### Q: How did Nasser come to power?

Nasser came to power through the army. As a young officer he founded the clandestine Free Officers movement in 1949, a network of junior officers who blamed the monarchy and the British for Egypt's humiliation in the 1948 Palestine war. On 23 July 1952 the Free Officers seized Cairo in a nearly bloodless coup, forced King Farouk to abdicate, and set up the Revolutionary Command Council. Muhammad Naguib served as the republic's first president while Nasser, the real power, consolidated control. In late 1954 Nasser removed Naguib, took charge of the republic, and had his rule confirmed by a 1956 plebiscite, becoming the president who would dominate Egypt and the Arab world for the next decade and a half.

### Q: What was the ideology of Nasserism?

Nasserism was the blend of nationalism and socialism that Nasser preached and practiced. Its nationalism was pan-Arab: it held that the Arab peoples were one nation divided by colonial borders and foreign bases, and that Egypt's mission was to lead their liberation. Its socialism was Arab socialism: the state would own large industry, banks, and utilities; land reform would break the old landlord class; and the government would guarantee free schooling, health care, and jobs. Its foreign policy was non-alignment: Egypt would take aid from both superpowers without joining either camp. Nasserism was more a set of emotional commitments than a precise theory, and its power lay in its promise that Arabs could be masters of their own fate.

### Q: How did Nasser die?

Nasser died of a heart attack in Cairo on 28 September 1970. He was only fifty-two and had suffered from heart disease and diabetes for years, conditions he hid from the public. He collapsed just after mediating an Arab summit called to stop the civil war in Jordan between King Hussein's army and Palestinian guerrillas, a crisis that had exhausted him. His death shocked the Arab world. Millions poured into the streets of Cairo for his funeral, one of the largest gatherings of mourners in history, and crowds wept in Damascus, Baghdad, and Algiers. His successor, Anwar Sadat, kept the body lying in state for days while grief rolled across the region.

### Q: Why was Nasser popular across the Arab world?

Nasser spoke to Arabs as one people, and millions answered. Through the Voice of the Arabs radio station and his spellbinding speeches, he gave voice to the humiliations Arabs felt under colonial rule and the dreams they held of unity and dignity. He stood up to the West, expelled British forces from the Canal Zone, and took aid from the Soviets without surrendering sovereignty. For ordinary Arabs from Morocco to Iraq, he was proof that an Arab leader could defy the great powers and win. Even the 1967 defeat did not destroy this bond, because his popularity rested on emotion and symbolism as much as on policy, on what he represented rather than only on what he achieved.

### Q: What was Nasser's legacy?

Nasser's legacy is the modern Arab political order he built and the contradictions he left behind. He created the Egyptian republic, ended British occupation, and made pan-Arabism the language of regional politics for a generation. His socialism gave millions of Egyptians schools, jobs, and land, but it also built a bloated state and a crushing bureaucracy. His authoritarianism jailed opponents and silenced the press, setting patterns his successors kept. The 1967 defeat discredited pan-Arabism as a program even as Nasser himself survived it. Historians remember him as the champion who restored Arab dignity and the autocrat who taught the region how power works.

### Q: What was Nasser's childhood and education like?

Nasser was born in Alexandria in 1918 to a lower-middle-class family; his father worked for the postal service, and the family moved between Alexandria, Cairo, and the village of Khatatba. As a schoolboy he joined anti-British student demonstrations and developed a lifelong hatred of the occupation and the palace. He first enrolled in law school but dropped out, then entered the Royal Military Academy in 1937 and was commissioned as an officer in 1938. The army gave a poor boy from the provinces a career and a brotherhood, and it exposed him to Egypt's humiliations firsthand. His education was less the classroom than the street demonstration and the barracks.

### Q: How did the 1948 Palestine war shape Nasser?

The 1948 war was Nasser's political education. Serving as a major in the Egyptian expeditionary force, he fought at Falluja, where his unit was surrounded for months by Israeli forces while Cairo's corrupt leadership failed to resupply it. He watched defective weapons fail, saw officers sell supplies, and concluded that Egypt's defeat came from the monarchy and the British, not from the soldiers. The experience turned his vague nationalism into a mission: Egypt needed a revolution from within before it could face its enemies. Nearly all the Free Officers shared this lesson, which is why 1948 became the founding memory of the 1952 coup.

### Q: What role did Nasser's army cell play in his rise?

The Free Officers cell was Nasser's ladder to power. He founded it in 1949 as a secret network of junior officers bound by hatred of the palace, the British, and the corruption exposed in Palestine. Through cells in each unit, Nasser built a parallel chain of command loyal to him personally, organized around study groups and whispered recruitment. When the coup came in July 1952, these cells seized communications, barracks, and the radio station within hours. The cell gave Nasser an organized force when he had no party and no public following, and it made him the indispensable man of the new regime from the first night.

### Q: How did Nasser build the one-party state?

Nasser built his political machine by replacing Egypt's old parties with organizations he controlled. After banning all parties in 1953, he created the Liberation Rally as a mass front, then the National Union in 1957, and finally the Arab Socialist Union in 1962 as the sole legal party. Each body mobilized peasants, workers, and students behind the regime while channeling all political life through Nasser's office. Elections became plebiscites with a single candidate. The press was nationalized, unions were folded into the state, and parliament became a rubber stamp. The result was a system where participation was mandatory but choice was absent.

### Q: Why did the United Arab Republic with Syria collapse?

The United Arab Republic, the 1958 union of Egypt and Syria, collapsed in 1961 because Nasser tried to rule Syria as an Egyptian province. Syrian officers and merchants resented Egyptian domination, the nationalization decrees, and the police-state methods imported from Cairo. When Syrian army units staged a coup in September 1961 and declared secession, Nasser first threatened to crush it, then let it go, calling the union a lesson learned. The failure exposed the gap between pan-Arab dreams and political reality: Syrians wanted partnership, Nasser offered absorption, and the Arab world's first unity experiment died in three years.

### Q: Why did Nasser oppose Western military alliances in the region?

Nasser saw Western alliances as colonialism in new dress. When Britain and America pushed the Baghdad Pact in 1955 to bind Middle Eastern states into an anti-Soviet bloc, Nasser campaigned against it as a scheme to keep Arab armies under Western command. He argued that Arab security should rest on Arab unity, not on treaties signed in London. His opposition won him popularity across the region and pushed him toward Soviet arms, since the West then refused him weapons. The fight over the pact was Nasser's first great pan-Arab battle, and it established him as the leader who said no to the West on behalf of all Arabs.

### Q: What did Nasser achieve at the 1955 Bandung conference?

At Bandung in 1955, Nasser joined Nehru of India, Tito of Yugoslavia, and Sukarno of Indonesia in founding the non-aligned movement. For a relatively unknown Egyptian colonel, sharing a stage with the giants of the post-colonial world was a triumph of recognition. He returned to Cairo with a doctrine, non-alignment, and a reputation as a world statesman, not just an Arab one. Bandung gave his foreign policy a name and a club, and it taught him that Egypt could matter by refusing to choose sides. The conference was the moment Nasser stepped onto the global stage.

### Q: What happened to the Muslim Brotherhood under Nasser?

The Brotherhood went from ally to enemy to crushed underground. It had supported the 1952 coup, but broke with Nasser over his refusal to build an Islamic state. After a 1954 assassination attempt on Nasser at Manshiya Square, which the regime blamed on the Brotherhood, Nasser banned the movement, executed its leaders, and jailed thousands in desert camps. A second wave of trials and executions followed in 1965 and 1966, including the hanging of the ideologue Sayyid Qutb. The crackdown drove the movement underground and into exile, where its ideas hardened, but inside Egypt the Brotherhood was broken for a generation.

### Q: Why did Nasser resign after the 1967 defeat and then return?

Nasser resigned on 9 June 1967, taking personal responsibility for the catastrophe of the Six-Day War, in which Israel destroyed the Egyptian air force and seized Sinai in days. He expected the gesture to be refused, and it was: millions poured into the streets of Cairo and Arab capitals begging him to stay. The demonstrations were genuine, driven by fear that his fall would mean foreign domination, and the regime did nothing to stop them. The next day he withdrew the resignation. He survived because the Arab world needed a symbol of dignity more than it needed accountability, and no rival could replace him.

### Q: What was Nasser's relationship with the Soviet Union?

Nasser's relationship with Moscow was a marriage of convenience, not of ideology. After the West refused him arms and dam financing, he bought Soviet-bloc weapons through Czechoslovakia in 1955 and later took Soviet engineers, loans, and advisers. The Soviets rebuilt his army after 1967 and defended him diplomatically. But Nasser never became a communist: he jailed Egyptian communists, rejected Marxist atheism, and kept his freedom to scold Moscow in public. He used the superpower rivalry the way non-alignment prescribed, taking what he needed from the East while refusing to become its client.
