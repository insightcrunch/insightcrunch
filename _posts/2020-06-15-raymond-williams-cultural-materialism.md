Raymond Williams was a Welsh critic who, writing from the 1950s onward, reformulated the oldest problem in Marxist cultural theory by refusing its terms. Cultural materialism, the position he eventually named, holds that writing, printing, publishing, and broadcasting are material production in their own right, with their own means, labour, and relations, rather than a decorative layer raised above an economic base that does the real work. Once culture is production, the question of how a superstructure relates to a base loses the shape that made it seem compulsory, and a problem that had organised decades of argument is not solved but dissolved. That single move, the dissolution, is the centre of his achievement, and this article examines it alongside the three analytical tools that practising critics actually carry away from his work: the structure of feeling, the triad of dominant, residual, and emergent, and his rebuilt account of determination, in which conditions bound a field and press upon the agents inside it.

![A border country signal box and a tutor's desk of marked essays facing one another across a valley, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-86.webp)

Williams matters for a further reason that the summaries often miss. His concepts were not forged in a seminar room and then applied to experience from a height. They were developed across fifteen years of teaching adults whose lives the inherited vocabulary of culture could not describe, and the pressure of that classroom is inside the theory itself. When he insisted that culture is ordinary, he was reporting what the teaching had taught him: that meanings are made by everyone, in workplaces and kitchens and chapels and union branches, and that a theory of culture which treats most of that making as a pale reflection of something else has failed before it begins. The tradition he worked within, and partly against, is surveyed in the [Marxist cultural theory guide](/2020/06/01/marxist-cultural-theory-guide/), which places his intervention among the others competing for the same ground.

The problem he dissolved deserves a moment of respect before its dissolution is described, because a reframing is only impressive if the original difficulty was genuine. Marxist accounts of culture had inherited a spatial picture in which a society rests on an economic base, and law, politics, art, and ideas form a superstructure erected upon it. The picture promised an explanation of why cultures take the forms they do, yet it generated a standing embarrassment. Nobody could specify the mechanism by which the base produced the superstructure without either reducing poems to disguised economics or retreating into vague talk of reflection. Williams concluded that the embarrassment belonged to the picture rather than to the phenomena, and that the remedy was to examine what cultural work actually is: people writing, setting type, rehearsing, filming, transmitting, using materials and institutions and skills, under definite conditions, for definite audiences. The full anatomy of the metaphor he refused is given in [base and superstructure explained](/2010/02/01/base-and-superstructure-explained/), and the reader who wants the problem in its classic form should go there, since this article is concerned with the man who stepped sideways out of it.

## The Formation: A Signalman's Son in the Adult Education Classroom

He was born in 1921 in Pandy, a village in the Welsh border country, into a family whose circumstances placed him at a crossing point that his whole work would later theorise. His father was a railway signalman, a skilled worker on the Great Western line, active in his union and in the Labour politics of the border. The household was literate, political, and chapel-adjacent without being enclosed by any of those worlds, and the boy grew up watching a working community conduct a rich cultural life that the official definitions of culture, centred on metropolitan art and letters, simply did not register. That mismatch between lived culture and accredited culture is the seed of everything that followed, and it is why the biography in his case is not decoration around the theory but part of its evidence.

A scholarship carried him to Cambridge in 1939 to read English, one of the generation of grammar school and scholarship boys for whom the university was both an opening and an estrangement. The war interrupted the degree. He served in the army, in an anti-tank regiment, through the campaigns in France and Germany, an experience that placed the border village boy inside a vast conscript institution where class, region, and education were mixed under pressure. He returned to finish his studies after demobilisation, and the postwar settlement, with its new universities settlement still unbuilt and its adult education movement expanding, offered him a vocation that fitted his formation exactly. From 1946 he worked as a tutor in adult education, teaching literature and related subjects to working-class students in Sussex towns and villages, under the extramural arrangements that connected universities to the Workers' Educational Association.

Fifteen years of that teaching, from 1946 until his return to Cambridge in 1961, formed the concepts before the books announced them. Adult students do not receive a canon passively. They test every text against their own experience of work, family, war, and locality, and they ask, with a directness that undergraduates rarely risk, what a poem or a novel has to do with the life they are actually living. A tutor who survives that questioning learns two things. He learns that the meanings made inside ordinary life are dense, articulate, and theoretically interesting, and he learns that the available critical vocabulary, which divided culture into a high tradition and a mass residue, had no words for what his students already knew. Williams's mature insistence on beginning from lived experience, and his suspicion of any category that arrives before the experience it claims to describe, were earned at those evening class tables.

### Why did the adult education years matter to the theory?

The classroom supplied both his evidence and his method. Students tested every inherited category against their own working lives, so the concepts he built had to describe meanings made outside accredited institutions. Teaching adults who read seriously without credentials convinced him that culture is made everywhere, and that conviction became the foundation of cultural materialism.

The famous essay of 1958 in which he declared that culture is ordinary grew directly from that setting. Written for a symposium on working-class culture, it refused two condescensions at once: the conservative lament that industrial civilisation had destroyed a finer older culture, and the left condescension that treated working-class life as a problem awaiting cultural rescue. Against both, the essay argued that a culture is a whole way of life, made and remade by the people living it, and that the meanings generated in a working-class community are not a deficient copy of meanings generated elsewhere. The phrase became a slogan, which has somewhat dulled its edge, but in context it was a methodological instruction. Begin where meanings are actually produced. Refuse to grade them in advance by the institution that houses them.

The book of the same year, Culture and Society, performed the complementary scholarly labour. It recovered a tradition of English writing about culture running from the Romantics through the Victorian sages to the twentieth century, and it showed that the very concept of culture, in its modern senses, had emerged as a response to industrialism: a word and an idea elaborated to name what industrial change was doing to human relations. The argument mattered twice over. It demonstrated that the concept itself had a history, that culture was not a timeless standard but a keyword whose meanings shifted with the society using it, and it placed the industrial transformation, rather than any decline of taste, at the centre of the story. A later vocabulary study would turn that insight into a method, but the historical sense of concepts, the refusal to treat them as fixed counters, was already present in 1958.

The Long Revolution, published in 1961, the year he took up his Cambridge lectureship, pushed further. It argued that the changes usually narrated separately, the democratic revolution, the industrial revolution, and the cultural revolution of expanding literacy and communication, formed a single long transformation, and it tried to describe the institutions of a society, educational, economic, communicative, as one connected process. Inside that large argument sat a small conceptual invention that would outlast the book containing it. Trying to name the peculiar quality of lived experience in a period, the sense of life as it is actually being lived, prior to its hardening into institutions and stated doctrines, he revived and sharpened a phrase he had first tried in film writing earlier in the 1950s: the structure of feeling. The phrase answered to something his adult students had shown him, that a generation inhabits a world in ways it cannot yet state, and that art often registers the unstated before any programme does.

Cambridge gave him a different audience and a slower argument. Appointed to lecture in English and elected to a fellowship at Jesus College, later holding the university's chair of drama, he spent the following decades producing a body of work that moved between literary history, drama, the novel, media, and theory without accepting the boundaries between them. Drama from Ibsen to Brecht examined the forms of modern theatre as structures of feeling made visible in technique. Modern Tragedy argued that tragedy was not a dead classical form but a continuing structure through which a liberal and then a revolutionary culture processed its contradictions. His history of the English novel, published in 1970, traced how communities that could be known whole gave way, under urban and industrial pressure, to societies that exceeded any single observer's knowledge, a problem he named through the idea of the knowable community. Each study used literary form as evidence about social experience, which is precisely the procedure cultural materialism would later justify in theoretical terms.

Alongside the scholarship ran a political formation that the concepts also carry. Williams was a central figure of the British New Left that formed after 1956, when the twin shocks of the Hungarian rising and the Suez invasion broke a generation's inherited allegiances and sent it looking for a socialism rebuilt from democratic and cultural materials. He helped to found the journals and campaigns of that milieu, contributed to the May Day Manifesto of the later 1960s, and remained, through essays and interventions, a socialist intellectual of an independent kind, loyal to the movement's questions and impatient with its orthodoxies. The setting is described in [New Left Marxism explained](/2016/03/01/new-left-marxism-explained/), and it matters here because the New Left's founding wager, that culture and education were not decorations on politics but part of its substance, is the political form of the conviction his teaching had already given him.

### What role did the New Left play in his formation?

The New Left gave his classroom convictions a political home and an argumentative audience. Formed after the crises of 1956, it treated culture and education as substantive political ground rather than decoration, which matched the lesson of his adult teaching exactly. Its journals and manifestos were the milieu in which his concepts were first tested publicly.

Two features of the formation should be held together, because separating them produces the standard misreading of the man. The first is the border. Williams wrote repeatedly, in essays and in his fiction, about the border country as a place where categories meet and fail: Welsh and English, rural and industrial, working-class home and scholarship university, the knowable village and the unknowable metropolis. His novel Border Country, published in 1960, dramatised exactly that crossing in the figure of a signalman's son returning as a historian to the valley that made him. The second feature is the discipline of the long view. A signalman's son who taught miners and railwaymen and then lectured at Cambridge had inhabited several Englands, and his theory is built to hold several temporal layers in one analysis, the surviving, the ruling, and the newly forming, which is the triad examined later in this article. Formation, in his case, is not background. It is the research programme wearing biographical dress.
## Keywords and the Historical Life of Words

The vocabulary study of 1976, Keywords, began as an appendix that outgrew its book. Williams had intended a short glossary for Culture and Society; the material expanded across two decades into a volume of entries on the words through which a society discusses itself: culture, society, industry, democracy, class, art, and dozens more, each traced through its shifting meanings from its earliest recorded uses to its contested present senses. The book remains in use across the humanities, cited by scholars who share none of his politics, because it demonstrated a procedure anyone can repeat. Take a word whose meaning feels obvious, reconstruct the pressures that bent it, and the social history inside the language becomes visible.

The procedure deserves attention because it is cultural materialism practised at the scale of the sentence. A keyword, in his treatment, is not a label attached to a thing but a site where meanings are made, contested, and inherited. The word industry once named a human quality, diligence, before it named a system of production; the word class carried senses from schoolroom divisions and biological taxonomy before it settled into its social meaning; the word culture travelled from the tending of crops through the tending of minds to a term for an entire pattern of living. Each entry shows speakers inheriting words already loaded with earlier settlements of argument, and bending them again under new conditions. Language, on this account, is not a superstructure reflecting social change from a distance. It is one of the places where social change happens, a material practice conducted in print, speech, law, and schooling, with its own institutions and its own means of production.

### What does a keyword entry show that a definition cannot?

A definition freezes a word at a single settlement of its meaning, while an entry reconstructs the argument the word has carried. Williams traces each term through its earlier senses, showing where meanings were added, contested, or reversed under social pressure. The entry therefore displays language as a record of conflicts, not a transparent medium for reporting them.

That method also explains his impatience with the vocabulary of the base and superstructure debate itself. Words like base, superstructure, reflection, and determination had been inherited by Marxist theory as if they were precision instruments, when in fact they were metaphors with histories, carrying spatial and mechanical implications that quietly governed the arguments built with them. A theorist who had spent twenty years showing how keywords smuggle settlements into thought was unlikely to accept a governing metaphor on trust. The theoretical statement of 1977, Marxism and Literature, is among other things a Keywords method applied to the Marxist vocabulary itself: each central term, base, superstructure, determination, hegemony, is reopened, its history examined, and its usable sense separated from the implications that had been doing damage unobserved.

Marxism and Literature was written as a compact, severe book, deliberately unlike the expansive historical studies that preceded it. Its brevity has made it the most assigned and the most contested of his works, the place where sympathetic readers find the system and hostile readers find the evasions. It proceeds by redefinition. Language is reclaimed as practical consciousness, material and social from the start. Hegemony, taken from Gramsci and reworked, becomes a tissue of meanings lived from inside, saturating experience instead of hovering above it in the manner of an ideology. Determination is stripped of its mechanical sense and rebuilt. And the base and superstructure pair is retired, not repaired, on the ground that cultural production was never the kind of thing the metaphor described. The next section takes that retirement as its subject, since everything in the later reputation turns on whether it was a liberation or an evasion.

One further element of the formation needs registering before the dissolution itself, since it accounts for the tone of the theoretical work. Williams distrusted systems that announced themselves as complete. His essays return repeatedly to the damage done when a provisional analysis hardens into a doctrine and the doctrine begins selecting the evidence. That distrust was not vagueness, although critics have charged it as such; it was a considered position about the relation between theory and a changing object. A society in motion, analysed by concepts that are themselves historical products, cannot be finally described, and a theory that knows this about itself will build revision into its categories. The threefold sorting into dominant, residual, and emergent is exactly such a self-revising category set, since it obliges the analyst to ask, of any formation, which of its elements are survivals, which are in command, and which are only now forming. The categories force the question that closed systems suppress.

His fiction belongs in the same account, briefly, because he regarded the novel as another instrument of the same inquiry rather than a recreation from it. Border Country and its successors work the border material directly: the returning son, the valley altered by the strike and the railway's decline, the historian's methods failing and succeeding against the claims of memory. The fiction is not the theory illustrated, and he resisted that description, but it shares the theory's central discipline, an attentiveness to lived life at exactly the point where the inherited vocabulary gives out. Readers who come to the concepts through the novels often grasp the structure of feeling faster than readers who meet it as a definition, which is itself a small confirmation of the concept.
## Cultural Materialism and the Dissolution of the Old Problem

The canonical statement of the position can be given in one sentence. Cultural materialism is the position that cultural production is itself a material process, with its own means and relations of production, rather than a superstructure reflecting a separate economic base, so that the analysis of culture is continuous with the analysis of production. Every clause of that sentence does work. Cultural production is a process, something people do with materials and tools. It is material, not because meanings are secretly physical, but because doing it requires bodies, instruments, buildings, capital, training, and time. It has means of production, presses, studios, transmitters, theatres, and relations of production, the arrangements of ownership, employment, patronage, and commissioning within which the doing occurs. And studying it runs on the same track as studying production anywhere else: identical kinds of question, about ownership of the means, about labour, about conditions, about markets and patrons, apply with no need of translation.

Set against that statement, the inherited problem can be stated fairly, in its strongest form, before any verdict is passed on Williams's handling of it. The strongest form runs as follows. A society's cultural output is not random; it correlates, across history, with the society's economic organisation in ways too systematic for coincidence. Slave societies, feudal societies, and capitalist societies produce recognisably different arts, laws, and beliefs, and the differences track the differences in how each society organises production and appropriation. Any adequate social theory must explain that correlation, and the base and superstructure model explained it by positing a direction of determination: the economic structure conditions, and in some versions causes, the cultural forms. The model's defenders could concede every refinement, that the influence runs through mediations, that the superstructure reacts upon the base, that the determination holds only in the last instance, while insisting that some asymmetric relation between economy and culture is a fact to be explained, not a metaphor to be retired. That is the steelman, and it is strong. A theory that simply declines to explain the correlation has not dissolved a problem; it has walked away from one.

### What problem did the base and superstructure metaphor create?

The metaphor spatialised a process. By picturing an economic base below and cultural storeys above, it fixed activities that are actually continuous into separate levels, and then generated an artificial puzzle about how the levels connect. Generations of argument about reflection and mediation were spent repairing a difficulty the picture itself had manufactured.

Williams's answer begins not with the correlation but with the picture. The metaphor, he argued, takes a set of activities and freezes them into an architecture. A base is a foundation, a fixed platform; a superstructure is what rests upon it; and once the architecture is accepted, the theorist's task becomes the hopeless one of describing traffic between storeys that were separated by the drawing rather than by the world. Writing a novel is not something that happens on a storey above the economy. It happens in rooms, with paper or a machine, under contracts, inside a publishing industry, drawing on a language maintained by schools, for readers produced by an educational system and a distribution network. Printing is an industry. Broadcasting is an industry. Even the apparently immaterial work of composing verse uses trained capacities that a society has produced and paid for, in materials it has manufactured, within institutions it maintains. The activities filed under the superstructure turn out, when inspected, to be productions themselves, analysable in the same terms as any other making.

### How is writing a material practice?

Writing consumes materials, tools, trained labour, and time, and it reaches readers only through industries of printing, distribution, and retail. A manuscript becomes a cultural object inside publishing relations of ownership, employment, and commissioning. Treating writing as material practice means analysing those conditions as part of the work's existence, not as external background to it.

The dissolution follows from that inspection, and its logic should be stated precisely, because the claim is easy to caricature. Williams did not deny the correlation between economic organisation and cultural form. He denied that the correlation is well described as a relation between two separable layers, one of which is real and productive and the other dependent and reflective. If cultural activities are themselves productive activities, embedded in the same material life as agriculture and manufacture, then asking how the superstructure connects with the base lapses, much as nobody asks how the textile industry connects with the base: cultural work is part of the productive life of the society, internally differentiated, unequally resourced, connected to other productions by markets, institutions, and shared conditions. The problem is dissolved rather than solved, in the way that a philosophical problem is dissolved when its governing picture is shown to have generated it. This article's governing claim names the move accordingly: the dissolution. Cultural materialism is therefore not a rival solution competing on the problem's own ground; it declines the ground, and the fair test is what the new framing opens to view and what it closes off from asking.

The verdict, stated with its defeater as this series requires, is that the dissolution succeeds as a critique of the metaphor and as a licence for a kind of analysis, and that its success is conditional in a way its author would have accepted. The judgement would collapse if someone demonstrated that the material analysis, honestly pursued, still leaves a residue of form explicable only by an asymmetric influence running from economy to culture, for then the retired question would have returned under fresh terminology. That defeater is not hypothetical decoration. It identifies the exact experiment: if the analyst of publishing or broadcasting finds, at the end of the material analysis, a residue of form that only an asymmetric economy-to-culture explanation can reach, the dissolved problem has returned wearing new clothes, and the honest cultural materialist must say so. Sections below return to this test when the criticisms are weighed, because the strongest critics, Eagleton among them, pressed precisely on the residue.

What the framing lets an analyst see can be shown concretely. Consider the publishing of a novel in the nineteenth century, the kind of case Williams himself worked. A reflection model asks what the novel expresses about the economic base, and hunts for the ideology inside the text. A cultural materialist analysis asks a connected set of questions that stay at one level. What were the means of literary production, the circulating libraries, the three-volume format imposed by those libraries, the serial parts, the royalty and copyright arrangements, and how did those means shape what could be written, at what length, with what endings, for which purchasers? How did the expansion of literacy, itself an educational and industrial process, create the audience the form presupposed? The answers explain features of the novels, their pacing, their cliffhangers, their moral settlements, without positing a mysterious transmission from economy to imagination. The form of the work is inside the analysis of its production, and the correlation the steelman insists upon is honoured, because the conditions described are economic through and through; they are simply not assigned to a separate storey.

The same procedure transforms the analysis of broadcasting, as the later section on television shows, and of theatre, whose productive apparatus runs from the playhouse and the company to the licensing regime and the actors' trained bodies. In each case the gain is the same. Questions that reflection theory could only answer with metaphors, influence, expression, reflection itself, are replaced by questions with empirical handles: ownership, labour, format, institution, audience, distribution. Critics who call the move evasive owe an account of what the metaphors explained that the handles cannot reach, and the debate between the dissolution and its critics is best conducted at exactly that level of specificity, case by case, rather than as a contest of slogans.

### What distinguishes a dissolution from a solution?

A solution answers a problem inside its own terms, while a dissolution shows that the terms manufactured the problem. Williams offered no machinery connecting an upper storey to a lower; he showed that the activities the picture files upstairs are themselves productions, so the drawing misdescribes them. Judge the gain by what the reframing lets analysis reach.

A further clarification protects the position from its commonest misreading. Rejecting the base and superstructure metaphor is not rejecting materialism, and Williams was explicit that the point of the exercise was to recover a materialism that the metaphor had betrayed. The orthodox picture, for all its materialist professions, treated culture as an abstraction, a realm of ideas and reflections, while reserving material reality for the economy. His counterclaim is that this division is itself idealist in effect: it grants meanings a ghostly separate existence and then puzzles over their connection to the world. Cultural materialism restores meanings to the material world by attending to the practices in which they are made, the signifying work of language, writing, performance, and transmission, conducted always by embodied people with real tools under real constraints. The materialism is in the method, the refusal to let any cultural fact float free of the conditions of its production.

Nor does the dissolution abolish the differences between kinds of production, a flattening of which the critics accused him and which the criticism section examines at full strength. Producing a ton of coal and producing a meaning are not the same activity, and nothing in the position requires pretending otherwise. The claim is about the order of analysis, not about identity of product. Both activities are material practices, socially organised, historically variable, and connected; they differ in their materials, their products, and the kinds of question each sustains. A theory may hold the continuity and the difference together, and Williams's own practice, moving between the economics of publishing and the finest discriminations of dramatic form, shows the two held together in operation. Whether the theory as stated secures the difference, or merely relies on its author's tact to supply it, is one of the live questions the verdict must carry, and it is carried openly in the assessment below.

The dissolution also repositions the concept of ideology, which this article links rather than redefines, since the tradition's treatment belongs to the cultural theory guide. Where ideology names a body of ideas imposed from above or reflected from below, Williams's alternative vocabulary speaks of hegemony as lived, of meanings and values as they are practised, and of the selective tradition, the standing process by which a culture keeps editing its inheritance. The effect is to move the analysis of belief from the contemplation of doctrines to the study of institutions and practices: schools selecting a curriculum, publishers selecting a list, broadcasters selecting a schedule. Selection, in his work, is where power inside culture is most visible, because selection decides what of the inherited and the newly made will be carried forward as the culture, and what will be allowed to lapse. The selective tradition, first developed in The Long Revolution, is the bridge between the dissolution and the three tools, and the article turns to the tools next, once the artifact table has laid them out together.
## The Three Tools Table and the First Tool

The three tools table sets the instruments side by side, because they were designed to work together and are usually taught apart, which is the first cause of their reduction to slogans.

| Concept | What it names | What problem it addresses | How to apply it to a case | The commonest misuse |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Structure of feeling | Lived experience before its precipitation into institutions or doctrine, experience held in solution | How to analyse meanings that are emergent and not yet institutionalised | Read the period's art and documentary forms for shared tones, tensions, and impulses that its official doctrines do not state | Treating it as a synonym for mood or atmosphere |
| Dominant, residual and emergent | The coexistence, in any formation, of the ruling order, surviving elements of earlier formations, and newly forming meanings and practices | How to analyse a culture as a temporal mixture rather than a single spirit of the age | Sort the elements of a case into the three modes, then ask of the residual and the emergent whether each is oppositional or merely alternative | Reading residual as outdated and emergent as automatically progressive |
| Determination as limits and pressures | The action of a whole social formation on outcomes, as the setting of limits and the exertion of pressures | How to keep causal weight in the analysis without mechanical prefiguration of results | Identify the limits a formation sets and the pressures it exerts, then trace how agents respond within, against, and across them | Collapsing the formulation back into either determinism or free-floating voluntarism |

### In what order should the three tools be read?

The table orders the concepts by the scale of what each handles. The structure of feeling addresses experience at the point of emergence, the triad addresses the temporal composition of a whole formation, and the redefinition of determination addresses the causal grammar connecting conditions to outcomes. Read together, they move from lived texture to structure to causation.

The first tool is the one the summaries keep, and it is also the one they most reliably damage, so it receives the fullest treatment here.

### What is a structure of feeling?

Williams's term for the lived experience of a period before it has been fully articulated in institutions or formal thought, what he called social experiences in solution. It gives a way of analysing meanings that are emerging and not yet settled into recognised forms.

The chemical metaphor inside the canonical definition repays slow reading. A substance in solution is present, active, and capable of effects, but it has not precipitated into a solid that can be isolated, weighed, and labelled. Social experience in solution is experience in that condition: real, shared, consequential, and not yet precipitated into institutions, doctrines, manifestos, or recognised genres. The analyst's difficulty is that the usual evidence of social analysis is precisely the precipitates, the formed institutions and stated beliefs, so a method is needed that can register the dissolved state before precipitation. Williams proposed that the arts of a period, and more broadly its documentary culture, its characteristic forms and conventions and tones, are that register, because artists work at the edge of the articulable, formalising impulses that a society feels before it can argue them.

The concept was built across three decades, and its history is part of its meaning. A first version appeared in the film writing of the early 1950s, where the problem was concrete: how to describe what a generation of filmmakers shared before any of them had stated a programme. The Long Revolution gave the term its social scope, applying it to the lived quality of a whole period. Marxism and Literature then performed the most careful operation, distinguishing the structure of feeling from both ideology and world-view. An ideology, in his usage, is a formalised system of beliefs, available for statement and inspection. A structure of feeling is what precedes and underlies such systems: meanings in their actively lived state, the particular sense of life, the community of experience that members of a generation share beneath their disagreements. Two political opponents in a period may hold opposed ideologies while sharing a structure of feeling, one shared ground of assumption, about possibility, about shame, about the purposes of a life, and the historian who reads only the ideologies will miss the deeper agreement that made their quarrel intelligible to both.

### How is a structure of feeling unlike a doctrine?

An ideology is a formalised and statable system of beliefs, while a structure of feeling is lived before it is stated. Members of a generation can share a structure of feeling across opposed ideologies, because the structure lies in the felt assumptions about possibility and value that their arguments presuppose rather than in the conclusions they reach.

The misreading to correct first is the gloss of the term as mood or atmosphere. A mood is private weather, brief and unaccountable; a structure of feeling is social, durable across a generation's formative years, and structured, which is the word in the phrase that the glosses drop. It has a shape: characteristic impulses, restraints, tones, and emphases that recur across a period's products, in its fiction and its advertisements, its architecture and its popular song, its ways of conducting childhood and courtship and work. The analyst demonstrates the structure not by asserting a spirit of the age but by showing the recurrence, the same configuration appearing in forms whose makers did not consult one another. That evidentiary discipline is what separates the concept from the Zeitgeist it superficially resembles. The Zeitgeist is invoked; the structure of feeling is documented.

A worked example shows the discipline in operation. Consider the English writing of the 1840s, a case the tradition has repeatedly used. The formal ideologies of the decade are a battlefield: utilitarian reformers, Tory romantics, Chartist democrats, evangelical moralists, each with stated programmes that contradict the others. Beneath the battle, a shared structure can be documented across the decade's novels, reports, and sermons: a new quality of attention to industrial urban life as a problem of knowledge, a felt uncertainty about whether the new society could be known at all, a characteristic movement between exposure and sympathy in the treatment of the poor. Writers who agreed about nothing else shared the terms of perception. The shared layer explains features of the period's writing that the ideology map leaves orphaned, and it predicts where the next precipitation would occur, in the social investigations and reform literatures of the following decades. The concept earns its place by doing that work, and where it cannot be documented by recurrence it should not be used.

The concept also carries a temporal claim that connects it to the triad. Structures of feeling belong characteristically to emergence. The lived experience of a rising generation, formed under conditions its elders did not share, registers first as feeling, as a pattern of impulses and unease without a name, and only later precipitates into the institutions and doctrines by which historians will identify the period. This is why the concept matters to a materialist analysis rather than decorating it. It gives the analyst a way to study social change at the moment when change is actually occurring, in solution, instead of reconstructing it afterwards from its precipitates and mistaking the precipitate for the process. The dominant order, by contrast, has long since precipitated; its meanings are institutionalised, taught, enforced, and therefore visible to cruder methods. A theory equipped only for precipitates will always understand a period too late, and will systematically underrate the young.

Honesty about the tool requires its limitation to be stated in the same breath as its power, and the criticism section returns to the point at full strength. The structure of feeling is descriptive rather than explanatory. It registers and documents a configuration of experience as lived; explaining how that configuration arose is further work, done with the material analysis that the companion tools and the dissolution provide. Williams did not claim otherwise for it, and the concession matters because the sharpest attack on his work, examined below, treats the concept as if it were offered as an explanation and convicts it of circularity, naming what is to be explained and calling the naming an account. The defence is not that the charge is groundless but that it is aimed at a claim the work does not make, provided the analyst actually performs the material analysis the framework demands. A structure of feeling left floating, unconnected to institutions and conditions, does degenerate into the mood-gloss its critics describe; the discipline is in the connection.

One further refinement, from the late theoretical work, should be recorded because practitioners use it constantly. Williams distinguished the structure of feeling from the more general notion of a period's sensibility by tying it to generation and to social position. A period does not have one structure of feeling distributed evenly. The emergent structure belongs to a class or a generation fraction, formed by its specific conditions, and it contends with the dominant structure that the institutions continue to teach. The analyst therefore asks whose structure of feeling a text registers, and the question protects the concept from the homogenising use, the spirit of an age shared by all, that its critics rightly distrust. In the fiction of a period, the registration is often double: a novel may formally inhabit the dominant structure while its energies, its sympathies and disturbances, belong to the emergent one, and that doubleness is frequently the source of the work's lasting interest.
## Dominant, Residual and Emergent: The Temporal Composition of a Culture

The second tool answers a defect in the way cultures are usually described, as if a period possessed a single character that everything in it expressed. Williams's counterproposal is that any developed cultural formation is a temporal mixture, containing at least three kinds of element whose relations the analyst must sort. The dominant names the order of meanings that effectively rules, carried in institutions, embodied in habits of practice, and reproduced continually, though never as completely as its own account of itself suggests. The residual consists of elements formed in earlier formations that remain active in the present, not as antiques but as lived meanings, sometimes incorporated by the dominant order, sometimes standing at a distance from it as an alternative or an opposition. The emergent consists of new meanings, values, practices, and relationships continually being created, some of which the dominant order will absorb, some of which constitute a genuine alternative or opposition to it. No analysis that collapses these into one spirit of the age can describe how cultures actually change, because change is precisely the shifting relation among the three.

The distinctions look simple and are constantly mishandled, so their edges deserve care. The residual is not the archaic. Williams insisted on the difference. The archaic is the wholly past, known as belonging to a former world and available now only for study or for nostalgia. The residual is still active: organised religion in a secularising society, the idea of rural community in an urban one, monarchy within a capitalist democracy, the values of mutuality carried in trade union and cooperative practice. Such elements were formed under earlier conditions, but they are lived now, and their present activity is a fact the dominant order must manage, either by incorporating them, translating them into terms it can use, or by tolerating them at a distance it patrols. An analysis that files the residual as mere survival misses its force, which is often the force of a standing reproach: the residual embodies values the dominant order cannot itself supply, and movements reaching for an alternative habitually reach backward to residual materials as well as forward to emergent ones.

### How can the residual remain active rather than archaic?

The residual was formed in an earlier social order but is still lived, practised, and institutionally carried in the present, whereas the archaic persists merely as something scholars examine. Organised religion, rural community values, and mutuality in cooperative practice are residual in this sense: effective now, available for incorporation or for opposition.

The emergent is the harder category to use well, because it must be detected before its precipitation, which returns the analyst to the structure of feeling as the instrument of detection. New practices appear first as disturbances, styles, tones, and informal arrangements that lack names and institutions. Most novelties are not emergent in Williams's strong sense. A fashion cycle generates novelty continuously, and the dominant order is expert at metabolising novelty as variation within itself. The emergent, properly speaking, is what arises from a new class or a newly positioned group, carrying meanings that the dominant order cannot incorporate without altering itself. The distinction is analytical and retrospective clarity is easier than present judgement, a limitation Williams acknowledged by building the test into the category: the genuinely emergent is known by the dominant order's difficulty with it, by the efforts at incorporation, denigration, and pre-emption that its appearance provokes.

The distinction that does the most work in practice, and the one the brief for this article flags as load-bearing, cuts across both the residual and the emergent. Elements in either mode may be alternative or oppositional. An alternative element offers a different way of living within the broad terms of the dominant order: a craft practice, a religious observance, a style of dress or diet that asks only to be left alone. An oppositional element, by contrast, aims to displace the very terms of the dominant order. The dominant order responds differently to each, tolerating and even marketing the alternative while contesting the oppositional, and it works continually to convert the oppositional into the alternative, to translate a challenge into a lifestyle. Much of what is called the politics of culture is that conversion process and the resistance to it. An analyst equipped with the triad and this cross-cutting distinction can describe, for any element in a case, not only its temporal mode but its political valence and the dominant order's strategy toward it, which is a great deal of descriptive power from three terms.

### What makes an emergent element oppositional rather than alternative?

An alternative element seeks room to live differently within the dominant order's terms, while an oppositional element seeks to displace those terms themselves. The test is the dominant order's response: alternatives are tolerated and often marketed, while oppositional elements provoke incorporation attempts, denigration, and pre-emption, because their spread would alter the order that hosts them.

Worked examples discipline the categories better than definitions. Take the place of organised religion in a society whose dominant order is secular and commercial. As residual, religious practice carries meanings formed under earlier formations: transcendence, obligation beyond contract, the sacredness of rest, communal belonging not mediated by purchase. The dominant order incorporates what it can, translating festivals into retail seasons and buildings into heritage, and tolerates the remainder as alternative, a private option. Where religious practice becomes oppositional, grounding a refusal of the order's priorities in the name of the residual values, the response changes character, and the analyst watching the response can read the valence from it. The same grid sorts the career of a musical form. A music arising from a newly positioned group appears first as a structure of feeling, a tone of defiance or release without a programme; it is emergent while its meanings exceed the market's formats, alternative as it is packaged into a genre shelf, oppositional where its institutions, its independent labels, dances, and gatherings, sustain a way of life against the dominant terms, and residual in its turn when a later generation inherits it as tradition and draws on its stored force.

The triad also corrects a sentimental error common in the tradition Williams inherited, the assumption that the emergent is by nature progressive and the residual by nature reactionary. He was explicit that no such assignment holds. Residual elements include the values of mutuality and care that socialist argument has always drawn upon, and also the deferences and exclusions of older orders. Emergent elements include democratic and egalitarian novelties and also the new brutalities that a rising order practises before it names them. The categories are temporal, not moral, and their discipline consists in forcing the analyst to establish the valence of each element by evidence rather than by its position on the timeline. Summaries that gloss the residual as mere obsolescence and the emergent as progress guaranteed have thrown away precisely the distinction that gives the tool its value.

A subtler feature of the scheme is its treatment of the dominant itself. The dominant is not a monolith that simply exists; it is a process of continual selection and incorporation, always defending its centrality against the residual it cannot digest and the emergent it cannot yet name. Williams borrowed and reworked the concept of hegemony for this process, and the borrowing matters. Hegemony, in his handling, is not a superstructure of ideas imposed on a passive population. It is a lived system, a saturation of the whole process of living by certain meanings and values, experienced as the real world itself, as common sense, as the limit of the thinkable. That is why the dominant is so hard to see from inside, and why the residual and the emergent matter analytically: they are the standpoints from which the dominant becomes visible as one order among possible others, rather than as reality itself. The analysis of a culture, fully conducted, is the mapping of that saturation and its incompletenesses.

### Why is hegemony described as lived rather than imposed?

Because the dominant order maintains itself less by commands than by saturation: its meanings and values are practised in work, schooling, family life, and entertainment until they are experienced as common sense and as reality itself. A hegemony that had to be imposed afresh each day would be weak; its strength is its residence inside ordinary practice.

The connection to the applied criticism in the series should be marked. The triad is the engine inside the readings collected in the guide to [Marxist literary criticism explained](/2020/07/01/marxist-literary-criticism-explained/), where sorting a text's elements among the three modes and detecting its structure of feeling make up the characteristic procedure derived from Williams. A novel of a transitional period will typically be found to argue in the dominant terms while feeling in the emergent ones, or to mount its protest in residual materials, and the interest of the reading lies in the friction among the layers. The procedure generalises beyond literature to any cultural product, a broadcast schedule, a curriculum, a platform's catalogue of forms, wherever a formation's temporal mixture is on display.
## Determination Redefined: Limits and Pressures

The third tool is a redefinition, and it addresses the charge that has dogged Marxist theory from outside and tormented it from inside: determinism. In the mechanical version of the theory, the economic base determines the superstructure, and determination means something close to prefiguration: given the base, the cultural outcome is fixed in advance, at least in its essentials, and the analyst's work is deduction. That version made the theory vulnerable to a simple refutation, since cultures under similar economic arrangements differ extravagantly, and it made the theory useless for its friends, since a deduced culture needs no analysis. Williams's response was not to abandon determination, which would have dissolved the theory's claim that material conditions carry causal weight, but to recover what he argued was the word's older and stronger sense, and to rebuild the concept around it.

The recovered sense runs as follows. To determine is to set limits and to exert pressures. A whole social formation, its productive arrangements, its institutions, its inherited meanings, determines a cultural outcome in the way that a terrain determines a journey: it closes some routes entirely, makes others costly, makes others easy, and channels effort along gradients, while the journey actually taken depends on the travellers, their purposes, their knowledge, their conflicts, and their luck. Determination in this sense is real and indispensable to the analysis. Outcomes are not arbitrary, and they are not freely invented; they occur inside limits that can be specified and under pressures that can be traced. But the specification of limits and pressures is the beginning of the analysis, not its conclusion, because within the limits, and against the pressures, people act, and their action is itself part of the material process. The formulation is quotable in a short fragment, setting of limits and exertion of pressures, and it is the tradition's most usable answer to the determinism charge, because it concedes what the charge gets right, that prefiguration is false, while retaining what the theory needs, that conditions govern.

Williams grounded the redefinition historically, in the Keywords manner. The word determine had carried, in earlier English, the sense of bounding and delimiting, of fixing the terms within which something occurs, before its modern hardening into the sense of causing an outcome outright. Marx's own usage, he argued, was more various than the doctrine built upon it, moving between the stronger and weaker senses as the argument required. The mechanical doctrine was therefore a selection from the tradition, hardened by later systematists into a law. Recovering the delimiting sense was not an evasion invented to save the theory from embarrassment; it was a restoration, argued from the texts and from the word's history, of a meaning the doctrine had suppressed. Critics may dispute the restoration, and some have, but they must dispute it as scholarship, on the evidence of usage, rather than dismissing it as a retreat, which is a stronger position than the summaries usually report.

### What does determination exclude in Williams's account?

It excludes prefiguration: the claim that a cultural outcome is contained in its conditions in advance, so that analysis merely deduces what must appear. Limits close some possibilities and pressures incline agents toward others, but the outcome is produced by action within and against those pressures, and remains a proper object of empirical study.

The redefinition changes the analyst's daily work in ways that can be enumerated without a list, by following a single case through it. Consider the emergence of a new dramatic form, the kind of case Williams handled in his theatre studies. A mechanical account would derive the form from the economic arrangements of its period and stop. An account under the redefinition begins by specifying limits: the physical theatres available and their economics, the licensing and censorship regime, the size and composition of the audience that ticket prices admit, the training of actors, the inherited conventions that define what a play is understood to be. It then specifies pressures: the commercial pressure toward repeatable success, the institutional pressure of the repertory, the pressure of new social experience demanding representation, the pressure of rival media. Inside those limits and pressures, dramatists experiment; most experiments fail, some precipitate a form. The form that emerges is determined, in the recovered sense, by everything named, and it is not deducible from any of it. The analysis explains without prophesying, which is the condition of most respectable explanation in the historical sciences.

Two failure modes flank the formulation, and Williams named both, because the concept is useless if it merely licenses drift toward whichever pole the analyst prefers. The first failure is relapse into the mechanical sense, where limits and pressures are invoked ceremonially and the outcome is then read off from the conditions as before; the vocabulary has changed and the determinism has not. The second failure is the opposite evaporation, in which determination becomes so weak that conditions merely form a background against which free creation occurs, and the theory has quietly become the voluntarism it was built to oppose. The discipline of the tool is to hold the middle by specificity: the limits named in a case must be actual limits, demonstrably closing actual possibilities, and the pressures must be traced in decisions, budgets, rejections, and revisions, not gestured at as climate. Where an analyst cannot name a limit that bit or a pressure that bent an outcome, the formulation has not been applied, only pronounced.

The redefinition also redistributes the burden in the long argument about freedom and necessity that runs through the whole Marxist tradition. Under prefiguration, human agency is an embarrassment, admitted in speeches and excluded from the model. Under limits and pressures, agency is a component of the model, the means by which pressures are answered, resisted, redirected, or succumbed to. This is why the formulation matters beyond cultural theory narrowly conceived. It offers the tradition a causal grammar in which people make their own history under conditions they have not chosen, without the grammar collapsing into either half of that famous sentence. The sentence itself, from Marx, states the duality; Williams's contribution was to give the duality a working analytical form, with instructions for use.

It is worth pausing on the relation among the three tools, because they form a set and the set is the theory in miniature. The first of the tools tells the analyst where to look for what is forming: in lived experience before articulation. The triad tells the analyst how to sort what is found: into ruling, surviving, and newly forming kinds, with the valence of each established separately. The redefinition of determination tells the analyst how to connect the sorted elements to their conditions: by limits and pressures, never by prefiguration. An analyst with all three can conduct a complete cultural analysis that is materialist in method, historical in texture, and open about causation. An analyst with only one will produce the characteristic deformations the critics have catalogued: feeling without structure, sorting without causation, or causation without life. The tools were designed against those deformations, and their joint use is the fairest test of the framework, a point the verdict on the criticisms will need.
## The Country and the City: The Escalator of Nostalgia

The study of 1973, The Country and the City, is the most beautiful of his books and the one in which the tools are least visible because most completely absorbed into the writing. Its subject is the long English literary negotiation between rural and urban life, from the classical pastoral through the country house poem, the eighteenth-century landscape tradition, the industrial novel, and into the metropolitan writing of the twentieth century. Its governing perception is that the contrast between country and city is not a description of two places but a structure through which a society thinks about its own transformation, loading the country with the values it fears it is losing and the city with the energies and corruptions it cannot disown. The book reads poem by poem and novel by novel, in chronological patience, and its most famous discovery emerges from that patience almost as a by-product.

The discovery concerns the location of the lost harmonious community, the organic England of integrated labour, neighbourliness, and known relations, whose passing English writing had mourned for centuries. Williams noticed that the mourning is always dated, and that the date moves. Each generation of writers places the true old order just before its own time, within reach of memory or of a father's memory, and treats its own moment as the fall. Follow the testimonies backwards, poet by poet, and the lost community recedes exactly as fast as it is pursued. The Georgians find it before the war; the Victorians find it before the railways; the Augustans find it before the enclosures and the money economy; the Elizabethans find it before the dissolution of the monasteries; the trail continues through the medieval complaints to a point where the organic community would have to be located in Eden. He gave the pattern a name drawn from the mechanism itself: an escalator of nostalgia, carrying each generation's golden age steadily backwards, which demonstrates that the pastoral of loss is an ideological form rather than a historical memory. A memory has a fixed object. The object travels with whoever observes, and a form whose object travels with the observer testifies about the observer's present rather than about the past it professes to describe.

### How does the escalator of nostalgia expose the pastoral?

Every generation of writers places the vanished organic community one generation back, in its parents' time, so tracing the testimonies rearward yields no origin, only a recession matching the speed of the pursuit. A genuine memory would hold its object still. The moving object reveals a form that expresses present discontent in the costume of the past.

The demonstration is worth dwelling on because it shows the method at its most characteristic. No single text is accused of bad faith. The country house poem that celebrates an estate's harmonious order is read closely and fairly, its genuine perceptions acknowledged, and then placed in the sequence, where the recurrence of the same gesture across four centuries does the analytical work. The ideological character of the pastoral is established not by unmasking any writer but by exhibiting the pattern that no individual writer could see from inside a single lifetime. This is the structure of feeling method applied historically: the evidence is the recurrence across unconnected makers, and the conclusion concerns the form, not the sincerity, of the testimony. It is also a model of how to conduct ideological critique without contempt, a quality of Williams's writing that readers across the political spectrum have remarked and that his theoretical opponents, even at their most severe, rarely denied him.

The positive half of the book matters as much as the exposure. Against the pastoral's false history, Williams sets the actual history of the countryside: enclosure, the destruction of common rights, the conversion of land into capital, the labourers' long resistance, the villages emptied by economic decision rather than by time. The real country was never the harmonious order of the poems; it was a place of work, exploitation, and struggle, and the poems' harmony is legible, once the history is restored, as a selection, the view from the house rather than from the fields that fed it. Here the selective tradition operates inside a single genre. And against the corresponding myth of the city as mere corruption, the book sets the city's actual record as a place of new solidarities, new consciousness, and new forms of collective life, the very conditions from which the emergent elements of later formations would come. Country and city are both, in his handling, achievements and wounds, and the binary that organises them into innocence and fall is the ideology under examination.

Inside the same book sits a concept that later urban and literary studies took up independently, the knowable community. In the village world that the early novel inherited, a community could in principle be known whole: its members, relations, and histories were available to a single observing consciousness, and the novel's forms were built on that availability. Industrial urbanisation destroyed the condition. The society exceeded any observer's possible knowledge, and the novel's subsequent formal history, its experiments with perspective, its crowds of strangers, its detective structures and its fragmented narrations, can be read as a series of responses to unknowability. The concept connects literary form to social structure through a cognitive mechanism, what can be known and by whom, rather than through reflection, and it is a compact demonstration of the dissolution at work in practice: form explained by the material conditions of knowledge and communication, with no storey between.

### What is a knowable community?

A social world small and stable enough for its members and relations to be known whole by a single observer, the condition the early novel presupposed. Industrial urban scale destroyed that condition, and the later novel's formal experiments, its shifting perspectives and crowds of strangers, register the loss of a world that could be fully known.

The escalator finding has travelled far beyond literary studies, because the pattern it names recurs wherever a culture narrates decline. Any reader can test it against the laments of their own moment, in which the healthy community, the serious newspaper, the disciplined school, or the courteous street is reliably located one generation back, in the childhood of whoever is speaking. Williams's point is not that nothing is ever lost; his own history of enclosure records losses that were real, dated, and resisted by name. The point is that the escalator pattern, loss without a locatable object, is the signature of the form rather than of the fact, and that the two must be separated before either can be discussed honestly. A criticism that cannot distinguish documented loss from the escalator's recession will be governed by the form it thinks it is describing.

The book also models the series pattern this article has been tracking, in a second register. Just as the dissolution removes a problem by reframing its terms, the escalator removes a pseudo-history by exhibiting its mechanism. In both cases the move is the same: what appeared to be a question about the world, how the superstructure connects, when the organic community was lost, turns out to be a question about the instrument, the metaphor, the literary form, and settling the second question makes the first evaporate. His consistency across the theoretical writing and the historical writing is the best internal evidence that the dissolution was a method, not a manoeuvre adopted for one difficult occasion.
## Television and the Analysis of Flow

The study of 1974, Television: Technology and Cultural Form, began in an experience of disorientation that Williams converted into method. Watching broadcast television in the United States, in a Miami hotel room, after a formation in a broadcasting culture of discrete programmes separated by intervals, he found himself unable to say where one item ended and the next began. Programmes bled into trailers for other programmes, advertisements echoed the dramas they interrupted, news items adopted the pacing of entertainment, and the evening presented itself as a single continuous sequence rather than as a shelf of separable works. The disorientation was the datum. A medium whose units could not be isolated by an attentive viewer was being analysed by critics as if its units were books, discrete texts for discrete judgement, and the mismatch between the analysis and the experience was the problem the book set out to repair.

The repair was the concept of flow. Broadcasting, Williams argued, is characterised not by the programme but by the planned sequence: the actual organisation of the evening as a continuous stream in which programmes, advertisements, trailers, announcements, and news are composed into a single experience by the broadcasting institution. He named the institution's work planned flow, and he showed that the sequence is designed, with junctions managed, tones modulated, and audiences held across boundaries that the official description of the medium pretends are walls. The analysis that follows from the concept is institutional before it is textual. To understand an evening of broadcasting, ask who planned the sequence, under what commercial or public obligations, with what assumptions about the audience's movement through domestic time, and with what techniques for converting the gaps between items into continuity. The questions are the cultural materialist questions, about means, relations, and institutions, applied to a form that textual criticism had been mishandling.

### What is planned flow in broadcasting?

Planned flow is the broadcasting institution's composition of an evening into a continuous sequence, in which programmes, advertisements, trailers, and announcements are arranged to hold the audience across the junctions between items. The unit of the medium is the sequence rather than the single programme, and the sequence is deliberately designed, not an accidental accumulation.

The technological argument of the book is equally durable and equally characteristic. Against the technological determinism that treats a new medium as an autonomous force that arrives in a society and produces effects, Williams reconstructed the history of broadcasting's development as a series of social decisions. The technologies of transmission were developed within institutions that had purposes: military communication, corporate organisation, the management of domestic consumption. Broadcasting as a cultural form, the address to a home audience of listeners and viewers conceived as families and individuals, was not inscribed in the apparatus. It was selected, financed, and regulated into being, one among several possible uses of the technical means. His name for the characteristic social arrangement that broadcasting served, mobile privatisation, describes the pattern exactly: populations increasingly mobile in their work and residence, and increasingly enclosed in private domestic units, connected to the world through communications received at home. The technology answered a social formation's needs and then, installed, became one of the pressures shaping that formation's further development: determination as limits and pressures, running in both directions, with no first cause enthroned.

### Why does the sequence matter more than the single programme?

Because the sequence is what the audience actually experiences and what the institution actually designs. A programme analysed in isolation misses the trailers that framed it, the advertisements that interrupted it, and the items it was placed to follow and precede, all of which shape its meaning and its audience. Flow analysis restores the composed whole.

The decay discipline governing this article requires the broadcasting analysis to be handled structurally, and the handling is natural because Williams's own claims were structural. Flow is a property of organised transmission to a domestic audience under conditions of institutional planning; it does not depend on any particular device, service, or platform, and it should not be illustrated by naming the services of a later period. What can be said structurally is that the concept's career vindicated its design. Media studies adopted flow as a standard term because analysts kept encountering sequences composed to hold attention across junctions, in medium after medium, and needed a word for the composed continuity and the institution composing it. The concept also generalises inward, to the analysis of a single broadcast hour, and outward, to the analysis of a channel's identity, which is nothing but flow considered at the scale of weeks: a characteristic sequence, tone, and promise by which an institution holds a returning audience. Wherever an institution composes a sequence for a recipient who experiences the composition rather than the items, the analysis of flow is the relevant instrument, and the questions it asks, who composed this, under what obligations, with what techniques of junction and retention, retain their edge.

A further distinction from the book deserves recovery, because it disciplines the use of flow against a lazy extension. Williams distinguished the flow of the planned sequence from the miscellaneous succession of items that a viewer might assemble by switching, and he was careful about the difference between the institution's composition and the audience's path through it. The audience is not the sequence's prisoner. Viewers arrive and leave, attend and drift, talk over the junctions, and construct their own evenings inside the offered one. Flow names the offer, the institution's designed continuity, not a guaranteed effect on consciousness. That care is the redefinition of determination operating inside media analysis: the institution sets limits and exerts pressures, of arrangement, emphasis, and default, and the audience acts within and against them. An analysis that treats flow as hypnosis has relapsed into the mechanical sense with a new vocabulary, the exact failure the third tool exists to prevent.

The book's institutional comparisons also remain instructive. Williams wrote with two broadcasting settlements in view, a commercial system financed by advertising and a public system financed by licence and obligation, and he refused the easy verdicts available about both. The commercial system's flow was shaped by the sale of audiences to advertisers, a fact that explained its junctions and its tones more exactly than any lament about taste. The public system's flow was shaped by its charter obligations and its paternalist inheritance, which explained its own characteristic mixtures of education and condescension. Neither system was the technology's natural expression. Each was a social settlement, historically made and therefore historically revisable, and the analysis of their forms had to begin from their institutions. That starting point, institutions before texts, settlements before effects, is the dissolution practised on a medium: no storey of content floating above a base of economics, only a single field of production in which programmes, schedules, financing, regulation, and domestic life are analysed together.

His wider media writing, placed in the longer tradition surveyed by [Marxist media theory explained](/2012/05/15/marxist-media-theory-explained/), extended the same treatment to the press and to communications generally, in a short book of the early 1960s that argued for democratic control of the means of communication as a cultural question of the first rank. The argument followed from the theory with unusual directness. If the means of cultural production are means of production, then their ownership and governance are political questions of the same kind as the ownership of any industry, and a socialism that nationalised coal while leaving the production of meanings in unexamined hands had misunderstood its own analysis. The New Left context of that argument is given in the series account of the movement, and its presence in Williams's work is a reminder that the theory was never only an academic instrument. The man who dissolved the base and superstructure problem did so partly to clear the ground for a politics in which culture, education, and communication stood at the centre rather than in the decorative margin.
## The Tools at Work: Drama, Tragedy and the Novel

The theoretical statement of 1977 summarised a method that the earlier studies had already been practising, and the fairest way to test the dissolution is to watch the practice. Three bodies of work, on drama, on tragedy, and on the novel, show the same procedure operating on different materials: form read as social evidence, institutions counted within the work's existence, and lived experience caught at the point where the inherited categories fail. A reader who grasps the procedure in the cases will understand the theory better than a reader who memorises the definitions, which is why this section lingers over the cases at the length their intricacy deserves.

The drama studies, gathered in Drama from Ibsen to Brecht and in the earlier volume on drama in performance, addressed a standing puzzle of theatre history. Between the late nineteenth century and the middle of the twentieth, the European drama changed its fundamental structures: the well-made play of exposition and climax gave way to forms built on atmosphere, silence, interruption, and the refusal of climax. Literary history tended to narrate the change as a sequence of geniuses and influences. Williams narrated it as a sequence of structures of feeling finding and failing to find adequate form. Ibsen's late plays, in his reading, strain the inherited dramatic structure from inside, loading the conventional drawing room with pressures of unspoken and unspeakable experience until the structure itself becomes the subject. Chekhov dissolves the climax into a texture of missed connections, a dramatic form for a class whose experience was of drift and blockage. Strindberg, expressionism, and finally Brecht represent successive attempts to build forms equal to experiences that the naturalist inheritance could not stage: the fractured consciousness, the city crowd, the historical process itself.

The Brecht analysis is the most instructive, because Williams neither worshipped nor dismissed the century's most theorised dramatist. Brecht's epic theatre, with its interruptions, placards, and alienation effects, was in his account a genuine formal discovery, a structure adequate to a new structure of feeling in which individuals are experienced as products and agents of historical forces rather than as self-contained souls. But the discovery carried a cost that Brecht's celebrants understate. The alienation that exposes social mechanism can also freeze the living complexity of experience into demonstration, and Williams traced, production by production and play by play, the tension between Brecht's analytic intention and the felt life that kept breaking through it. The reading is a model of the method's fairness: the form is explained by the experience it was built to carry, the explanation honours the achievement, and the residue that the form cannot carry is named without triumph. A verdict with its defeater, conducted at the level of the single scene.

Modern Tragedy, published in 1966, performed the same operation on a genre that academic criticism had declared historically closed. The orthodoxy held that tragedy belonged to worlds with shared beliefs and ranked orders, and that modern conditions, egalitarian, sceptical, and bureaucratic, could produce pathos but not tragedy. Williams answered historically. Tragedy, he argued, is not a single eternal form but a series of structures, Greek, Renaissance, liberal, each organising a culture's experience of suffering, agency, and death, and the modern period has its own tragic structures, in the liberal tragedy of the isolated individual destroyed by the very individualism that defines him, and in the revolutionary tragedies of the twentieth century, where the suffering is inflicted by the forces of liberation themselves. The argument's edge was directed at the present of its writing. A culture that declares tragedy impossible consoles itself that its catastrophes are accidents or pathologies, beneath the dignity of the form, and Williams insisted that the catastrophes of his century, war, revolution, and their betrayals, were tragic in the full structural sense and had to be thought with the seriousness the form commands.

The tragedy book also shows the political stakes he attached to questions of form. The liberal structure of feeling, in his analysis, locates tragedy in the conflict between the individual and society, and its characteristic ending reconciles the audience to the individual's defeat by ennobling it. The revolutionary structure relocates the conflict inside the process of change itself, where the destroyer and the builder are the same people, and no ennobling reconciliation is available. To analyse a period's tragedies is therefore to analyse its deepest settlements about agency and suffering, settlements that its explicit doctrines often contradict. Here the structure of feeling is doing the work that ideology analysis cannot: the doctrines of a period may proclaim progress and mastery while its tragedies record helplessness and waste, and whoever confines the reading to the proclamations will misunderstand what the period felt like from inside, and therefore what it did.

The 1970 book on the English novel extended the knowable community argument across the whole development of the form. Dickens stands at the threshold, the novelist of the new city who devised forms, the multi-plot urban novel, the crowd treated as a character, the web of connections disclosed by coincidence and investigation, to render an unknowable society partly knowable on the page. The achievement was real and the limitation was real, since the connections Dickens reveals are finally moral and providential where the society's actual connections were economic and indifferent. George Eliot, working the provincial material, built the novel of the knowable community under strain, a world still surveyable by an omniscient sympathy at the moment when its surveyability was ending. Hardy registers the invasion of the rural community by forces, markets, railways, and educated mobility, that dissolve its knowability from inside. By Lawrence, the community that can be known has contracted to the intense local cell, and the novel's energies have moved into the registration of feeling itself, the structure of feeling becoming the explicit subject as the shared world that once carried it recedes.

Read as a sequence, the three bodies of work disclose the unity of the method. In each case a literary form is treated as a solution, attempted under definite material and institutional conditions, to a problem of experience: how to stage a consciousness the drawing room cannot hold, how to think a catastrophe the doctrines cannot name, how to know a society that exceeds knowledge. The forms succeed partially and fail instructively, and their failures are as much evidence as their successes, because the failure marks the exact point where the period's experience exceeded its available means of articulation, which is the definition of the ground on which a structure of feeling forms. This is cultural materialism before the name: no reflection, no deduction from a base, only the continuous analysis of making, under conditions, with tools inherited and improvised, by people whose lived pressures are inside the work. The theory of 1977 did not invent the practice. It generalised it, named it, and armed it with the three tools, and the priority of practice to theory in his career is one reason the theory retains the texture of use.

Something should be added about the essays on individual writers and on the Welsh industrial novel, since they display the method's range at a smaller scale. His recovery of the industrial fiction written from inside the Welsh coalfield, by miners and the sons of miners, applied the selective tradition argument in reverse: here was a literature the accredited tradition had simply not selected, whose existence proved that the tradition's map of the period's writing was an artefact of selection rather than of production. The essays on Orwell performed a similar service for a writer claimed by every camp, reconstructing the actual trajectory, the imperial policeman, the tramping investigator, the democratic socialist of the war years, the dying prophet of the late books, against the selective Orwells manufactured by later argument. In each case the procedure is identical: restore the full record, identify the selection that produced the received figure, and ask whose purposes the selection served. The procedure is available to any researcher, which is part of its importance for the persona of researchers addressed later in this article.
## The Criticisms at Full Strength: Eagleton and the Charge of Insufficient Theory

No verdict on Williams is worth reading that has not first passed through the strongest case against him, and the strongest case has a name, a book, and a year. Terry Eagleton's Criticism and Ideology, published in 1976, a year before Marxism and Literature, mounted the most consequential attack Williams ever received from within the Marxist tradition he shared. The series treats the exchange between the two men in its own right in the profile of [Terry Eagleton and Marxist criticism](/2020/09/01/terry-eagleton-marxist-criticism/); here the attack is examined as the test the dissolution and the tools must survive. Eagleton wrote as an admirer armed with a rival theory, which is what makes the book dangerous to Williams in a way that hostile reviewing never was. He had absorbed the Althusserian and Machereyan reconstruction of Marxist criticism, with its insistence on science against ideology, on structures against experience, and on the text as a production that works its materials rather than expressing its author, and he measured Williams against that standard and found him wanting at the foundations.

The charges, stated at full strength and in the order of their severity, are these. The first and gravest is that Williams's work substitutes a vocabulary of experience for an analysis of structures. On this charge, the structure of feeling names what is to be explained rather than explaining it: to say that a generation shared a structure of feeling is to redescribe the fact that its products resemble one another, and the resemblance was the phenomenon demanding explanation. The concept is therefore circular in use, however evocative in statement, and its career in weaker hands, where it licenses the impressionistic invocation of period atmospheres, is not an accident but the unfolding of the circularity built into it. The second charge holds that the founding move, culture treated as material production, flattens a distinction the theory needs. Producing a commodity and producing a meaning are different orders of activity: the meaning is not consumed in its use, is not exhausted by exchange, and works upon its recipients in ways that no analysis of its production conditions, however thorough, will capture. By insisting on the continuity of cultural with material production, Williams allegedly dissolves not a pseudo-problem but a real one, the specific difference of the ideological and the aesthetic, and leaves his theory unable to say what is distinctive about the objects it studies.

The third charge concerns the subject who experiences. Williams's categories presuppose, on Eagleton's reading, a humanist subject, whole, continuous, and prior to the structures that form it, whose lived experience can then be consulted as evidence. The rival theory held that the subject is itself an effect of structures, of language, ideology, and the unconscious, and that appealing to lived experience as a foundation merely consults an effect as though it were a cause. Experience, for the structuralist generation, was the most ideological category of all, the place where a formation's determinations are misrecognised as personal authenticity. A theory built on experience was therefore not insufficiently rigorous in detail but wrong in architecture, and Williams's celebrated attentiveness to the lived was, on this account, the symptom of his limitation: a romantic organicism, inherited from the Leavisite criticism he had begun by opposing, dressed in socialist vocabulary.

The fourth charge is political, and it is the one that explains the temperature of the exchange. Eagleton's generation suspected Williams's gradualism, the long revolution of his title, as a theory of change so patient that it excused the absence of rupture, and they suspected the cultural emphasis as a displacement of the economic and the political by the educational. A Marxism centred on meanings, feelings, and the slow transformation of an entire lived order could appear, from the vantage of a more militant structuralism, as a Marxism that had made its peace with the existing order's timetable. The charge was sharpened by biography in a way Williams deeply resented: the suggestion that the adult education tutor's formation explained, and limited, the theory, that the work was the ideology of a particular professional stratum. He answered that suggestion with unusual sharpness in the Politics and Letters interviews at the decade's end, and the resentment is understandable, since the move from formation to limitation was one that his own method, applied to other writers, had taught the critics to perform.

### Where did the charge of insufficient theory come from?

The charge was pressed most consequentially by Terry Eagleton in Criticism and Ideology in 1976, from within the Marxist tradition and under the influence of the structuralist reconstruction of Marxist criticism. Its core claim was that Williams's vocabulary of experience redescribed phenomena instead of explaining them, and that his humanist subject was theoretically untenable.

The steelman requires one more element before the answer is attempted: the acknowledgement that Eagleton's book was not a demolition but a reckoning between heirs. He treated Williams as the most important Marxist critic in English, the figure through whom the tradition had been renewed for a generation, and the severity of the critique was proportional to the inheritance at stake. The structuralist reconstruction needed the ground Williams occupied, the analysis of English culture and literature from a Marxist position, and it could only take that ground by showing that the occupant's foundations would not bear the building the times required. Read in that light, the book is evidence for Williams's centrality as well as against his sufficiency, and any honest account must hold both. It must also record what happened afterwards, because the sequel changes the weight of the charges. Eagleton himself, in later writing and in retrospect, withdrew much of the severity. The structuralist moment passed; its own foundations, the science of ideology, the dissolved subject, came under pressures it could not answer; and Eagleton's mature assessments of Williams are admiring, treating the older critic's achievement as foundational and the 1976 reckoning as the necessary severity of a younger man in a hurry. The retraction does not refute the charges, which stand or fall as arguments, but it removes their author's authority from behind them, and a reader weighing the exchange should know that its prosecutor eventually joined the defence's estimate of the defendant's importance.

### What did Eagleton later withdraw?

In his later writing Eagleton softened the 1976 judgement considerably, treating Williams's achievement as foundational to Marxist criticism in English and the earlier severity as the product of a theoretical moment that had itself passed. The withdrawal concerns the verdict on Williams's importance and method; the individual arguments remain available for any reader to test.

Two further criticisms, from outside the Eagleton line, complete the case at full strength. The first comes from historians and sociologists who find the central concepts under-specified for empirical use. His central concept, they observe, has no agreed procedure of identification: two analysts can examine the same decade and return different structures, each documented by recurrence after a fashion, and the concept supplies no tribunal. The triad, similarly, can be applied so flexibly that any element can be sorted to suit the argument, residual when the analyst honours it, emergent when the analyst hopes for it. A tool that cannot return a wrong answer, on this charge, is not a tool but a vocabulary, and the suspicion grows that the framework's celebrated openness is the openness of a system that has insured itself against refutation. The second criticism comes from feminist scholars who rebuilt portions of this field in the years following his death. Williams's whole way of life, it was shown, had a gendered interior that his categories did not reach: the lived experience his structures of feeling registered was disproportionately the experience of men in public and industrial life, while the domestic labour and the women's cultures that sustained that life appear at the margins of the analysis. The charge is not that he was unusually blind by the standards of his formation, but that the method's central promise, to begin from lived experience, was incompletely kept, and that completing it required correcting the archive from which experience was read.

The case against, assembled, is formidable: a central concept accused of circularity, a founding move accused of flattening the specificity of meaning, a subject accused of being a humanist survival, a politics accused of gradualist evasion, a method accused of unfalsifiable flexibility, and an archive accused of gendered partiality. Any verdict that does not feel the weight of that assembly has not understood the work's situation. The next section gives the answer the framework can honestly make, with the concessions it must honestly carry.
## The Answer, the Concessions and the Verdict

The answer begins by separating the charges that strike the work from the charges that strike a caricature of it, and it can be conducted charge by charge, provided the concessions are paid in full where they are owed. The circularity charge against the structure of feeling is the natural first item, since it is the gravest. The defence established earlier in this article holds: the concept is descriptive rather than explanatory, and its author did not claim otherwise. It identifies and documents a configuration of lived experience whose existence is the precondition of explanation, not its substitute. Every historical science possesses such concepts. The geologist's stratum and the economist's market sentiment alike organise phenomena for causal inquiry without themselves causing anything, and they are convicted of circularity only when an analyst mistakes the organisation for the account. Williams's own practice, in the country and city study, in the drama history, in the novel history, invariably proceeds from the documented structure to the material conditions, the enclosures, the theatre economics, the literacy expansion, that explain it. Where his followers have invoked the phrase as a self-sufficient explanation, the failure is theirs, and it is a real failure with a real frequency, which the verdict must register as a cost of the tool's design: a concept that requires discipline to use will be used undisciplinedly, and its inventor bears some responsibility for the foreseeable misuse.

The flattening charge, that the dissolution erases the specificity of meaning, deserves a longer answer because it identifies a genuine tension rather than a misunderstanding. The defence is that the dissolution asserts continuity of analysis, not identity of product. Nothing in the framework denies that a meaning differs from a loaf in its mode of existence, its durability, its reproducibility, or its action upon recipients. What the framework denies is that the difference is well modelled by assigning the two to separate storeys of a building, one material and one not. The loaf's production and the poem's production are continuous in that both are socially organised makings with means, relations, and histories, and they are different in everything that follows from their materials and uses. Yet the concession must be made that the theory as stated in 1977 asserts this distinction more confidently than it develops it. Williams's account of signifying practice, of language as practical consciousness, gestures toward a theory of the specific difference of meaning, but the gesture is compressed, and readers who need the difference theorised, how a meaning works upon a consciousness, what a text does that its production history cannot state, will find the rival theories of the period more elaborated at exactly that joint. The verdict records this as the framework's thinnest wall: the dissolution survives the charge, but the building behind it is unfinished at the point where the charge presses.

The charge of the humanist subject meets a similar divided fate. Williams did presuppose experiencing subjects whose testimony counts as evidence, and he did so on principle, against a structuralism that dissolved the subject into its conditions and then could not explain why conditions are ever resisted, since resistance requires someone who experiences the condition as intolerable. His wager was that a theory which cannot accommodate the experience of the people whose emancipation it professes has lost its object, whatever its rigour. The subsequent history of the rival position lends the wager retrospective support: the dissolved subject proved uninhabitable, and the structuralist programme's own heirs rebuilt accounts of agency and experience, often in vocabularies that converge with his. But the concession stands that his subject is undertheorised in one direction: the formation of experience by language and by unconscious process is acknowledged in his work rather than analysed, and a reader who needs that analysis must supplement him. Supplementation, notably, is possible without demolition, which distinguishes his framework's gaps from its rivals' collapses. A gap invites building; a collapse invites archaeology.

The political charge, gradualism, is answered by the record of the concepts rather than by the biography, which this article has refused throughout to use as authorisation. The long revolution was never a counsel of patience with the existing order; it was a claim about the depth of the transformation socialism requires, extending into education, culture, and the whole organisation of meanings, and therefore a claim that a political rupture which leaves the culture untouched will watch the old order reassemble itself inside the new institutions. History supplied the argument with evidence its author would have traded away gladly: the twentieth century's revolutionary states, whose record this series examines in its Soviet cluster, repeatedly discovered that seizing the commanding heights of production left the heights of meaning in inherited hands, with consequences the record shows. Whether the cultural emphasis displaces the economic in his work is a fair question of proportion, and the honest answer is that the proportion is deliberate and contestable: he wrote about culture because the tradition had not, and the corrective, like most correctives, leans. A reader who restores the balance by reading him alongside the tradition's economists is using the series as it was designed to be used.

The flexibility charge, that the tools cannot return a wrong answer, is the one the verdict must treat with most respect, because it concerns not this or that claim but the framework's scientific character, a question this series has examined directly in its treatment of whether Marxism is a science or a faith. The defence available is partial and should be stated as partial. The tools do exclude answers. An analysis that sorts a formation's elements and finds no residual activity where the record shows surviving institutions at work has misapplied the triad and can be corrected against the record. An invocation of a structure of feeling without documented recurrence across unconnected makers violates the method's stated evidentiary rule and can be struck down by appeal to it. A claim of determination that names no limit that bit and no pressure that bent an outcome fails the third tool's own test. The framework therefore has internal standards of misuse, which is more than a vocabulary possesses. What it lacks is a procedure that forces convergence between competent analysts, and in that lack it resembles the other instruments of historical interpretation rather than the instruments of the laboratory. The verdict on this charge: the framework is a method carrying standards, not an instrument returning verdicts, and readers who require the second should not pretend the first is a failed version of it.

The feminist correction stands in a different relation to the framework, because it operates as the framework's own method turned on the framework's archive. To show that the lived experience registered in the canonical studies was partial, and that the whole way of life contained interiors the categories missed, is to perform exactly the operation Williams performed on the pastoral: exhibiting the selection inside the received record. The correction therefore extends the work rather than refuting it, and the subsequent scholarship that rebuilt the analysis of culture with the domestic, the reproductive, and the feminine inside it used his tools, the structure of feeling above all, to do the rebuilding. A framework is vindicated in a distinctive way when its sharpest corrections are executed with its own instruments, and the verdict counts this heavily.

The verdict can now be stated in full, with the defeater the brief requires carried inside it. The dissolution succeeds: the base and superstructure problem, as a problem about traffic between storeys, was an artefact of its metaphor, and removing the metaphor released an analysis of cultural production that has demonstrably borne fruit across literary history, media studies, and the history of concepts. The three tools succeed as a method with standards, descriptive where description is the precondition of explanation, and honest, in their author's handling, about their own limits. The framework's unfinished business is the theory of meaning's specificity and of the subject's formation, where supplementation is needed and possible. And the whole judgement carries its defeater openly: the judgement that the dissolution succeeds would be defeated by a demonstration that treating culture as material production still requires an account of why cultural production takes the forms it does under given economic conditions, which would reinstate the original question under another name. No such demonstration has commanded the field in the decades since 1977, but the verdict does not close the file. It specifies what would reopen it, which is what a verdict in this series is for.

### How should the old model be graded?

The dissolution grades the model as a misleading picture rather than as a false proposition. Its error was architectural: it froze continuous activities into storeys and then demanded an account of the traffic between them. Graded as a framing, it fails; graded as the intuition that economic organisation and cultural form correlate, it survives inside the new framing.

That last distinction, between the model's picture and its intuition, is the point on which students most often lose the thread, and the extra questions in the closing section return to it. The correlation between how a society produces and what it makes culturally is a datum. The two-storey architecture is one attempt to house the datum, and it leaked. Cultural materialism houses the same datum in a single field of productions, internally differentiated and unequally resourced, and the test between the housings is not loyalty but performance: which one generates questions with empirical handles, and which one generates ceremonies of mediation. On performance, the half-century since the theoretical statement is itself evidence, since the research programmes that adopted the single-field housing, in book history, in media studies, in the sociology of culture, produced findings, while the mediation literature produced, in the main, further refinements of the mediation problem. A framing is vindicated by the work it enables, and the work exists.
## The Method in Practice: Two Cases Worked Slowly

Frameworks earn their keep in application, and this section works two cases slowly enough for the procedure to be visible, one from the history of the book and one from education, the field where the method began. Neither case is taken from Williams's own writings, because a method that only its inventor can operate is a style rather than a method, and the point of the exercise is transferability: the questions, in their order, with the evidence each question demands.

The first case is the transformation of the novel's production in the early nineteenth century, as steam printing, machine-made paper, and the circulating library system rebuilt the material basis of fiction within a generation. Begin where the dissolution instructs, with the means of production and their ownership. The steam press cut the cost of impressions and raised the capital threshold of publishing, concentrating the industry in firms that could finance machinery and paper stocks. The circulating libraries, commercial enterprises renting books to subscribers, became the dominant purchasers of new fiction, and their requirements, for the three-volume novel at a price no individual buyer would pay, for moral safety certifiable to subscribers, for length sufficient to fill three volumes, entered the work itself. The analyst under the old model would now ascend to the superstructure and ask what ideology the novels reflected. The analyst under the dissolution stays on the one level and asks the connected questions. How did the three-volume format, an artefact of library economics, shape narrative architecture, its pacing across volumes, its distribution of climaxes, its padding and its postponements? How did the library's moral censorship, a commercial relation rather than a doctrine, determine which subjects could be treated and which endings were saleable? How did the expansion of the reading public, itself produced by schooling, cheap print, and railway distribution, alter the audience presupposed by the prose, its explanations, its allusions, its assumed knowledge?

Each of those questions has an empirical handle: publishers' archives, library catalogues, contracts, print runs described qualitatively through the scholarship, the physical evidence of the books themselves. And each answer explains features of the fiction that reflection talk leaves as mysteries of the spirit. The triple-decker's notorious longueurs are not a failure of Victorian taste; they are a format's requirement meeting an author's contract. The marriage ending's dominance is not the age's ideology floating free; it is a saleable settlement certified by the purchasers who controlled access to readers. None of this denies that the novels also think, feel, and judge in ways irreducible to their production, and the method's second movement, the analysis of structures of feeling within the works, addresses exactly that residue with the first tool. The point is the order of operations and the unity of the field: production first, because production is where the evidence is hardest, and experience throughout, because experience is what the production was for.

The second case is the adult education classroom itself, treated as an object of the analysis it generated. The means of cultural production in the extramural system were peculiar: university syllabuses carried into church halls and miners' institutes, a tutor employed by a delegacy, students whose fees and whose labour movement organisations sustained the classes, a stock of books supplied through libraries built by subscription and bequest. The relations were equally peculiar, since the students were volunteers, adults, and frequently the tutor's equals or superiors in every knowledge except the accredited kind. Ask the triad's questions of this formation. The dominant element was the accredited literary tradition, the canon as the universities had selected it, carried into the hall as the class's official content. The residual elements were the cultures the students brought: chapel and union, the autodidact inheritance of the mechanics' institutes, the Welsh and the regional traditions, each carrying meanings formed under earlier conditions and still actively lived. The emergent element was the thing the classroom itself was producing and could not yet name: a democratic culture in which what counted as literature would be selected by the entire community of readers instead of being inherited from the institution, a structure of feeling before it was a programme. Williams's entire later theory can be read as the generalisation of that classroom's tensions, and reading it so is not biographical reduction, because the generalisation was argued, tested against other materials, and revised for thirty years. The case shows what the method is for: it finds, inside an ordinary institution, the temporal mixture and the unformed future that flatter descriptions, the spread of education, the meeting of town and gown, render invisible.

Two practical corollaries follow for researchers, and they are stated here because the persona section will assume them. The first is that the archive of cultural materialism is the archive of production: contracts, catalogues, schedules, syllabuses, accounts, the paperwork of making, which institutions preserve unevenly and researchers undervalue because the tradition taught them to begin with the finished work. Begin with the paperwork and the work changes meaning. The second corollary concerns selection. Every archive, syllabus, and canon is a selective tradition in operation, and the analyst's standing question, what went unselected, at whose hand, and under which pressures, recovers the residual and the emergent that the selection suppressed. The suppressed material is rarely hidden; it is merely uncatalogued, unlisted, untaught, and it waits in the record for the question that retrieves it.

## Uptake: The Tools at Large in the Disciplines

The adjacent-discipline uptake of Williams's instruments is a matter of record rather than of advocacy, and it can be reported without partisanship because much of it was accomplished by scholars with no Marxist commitments to defend. His first tool entered cultural studies at its founding, shaping the analysis of youth cultures, popular music, and television audiences, wherever the object was a lived pattern not yet precipitated into doctrine. It entered literary studies as a standard way of handling period experience, taught in methods courses whose instructors would not describe themselves as working in Williams's tradition at all. The dominant, residual, and emergent triad travelled with it, becoming a routine sorting device in the study of genres, institutions, and national cultures, valued precisely for the discipline it imposes on talk about change: the analyst must declare which elements survive, which rule, and which are forming, and must defend every assignment from the evidence.

The analysis of broadcast flow had the most complete institutional victory of the three. Media studies took the term into its basic vocabulary, where it names the composed sequence as the unit of broadcasting and directs attention to the institution composing it. Generations of students have learned to analyse an evening, a channel, and a schedule rather than a programme in isolation, and the learning is so thoroughly absorbed that its source is often unfootnoted, the fate of concepts that win completely. The vocabulary study had a parallel career across the humanities: Keywords is cited in history, sociology, linguistics, and philosophy as the model of a procedure, the historical semantics of contested terms, and its entries remain starting points for the study of the words it covers, extended and corrected by successors who adopted the method wholesale.

The uptake matters to the verdict for a specific reason. A framework's concepts can travel for two reasons: because they are fashionable, or because they do work that practitioners repeatedly need done. Fashion decays on a scale of years; the Williams instruments have been in continuous service for decades, across disciplines that agree about little else, and through changes of theoretical weather that retired many of their contemporaries. The structuralist vocabulary that was to replace his has itself become a period piece in several of its strong forms, while the structure of feeling is assigned to new cohorts each year. Durability under hostile weather is not proof of truth, and the verdict does not offer it as such. It is evidence of utility, and utility, for a method, is a large part of what truth amounts to.

There is also a quieter uptake to record, in the practice of teaching. The proposition that culture is ordinary, carried into classrooms by teachers formed on his work, changed what counts as an object of study and whose experience counts as evidence. Students whose home cultures were once treated as the absence of culture learned to analyse those cultures as formations, with their own dominant, residual, and emergent elements, their own selective traditions, their own structures of feeling. The democratisation of the object of study is his most widely distributed legacy and the least often credited, because it operates as a permission rather than as a citation. The adult education tutor's wager, that the meanings made in ordinary life are theoretically interesting, is now the working assumption of several disciplines, and the disciplines have largely forgotten that the assumption was ever contested, or by whom it was won.
## Language as Practical Consciousness

One element of the theoretical statement deserves a section of its own, because it carries more of the framework's weight than its compressed presentation suggests: the account of language. The inherited Marxist treatment of language placed it ambiguously, sometimes among the superstructures, as a reflection of social being, sometimes beneath theory's notice altogether, as a transparent instrument. Williams's counterclaim, argued through the Keywords method applied to the philosophy of language itself, is that language is practical consciousness: a material activity, conducted by embodied speakers in social relations, in which consciousness is not first formed privately and then expressed, but formed in the expressing. The claim dissolves another two-storey picture, the one that places thought above and words below, and it does for the philosophy of mind what the larger dissolution does for the theory of culture.

The evidence he marshalled was historical as well as philosophical. The vocabulary of a language, as Keywords had shown entry by entry, is a sedimented record of social activity: meanings deposited by past practice, inherited by speakers who did not choose them, and reworked in every generation's use. A speaker's most intimate thought is therefore conducted in a social material, learned from others, maintained by institutions of schooling and print, and altered by the very acts of speech it enables. This is determination as limits and pressures operating inside the skull: the inherited language closes some thoughts, makes others costly, makes others ready to hand, while the speaker's purposes, formed in a specific life, answer the pressures and occasionally bend the material into a new shape that others then inherit. The triad operates here too. A living language at any moment contains its dominant usages, enforced by education and accreditation; its residual usages, surviving from earlier formations and available for reproach or consolation; and its emergent usages, the new coinages and bendings in which a structure of feeling is precipitating into speech before it precipitates into anything else. The analyst of a period's language is therefore holding the same instrument as the analyst of its novels, and the unity is the theory's signature.

The account also explains his lifelong attention to the actual institutions of language: the schools that teach a standard, the publishers who enforce a house style, the broadcasters whose pronunciation policies once decided which voices could address the nation. Each institution is a means of production of consciousness in the strictest sense the framework allows, and each is therefore a proper object of political as well as scholarly attention. When Williams argued, in the communications writings of the early 1960s, for democratic governance of the means of communication, the argument was not an extension of the theory into politics but the theory itself, since a theory that locates the making of consciousness in socially organised practices cannot treat the organisation of those practices as a side issue. The point connects his cultural theory to the democratic socialism of the New Left formation described earlier, and it distinguishes his position from both the liberal defence of a free market in expression and the orthodox subordination of communication to a prior economic programme.

A caution belongs in the record, and the verdict section has already paid part of it. The account of language as practical consciousness is, in his work, more fully asserted and illustrated than systematically built out, and the philosophy of language of his period, in its analytic and its structuralist branches alike, raised difficulties about reference, about the sign's arbitrariness, and about the constitution of the speaking subject that his compressed treatment touches rather than settles. The honest assessment is the one this article has applied throughout: the framing directs inquiry profitably, toward the institutions and histories of speech, and away from the picture of words as labels pasted onto a ready-made world, while leaving theoretical work at the joints for successors. Successors in cultural studies and in the sociology of language did take up the work, raising accounts of discourse and of linguistic markets on foundations he helped lay, and that continuation belongs to the uptake record, though the building there remains, as he would have expected, unfinished and contested.

## The Long Revolution and the Wager on Education

The title of his 1961 book named his largest historical claim, and the claim organises the whole career when it is understood correctly. The long revolution is the proposition that the transformations conventionally separated, the extension of democracy, the industrial reorganisation of production, and the cultural expansion of literacy, education, and communication, are a single process, centuries deep, still incomplete, and driven at every stage by the pressure of ordinary people toward fuller participation in the making of their common life. The claim is historical, since it redescribes the past three centuries as one connected movement rather than as a political story with cultural decorations. It is also strategic, since it locates the socialist project inside that long movement as its conscious continuation, rather than as an interruption to be imposed from outside the society's own development. And it is educational in its centre of gravity: the revolution's decisive front, in his account, is the expansion of the means of learning and expression to everyone, since a people excluded from the making of meanings will be governed by meanings made elsewhere, whatever the formal constitution says.

That centre of gravity explains the otherwise puzzling proportions of his career, the fifteen years given to evening classes, the essays on education's expansion, the insistence that the universities' walls were a political question. Education, in the framework, is not the superstructure's finishing school. It is one of the principal means of production of a culture, the institution where the selective tradition is administered to each new generation and where, therefore, the tradition can be contested and widened. His analyses of curricula and canons apply the standing question of selection with institutional precision: who selected this list, under what pressures, from what archive, and whose experience does the selection render invisible? The questions were asked of the English syllabus he himself taught, and the asking was not rhetorical, since his own teaching practice, in the adult classes and at Cambridge, worked to widen the archive his students encountered, admitting the industrial novel, the working-class autobiography, and the literatures of the border country into rooms where they had not previously been studied.

The wager on education also attracted the period's sharpest version of the gradualism charge, examined among the criticisms, and the answer given there applies here with an addition. The addition is that the long revolution was a description before it was a strategy. The centuries of expanding literacy and participation are a fact of the record, whatever one's politics, and the description reorganises the record in a way that purely political or purely economic narratives cannot match, since it alone explains why the cultural institutions, the schools, the press, the broadcasters, became the battlegrounds they did. A strategy built on a true description has a different standing from a temperament dressed as a theory, and the charge of gradualism, to the extent it denies the description, fails; to the extent it disputes the strategy, it identifies a genuine and continuing argument within the tradition, which this article records without pretending to settle.

His late work extended the long revolution's frame to the ecological and international pressures that his final decade forced into view. Towards 2000, published in 1983, surveyed a world of interlocking crises and argued, against both despair and complacency, for the resources of hope he located in the same long movement: the accumulated capacities of ordinary people, organised in new social movements as well as in the older institutions, to extend the revolution's unfinished business. The book is the least characteristic of his major works in its speculative reach, and the most characteristic in its refusal to let a structure of feeling, the period's gathering sense of civilisational danger, pass unexamined into doctrine. He died in 1988, with a study of modern writing's forms unfinished on his desk and the long revolution, by his own account, still in its early stages, which is the ending his theory predicts for all such processes and the one his readers have generally found fitting.
## The Selective Tradition: How a Culture Edits Its Past

Between the dissolution and the tools stands a concept that the summaries often omit and that practising analysts use weekly: the selective tradition. Its statement is simple. What presents itself as the tradition of a culture, its canon of great works, its honoured history, its settled account of itself, is never the whole record. It is a selection from the whole record, made continuously by the culture's institutions, in the image of the dominant order's interests and self-understanding, and then naturalised, so that the selection is experienced as the tradition itself and the unselected remainder lapses into invisibility. The concept was developed in The Long Revolution and refined thereafter, and it converts an apparently antiquarian question, what has been preserved and taught, into a question about power in its cultural form.

The mechanism deserves the slow treatment, because selection sounds like a conspiracy and operates like a routine. No committee decides to suppress the industrial novel or the women's autobiography or the regional theatre. Publishers decline lists, syllabus committees inherit lists, examination boards reward the already taught, libraries stock what is requested, and each routine decision, defensible in itself, ratifies the previous selections and narrows the next. The dominant order's part in the process is not censorship but gravity: works that confirm its account of the culture are easier to teach, easier to publish, easier to prize, and the ease accumulates into a canon. Meanwhile the selection performs a second service for the order that benefits from it. By presenting a curated past as the culture's essence, it defines what the culture is, and therefore what counts as a contribution to it, and therefore who may plausibly claim to speak in its name. The selective tradition is hegemony operating on the archive, and its analysis is the point where Williams's historical scholarship and his politics most completely coincide.

### How does the selective tradition work?

A culture's institutions continuously select, from the whole record of its making, the works and accounts that will be preserved, taught, and honoured, and the selection, made under the dominant order's gravity, is then experienced as the tradition itself. The unselected remainder is rarely suppressed; it is unlisted, untaught, and gradually rendered invisible.

The concept cuts in two directions, and its second direction is the one that keeps it honest. If the received tradition is a selection, then the record contains more than the tradition admits, and the analyst's recovery work, the unselected novel, the forgotten movement, the defeated alternative, is a recovery of real materials for present use. The residual, in the triad's terms, is largely composed of what selection failed to bury: meanings formed under earlier conditions that survive in the archive and in practice, available to be reactivated when a later generation goes looking for ancestors for its emergent purposes. Williams's own recoveries, of the culture and society tradition in 1958, of the industrial novelists, of the Welsh writing of the coalfield, were demonstrations of the method on his own archive. Each recovery changed the map of the period it touched, not by inventing a counter-tradition but by exhibiting the selection that had produced the old map, and the demonstrations remain the best training in the method available, since the reader watches the selection being caught in the act.

The concept also disciplines its user against a reciprocal error. To discover that a tradition is selective is not to discover that it is worthless, and the selected works are frequently selected partly because they are strong, the gravity of quality operating alongside the gravity of power in ways no honest analysis can fully separate. Williams's own relation to the canon he interrogated was one of serious attachment: he taught Shakespeare and the great novelists with an intensity his students remembered, and his recoveries were additions to a tradition he honoured, not exposures of a fraud. The method's question is never whether the selected deserve their place in the abstract, but what the selection as a whole describes, whose experience it centres, whose it marginalises, and what the pattern of centring and marginalising serves. Asked in that form, the question survives the discovery that the canon contains masterpieces, because a selection of masterpieces is still a selection, and its pattern is still evidence.

Contemporary applications of the concept, conducted under this article's decay discipline, must remain structural, and the structure is easy to state without naming any institution of the present. Wherever a body maintains a catalogue, a syllabus, a schedule, a chart, or a hall of fame, a selection is being administered under pressures that can be analysed: the pressure of the already listed, of the measurable success, of the accredited judges, of the audience the body believes it serves. The analyst asks the standing questions. From what whole record is this selection drawn? What are the criteria, stated and operative, and where do they differ? What would the selection look like if drawn by the unselected, and what does the difference reveal about the order the selection serves? The questions are Williams's, and their portability across every institution that keeps a list is a measure of the concept's reach.

## Misreadings to Correct

The framework's popularity has generated a standard set of misreadings, and correcting them in one place serves the reader better than scattering the corrections through the article, though each has been touched already in its proper section. The first misreading treats the central concept as a licence for impressionism, a refined name for whatever atmosphere a sensitive reader detects in a period. The correction is the evidentiary rule: the structure must be documented through recurrence across makers and forms that never consulted one another, and it must be connected, before the analysis rests, to the material conditions that explain its emergence. A structure of feeling that floats free of both requirements is the mood-gloss, and it is the critic's failure, though a foreseeable one, rather than the concept's meaning.

The second misreading assigns moral values to the triad's temporal positions, treating the residual as the dead hand of the past and the emergent as history's promise. The correction is the distinction between the alternative and the oppositional, which cuts across both positions, together with the record's plain teaching that the surviving stock includes traditions of mutuality on which every humane politics has drawn, and the forming stock includes the characteristic brutalities of orders not yet obliged to justify themselves. The triad sorts by time and by relation to the dominant; it does not grade by worth, and the analyst who imports the grading has replaced the tool with a hope.

The third misreading reports that cultural materialism solves the base and superstructure problem, offering a better mechanism of connection between economy and culture. The correction is the article's governing claim. There is no mechanism, because there are no longer two realms for a mechanism to connect. The position must be assessed as a reframing, judged by the inquiries it enables and the pseudo-questions it retires, and any summary that grades it as a competing solution has missed the move it exists to describe. The fourth misreading inverts the third, reporting that Williams rejected materialism along with the metaphor, becoming a cultural idealist for whom meanings float free. The correction is the method's entire practice: the insistence that every cultural fact be pursued into its conditions of production, its institutions, its materials, and its costs. No major figure in the tradition was more relentless in asking who owned the press, who paid the company, who set the syllabus, and a reading that loses those questions has lost the work.

The fifth misreading is biographical, and it has been refused throughout this article on principle. It treats the adult education years as colour, the border childhood as atmosphere, the signalman's son as a brand. The correction is that the formation is the research programme in biographical form: the classroom generated the questions, the border generated the categories of mixture and crossing, and the concepts cannot be fully understood apart from the experiences they were built to describe, though they stand or fall, as all concepts must, by their performance on materials their author never touched. To use the biography to authorise the theory would be the reciprocal error, and the verdict given in this article has rested on performance, on the fruitfulness of the dissolution and the durability of the tools, and on nothing else.
## Is Cultural Materialism Marxist?

The question is asked of Williams more persistently than of any comparable figure in the tradition, and the persistence is itself diagnostic. He revised or retired the tradition's central metaphors, declined its orthodoxies of party and doctrine, and built his account around categories, experience, feeling, the ordinary, that the tradition's guardians regarded with suspicion. Critics from the orthodox side concluded at intervals that he had left the tradition while keeping its vocabulary, and the question deserves a direct answer rather than a diplomatic one, since this series exists to grade such claims.

The answer turns on what is taken to be the tradition's core. If the core is a fixed set of propositions, the two-storey model, the mechanical determination, the philosophy of history with its guaranteed destination, then Williams departed from the core, said so, and explained why each element failed the tests of evidence and use. If the core is a method and a commitment, the analysis of social life as a material process made by people under inherited conditions, directed toward the democratic transformation of those conditions, then he stands at the tradition's centre and spent a career arguing that the fixed propositions were the tradition's own betrayals of its method. His self-understanding is not in doubt: he described his work as Marxist, quarrelled with Marxisms from inside, and treated the tradition as a living body of inquiry to be corrected by its own standards rather than as a creed to be recited. The quarrels themselves are the evidence of membership. Outsiders do not fight over the correct reading of a tradition's founding concepts with the ferocity, or the learning, that his engagements display.

A useful test is the company his revisions keep. The redefinition of determination was argued from Marx's own various usage and from the word's history, not imported from outside the tradition. The dissolution was argued as a recovery of materialism from a metaphor that had made it idealist in effect, which is an internal correction in form as well as in intention. The triad generalises the tradition's own historical sense, its awareness that formations contain survivals and anticipations, into an explicit instrument. Even the structure of feeling, the concept most often cited as un-Marxist, answers a question the tradition's own theory of ideology had posed and left unworked: how new consciousness forms before it becomes doctrine. A thinker whose every revision is argued from the tradition's texts, against the tradition's orthodoxies, in the tradition's cause, is a figure of the tradition in its self-critical mode, and the series files him accordingly, as the cultural theorist against whom the tradition's other cultural theorists are measured.

The question also has a practical edge for readers deciding where to place him on their shelves and syllabuses. Placing him outside the tradition, as a cultural critic with Marxist sympathies, licenses the extraction of the portable tools from the framework that disciplines them, and the extraction is the mechanism by which the tools decay into slogans: the structure of feeling without the material analysis, the triad without the valences, the dissolution without the defeater. Placing him inside the tradition, as this article does, keeps the tools attached to their discipline, and keeps the discipline answerable to the tradition's standing questions about production, power, and transformation, which are the questions his work exists to keep open. The placement is therefore not a courtesy. It is an instruction for use.

## Where to Start and How to Read Him

The closing guidance of a thinker profile owes the reader a route, and his case is unusually well supplied with one, because he wrote at every level of difficulty and said, in interviews, how he hoped to be read. The route that the scholarship has converged upon begins with the essay that declares culture ordinary, short enough to read in one sitting and strong enough to reorganise a reader's assumptions, and proceeds to the two books of the turn of the 1960s: Culture and Society for the historical recovery of the concept, and The Long Revolution for the structure of feeling in its first full social setting and for the long revolution thesis itself. Those three texts give the formation, the method's historical wing, and the animating claim, and a reader who stops there possesses the essential Williams, though not the systematic one.

The systematic Williams is Marxism and Literature, and it should be read after the earlier books rather than before them, because its compressions are expansions in disguise: every redefined term carries the weight of the studies that produced it, and the reader who arrives with the studies in hand will find the short book lucid where the unprepared reader finds it gnomic. Keywords belongs beside it, used as he intended, not read through like a novel but consulted and browsed, its entries followed across their cross-references until the web of a culture's self-description becomes visible. The applied masterpieces, The Country and the City above all, and the television book for the media argument, complete the core, and the interviews collected as Politics and Letters supply the author's own answers to his critics, including the sharpest ones, in his own voice.

Two reading disciplines are worth stating for students in particular, since the persona section will build on them. The first is to read the definitions in their successive versions rather than in their final form alone. The structure of feeling of the film writing, of The Long Revolution, and of Marxism and Literature are three instruments of increasing refinement, and watching a concept being sharpened across twenty-five years teaches more about how concepts work than any single statement of the result. The second discipline is to keep the defeater habit: for each of his claims, state what evidence would reverse your assent, as this article has done for the dissolution. He is a writer who rewards that treatment because he practised it on himself, revising in public, withdrawing formulations that failed, and leaving the record of the revisions in print for his readers to audit. Few theorists supply their own audit trail; his is part of the bequest.

For the reader whose interest is the argument with his critics, the sequence matters as much as the texts. Read Marxism and Literature, then Eagleton's Criticism and Ideology, then the Politics and Letters interviews, in that order, and the shape of the tradition's central quarrel about experience and structure becomes intelligible as a quarrel, with positions that shift and a prosecutor who later revises his own case. The series profile of Eagleton gives the other side of the exchange in full, and the two articles are designed to be read as a pair.
## Hegemony Reworked: The Gramsci Inheritance

Williams's treatment of hegemony is the clearest instance of his general procedure applied to a concept borrowed from another thinker, and it clarifies the procedure for every other case. Antonio Gramsci, writing in prison under fascism, had developed hegemony to name the way a ruling class maintains its dominance through consent as well as coercion, through the institutions of civil society, its schools, churches, press, and associations, which organise the subordinate classes' experience of the world in terms favourable to the order that subordinates them. The concept reached the English-speaking tradition late and partially, and Williams was among its earliest serious English readers, which gave his reworking a formative influence on how the term entered Anglophone cultural studies.

The reworking proceeded in his characteristic direction, from the institutional toward the lived. Where a cruder reception treated hegemony as a synonym for ideology, a set of ruling ideas pumped into passive heads, Williams insisted that hegemony in Gramsci's strongest sense is a whole body of practices and expectations, a lived system of meanings and values which, as they are experienced as practices, appear as reciprocally confirming. Hegemony saturates. It is in the organisation of work and the conduct of schooling, in the forms of family life and the tones of public speech, and its deepest effect is that it is experienced not as an imposition but as the world, as common sense, as the pressure of reality itself. This is why mere counter-argument, the refutation of ruling ideas by better ideas, so rarely disturbs it: the order does not rest on the ideas but on the practices in which the ideas are lived, and it must be countered at the level of practice, by alternative institutions and alternative formations, which is where his analysis joins his politics of education and communication.

Two refinements in his handling have become standard because they repair obvious defects in the concept's cruder uses. The first is that hegemony is never total. A lived hegemony is always in process, always being renewed, resisted, and modified, and it always coexists with residual survivals and emergent formations of the kind the triad names, which is why the triad and the hegemony concept belong to a single framework and not to separate theories. An analysis that announces a hegemony and stops has described a tomb; Williams's hegemony is a battlefield whose front lines move. The second refinement is the alternative and oppositional distinction, already examined, which hegemony theory needs in order to explain the dominant order's differential behaviour: hospitality toward alternatives, which it incorporates as variety, and vigilance against opposition, which it works to incorporate, marginalise, or destroy. The incorporation process itself, by which an emergent practice is detached from its oppositional meanings and reissued as a style, is one of the most observed phenomena in the cultural life of the societies he studied, and his framework remains the standard instrument for describing it.

The Gramsci inheritance also locates Williams in the tradition's geography. He belonged to the Western Marxist line that took culture, consciousness, and civil society as its central problems, the line running through Gramsci and the early Lukács, rather than to the economistic line for which those problems were decoration. His differences within that line are as instructive as his membership. Where Lukács centred the analysis on class consciousness as an imputed standpoint, Williams centred it instead on experience as lived and documented in cultural forms, a divergence that runs through all his work and accounts for both its empirical richness and its theoretical exposures. The structural of feeling is, in this light, the Western Marxist problematic rebuilt by a man who had spent fifteen years listening to the consciousness in question, in evening classes, and who therefore knew its texture at first hand and refused every account of it that the texture contradicted.

## Place, Nation and the Border

A dimension of the work that English-language summaries routinely understate is its Welshness, and the understatement distorts the theory, because the border formation supplied not only the biography but several of the categories' experiential originals. Williams was formed in a community that was Welsh in its history and its chapel and union cultures, administered and schooled in English, economically bound to the English industrial system and to the railway that ran through his father's signal box, and culturally condescended to by the metropolitan centre that defined the nation's literature. To grow up there was to learn, before any theory, that a culture could be lived richly and accredited not at all, that the map of a nation's culture drawn from its centre misdescribes the territories, and that the misdescription is not an innocent error but one of the ways a centre rules. The whole apparatus of his mature work, the suspicion of accredited definitions, the attention to the unselected, the insistence that culture is made in the territories and not only in the capital, has this formation inside it.

His writing on Welsh culture and on the coalfield's industrial fiction performed the recovery work for his own territory, exhibiting a coalfield literature, in two languages, by miners and miners' sons, which the accredited history of the English novel had simply left out, though it contained the most direct record in the language of industrialisation experienced from inside. The omission was the selective tradition at its most legible: a national literature defined so that the nation's principal industrial experience, undergone by the population least represented in its universities and publishing houses, did not appear. The recovery changed the syllabus where it was adopted, and it stands as the clearest demonstration that his method's politics were not an abstraction. They began at home, with the signal box and the valley, and the concepts retain the mark: the residual, in his hands, is so often the culture of a place that the centre has declared past, still active, still carrying meanings the centre cannot supply.

The border also gave him his most durable image of the intellectual's position, worked through the fiction and the essays alike. The scholarship boy who crosses into the accredited world does not simply advance; he is translated, and something is gained and something is stranded in the translation, and his mature work is written from the crossing rather than from either bank. That position, inside the university and answerable to the valley, inside the tradition and answerable to the classroom of adults the tradition had excluded, is the biographical form of the theory's central refusal: the refusal to accept the accredited storey and the lived ground as separate realms, with the scholar officiating between them. The dissolution, stated abstractly, is that refusal generalised. A reader who sees the connection will not make the error of treating the theory as a bloodless manoeuvre, nor the opposite error of treating it as authorised by the life. The life proposed the questions. The arguments, and their performance on materials far from the border country, supply the answers' warrant.
## A Formation Read Whole: The Method Applied to a Single Period

The preceding sections have expounded the tools one by one and defended them against their critics. This section performs the joint use that the verdict recommended, applying the full apparatus to a single formation, the England of the middle nineteenth century, at a length sufficient to show the gears engaging. The choice of period is deliberate. It is distant enough for the analysis to be checked against a settled scholarly record, central enough to his own studies for the application to be faithful to his practice, and rich enough in surviving documentation for the evidentiary rules to be exercised rather than merely recited.

Open, as the dissolution directs, from the means of cultural production and their transformation. Between the 1830s and the 1860s the material basis of English print was rebuilt: steam-driven presses, machine-made paper, the repeal of the taxes on knowledge in stages, railway distribution with its bookstalls on every platform, the great circulating libraries, and a rapid expansion of elementary schooling that manufactured readers by the million. The relations of production changed with the means. The author became a professional negotiating with firms and libraries rather than a dependent seeking a patron; the publisher became a capitalist of a recognisable modern kind; the reviewer, the serial part, the triple-decker, and the magazine miscellany organised the labour of writing into forms as definite as any factory's. Nothing in this stratum of the analysis is metaphorical, and none of it is background. The fiction of the period, its lengths, its cliffhangers, its moral settlements, its very sentence rhythms under serial deadlines, is unintelligible without it, and a criticism that begins with the novels' ideas and treats this stratum as context has already accepted the two-storey picture the dissolution retires.

Now sort the formation's cultural elements by the triad. The dominant order of meanings was the complex that the period's own spokesmen called improvement: the gospel of progress through industry and commerce, the sanctity of domestic order, the gradations of respectability, the providential reading of success and failure. It was carried by institutions of formidable reach, the pulpit, the school, the improving tract, the great exhibitions, and it saturated ordinary practice in the manner hegemony describes, so that its categories, the deserving and the undeserving, the respectable and the rough, were the terms in which even its victims described themselves. The residual elements were numerous and powerful, because the formation was young and the orders it displaced were within living memory. The paternalist obligations of the old landed order survived as a standing reproach to the cash nexus, available to Tory critics of the new industry and to working-class movements alike. The dissenting chapel cultures carried an older discipline of mutual scrutiny and self-improvement that the dominant order alternately used and feared. The rural world's calendar of customs persisted inside the industrial towns their migrants had built, carried in wakes and feasts and friendly societies, neither archaic nor absorbed.

The emergent elements are where the analysis earns its difficulty, because they must be detected in solution. In the fiction and the documentary writing of the 1840s a new structure of feeling can be documented by the recurrence rule: across novels, parliamentary reports, sermons, and journalism whose makers shared no programme, there appears a common disturbance, a quality of attention to the industrial city as a place that cannot be known, a movement between fascination and horror in the description of crowds, a new uncertainty about whether sympathy can cross the distance between the classes that the city's geography had made literal. The disturbance precedes its doctrines. It is present before the socialism that would later name the city's order as its object, before the sociology that would claim the city as its science, and it registers, in solution, the experience of a generation for whom the old knowable community had dissolved and no new account of the whole had precipitated. The analyst who reads only the period's stated ideologies, utilitarian, evangelical, Chartist, conservative, will map the arguments and miss the shared disturbance beneath them, and will therefore be surprised by everything the period did next.

Apply, finally, the redefinition of determination to the whole. The limits set by the formation were real and can be named: a fiction that offended the libraries' moral certification could not reach its public, a politics that required the unpropertied to read could not outrun the schooling that existed, a drama that needed a paying metropolitan audience could not stage what that audience's censors forbade. The pressures were equally traceable: the market's pressure toward the serial and the sensation, the pulpit's pressure toward the providential ending, the pressure of the new urban experience itself, demanding forms that did not yet exist and bending the inherited forms until some of them broke into new shapes. Within those limits and under those pressures, the period's makers acted, and their actions, Dickens inventing the multi-plot city novel, the Chartist poets forcing the newspaper column into literature, the investigators inventing a prose for the unknown city, are the formation's culture, determined in the recovered sense and deducible in none. The analysis has explained a great deal, forms, careers, silences, and sudden inventions, without once ascending to a superstructure or descending to a base, and it has left, honestly marked, the residue it cannot reach: why this writer, among the hundreds subjected to the same pressures, invented the form that lasted. That residue is not a scandal. It is the space the framework reserves for agency and gift, and a framework without such a space would be the mechanical doctrine under a new name.

The exercise generalises, and that generalisation is the transferability test of the method. Substitute another formation, another century, another medium, and the questions recur in their order: how the culture in question is produced, and under what relations; the sorting of its elements among what rules, what survives, and what is taking shape, each valence proven from the record; the detection of its structure of feeling by recurrence; the specification of its limits and the tracing of its pressures; and the honest marking of the residue. A researcher who performs the sequence has conducted a cultural materialist analysis whether or not the label is used, and a researcher who skips the production stratum, or asserts the feeling without the recurrence, or invokes the pressures without tracing one, has used the vocabulary without the method, which is the distinction this article has maintained throughout.

## Drama in Performance: The Body as a Means of Production

One further case, from his earliest sustained work, deserves recovery because it shows the dissolution operating before its author had the vocabulary for it, and because it extends the framework to the art where the means of production include the human body. His first book-length study examined drama in performance, and its founding observation was that the dramatic work does not exist in the text alone. A play is produced, nightly, by a company of trained bodies in a licensed building before a paying assembly, under economic arrangements, repertory systems, star contracts, touring circuits, that are as much a part of the work's existence as its dialogue. The criticism that treats the play as a poem that happens to be staged has performed the two-storey error in miniature, assigning the meaning to a realm above the material event in which alone the meaning occurs.

The consequences he drew remain the framework of serious theatre history. The acting style of a period is a means of production, trained into bodies by institutions, and it determines, in the recovered sense, what can be meant on its stage: a company formed in declamation cannot play silence, whatever the text instructs, until the training changes, and the training changes under pressures, of new audiences, new buildings, new rivals in the cinema, that the analysis can trace. The building itself is a means of production, its sightlines and its social geography of boxes, stalls, and gallery distributing the audience into the class order the play then addresses as if it were nature. Even the interval, the lighting, the licensing of what may be shown, enter the work. A cultural materialism of the theatre is therefore not a sociology added to an aesthetics; it is the recognition that the aesthetic object was always a produced event, and that its production, recovered from prompt books, accounts, and playbills, is first-order evidence about what the work could mean to the people who made it and paid for it.

The case matters beyond the theatre because it generalises the dissolution's most radical implication. If a play's meaning is inseparable from its nightly production, then no cultural object is fully present in its ideal form, and every analysis that proceeds from the ideal form alone, the text without the performance, the programme without the schedule, the canon without the syllabus, has abstracted away a constitutive part of its object. The abstraction is sometimes necessary and always costly, and the framework's standing instruction is to know the cost: to state, for any analysis, which stratum of production has been bracketed and what the bracketing might be hiding. That instruction, modest as it sounds, would invalidate a substantial share of the criticism written in every period including his own, and it is the one his students and successors have most consistently carried into their own fields.
## The Masses Passage and the Critique of Mass Culture

Inside the conclusion of Culture and Society sits the passage for which he is most often quoted outside the scholarship, and it deserves its place in a full account because it condenses the whole framework into a single act of attention. Surveying the new vocabulary of mass communication, mass culture, and the masses themselves, he objected to the noun at the root of the family. There are in fact no masses, he wrote, in a sentence whose sense can be carried in a short fragment: there are only ways of seeing people as masses. The observation is not a pleasantry about politeness. It identifies a technique of perception with a function. To see people as masses is to see them as a single undifferentiated substance, manipulable, credulous, and threatening or pitiable by turns, and the seeing licenses the seer: the broadcaster who must simplify for them, the advertiser who must excite them, the politician who must fear or flatter them, and the cultural critic whose own refinement is defined against their crudity. The mass, in short, is not found in the population. It is manufactured in the description, by institutions whose business requires a describable audience of that shape.

The passage's analytical structure is the dissolution in miniature. A pseudo-object, the masses, had generated a pseudo-problem, how the masses are to be protected from mass culture or raised above it, around which a whole industry of lament and policy revolved. Williams dissolved the object by examining the practice that produced it, the practice of describing audiences from the standpoint of the institutions that address them, and the problem changed shape accordingly. The real questions became institutional and answerable: who describes the audience, in what terms, for what purposes, and what the description omits, which is everything that audiences actually do with what they receive, the selecting, interpreting, discussing, and remaking that his adult education experience had taught him to expect. Audience research in the decades after his death, with its findings of active, discriminating, socially situated viewers and readers, vindicated the expectation so thoroughly that the older picture of the passive mass now survives mainly as a period artefact, and the vindication belongs in the uptake record alongside the more technical adoptions.

The same conclusion performed the complementary recovery on the word culture itself, gathering the senses his history had traced into the definition that the whole later work presupposes: culture as a description of a particular way of life, which expresses certain meanings and values not only in art and learning but in institutions and ordinary behaviour. The definition's reach is the point. By extending culture from the accredited arts to the whole texture of a lived order, while retaining the arts as its most concentrated evidence, it founded the programme that cultural studies would institutionalise: the study of a society's meanings wherever they are made, in the factory and the football crowd and the soap opera as seriously as in the novel, with the same instruments and the same respect. The programme's later institutional history, the founding of the Birmingham centre for contemporary cultural studies in the following decade by Richard Hoggart, whose Uses of Literacy of 1957 had worked the adjacent ground of working-class culture's texture, and the centre's development under Stuart Hall, belongs to the history of a discipline rather than to this profile, but the genealogy runs through Williams's definitions, and Hall himself acknowledged the descent repeatedly and on the record.

His relation to that founding generation is worth one paragraph of precision, since the three names are habitually grouped and the grouping blurs real differences. Hoggart's strength was the close description of a working-class culture's texture from inside, elegiac about its erosion by commercial provision. Thompson, whose Making of the English Working Class appeared in 1963, rebuilt a class's history as a history of experience and agency, and shared with Williams the conviction that experience counts as evidence rather than decoration, while differing from him in temperament and in the centrality of political conflict to the account. Williams was the theorist of the three, the one who abstracted the shared practice into concepts, determination, hegemony, the structure of feeling, that others could carry away. The New Left milieu held them in one conversation, and the conversation, with its agreements and its sharp internal quarrels, was the seedbed of British cultural studies. A reader who wants the tradition around the man will find it in the series guide to the cultural theory of the period, linked at this article's opening, and will find there the reason this profile has treated its subject as a node in a network rather than as a solitary inventor: the concepts were his, and the conditions that made them necessary, and partly made them possible, were a generation's.

## The Fiction as a Second Instrument

The novels require a final word beyond the mention they received in the formation section, because he regarded them as continuous with the criticism in a way that the modern division of academic labour finds hard to credit. Border Country, published in 1960, sends a historian back to the border village of his childhood, to a father dying after a lifetime in the signal box, and it makes the return journey into an inquiry into what can be known about a place and a life from inside and from outside at once. The son's professional instruments, the archive, the statistic, the interview, are tested against the father's knowledge, which is of another kind, carried in practice and memory, and the novel refuses to award the victory to either. Readers who know the later theory will recognise the epistemology: two modes of knowing, the accredited and the lived, and a refusal to house them on separate storeys. The recognition should not be allowed to flatten the novel into an allegory of the theory, a flattening he resisted for his own work as for others'. The fiction came first in the order of composition more often than the criticism did, and its knowledge is the novels' own: the weight of a particular valley, a particular strike, a particular father's hands on the levers, rendered at the density that only narrative patience achieves.

The later fiction widened the canvas. Second Generation examined the Oxford of the 1960s, its car factory and its university facing one another across a class divide the novel refuses to simplify. The Volunteers took the political thriller's form and bent it toward an inquiry into the Welsh industrial present and its discontents. The Fight for Manod, published in 1979, returned to the border country to stage a contest over a planned development in a Welsh valley, a new town proposed for a drowned and emptied landscape, and it is perhaps the fiction in which the theory's categories are most active under the surface: the residual culture of the valley, the dominant order of planners and developers, the emergent possibilities contending in the inquiry's hearing room. Loyalties, his last completed novel, followed a generation of the left through the century's betrayals. Taken together, the fiction is the record of the same attention the criticism theorised, directed at the materials the criticism could not hold at novelistic density, and a complete account of the man must register that he needed both instruments and said so.
## Solitude and the Late Essays: Communications, Ecology and Hope

His communications writings from the first years of the 1960s have been mentioned for their democratic argument, and they repay a fuller summary because they contain his most concrete institutional thinking, the place where the theorist of dissolution drafted settlements. Communications, published in 1962 and revised thereafter, examined the press, broadcasting, and advertising as an interlocking system of cultural production, and asked the question his framework makes unavoidable: if these are industries that manufacture the common world of meanings, by what right are they governed as ordinary property, and what forms of public control would answer to their actual function? His answers were institutional and specific, a hostility to both the commercial monopoly and the paternalist corporation, and a family of proposals for publicly accountable bodies at arm's length from both the state and the market, financed publicly, governed representatively, and charged with provision rather than with tutelage. The proposals were of their moment and their moment passed, but the form of the question survived into every subsequent debate about the governance of communication, because the dissolution leaves no honest alternative: what produces a society's culture is productive apparatus, and who owns that apparatus is a political question that cannot be retired by calling its product speech.

The late essays gathered in Resources of Hope, published after his death, extend the framework to the questions of his final decade: the ecological crisis, the peace movement, the new social movements, and the reorganisation of work. The extension is instructive because it tests the tools on materials their inventor had not designed them for, the fairest trial a method can undergo. The ecological argument, in his handling, refused both the pastoral temptation, the escalator's green version, in which a lost harmony with nature recedes before each generation's fall, and the technocratic temptation, in which the crisis is a management problem for the order that produced it. He treated the relation to the natural world as a cultural formation like any other, with its dominant order of extraction and consumption, its residual practices of stewardship and commoning surviving from earlier formations, and its emergent meanings, in the movements that were beginning to articulate a different settlement in feeling before they could state it in policy. The triad sorts the case without strain, and the structure of feeling instrument detects, in the period's fiction and documentary record, the disturbance that the policy debate had not yet precipitated. A method that handles its inventor's last questions as readily as his first has passed the transferability test in its hardest form, across the inventor's own lifetime of changing objects.

The same essays clarify his final position on the tradition he had spent a career correcting. The socialism he argued for in the 1980s was explicitly a socialism of the long revolution's kind: democratic in its means as a condition of being democratic in its ends, centred on the extension of common control over the conditions of life, cultural and educational as well as industrial, and hospitable to the new movements as the emergent elements of its own formation rather than as rivals to be disciplined. He was unsparing about the actually existing alternatives, whose record the Soviet cluster of this series examines, and he located the failure, in terms his framework supplies, in the attempt to transform the base while leaving the whole cultural formation, its institutions of meaning, its selective traditions, its lived hegemonies, substantially inherited. The judgement connects the cultural theory to the largest historical questions the series treats, and it is stated here as his position, with the record available elsewhere in the series for readers who wish to test it.

## What the Framework Leaves Open

A profile that has praised a method's honesty should end its analytical work by inventorying, in the same spirit, what the method does not do, beyond the concessions already recorded against the criticisms. Four open items constitute the honest inventory. The first is the theory of the sign, already noted: the specific mechanics by which meanings act upon consciousness are gestured at rather than built, and the builders who came after, in semiotics and in discourse theory, built in directions he regarded with reserve, so that the joint remains the framework's most visible unfinished wall. The second is the analysis of the unconscious. His subjects know what they feel and can, in principle, say it; the possibility that experience is systematically opaque to the experiencer, that the structure of feeling might be a defence as well as a registration, is acknowledged at the framework's edges and never theorised at its centre, and readers formed by psychoanalysis will feel the absence as a structural one.

The third open item is economic in the strict sense, and it is the one the defeater guards. The framework brackets, for its own purposes, the question of how far the forms of cultural production are governed by the general laws of the capitalist economy within which most of them operate: the concentration of ownership, the drive to new markets, the commodification of attention itself. Williams wrote about all of these, in the communications work especially, but the theory of 1977 does not integrate them into a political economy of culture, and the integration has been the work of successors in the political economy of communication, whose findings sometimes sit comfortably with the dissolution and sometimes press against it, since a sufficiently powerful explanation of cultural form from economic condition is precisely the demonstration the defeater names as the dissolution's potential undoing. The file, as the verdict said, remains open at exactly that joint, and the openness is a research programme rather than an embarrassment: it tells the next analyst where to dig.

The fourth item is the question of value. Williams was a critic before he was a theorist, formed in a Cambridge where the judgement of works, their discrimination into the achieved and the failed, was the discipline's centre, and he never abandoned the practice of judgement or the belief that some works are better than others and that saying so is criticism's duty. Yet the framework supplies no theory of value to underwrite the practice. A structure of feeling registers a period's experience; it does not grade the registrations. The selective tradition explains how canons form; it does not say which selections deserve to stand. His own judgements, on Lawrence, on Dickens, on the dramatists, are delivered with a confidence the theory does not fund, and the gap between the funded analysis and the unfunded verdict is the point at which his practice most exceeds his system. Successors in the tradition have mostly resolved the tension by abandoning the judgement, which he would have regarded as a dereliction, or by importing a theory of value from elsewhere, which concedes the gap. The inventory records the item without resolving it, as he left it: a critic's conscience operating ahead of its credentials, in the open.
## Keywords in Depth: Culture, Society, Class

The vocabulary study rewards a closer look at individual entries, because the method's power is in the particulars and because three of its entries carry the framework's whole argument in miniature. Take culture itself, the entry with which the book effectively begins. The word enters English from the tending of natural growth, the cultivation of crops and animals, and extends by metaphor to the cultivation of minds, the culture of a person as an achieved refinement. In the period his history covers, the word undergoes its decisive transformation: it acquires an independent abstract life, naming first a general process of human development, then the works and practices in which that development is embodied, and finally, in the usage his own work did most to establish, a particular way of life in its entirety. Each stage of the journey is a settlement of an argument. The refinement sense carries the class settlement, culture as the property of the cultivated. The process sense carries the democratic counterclaim, culture as the growth of a whole people. The way of life sense carries his own intervention, prepared by the anthropologists and redirected by him toward the societies doing the describing. A reader who follows the entry has watched a society argue with itself for two centuries inside a single word, and has acquired the standing suspicion, which is the book's real bequest, that every important word is such an argument in progress.

Society carries a complementary history. The word's early senses centre on companionship, fellowship, the company of others, a meaning still audible in the phrase the society of friends. Its modern dominant sense, the abstract system within which lives are led, the entity that has problems and demands and a structure, is a later hardening, and the hardening has consequences his entry traces with care. A word that names a system invites the picture of individuals inside a container, acted upon by the whole they compose, and the base and superstructure architecture is one of that picture's theoretical descendants: society as a building, persons as its occupants, culture as its furnishings. His own usage, in which a society is a set of practices and relations continuously made by its members, is the dissolution applied to the container picture, and the entry for society is therefore a quiet companion to the theoretical statement of the following year.

Class, the third entry, shows the method handling the tradition's central term with the same irreverence it applied to the tradition's metaphors. The word arrives in English with senses from the schoolroom and from taxonomy, divisions for sorting, and acquires its social meaning in the period of industrial transformation, displacing the older vocabulary of ranks, orders, and estates. The displacement records a real change: a society of inherited stations becoming a society of positions within a productive system, experienced as mobile in principle and rigid in practice. But the entry also records the new word's internal divisions, class as an economic category, class as a social description, class as a consciousness and a loyalty, which the tradition's usage habitually confuses, sliding among the senses as argument requires. His own practice, distinguishing the senses and insisting on the third as a matter of formation rather than of automatic consequence, is the entry's lesson applied: a class in the full sense is made, in experience and organisation and culture, and not merely found in the census of occupations. Thompson's great history of the English working class had argued the same proposition historically; the Keywords entry argues it semantically, and the two arguments, from his closest ally in the New Left generation, reinforce one another across the shelf.

The entries can be multiplied, industry travelling from a human quality to a system, democracy from a term of abuse to a universal claim, art from a skill to a special realm, each with its settlements and its live contests, but the three examined here establish the method's claim on the reader. The vocabulary of social description is not a transparent instrument that the analyst picks up clean. It is an inheritance of arguments, some settled so long ago that their settlement is invisible, some still hot, and the analyst who has not audited the inheritance will be governed by it. Williams's audit, conducted word by word with a historian's patience, is the least dramatic of his works and in some ways the most radical, since it withdraws the ground from under every theory, his own included, that builds with unaudited words. He applied the audit to his own key terms as rigorously as to others', which is why the redefinitions in the theoretical statement arrive with their histories attached, as honest tools should.

## The Idea of a Common Culture

Running through the work from the essay of 1958 to the final interviews is a normative proposal that summaries often soften into a mood: the idea of a common culture. It must be stated precisely to be distinguished from its neighbours. A common culture is not a uniform culture, in which everyone consumes the same works and holds the same tastes; he was explicit that uniformity is the commercial and the bureaucratic dream, not his. Nor is it a national culture in the accredited sense, the selective tradition with a flag on it. A common culture, in his formulation, is a culture in which the means of making meanings, the education, the institutions, the channels of communication, are held in common and open to all, so that the culture's content is continuously made by the whole community of its members rather than delivered to most of them by a few. The emphasis falls on the means and the making. Diversity of content is the expected result of common means, since a people actually making its culture will make it variously, in regions and classes and generations whose differences the common institutions exist to connect rather than to erase.

The proposal gathers the whole career into a single sentence's worth of politics. The adult education classroom was a fragment of a common culture in operation, its means, a tutor, a syllabus, a room, held open to whoever came, its content remade nightly by the encounter between the accredited tradition and the students' experience. The communications proposals were the same fragment scaled to a society: publicly accountable means of expression, open in principle to the whole community of makers. The critique of the selective tradition was the proposal's negative form, the demonstration that the existing means were not common but administered, and that the administration, not any deficiency in the unselected, explained the map of the accredited culture. And the dissolution underwrites the whole, since only if culture is production does the question of who owns its means arise in its full force. A superstructure can be democratised only in its consumption, by wider access to its products. A production can be democratised in its making, and the difference between those two democratisations is the difference between his politics and the cultural policy of every government that has preferred the cheaper one.

He was under no illusion about the distance between the proposal and any existing society, and the late work's tone, the resources of hope held against an honest inventory of defeats, reflects the measurement. The common culture remained for him a direction rather than a destination with a date, the long revolution's cultural form, and he treated its partial realisations, the public library, the extramural class, the public broadcaster at its best, as earnest money rather than as achievements to be celebrated into complacency. The proposal is recorded here, at the close of the analytical sections, because it is where the framework's user must decide what the framework is for. As an instrument of analysis it stands without the proposal, and scholars who share nothing of his politics use it daily. As the work of the man who built it, it was built for the proposal, and the profile would falsify its subject if it presented the tools as neutral techniques that happened to be found in a socialist's workshop. They were forged for a purpose, and the purpose is part of their design.
## Reception and Reputation: The Audit Trail of a Career

The reception of the work across his lifetime and after his death in 1988 forms a pattern that the selective tradition concept describes almost too neatly, and the pattern is worth recording because it disciplines the claims this article has made for his standing. The early reception placed him as a critic in the line of Leavis, a formidable reader of literature with a social conscience, and the placement, flattering and limiting at once, governed the reviewing of the books of the 1950s. Culture and Society was received as a work of literary history with a polemical edge, which it is, and its status as a founding text of a new field was invisible to its first reviewers because the field did not yet exist to receive it. The Long Revolution extended the puzzle: reviewers reached for the categories they had, sociology, history, criticism, education, and the book's refusal to sit in any of them was recorded as a defect of definition rather than as the announcement of a method. A writer is at the mercy of the accredited categories in his own reception as surely as a culture is in its canon, and his early career is a case study in the costs of arriving before one's shelf exists.

The 1970s changed the placement. The theoretical statement of 1977, arriving into a field that the structuralist reconstruction had taught to demand theory, forced the reckoning that Eagleton's book led, and for a decade the reputation was contested at the highest level of seriousness: founder or obstacle, the indispensable precursor or the humanist survival. The interviews of Politics and Letters, in which the New Left Review's editors pressed him over three days on the entire record, remain the most remarkable document of the contest, because the pressed man answered with a candour about his own formation, his disagreements, and his second thoughts that converted many readers whom the treatises had left unpersuaded. The reputation that emerged from the decade was of a different kind from the one that entered it: no longer a critic with a social conscience but a theorist with a body of work behind the theory, to be answered at the level of first principles.

The posthumous reception completed a further turn. The structuralist moment that had prosecuted him receded, its own categories entering the archive as period pieces, while his instruments remained in service, and the obituaries and reassessments of 1988 and after, including the prosecutor's own revised estimate, settled into the judgement that the subsequent decades have not disturbed: the most important Marxist cultural theorist in the English language, and a founder, with the Birmingham school he influenced and sometimes quarrelled with, of the modern study of culture. The settlement should be recorded with the framework's own caution. A reputation is a selection administered in the open, and his has been selected, inevitably, in ways he would have audited: the portable concepts burnished, the politics softened in the commemorative literature, the fiction allowed to lapse from print and memory while the theory stayed in the syllabus. The corrective is the one his method prescribes. Read the whole record, including the novels, the interviews, the political essays, and the late work, and let the selection pressure show.

His influence on the generation that built cultural studies as a discipline is the largest single item in the reception record, and it operated through teaching as much as through print. Stuart Hall, who directed the Birmingham centre through its most creative period, described encountering Culture and Society and The Long Revolution as a formation: the discovery that the questions he needed existed and already had a literature. The description was repeated, with variations, by a generation of scholars across several countries, and it identifies the specific form of his influence: not a doctrine transmitted but a permission granted and a set of instruments supplied. Doctrines found schools that defend them and are buried with them. Instruments found workshops. The durability recorded in the uptake section is the durability of the workshop kind, and it explains why his name attaches to fewer positions than to procedures: the structure of feeling as a way of working, the triad as a way of sorting, the audit of keywords as a way of reading, flow as a way of watching. A theorist survives in his procedures or not at all, and the survival, in this case, is documented in the daily practice of disciplines that have forgotten most of the quarrels in which the procedures were forged.

One more reception fact belongs in the record, because it bears on the verdict's defeater. The dissolution has now been available for nearly half a century to a scholarly world with every incentive to refute it, including whole schools whose founding gesture was its rejection. The refutation the defeater specifies, a demonstration that the material analysis of culture still needs an asymmetric explanation of cultural form from economic condition, has been attempted in fragments, in the political economy of communication and in revivals of ideology theory, and the fragments have produced valuable work without commanding the field's assent as a refutation. The verdict of this article therefore stands where the evidence leaves it: the dissolution succeeds, conditionally, with its file open at the joint the defeater names, which is the strongest position any reframing in the human sciences can honestly occupy.
## Education as the Decisive Means of Production

The education argument deserves extraction from the long revolution section where it was introduced, because it is the point at which Williams's theory makes its most testable institutional claim and because the claim's fortunes since his death supply evidence both ways. The claim is that education is not a service delivered to a culture formed elsewhere, nor a ladder by which individuals escape their class, but one of the central means by which a culture produces and reproduces itself, selecting what of the inherited record each generation will receive, training the capacities by which new meanings are made, and distributing those capacities, unequally, across the population. It follows that the governance of education, its curricula, its admissions, its funding, and its purposes, is cultural politics in the primary sense, and that a movement which treats education as a welfare provision to be expanded after the serious business is settled has misunderstood the order of its own priorities.

His evidence was the record he had helped to make. The extramural system in which he served was itself a product of the long revolution's pressure, the demand of organised working people, through their unions and associations, for access to the accredited knowledge from which their schooling had excluded them, and its classes were a working model of education as a common means of production: the syllabus negotiated between the institution's inheritance and the students' purposes, the tutor's authority resting on the material rather than on the certificate, the outcomes measured in the students' subsequent making, in their unions, their writing, their teaching of others. When he wrote about the expansion of the universities and the reform of curricula in the 1960s, the extramural model stood behind the argument as its proof of concept: adults whom the system had classified as incapable of higher learning conducted it at the highest level when the means were held open to them, and the classification, not the capacity, was the artefact.

The counter-evidence and the complications must be entered honestly, since the claim is empirical as well as normative. The expansion of education in the decades after his formative period did not produce the common culture in anything like the form he hoped, and its systems learned to select with new instruments as fast as the old ones were dismantled: the examination reformed becomes the examination stratified, the open syllabus becomes the marketed credential, and the means of cultural production, expanded, are administered in the dominant order's interest with a sophistication his generation underestimated. He registered the beginning of this disappointment himself, in the later essays' insistence that expansion without a change in the relations of education, who governs, who selects, whose experience counts as knowledge, reproduces the old selection at a larger scale. The framework survives the disappointment because the disappointment is one of its own predictions in another dress: a means of production expanded under unchanged relations will serve the relations, and the remedy the theory prescribes is the one it always prescribed, to contest the relations and the selection together, in the institution, with the instruments of analysis the institution itself can be taught to supply.

For the teachers addressed in the persona section, the practical residue of the argument is a set of questions to be asked of any syllabus before it is taught. What selection does this list enact, and from what whole record was it drawn? Whose experience does the selection centre, and whose does it render invisible or merely illustrative? What capacities does the course train, the capacity to receive the tradition, to interrogate it, to add to it, and in what proportions? And what would the list look like if the unselected were admitted as evidence rather than as decoration? The questions are not an attack on syllabuses, which are selections by necessity. They are the method's audit applied to the teacher's own means of production, and a course that has passed the audit can be taught with a cleaner conscience and, in his experience and in the experience of the tradition he founded, to considerably better effect.

## The Orwell Book and the Ethics of Reading a Life

His short study of George Orwell, published in 1971, is easily overlooked among the larger works, and it earns a section in a full profile because it is the purest display of his critical ethics, the fairness that even his prosecutors conceded. Orwell was, by 1971, the most fought-over corpse in English letters, claimed by the Cold War's camps as a prophet, denounced by the left he had belonged to as a renegade, and reduced by both to a selective tradition of one. Williams's procedure was the method's own. He restored the whole record, the imperial policeman in Burma, the investigator who tramped and dishwashed his way into the lives of the poor, the democratic socialist of the Spanish war and the wartime broadcasts, the dying writer of the last terrifying book, and he showed the continuities that the selections concealed: a single attention, to power and to the corruption of language by power, working itself out across a life whose positions changed because its holder kept looking. The famous paradoxes of the received Orwell, the socialist who hated socialists, the democrat who feared the people, dissolved under the restoration, as pseudo-problems do, into a record of a man arguing with his own experience in public.

The ethics on display generalise into a rule for reading any thinker, and the rule has governed this article's treatment of its own subject. Restore the whole record before judging the selection. Distinguish the writer's questions, which are usually durable, from the writer's answers, which are usually dated. Identify the selective tradition that produced the received figure, including the selection performed by the writer's enemies and the one performed by his friends, which is commonly the more effective. And extend to the dead the fairness of the defeater: state what in the record would change your verdict, and go and look for it. The Orwell book performs all four operations in a hundred pages, and it is recommended to researchers in the persona section as the shortest complete course in his method that he ever wrote, shorter than the theoretical statement and, for many readers, more persuasive, because the method is shown persuading rather than described.
## The Industrial Novel and the Evidence of the Unselected

The recovery of the industrial novel, pursued across several essays and woven through the larger studies, is the recovery project in which the method's evidentiary claims can be most exactly assessed, because the materials are concrete and the selection that suppressed them is documented in the accredited histories themselves. The novels in question were written in the middle decades of the nineteenth century, in the first shock of industrialisation, by writers positioned inside or beside the world they described: the factory districts' own observers, the Chartist poets and journalists, the Welsh coalfield's emerging writers in the later part of the story, and the better-known condition of England novelists who visited the industrial districts as investigators. The accredited history of the Victorian novel, as it settled in the universities, centred a canon from which most of this writing was absent, and the absence was explained, when it was noticed at all, by quality: the omitted works were crude, propagandist, aesthetically null.

Williams's counter-demonstration proceeded by the method's steps. He exhibited the whole record, or enough of it to break the selection's naturalness: a substantial literature of industrial experience, formally various, much of it competent and some of it extraordinary, whose existence the accredited map did not register. He then identified the selection's operative criteria, which proved, on inspection, to be criteria about the position of the writer and the destination of the work as much as about the work's qualities: writing that treated industrial life as its centre rather than as a problem to be visited, writing addressed to the class it described rather than to the class that bought novels, writing in forms, the newspaper serial, the Chartist journal's fiction column, that the accredited archive had not preserved. And he drew the conclusion the method exists to draw: the map of the period's literature in the accredited histories is a map of the selection, and a literary history that does not audit its archive will mistake the selection's boundaries for the period's.

The case matters beyond its period because it supplies the template for a question researchers in every field of cultural study now ask as a matter of routine, and the routine is his bequest. What does the archive not hold, and why? Which institutions preserved, and what were their criteria, and whose making fell outside them? The answers are rarely a story of bonfires. They are the gravity story of the selective tradition, the unlisted, the unreprinted, the untaught, lapsing across two generations into an invisibility that later scholars then cite as evidence of non-existence. Against that gravity his whole career is a counterweight, and the counterweight's mechanism is worth stating plainly for the researchers who will use it: recovery is not an act of piety toward the forgotten but an evidentiary correction, undertaken because an analysis built on a selected record will reproduce the selection's errors at the level of theory. The structure of feeling of a period cannot be documented from its accredited products alone, since accreditation is one of the forces the structure was forming against. The unselected archive is where the emergent keeps its earliest records, and the analyst who confines the search to the canon has decided the conclusion before opening a book.

## Modernism and the Metropolitan Perception

A late essay, on modernism and the metropolitan perception, shows the method operating on the most accredited material of all, the canonical avant-garde, and it is included here because it demonstrates that the audit of selection is not a weapon reserved for the tradition's enemies. The essay examines the formation of modernist writing in the great metropolitan centres and identifies the social experience precipitating its characteristic forms: the city of strangers, the crowd of immigrants and exiles, the liberation and the desolation of a world in which no community is knowable and every perception is mediated. The modernists, in his reading, were disproportionately the displaced, provincials, colonials, and foreigners in the capitals, and their formal innovations, the fractured narration, the stream of consciousness, the collage of voices, register the structure of feeling of deracination at the centre of the modern world. The reading honours the achievement completely. It also performs the audit: the metropolitan experience, universalised by the accredited tradition into the human condition as such, was the experience of a specific and privileged fraction, and the universalisation was a selection, which rendered invisible the other modernities being lived and written elsewhere, in the territories the metropolitans had come from and in the classes beneath them.

The essay's importance for the profile is its demonstration that the method has no protected objects. The same questions, whose experience, selected by whom, under what institutional gravity, are asked of the avant-garde he admired as of the pastoral he dismantled, and the answers enrich rather than diminish the admired work, since a form understood as the registration of a specific experience is more interesting, not less, than a form worshipped as a timeless breakthrough. Students meeting the essay often report that it changes their relation to the modernist canon more than any other single text of his, and the report is intelligible: the essay models the exact posture the whole framework recommends, attachment without idolatry, judgement without contempt, and the audit conducted as an act of attention rather than of prosecution.
## Culture and Society Revisited: The Tradition He Recovered

The recovery performed in the 1958 book earns a section to itself at this depth of treatment, as the founding demonstration of the selective tradition method and because its argument about the word culture has been absorbed so completely that its originality is now hard to see. The book's procedure was to take the line of English writers who had responded to industrialism by elaborating the idea of culture, from Burke and Cobbett through Coleridge and Mill, Carlyle and Ruskin, Morris and Arnold, Lawrence and Eliot, Orwell and Leavis, and to read them as a tradition: not a sequence of isolated opinions but a continuous inquiry, carried across opposed politics, into what the industrial transformation was doing to human life and what in human life could be set against its degradations. The tradition had not been visible as a tradition before the book assembled it, and the assembly itself was the argument's first move: these writers, conservatives and socialists, romantics and utilitarians, shared a discovery, that the changes summed up as industrialism required a word and a standard by which the quality of a whole lived order could be judged, and that the word they converged upon was culture.

The second move was the audit of the tradition's own selection. The line as he recovered it was not innocent. It centred a particular England, literary, southern in its institutions, male in its voices, and it conducted its inquiry into the quality of life largely without the testimony of the people whose lives were most transformed, the industrial workers who appear in its pages as objects of concern, of fear, of improvement, rarely as makers of meanings in their own right. His conclusion drew the consequence his whole career would execute: the tradition's question was the right question and its archive was too narrow, and the inquiry had to be continued with the archive widened to include the culture that working people had actually made, in their institutions, their writing, and their ordinary practice. Culture is ordinary is that conclusion stated as a slogan; Culture and Society is the conclusion earned as scholarship. The two texts of 1958 belong together, the essay as the manifesto of the book's implication, and readers who take the slogan without the scholarship mistake a research programme for a sentiment.

The third move, easiest to miss, was the redefinition of the tradition's key term by the history of its use. By the book's end, culture has been shown in its successive senses, as a process of development, as the works in which development is recorded, as a standard of judgement abstracted from the works, and the confusions among the senses have been exhibited as the source of the period's characteristic muddles, in which a defence of certain works masquerades as a defence of development itself, or a judgement of taste presents itself as a judgement of a civilisation. The clarification he proposed, culture as the description of an entire pattern of living, its meanings and values expressed in institutions and ordinary behaviour no less than in art and learning, is the anthropological sense redirected homeward, and its proposal in 1958, from inside English literary studies, was the founding act of the field that would later call itself cultural studies. Founding acts are usually recognised late, and this one was recognised across the following two decades as the discipline assembled itself in the space the definition had opened.

## Advertising and the Magic System

One essay, on advertising, has had a career so disproportionate to its length that a full profile must account for it. Written at the turn of the 1960s and later incorporated into the communications writings, it proposed that advertising be understood as a magic system: a highly organised form of social communication in which objects are validated not by their uses but by their association with desired states of being, so that the purchaser is offered, with the soap or the car, a transformation of the self and its relations. The comparison to magic was exact rather than decorative. Like the magic of the societies anthropologists described, advertising operates by a logic its participants half believe and half know to be a performance, and its real function is social: it is the official art of a society organised around consumption, the place where that society states, in concentrated and therefore legible form, what it takes a good life to be.

The essay's analytical yield has kept it in print. By treating advertisements as evidence, as the documentary record of a formation's official desires, it licensed the reading of commercial culture with the same seriousness as accredited culture, a licence the subsequent discipline used to the full. By insisting that the magic works not by deceiving fools but by addressing real needs, for esteem, for love, for security, for a legible identity, in a vocabulary that attaches those needs to purchasable objects, it avoided the contempt for audiences that disfigured the mass culture critique and located the criticism where his framework always locates it: in the organisation of production, in an industry whose business is the manufacture of association, rather than in the deficiencies of the associated. And by historicising the system, tracing its rise with the organisation of mass consumption, it treated advertising neither as an eternal temptation nor as a conspiracy but as an institution with a history, whose forms could therefore be otherwise. The essay is the dissolution applied to a single industry in a few thousand words, and it remains the shortest route into his way of seeing for a reader who wants the method before the system.
## The Welsh Industrial Novel and the Question of a National Culture

The Welsh material, introduced earlier through the border formation, supports one more sustained treatment, because in the essays on the Welsh industrial novel he worked out, for a small nation inside a larger state, the general theory of how a people's culture is rendered invisible and how the rendering is reversed. The coalfield of the south, and the quarrying and steel districts beside it, had produced by the early twentieth century a substantial literature in two languages: novels, stories, poems, and autobiographies written by miners, by the sons of miners, and by the teachers and preachers of the coalfield communities, recording from inside the experience that the English industrial novel had recorded from the visiting investigator's standpoint. The accredited history of the novel in English registered almost none of it. The accredited history of Welsh literature, organised around the Welsh language and its classical forms, registered the Welsh-language portion partially and the English-language portion, the larger part, hardly at all. The coalfield's writing fell between two selective traditions, invisible to each for opposite reasons, and its invisibility was therefore doubly secured.

The recovery he performed, in essays and in his editorial encouragement of the writers' republication, followed the method exactly and produced the method's characteristic result: not a plea for inclusion on grounds of representation, but a demonstration that the record of British industrial experience is incomplete, and every generalisation built on it unsound, until the coalfield's testimony is admitted as evidence. The generalisations at stake were not minor. The accredited account of the industrial novel described a form that observes the factory from outside, through the investigator, the reformer, the anxious middle-class visitor, and theorised the form accordingly. The coalfield novels exhibit a different form, in which the industrial world is the known community itself, its solidarities and its internal conflicts the substance rather than the background, and the difference is not a variation within the form but a refutation of the form's accredited definition. One recovered archive, admitted, revises the theory of the genre. That is the evidentiary argument for recovery in its strongest shape, and it is why his recovery work was never antiquarian: each recovery was a test of an accredited generalisation, and the generalisations that failed the test were replaced.

The national question that runs beneath the essays should be stated, since it connects the Welsh work to the framework's largest claims. A national culture, in his treatment, is not an essence carried in blood or language alone, though language matters to it profoundly, as his respect for the Welsh-language tradition shows. It is a people's whole way of life as made and remade in its institutions and ordinary practice, which means that the industrial valleys are as much the nation as the rural heartland of the accredited image, and that a national culture defined by the heartland alone is a selective tradition performing its usual service, in this case for a romantic nationalism as much as for the metropolitan centre. The double refusal, of the centre's map and of the heartland's counter-map, is characteristic: the method audits every selection, including the selections made by the side the analyst favours, and the audit's impartiality is the ground of its authority when it is turned, as it mostly is, against the powerful.

## Science Fiction and the Futures He Read

A smaller item in the record illuminates the range of his attention and the consistency of the method at its edges. He read and wrote about science fiction, a form the accredited criticism of his formation treated as beneath notice, and he treated it as he treated every form: as a registration, in solution, of a period's structure of feeling about its own trajectory. The futures a society imagines, in its popular forms especially, are evidence of the first order about its present, since the imagined future is assembled from the present's hopes and dreads with the censorship of plausibility relaxed. His interest in the form was of a piece with his interest in the industrial novel and the television schedule: the unaccredited archive is where the emergent announces itself before the accredited forms have found room for it, and a criticism that waits for accreditation will always arrive after the precipitation it claims to study. The point is small in the record and large in the method, and it is recorded here because students consistently find it the most liberating of his permissions: the whole documentary culture of a period, its advertisements and its genre fictions and its schedules included, is the archive, and the analyst's discrimination is to be exercised inside the archive, on the evidence, rather than at its gate, on the accreditation.
## Determination in the Workshop: Tracing a Single Pressure

The third tool's discipline, that a pressure must be traced and not gestured at, is easier to demand than to exhibit, and this section exhibits it on a single well-documented pressure from the history he knew best: the pressure exerted on the nineteenth-century novel by the circulating library's moral certification. The purpose is pedagogical in the honourable sense. A student who watches one pressure traced from institution to sentence will know what the tool requires in a way no definition can teach, and a critic who cannot perform the tracing for the pressures they invoke has been warned, by example, where the standard lies.

The institution first. The great circulating libraries of the middle of the century were commercial enterprises selling subscriptions to households, predominantly respectable and predominantly managed, at the point of purchase, by the women of the household and supervised by its men. Their commercial interest was in stock that no subscriber could object to, since a single objectionable volume, discovered by a daughter, could cost a subscription and a reputation. The libraries therefore operated a certification regime, informal, unaccountable, and nearly absolute, because their purchases, in bulk, for the three-volume editions that individual buyers could not afford, constituted the market for new fiction. A publisher who lost the libraries lost the edition; an author whom the libraries declined was, with rare exceptions, an author without an income. No law enacted this censorship and no conspiracy administered it. It was a relation of production, a fact about who bought books and on what terms, and its effects on what could be written were as systematic as any statute's.

Now trace the pressure into the work. It appears first at the level of subject: the sexual lives of adults, the interior of marriages, the existence of desire outside the marriage plot's approach, could not be treated with the directness the period's own French fiction was developing, not because English writers lacked the nerve but because the certification stood between the treatment and the market. It appears next at the level of structure: the heroine's history must be conductible through the volume a young subscriber might open, which shapes the distribution of knowledge in the narrative itself, what the narration can show and what it must report at a distance or omit into the famous ellipses. It appears finally at the level of the sentence and the scene, in the period's characteristic indirections, its meaningful glances and interrupted confessions, which the accredited criticism read as the age's prudery of feeling. The tracing corrects the reading. The indirection is not primarily a feeling; it is a settlement between a writer's intention and a market's certification, renewed in every chapter, and it coexists, in the same writers, with a directness on other subjects, industrial cruelty, religious doubt, the economics of marriage itself, where the certification did not bite. A pressure is known by its differential effects, biting here and not there, and the differential pattern is the signature that distinguishes a traced pressure from a climate of opinion invoked to explain everything and therefore nothing.

The example generalises into the tool's operating instruction. For any cultural product, identify the certifications that stood between the making and the audience: the purchaser whose bulk order governed the edition, the board whose licence governed the stage, the scheduler whose junction governed the programme, the board of studies whose list governed the classroom text. Then trace the pressure differentially, finding the subjects, structures, and sentences it bent and the neighbouring ground it left unbent, because the unbent ground is the control that proves the pressure real. The procedure is laborious and its results are checkable, which is the point. Determination, in the recovered sense, is not a philosophy to be believed but a research practice to be performed, and its performance, repeated across the cases in this article, is the strongest evidence that the redefinition was a contribution to method rather than a verbal manoeuvre in a doctrine's defence.

## The Essay as His Characteristic Form

A word about his forms as a writer belongs in a complete profile, because the choice of form is itself one of his subjects and his practice embodies his teaching. The essay, in its older sense of an attempt, a trying out, was his characteristic instrument alongside the historical study, and the preference was principled. The essay permits the provisional, the exploratory movement of a mind testing a formulation against materials and registering the resistance, where the treatise demands the appearance of a completed system. A theorist who believed that concepts must remain open to revision by experience would naturally prefer the form that displays revision in progress, and his essays, on Orwell, on advertising, on the country and the city in their first versions, show the concepts arriving: the structure of feeling tried against a decade's writing, the selective tradition against a canon, flow against an evening's viewing, each formulation offered with its evidence and its seams visible.

The historical studies embody the complementary virtue, patience. The Country and the City reads four centuries of writing in sequence without hurrying any item of it, and the patience is the method: the escalator is visible only to a reader who has walked the whole staircase, and no shortcut through selected highlights will reveal a pattern whose essence is recurrence across the unselected. There is a discipline here for every researcher the persona section addresses. The pattern that convicts a form of ideology is rarely in the famous instance. It is in the fiftieth instance, the minor poet, the forgotten novel, the routine schedule, where the gesture repeats without the genius that might excuse it as invention. His books teach that discipline by practising it at a length that modern academic incentives discourage, and the discouragement is itself a datum for his framework: the means of production of scholarship, its career structures and its publication economies, exert pressures on the forms knowledge takes, and the long, slow, archive-saturating study is among the forms those pressures now make costly. He would have recognised the situation, and named it, and gone on writing at his own length, which is perhaps the most practical lesson the profile can pass on.
## Williams Among the Theorists: A Comparative Placement

Placement among contemporaries sharpens a profile, and his position can be fixed by three contrasts that the scholarship returns to. The first is with the structuralist Marxism that prosecuted him, represented in the criticism section by Eagleton's book and behind it by the Althusserian reconstruction. The contrast is architectural. The structuralist account begins from structures that constitute subjects and distributes experience to the level of effect; his account begins from lived experience as documented in cultural production and builds structures as the documented patterns of that experience under specifiable conditions. Each architecture has its characteristic blindness. The structuralist cannot explain change from inside, since effects do not act, and must import rupture as an uncaused event. His account cannot, without supplementation, explain how experience is systematically opaque to itself, how a structure of feeling can misrecognise as well as register. The subsequent history of the field is largely the history of attempts to build the bridge between the two architectures, and the bridge builders, working across cultural studies and the social study of culture, have generally started from his bank, because his bank has the archive on it.

The second contrast is with the Frankfurt tradition of the culture industry thesis, which he respected and resisted in equal measure. The Frankfurt account, in its classic form, treats the commercial production of culture as a machinery of deception whose products standardise consciousness, and it reserves genuine art for a negativity preserved outside the market's reach. His resistance was empirical and democratic at once. Empirically, the audiences his life had acquainted him with did not behave as the thesis required: they selected, argued, remade, and used commercial products for purposes the producers had not scripted, and a theory contradicted by the classroom and the household needed revision at its centre, not decoration at its edges. Democratically, the thesis reproduced, in a left vocabulary, the mass culture lament he had dismantled in 1958, with the critic's own taste installed as the standard the masses fail. His alternative, the analysis of commercial production as production, with its institutions, its pressures, and its real but limited determinations, and of audiences as makers within limits, has worn better than the thesis it resisted, and the active audience research of later decades is its vindication in the record.

The third contrast is with the poststructuralist turn that followed, in which the text dissolved into discourse and the subject into positions within it. He watched the turn's beginning with a sympathy and a reserve that his last essays record: sympathy, because the turn's attention to language as constitutive answered to his own treatment of language as practical consciousness; reserve, because the dissolution of the subject into discourse threatened, in his judgement, to lose the experiencing, labouring, resisting person in whom his whole framework anchors, and with the person, the politics. The reserve has also worn well in parts. The disciplines that passed through the turn emerged with their textual sophistication permanently raised and with a renewed need, which they have spent decades supplying, for accounts of agency, institution, and material production of exactly the kinds his framework had stockpiled. Placement, then: the theorist of the lived and the made, standing between the structuralists who dissolved experience and the textualists who dissolved the maker, holding the position that a culture is what people do, under conditions they inherit, with means they can collectively come to own. The position was unfashionable at least twice in his lifetime and after, and the record of its returns is the reception history already given.

## The Concept of Experience, Defended and Bounded

Since the gravest charges converge on the word experience, the concept deserves a final precise treatment, gathering what the article has distributed. Experience, in his usage, is not the raw feel of a moment, private and incorrigible. It is the full process by which a person or a class lives its conditions: the work done and the meanings made in the doing, the suffering undergone and the account formed of the suffering, the inherited categories applied and found wanting and revised. Experience so defined is social through and through, since the categories are inherited, the conditions are shared, and the accounts are formed in language held in common. It is also evidential, since it is the only register in which the human cost and the human meaning of a social order appear, and a theory that discounts it has discounted the phenomenon it exists to explain. His defence of the concept against its structuralist critics was therefore not nostalgia for immediacy. It was a claim about where the evidence lives.

The bounds he placed on the concept are as important as the defence, and they are the answer to the charge that experience, in his hands, is self-certifying. Experience is not transparent to itself, and he knew it: the whole apparatus of the selective tradition and of hegemony describes the mechanisms by which experience is organised, edited, and misled from inside. A structure of feeling can be cross-examined, its documentation demanded, its conditions traced, its partiality corrected against the experience of others, which is why the method multiplies archives and standpoints rather than enthroning any single testimony. Experience is the evidence, and evidence is not verdict; it is what verdicts must answer to and what cross-examination exists to test. The courtroom figure is his in spirit if not in letter: testimony is indispensable, testimony is examinable, and a court that excluded testimony in favour of the architecture of the building would not be more rigorous, only less informed.

One consequence of the bounded defence deserves emphasis, because it explains the framework's appeal to historians in particular. If experience is evidence, then the archive of experience, the diaries, the autobiographies, the oral testimonies, the working-class writing his recovery projects championed, is a primary archive for social theory and not an illustrative supplement to the real documents of state and economy. The reordering of archives that followed in social and cultural history, the turn to the sources in which the unaccredited recorded their lives, is among his most concrete legacies, and it was achieved not by a plea for empathy but by an argument about evidence: a theory of a society that cannot hear most of its members is not being rigorous when it proceeds without them, only deaf at a higher level of abstraction.

## The Politics of the Long Revolution, Restated

The political teaching can be gathered, at the end of the analysis, into the three propositions that his work, taken whole, advances. The first is that the transformation of a society's material production and the transformation of its cultural production are one process, and that a politics which sequences them, first the economy, then, in some later stage, the meanings, will find the untransformed culture reproducing the old order inside the new economy's institutions, as the record of the revolutionary states, examined elsewhere in this series, repeatedly shows. The second is that the means of cultural production, education, publishing, broadcasting, and their successors in function, are therefore primary political objects, to be brought under common and democratic control with the same seriousness as any industry, and that the forms of that control must themselves be democratic, since a cultural production administered by a tutelary state merely changes the selector. The third is that the agent of the transformation is not a vanguard armed with the correct consciousness but the people themselves in their long, uneven, self-educating movement, the long revolution, whose structures of feeling register its advances before its programmes state them, and whose education, in the widest sense, is both the means and the end of the whole process.

Each proposition is contestable and has been contested, from the orthodoxies he quarrelled with and from the liberal order he opposed. They are stated here as the content of his politics rather than as this article's endorsements, in keeping with the neutrality this series observes toward the doctrines it profiles: the steelman has been given, the criticisms have been given at full strength with their author's retraction, the concessions have been paid, and the verdict, with its defeater, has been stated on the framework's performance. The politics is the framework's animating purpose, recorded because a profile that omitted it would misdescribe the tools' design, and weighed by the reader with the same instruments the article has supplied.
## Working Definitions: A Consolidated Glossary in Prose

Students asked to consolidate the vocabulary before an examination are best served by a single passage that states each term in its working form and in its relation to the others, and this section supplies it, in prose, as the article's other sections have supplied the arguments. Cultural materialism is the framework's name for the doctrine that making culture is material making, possessed of means and relations of its own and studied on one level with production at large. The dissolution is the framework's founding move: the removal of the base and superstructure problem by reframing, rather than its solution within its own terms. A structure of feeling is a period's or a generation's experience as lived before precipitation into institutions and doctrines, experience in solution, documented through recurrence among makers who never consulted one another. The dominant is the ruling order of meanings and values, lived as hegemony, a saturation of practice experienced as common sense. The residual is what survives actively from earlier formations, available for incorporation, for toleration as the alternative, or for reactivation in opposition. The emergent is what is newly forming, detectable first as feeling, and graded by the dominant order's difficulty in absorbing it. The selective tradition is the continuous editing of a culture's record by its institutions, under the dominant order's gravity, naturalised as the tradition itself. Determination names the bounding of a field and the pressing upon its occupants, never the scripting of outcomes in advance. A knowable community is a social world surveyable whole by its members, whose passing under industrial scale reorganised the forms of narrative. Flow is the composed sequence of broadcasting, planned by an institution to hold an audience across junctions, and the proper unit of that medium's analysis. The escalator of nostalgia is the recession of the lost organic community at the speed of its pursuit, exposing the pastoral as a form rather than a memory. The long revolution is the single centuries-deep process of democratic, industrial, and cultural expansion, still incomplete, within which the framework's politics is set.

The relations among the terms matter more than the terms, and they can be stated in one movement. A formation at any moment is a dominant order, lived as hegemony and editing its past through a selective tradition, coexisting with residual elements it has not digested and emergent elements it cannot yet name. The emergent announces itself as a structure of feeling, in the period's art and documentary record, before it precipitates into institutions. The whole is held in a field of determination, limits set and pressures exerted, within which people act and occasionally bend the field itself. The analyst's instruments map onto the movement one to one, which is the mark of a designed framework rather than an accumulated vocabulary: each term has its question, each question its evidence, and the evidence, in every case, is the record of cultural production and the experience it registers.

## The Examination of the Framework: A Summary for Revision

For the student revising the whole argument, the article's reasoning compresses into a sequence that can be rehearsed. A Welsh critic formed by adult education found the inherited Marxist account of culture unusable, because its governing metaphor froze production into storeys and its governing causal concept prefigured what it should have explained. He rebuilt the account from the practice of cultural making itself: writing, printing, publishing, and broadcasting as material production, language as practical consciousness, hegemony as lived saturation. On that foundation he built three tools, for detecting the unformed, for sorting the temporal mixture of any formation, and for stating causation without mechanism. He tested the apparatus across forty years of applied work, in literary history, drama, the novel, the country and the city, and the analysis of broadcasting, and the tests produced findings, the escalator, the knowable community, flow, that other disciplines adopted. The strongest criticism, from within his own tradition, charged the apparatus with redescribing rather than explaining and with flattening the specificity of meaning; the answer concedes the descriptive character of the central concept and the thinness of the theory of meaning, and rests the framework on its performance. The verdict carries its defeater: a demonstration that the material analysis of culture still requires the old asymmetric question would reinstate the problem the dissolution removed, and until that demonstration commands the field, the dissolution stands as the tradition's most consequential reframing of its oldest problem.

That compression is offered as a revision instrument and not as a substitute for the article, since the qualifications, the concessions, and the evidence are the argument, and an examination answer that reproduces the compression without them will read, to a marker formed in this tradition, exactly as thin as it is. The persona section that follows takes up the three readerships in turn.
## Two More Cases: The Detective Story and the Song

Transferability is best proved on materials the framework's inventor did not treat at length, and two compact cases, chosen from the popular forms he taught his readers to take seriously, close the analytical argument. The first is the detective story, whose classic form precipitated in the late nineteenth century and whose career since is a triad study in miniature. The classic form's structure of feeling is documentable by recurrence: across writers who shared no programme, the fiction registers a common settlement, that the social world, however darkened by crime, is knowable, that a trained attention can reassemble the whole from its traces, and that the restoration of knowledge is the restoration of order. The settlement answers the period's emergent anxiety, the unknowable city of the earlier analysis, with a fantasy of method: the detective as the one consciousness for whom the metropolis remains a knowable community, because he reads it as a text of clues. The form's dominant career, its absorption into the accredited entertainment of several generations, followed. Its residual phase is visible wherever the classic form persists as consolation, the country house, the village, the closed circle of suspects, a knowable community preserved in genre amber long after the society outside had surrendered the condition. And its emergent mutations are detectable by the standard test, the dominant order's difficulty with them: the versions in which the detective's method fails, in which the order restored is itself the crime, in which the knowledge reassembles into an accusation against the institutions that commissioned it. Those mutations, oppositional within a form built for reassurance, register a later structure of feeling about knowledge and power, and the analyst who sorts them by the triad can describe the genre's whole history as one formation in motion, without once treating any phase as the form's timeless essence.

The second case is the popular song, and it is chosen because it is the form in which the structure of feeling most often announces itself to the largest numbers with the least institutional mediation. A song that precipitates a generation's inarticulate settlement, about work, about love, about the city, about the war its elders are preparing, circulates before any doctrine has stated the settlement, is sung by people who would reject the doctrine when it arrives, and is remembered, decades later, as the period's truth in a way its editorials are not. The framework instructs the analyst to take the song as evidence of the first order, to document its recurrence and its variations, to trace its production, the industry that recorded it, the formats that carried it, the certifications that admitted or excluded it from the channels of the day, and to sort its elements: the residual forms it revives, the dominant sentiments it confirms, the emergent feeling it carries in a melody and a tone that its words, read cold, would never confess. The case is deliberately ordinary. A method that requires extraordinary objects is a taste; a method that works on the song everyone was humming is an instrument, and the democratisation of evidence, the ordinary admitted to the archive, is the framework's founding gesture returning in its smallest change.

## The Institutions of Criticism: Journals, Reviews and the Making of a Reputation

One stratum of his world has gone unexamined in this profile, and the framework itself demands its examination, since the production of criticism is cultural production like any other. The mid-century decades in which his reputation was made possessed a distinctive apparatus for the making of critical reputations: the small journals with their editorial collectives, the review pages of the serious press, the university syllabuses that converted reputations into reading lists, and the publishing houses whose lists defined the available positions. His career moved through every part of that apparatus, and the apparatus left its marks on the work's reception in ways his own concepts predict. The journals of the New Left were his first institution, and they formed a selective tradition in embryo, accrediting a line of inquiry, his own, Thompson's, the early cultural studies work, that the university journals of the day would not carry. The university syllabus was the second institution, and its slow admission of his books across the 1960s and 1970s was the mechanism by which a contested position became a taught one, with the usual consequence that the taught version simplified the contested one, extracting the portable concepts and leaving the quarrels in the journals where they began.

The lesson generalises, and it is the last piece of method this profile transmits. A researcher's own field has its apparatus, its journals and lists and hiring committees, and the apparatus is a selective tradition administering the researcher's reputation and everyone else's. To know the apparatus, its criteria, stated and operative, its gravities and its blind spots, is not cynicism about one's profession. It is the framework's standing instruction applied at home: begin from the means of production, including the means by which knowledge about culture is itself produced, and let no product, including this article and the series that carries it, exempt itself from the audit. The series the reader is holding is itself a selection, with its criteria and its gravities, and the instruction it most hopes to transmit is the one that turns on its transmitter: ask of every account, this one included, what it has selected, what it has left unselected, and under what pressures the selection was made.

## The Final Interviews and the Habit of Revision

Politics and Letters, the book of interviews published in 1979, has been cited repeatedly in this profile, and it earns a closing treatment as a document in its own right, because it is the record of the habit on which the framework's authority finally rests: revision conducted in public, under hostile and friendly pressure alike, with the record left open for audit. The interviewers, editors of the journal that had been his political home, pressed him across three days on the whole formation: the border childhood, the war, the adult classes, the books one by one, the quarrels with the structuralists, the political choices of the New Left years. The performance that resulted is without parallel in the literature of the tradition, not because he won every exchange, he did not, and the transcript shows the losses, but because he treated every question as an occasion to re-examine the position rather than to defend the formulation. Formulations that had hardened in the books are softened, qualified, occasionally withdrawn. Judgements of contemporaries are revised in both directions. The structure of feeling itself is restated with a care that concedes half the prosecutor's case about its misuse while holding the concept's ground.

The habit matters to the verdict because it answers, in conduct, the charge of unfalsifiable flexibility in its personal form. A thinker whose system insures itself against refutation will defend every formulation to the last; a thinker whose method is a discipline of inquiry will revise the formulations and keep the discipline, and the transcript shows the second behaviour sustained across three days of skilled interrogation. It also models, for the students addressed below, the exact intellectual posture the examination of this material rewards: the position held firmly and the formulation held lightly, the defeater stated before the opponent demands it, the concession paid in the coin of the specific case rather than in the small change of general humility. He was, by the testimony of allies and prosecutors alike, unusually willing to say that a sentence of his own had been wrong, and the willingness, practised over a career, is the biographical root of the framework's most distinctive feature, its built-in instruction to state what would reverse it. The dissolution carries a defeater because its author had spent forty years supplying defeaters for his own claims and watching the claims grow stronger for the exercise.
## An Evening Read as Flow: The Method Performed on Broadcasting

The television section expounded flow as a concept; this section performs the analysis once, on a generic broadcast evening of the kind his book examined, so that the reader can watch the instrument work and can repeat the performance on any sequence, in any period, without invoking the services of a subsequent age. Take an evening's transmission from a commercial channel of the classic broadcasting era, composed, as such evenings were, of a news bulletin, a situation comedy, a crime drama, a feature film or a variety programme, and the connective tissue of advertisements, trailers, and continuity announcements that the official account of the medium treats as interruptions. The flow analysis begins by refusing the official account's units. The evening is the text, and its author is the institution.

Read the junctions first, because the junctions are where the institution's hand is plainest. The news bulletin ends not with a full stop but with a bridge: a trailer for the comedy that follows, cut to echo the bulletin's last item's tone or to relieve it, an advertisement whose domestic scene rhymes with the comedy's setting, so that the viewer's attention crosses the boundary without a decision ever being taken to continue. Inside the comedy, the act breaks fall where the advertising pods require them, and the writing obeys the pods: a minor climax before each break, a resumption that re-establishes the situation in a line for the viewer who has returned from the kitchen, a pacing of jokes that thickens toward the breaks to hold the channel against the rival sequence one button away. The crime drama that follows is scheduled as the evening's deepening, its darker tone licensed by the hour and by the comedy's preceding lightness, and its own breaks are managed by the same grammar at a higher tension. The trailers threaded through the evening are not information about other programmes; they are the institution composing tomorrow's audience out of tonight's, converting a sequence experienced once into a habit. Even the final continuity announcement, wishing the viewer goodnight over the channel's identifying music, is a composed junction, between the evening and the viewer's sleep, designed to make the channel the last presence in the domestic day.

Now ask the framework's questions of the whole. The means of production are the studios, the transmission network, the advertising market that finances the sequence by purchasing its audiences in segments, and the scheduling department, that unexamined author, whose decisions about order and junction are the evening's real screenplay. The relations of production include the advertiser's certification, quieter than the circulating library's and as effective, which determines what the sequence's items may show by determining what environments the purchasers of audiences will pay to adjoin. The dominant meanings are carried less by any item than by the sequence's total settlement: the world as a succession of manageable excitements, consumption as the connective tissue between all activities, the home as the terminus of every journey the evening depicts. The residual appears in the forms the sequence preserves from earlier formations, the variety programme's music hall lineage, the newsreader's borrowed authority from an older public world. The emergent appears, when it appears, as disturbance at the junctions, a drama whose tone the surrounding flow cannot digest, a documentary that makes the advertisements after it look obscene by juxtaposition, and the institution's handling of the disturbance, rescheduling, reframing, apologising, is the analyst's evidence of its valence, by the standard test.

The performance generalises, and its generalisation is the reason flow survived its medium's transformation. Any institution that arranges a sequence for a recipient who meets the arrangement rather than the items, a schedule, a syllabus, an exhibition, a feed of items arranged by an interested party, can be read by the same questions: where are the junctions, who composed them, what certifies the items, what settlement does the whole enact, and where does the disturbance show? The questions are the dissolution's questions, asked of a sequence instead of a storey, and a reader who has performed the analysis once possesses the instrument in the only way instruments are ever possessed, by use.
## Ideas of Nature: The Audit Extended to the Non-Human

The essay on ideas of nature, written at the end of the 1970s and collected in the volume of essays on problems in materialism and culture, extends the audit of keywords to the term that underlies the country and city contrast and much else. Nature, he showed, is among the most complex words in the language precisely because it carries, in a single term, an argument about what is given and what is made. In one of its senses nature is the essential character of a thing, what it is in itself. In another it is the inherent force that directs the world, a power behind appearances. In a third, the sense that grew dominant as the others were personified and then retired, nature is the material world itself, increasingly specified as the part of that world not made by human beings. The third sense performs a quiet eviction. By defining nature as what humans have not made, it renders the human making of the lived world invisible at the exact historical moment when that making became planetary: the enclosed and drained and planted countryside, the bred animals and the selected crops, the managed forests and the diverted rivers, all the worked world that the country tradition contemplated as nature, was a product of labour as surely as the city was, and the word's settlement concealed the labour in the one case while advertising it in the other.

The essay's bearing on the framework is direct. The country and city contrast, whose ideology the earlier book had exposed, rests on the third sense of nature: the country as the given, the city as the made, with value distributed accordingly. The audit of the word dissolves the contrast's foundation as the dissolution dissolved the base and superstructure picture, by the same operation, exhibiting the picture's manufacture. And the essay's positive claim extends cultural materialism to its widest scope. If the material world as humans actually inhabit it is substantially a produced world, made by labour within inherited conditions, then the analysis of production is not one department of social inquiry among others. It is the inquiry into the conditions of human life as such, and the distinction between the economic and the cultural, already retired in the theory of culture, is retired again at the level of the landscape: the field and the poem are both made things, made under different conditions for different uses, and the theory that can hold both in one analysis has earned its name. His later ecological writing, noticed in the section on the late essays, builds directly on this foundation, which is why the environmental movements that discovered him in the years after his death found not a pastoral mourner but a materialist of the worked world, armed against the escalator in its green form as in its literary one.

## The Long Argument Before the Book: The Essay Behind the Statement

The theoretical statement of 1977 had a public rehearsal that students of the argument should know, because it shows the dissolution being tested against the tradition's defenders before it was fixed in print. The essay on base and superstructure in Marxist cultural theory, published in the New Left Review in 1973, laid out the core moves four years before the book: the objection to the spatial metaphor, the insistence that the activities assigned to the superstructure are material practices, the recovery of determination as limits and pressures in an early form, and the reworking of hegemony as a lived process that must include the residual and the emergent to be intelligible. The essay's reception rehearsed the book's. Sympathetic readers found the liberation they had been waiting for, a Marxist cultural theory that could be practised without embarrassment. Orthodox readers found the evasion they had suspected, the dissolution of the theory's explanatory core in the name of its renewal. And the argument between the receptions, conducted in the journals of the decade, was the medium in which the concepts were sharpened, so that the book of 1977 is best read as the essay's fortified revision, its formulations tightened at exactly the joints where the essay's critics had pressed.

The episode carries a methodological lesson that this profile has applied throughout and now states as a rule for the researchers it addresses. A reframing is tested by the quality of the objections it attracts and by its author's conduct under them. The dissolution attracted the strongest objections available in its tradition, from its most formidable living critic, and its author answered in essays, in a book, and in three days of interviews, conceding where the objections bit and holding where they missed, and left the whole exchange in print as the concept's audit trail. A reader who wishes to grade the dissolution should therefore grade the exchange and not the slogan, and the materials for the grading are unusually complete, which is itself one of the reasons the framework has worn well: its audit trail is public, and positions with public audit trails can be trusted in proportion to what the trails show.
## Drama in a Dramatised Society

His inaugural lecture as professor of drama, delivered in 1974, condensed the media argument and the drama argument into a single perception that has only grown more legible since. The perception is that drama had escaped the theatre. A society saturated with performed representations, in its broadcasting, its advertising, its public rituals of politics and celebrity, had made the dramatic into a general condition: people increasingly experienced their own lives through the forms of performance, rehearsing identities, watching themselves being watched, living at a pitch of dramatisation that the older society, which visited drama in a building on set occasions, had not known. The lecture's question followed from the perception. If drama is everywhere, what is the theatre for? His answer was characteristic in its refusal of both available consolations. The theatre is not a refuge of authenticity from a dramatised world, since its own forms are part of that world and share its conditions. Nor is it obsolete, superseded by the broadcast forms that generalised its techniques. It is the place where the dramatisation itself can be made visible and examined, where a society that lives by performance can watch its own performing with the distance that only a live, embodied, unrepeatable event provides.

The lecture is a small masterclass in the method's movement. A change in the means of cultural production, the generalisation of performed representation through broadcasting, is traced into a change in lived experience, the dramatised consciousness, which is then read back into the fortunes of an accredited art form, whose crisis is explained without recourse to declining standards or lost audiences. Limits and pressures do the causal work: the theatre's economics, its buildings, its trained bodies, set the limits within which dramatists answered the pressure of a world already theatrical, and the answers, the period's experiments with documentary forms, with the audience's implication, with the exposure of the performance's own machinery, are the record of the negotiation. A student who grasps the lecture has grasped the framework's portability across media and across the boundary between the accredited and the popular, which is the portability on which the whole reputation rests.

The dramatised society perception also supplies the missing link between the television book and the tragedy book, and the link is worth drawing explicitly. A society that dramatises itself continuously will produce characteristic structures of feeling about agency and suffering: the sense of living inside a script written elsewhere, of performing a self for audiences that cannot be seen, of catastrophe as spectacle consumed at home. Those feelings, documented in the period's fiction and drama by the recurrence rule, are the modern tragic material in his sense, and the dramatists who found forms for them were doing what the dramatists of every period in his histories do, building structures adequate to an experience that the inherited forms could not stage. The continuity of the argument across thirty years of changing objects, from Ibsen's drawing rooms to the broadcast evening, is the strongest internal evidence that the framework is a method and not a set of opinions about particular works.
## Community: The Warm Word and Its Uses

The Keywords entry on community contains an observation he made almost in passing that has become one of his most quoted findings, because it arms any reader against a whole register of public speech. Community, he noticed, is unlike the other terms of social organisation in that it never seems to be used unfavourably. State, nation, society, even culture can all be spoken against; community carries an unbroken warmth, naming existing relationships as close and direct or invoked as the standard by which colder arrangements are judged and found wanting. A word that cannot be used unfavourably is a word that has been exempted from argument, and his entry performs the usual audit on the exemption: the warmth is a settlement, the deposit of the word's history in the face-to-face worlds of parish and village, carried into an age that invokes community precisely where its substance is most doubtful, in the names of institutions and policies that administer people at a distance.

The audit matters to the framework because community was one of his own load-bearing words, the knowable community above all, and the entry is therefore the audit turned on his own instrument in public. His defence of the term, conducted against his own finding, is instructive. The word's warmth records a real and persistent human judgement, that the quality of direct, mutual, and responsible relationships is a good against which other goods are measured, and the judgement survives the word's abuse by the institutions that borrow its glow. The analyst's task is the discrimination the entry teaches: to ask, of every invocation, whether the relationships named are actually of the kind the word promises, direct and mutual and sustained by common means, or whether the warmth is being drawn on credit by an arrangement that offers none of them. The question is the selective tradition audit in miniature, applied to a single word in a speech or a prospectus, and it is perhaps the most frequently usable item in the entire vocabulary study.

The concept of the knowable community, revisited in this light, gains its full weight. The early novel's world could be known because its relationships were substantially of the direct kind, conducted among people whose lives intersected at many points and over whole lifetimes. The industrial city's unknowability was not merely a matter of size, though size was its condition; it was the replacement of multi-stranded relationships by single-stranded ones, the workmate unknown at home, the neighbour unknown at work, the fellow citizen unknown altogether, so that the social whole existed nowhere as lived experience and could be represented only by the statistical and the detective forms his novel history traced. Community, in the strong sense his work defends, is the name for the multi-stranded condition, and its loss is a material fact about the organisation of life, not a mood about the past. The escalator warning applies to its invocation, not to its analysis: the analyst who dates the loss, names its mechanisms, and counts its costs is doing history, while the speaker who locates true community one generation back, in the warm haze, is riding the escalator, and the difference between the two is the difference this whole article exists to teach.
## The Unfinished Desk: Posthumous Books and the Open File

He died in January 1988, in Cambridge, with work in progress on his desk, and the manner of the ending suits the method in a way his readers have often remarked. A study of the forms of modern writing lay unfinished among his papers. Lecture notes, drafts toward a book on the problems of materialism and culture in their next round, and the accumulated files of a working life were left for editors and executors to sort. What followed was a decade of posthumous publication: essays gathered under titles he had projected, the lectures on modernism assembled by other hands, the interviews and occasional pieces recovered from journals. Each posthumous volume arrived with an editorial apparatus explaining its provenance, and each therefore enacted, in the plainest bibliographical form, the process his theory describes. A dead writer's remains are a record awaiting selection. Which drafts are finished into books, which lectures are transcribed, which letters are printed and which withheld, are decisions taken by institutions, publishers, estates, universities, under pressures of reputation and market and scholarly demand, and the figure who emerges from the posthumous record is, like every figure in a selective tradition, partly made by the selection.

He would have insisted on the audit being applied to his own remains, and applying it yields a finding worth registering. The posthumous selection has favoured the theorist over the novelist and the lecturer over the activist, because the academy that administers his reputation has syllabuses for theory and fewer uses for the rest. The fiction went out of print in the years when the theoretical books were being canonised, an outcome his own analysis predicts and his own politics would have resisted, since the novels were not recreation from the work but half of it. Recoveries have since been attempted, reissues arranged, the Welsh writing restored to discussion, and the correction is proceeding by the method he supplied: exhibit the selection, name its pressures, and readmit the unselected as evidence. A profile that ends by turning its subject's instrument on its subject's afterlife has not indulged a paradox. It has paid the framework the only compliment the framework accepts, which is use.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Raymond Williams?

He was a Welsh writer and critic (1921 to 1988) whose work remade the Marxist study of culture in Britain. Raised in Pandy, a border village, in the household of a railway signalman, he won a Cambridge scholarship, served in the army during the war, and then taught working adults in evening classes for fifteen years, an apprenticeship that shaped everything he later theorised. Recalled to Cambridge in 1961, he rose to its professorship of drama. His major books run from Culture and Society and The Long Revolution through Keywords to the theoretical statement of 1977, and he also wrote novels set in his home country.

### Q: What is cultural materialism?

It is the doctrine, named and defended in his 1977 book, that making culture is a material activity in the full sense. A poem, a play, or a broadcast does not descend from an economic foundation into a realm of spirit; it is produced, by trained people using definite materials, presses, studios, and transmitters, inside definite arrangements of ownership and employment. Because cultural work is production, its study belongs inside the general study of how a society makes its life, not in a separate annexe for ideas. The old puzzle about how an upper storey connects with a lower one lapses, because the storeys were a drawing, not a discovery.

### Q: What is a structure of feeling?

The phrase names the way a generation inhabits its world before that inhabitation hardens into creeds, institutions, and programmes. Williams pictured such experience as held in solution: real, widely shared, and active, yet not precipitated into anything that can be bottled and labelled. Its evidence is recurrence. When unconnected writers, reporters, and artists of a decade keep striking the same tone, worrying at the same tension, and reaching for the same images, the analyst is entitled to infer a common formation of feeling beneath their disagreements, and then obliged to explain, from material conditions, how that formation arose.

### Q: What are dominant, residual and emergent cultures?

The labels distinguish three kinds of element present together in any developed formation. One kind rules: the meanings and practices that the main institutions teach, reward, and reproduce, experienced by most people as simple common sense. A second kind survives from previous formations, religion or mutual aid or deference, still practised and still capable of sheltering values the ruling order cannot supply. A third kind is newly coming into being, announced in altered tones and half-formed practices before it owns a name. Survival and novelty alike can be harmless alternatives that the ruling order tolerates, or genuine oppositions that it works to absorb or defeat.

### Q: How does Williams redefine determination?

He takes the word back to an older force: to determine is to bound a field and to press upon what moves inside it. On his account a formation's arrangements rule out certain cultural outcomes altogether, tax others heavily, and subsidise others again, but they do not script the result, because people respond to the pressure, evade it, combine against it, or yield. Determination therefore survives as a research instruction, go and find the actual limits and the actual pressures in contracts, budgets, licences, and rejections, and dies as a prophecy. Nothing in a work's conditions entitles the analyst to deduce the work.

### Q: Why does Williams reject the base and superstructure metaphor?

Because the image does the damage it then asks theory to repair. Draw a society as a foundation with floors above it, and cultural life is exiled to the upper floors by the drawing itself; the theorist must then invent traffic, reflections, mediations, reciprocal actions, to reconnect what the picture separated. Williams inspected the supposed upper floors and found workshops: publishers' offices, rehearsal rooms, printing plants, studios, each with tools, wages, and owners. A metaphor that misclassifies workshops as decorations had to go. He insisted the refusal strengthened materialism rather than abandoning it, since it returned meanings to the world of making from which the picture had banished them.

### Q: What is the escalator of nostalgia?

It is the pattern he exposed in The Country and the City by reading centuries of rural writing in sequence. Generation after generation, English authors mourn a harmonious, neighbourly, organic countryside and date its disappearance to just before their own lifetimes. Pursue the mourning backwards and the paradise keeps retreating at the pace of the pursuit, through the railway age, the enclosures, and beyond, until it would have to be sited in Eden. Anything whose object moves with the observer is not a recollection but a device, and the device tells the truth about the mourner's present discontents while lying about the past it claims to remember.

### Q: What did Williams argue about television?

Watching American broadcasting after a British formation, he concluded that nobody watches programmes one by one; they watch an evening. The medium's real unit, he argued in his 1974 book, is flow, the deliberately composed succession of shows, commercials, trailers, and announcements by which a channel carries its audience across every junction. Criticism that isolates single programmes therefore studies objects the institution never offered. He also denied that the apparatus dictated its own uses: broadcasting took its domestic, privatised form from social choices and commercial settlements, not from the nature of the technology, and different settlements would have composed different evenings from the same transmitters.

### Q: Did Eagleton attack Williams?

He subjected him to the most searching critique anyone in their shared tradition attempted, in a book of 1976 devoted to rebuilding Marxist criticism. Eagleton argued that an appeal to lived experience could not found a theory, because experience is itself an effect needing explanation; that the celebrated central concept redescribed resemblances among works instead of accounting for them; and that absorbing culture into production blurred what is distinctive about meanings. The critique was an heir's reckoning, not an enemy's, and its author said in later years that he had been too severe, coming to describe the older man's work as the foundation his own generation built on.

### Q: Where should you start with Williams?

Begin with the brief manifesto essay in which he argues that culture is ordinary, then take the two books that made his name at the start of the 1960s, the recovery of the English argument about culture and society, and the study that introduced his signature concept inside a history of modern Britain's long transformation. With those in hand, the short, dense book of 1977 in which the theory is finally stated will read as clarification rather than as code. Keep the vocabulary study by you as a reference to browse, save the great study of country and city writing for when the method is familiar, and finish with the published interviews, where he answers his critics himself.

### Q: How does the dissolution grade the base and superstructure model?

It returns a split verdict. As a literal architecture the model is condemned: there are not two floors, and the centuries of ingenuity spent designing stairs between them were spent on a defect of the blueprint. As a rough registration of a fact, that how people earn and organise their living is entangled with what they sing, print, and believe, the model pointed at something genuine, and the reframing preserves that something. What changes is the handling: entanglement is investigated through workshops, markets, schools, and schedules, where it can be documented, instead of being asserted through a picture, where it can only be repeated.

### Q: What should a student separate between dissolving a problem and solving it?

Keep apart the two ways an inquiry can end. A solution accepts the question as posed and supplies the missing answer; a dissolution interrogates the posing and shows that an assumption inside it, usually a picture, generated the difficulty. The examination trap is to present Williams as offering a cleverer solution, a subtler machinery linking economy and culture, when his claim is that no machinery is needed because nothing was ever separated. Marks follow the student who states which operation is being performed, shows the picture doing the damage, and then judges the reframing by the work it makes possible and by the evidence that would overturn it.

### Q: Why did Williams refuse the base and superstructure vocabulary?

His decades of work on the history of words had left him unable to trust inherited abstractions. Terms like base, superstructure, and reflection sound neutral and technical, yet each drags a picture behind it, foundations and floors, mirrors and images, and the picture silently decides what kinds of answer will count before the evidence is heard. He had watched the same smuggling in ordinary words, in industry and class and culture itself, and he applied the lesson at home: a materialism that never audits its own metaphors will be ruled by them. The vocabulary was refused so that the phenomena, workshops and practices all the way down, could be described in words that did not prejudge them.

### Q: How does the three tools table order the concepts?

The arrangement follows the path an analysis travels. First comes the instrument for catching experience while it is still unformed, since that is where change is earliest visible. Next comes the sorting instrument, which distributes everything found in a formation among the ruling, the surviving, and the arriving, so that the catch can be placed in a temporal map. Last comes the causal instrument, which states how conditions bear on outcomes without scripting them. Experience, composition, causation: the sequence is a method in miniature, and reversing it, beginning with causal claims before anything has been caught or sorted, reproduces the mechanical theory the table was built to replace.

### Q: What separates a structure of feeling from an ideology?

Ideology, as he uses the contrast, is belief that has been worked into a system: statable, arguable, printed in programmes and catechisms. Feeling, in his special sense, is what a generation lives before and beneath its systems, the shared sense of what can be hoped, what must be endured, and what goes without saying. The two can therefore coexist in any combination. Bitter ideological enemies inside one decade regularly turn out, on inspection of their novels, sermons, and songs, to inhabit the same underlying formation of feeling, which is why their quarrels were intelligible to each other and why a historian who confines the reading to the systems misunderstands the decade.
