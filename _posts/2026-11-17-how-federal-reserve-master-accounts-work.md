## The Account at the Center of the Dollar Payment System

A Federal Reserve master account works as the settlement account an eligible institution holds at its district Federal Reserve Bank, where it keeps reserve balances and from which payments between institutions are debited and credited in central bank money. When Institution A pays Institution B, the Reserve Bank reduces the balance in the account of A and increases the balance in the account of B, and that paired entry completes the interbank leg of the payment. A master account is not a bank account with a prestigious address but the point where private money becomes final: whoever holds one settles in the central bank's own liabilities, and the fight over access is therefore a fight over who is allowed to finish a payment without borrowing another institution's permission. Every later question about eligibility, pricing, interest, and discretion grows out of that single mechanical fact.

The Federal Reserve System was created by the Federal Reserve Act of 1913, and the system was built as twelve regional Federal Reserve Banks rather than as one office in Washington. An institution deals with the Reserve Bank for its district, and the master account sits on the books of that Reserve Bank. The Board of Governors in Washington sets system policy, including the interest on reserve balances rate and the Payment System Risk Policy, while the Reserve Bank operates the account day to day, processes instructions, applies limits, and enforces the conditions attached to the relationship. That division matters because an access dispute is usually a dispute with a district Reserve Bank applying systemwide standards, not an appeal to an abstract central bank.

![Federal Reserve Master Account Settlement Ledger and Payment Rails - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The account also works as a hub rather than as a single purpose wallet. Through one account an institution reaches the Fedwire Funds Service for large value transfers, the Fedwire Securities Service for securities settlement, FedACH for batches of smaller payments, check services, FedNow instant payments, and currency and coin services. Each service has its own messages, deadlines, and operating rules, yet the money leg converges on the same balance. A securities delivery, an automated clearing house settlement, and a wire transfer look different at the level of paperwork and software, and they become alike at the level of the account, where each one ends as a debit and a credit between Reserve Bank liabilities.

A concrete payment shows the hub at work. A manufacturer in Ohio instructs its bank to pay a supplier whose account sits at a bank in Texas, and the two banks hold master accounts at different Reserve Banks, the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland and the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas. The Ohio bank verifies the instruction and submits it through a Federal Reserve service, the Reserve Banks coordinate the debit and the credit across their books within the Federal Reserve System, and the Texas bank credits the supplier against a balance that no longer depends on the Ohio bank at all. Neither firm sees a Reserve Bank entry, neither firm holds an account there, and yet the finality both firms rely on comes entirely from that invisible pair of entries. Remove the master accounts and the same commercial payment would rest on a chain of private promises between banks, each promise only as good as the bank that made it.

Understanding the account therefore requires holding two ideas together at once. At the level of bookkeeping the arrangement is plain, because a payment is an instruction, a check against available funds or permitted intraday credit, and a pair of entries. At the level of institutional power the arrangement is consequential, because the institution without an account must route that same pair of entries through the account of another institution, accepting the price, the delay, and the possible refusal that come with dependence. The sections that follow separate those two levels, starting with what the balance itself is and what it is not.

## What a Master Account Holds and What It Is Not

A master account holds reserve balances, and those balances are liabilities of the Federal Reserve Bank where the account sits. The distinction sounds technical and it carries the whole subject. A deposit at a commercial bank is a claim on that bank, backed by its loans, securities, and capital. A balance in a master account is a claim on a Reserve Bank, settled in the money the central bank itself issues. The holder does not own a pile of currency in a vault with its name attached. It owns a recorded balance that the Reserve Bank honors by debiting and crediting accounts on its own books, and the record is the asset.

The account is not a customer deposit account of the kind a household or a firm opens at a bank. No teller network stands behind it, no debit card draws on it, and no depositor protection scheme insures it in the manner of a retail deposit, because the balance is already central bank money rather than a promise to pay central bank money on demand. An institution cannot walk into a Reserve Bank and open one by filling out a form and funding a minimum balance. Eligibility under federal law comes first, review under the Board's Guidelines for Evaluating Account and Services Requests comes second, and only after approval does the operational work of agreements, credentials, and connection testing begin.

Nor is the account a license to borrow. A master account and discount window borrowing are separate relationships, and an account by itself confers no borrowing privileges. Lending by a Reserve Bank rests on its own eligibility and collateral rules, with pledged assets valued, haircuts applied, and credit decisions made apart from the settlement function. An institution can hold an account, settle payments through it for years, and still have no standing to borrow, just as an institution in good standing for settlement can face separate questions when it pledges collateral. Collapsing the two relationships into one idea of being inside the Federal Reserve misleads every later judgment about risk.

The account is also not a vault for every asset an institution owns. Loans stay on the balance sheet of the institution. Securities held through the Fedwire Securities Service move against payment through linked settlement arrangements, yet ownership records and custody arrangements remain distinct from the cash balance. What the master account records, narrowly and completely, is how much central bank money the institution holds at the Reserve Bank at a given moment. During an operating day that figure rises and falls with every incoming and outgoing payment, every securities settlement, every automated clearing house posting, and every currency order or deposit. At the close the remaining balance is the reserve position with which the institution begins the next operating day, earning interest at the interest on reserve balances rate set by the Board when the rules for interest apply.

The balance also differs from funds kept with a correspondent. Funds held at another commercial bank expose the holder to that bank's failure at the moment when payment capacity matters most. Funds in a master account expose the holder to no commercial bank at all, because the counterparty is the Reserve Bank itself.

That narrowness is a strength rather than a limitation. Because the balance means one thing only, other institutions accept a credit to it as settlement without investigating the payer. The payment system does not need each receiver to audit each sender during the operating day. It needs a shared ledger at the center whose entries everyone treats as final, and the master account is an institution's place on that ledger.

## Why Settlement in Central Bank Money Ends the Chain

### Why is central bank money safer than commercial bank money?

Central bank money is safer because a balance at a Federal Reserve Bank is a liability of the central bank itself, with no commercial bank standing between the holder and final settlement. Commercial bank money is a claim on one institution whose assets and capital must support that claim under stress.

A payment between customers of different banks creates a problem that ordinary language hides. When a buyer pays a seller, the buyer's bank promises the seller's bank that value has moved, and each bank updates the account of its own customer. Behind those customer entries the two banks still owe each other settlement. If they settled by creating further claims on each other without limit, every payment would leave a residue of interbank debt, and confidence in the payment would depend on confidence in the weakest bank in the chain. Settlement in central bank money breaks that regress. The paying bank gives up reserve balances, the receiving bank gains reserve balances, and neither bank holds a claim on the other for that payment afterward.

The mechanism runs in a fixed sequence. The paying institution sends an authenticated instruction through a Federal Reserve service. The Reserve Bank checks the instruction against the account, the available balance, and any applicable intraday limit under the Payment System Risk Policy. It then debits the master account of the payer and credits the master account of the receiver. Because both accounts sit on the books of the same Reserve Bank for that settlement, or on linked books across districts within the Federal Reserve System, the transfer is a change in the composition of Reserve Bank liabilities rather than the creation of a new private promise. The receiver can then credit its customer, pay onward, or hold the balance, and each later use starts from money that has already settled.

Finality is the legal name for that ending. Under Regulation J and Article 4A of the Uniform Commercial Code, a Fedwire transfer becomes final and irrevocable when the receiving Reserve Bank credits the master account of the receiving institution. Before that credit the instruction can be rejected for insufficient funds or for failure to meet operating requirements, and error correction follows defined procedures. After that credit the payment does not unwind because the payer later regrets the transfer or because the payer fails later in the day. A fraud claim, a mistaken payment claim, or a court order can create a separate obligation to return value, yet the settlement entry itself stands. That separation between a settled payment and a later legal claim is what allows receivers to release goods, securities, and funds against a wire without waiting to see whether the payer survives the week.

The same logic governs payments across districts. Where the accounts sit at different Reserve Banks, the Federal Reserve System coordinates the entries between districts so that the payer is debited and the receiver is credited as one settlement rather than as two unrelated promises, and the receiver treats the credit as final on the same legal basis.

Commercial bank money remains essential for credit, for customer relationships, and for the ordinary business of banking, and nothing in the settlement argument diminishes that role. The point is narrower. A claim on a commercial bank is only as strong as that bank, while a balance at the Reserve Bank removes the interbank leg from private credit risk. Settlement in central bank money therefore ends the chain of promises at the one institution whose liabilities define the unit in which all the other promises are denominated.

## The Services That Run Through the Account

The master account earns its name because many services settle through it, and each service solves a different payment problem while using the same final balance. The Fedwire Funds Service handles large value, time sensitive transfers between institutions, the kind of payment where a house purchase closing, a corporate disbursement, or an interbank obligation cannot wait for a batch cycle. The Fedwire Securities Service handles the cash leg when United States government securities and other eligible securities move between holders, linking delivery of the security to payment so that neither side must surrender its asset before receiving value. FedACH processes batches of smaller recurring and one time payments, including payroll credits and bill payment debits, where volume matters more than the settlement of any single item in isolation.

Check services and currency and coin services show the physical history inside an electronic system. Checks presented through Federal Reserve channels settle through the account even as the paper or its image travels by a separate route, and the debit and credit to master accounts turn a geographically scattered clearing process into a single accounting result. Currency and coin work in the opposite direction from a wire. An institution orders currency from its Reserve Bank and pays by a debit to its master account, and it deposits surplus currency and receives a credit. Armored carriers, vault counts, and fitness sorting handle the notes themselves, while the account handles the value, so a shipment of worn bills and a wire transfer of the same amount produce mirror image entries on the same ledger.

FedNow, which launched in 2023, extends the account into instant payments between participating institutions, with settlement occurring through master accounts or through arrangements the Federal Reserve permits for participants using a joint account structure. The customer experience is a payment that arrives in seconds at any hour covered by the service. The settlement experience for the institutions remains familiar, because the Reserve Bank debits one balance and credits another, and the speed of the customer message does not change the nature of the money that moves. Speed is a property of messaging and operating hours. Finality is a property of whose liability settles the payment.

Running several services through one account creates a netting and liquidity discipline that a separate account for each service would destroy. Incoming automated clearing house credits can fund outgoing wires. A securities settlement can draw on a balance built by earlier receipts. The Reserve Bank and the institution both see a consolidated position, so an intraday shortfall in one activity can be measured against receipts in another rather than triggering a mechanical failure in a silo. The same consolidation creates the central operational risk of the account, because an error, a fraud attempt, or a liquidity drain in any one service reaches the single balance on which all the others depend. Access controls, transaction limits, reconciliation, and intraday monitoring exist for that reason, and they protect a shared balance rather than a collection of independent pots.

The service list also explains why an account decision is never only a decision about wires. Denial of a master account closes the whole bundle at once, and approval opens a bundle whose parts an institution may adopt at different times and at different volumes. A small institution might begin with FedACH and currency services and never send a Fedwire transfer in a given month, yet its settlement still ends in the same central bank money as the settlement of the largest dealer bank.

## Fedwire and the Mechanics of a Same-Day Transfer

A retail payment begins far from the master account and still ends there. A shopper completes a purchase through [contactless payments explainer](/2026/10/04/how-contactless-payments-work/), the merchant's bank and the cardholder's bank exchange authorization and clearing messages, and the interbank obligation created by that tap is later settled between institutions, with balances at Reserve Banks supplying the final leg underneath the card network's own accounting. Fedwire sits at the wholesale end of that same layered structure, where institutions move value for themselves and for customers whose payments are too large or too urgent for batch processing.

### How long does a Fedwire transfer take to settle?

A Fedwire transfer settles on the day the Reserve Bank accepts and processes the instruction, once the sending institution has funds or permitted intraday credit available. The Reserve Bank debits the sender and credits the receiver in sequence, and that credit makes the transfer final and irrevocable under Regulation J.

The full sequence begins before any money moves. The sending institution verifies its customer's instruction, screens the payment under its compliance procedures, and formats a message that identifies the receiver, the receiving institution, the amount, and the account information needed for crediting. Authentication controls and internal approval limits guard the release of the message, because a Fedwire instruction, once accepted and credited, cannot be recalled by the sender acting alone. The institution then submits the instruction through its Federal Reserve connection during Fedwire operating hours, and the Reserve Bank validates the format and the authority of the sender before processing begins.

At the Reserve Bank the decisive test is funds and risk control. If the master account holds a sufficient balance, the Reserve Bank debits that account and credits the master account of the receiving institution. If the balance falls short, the instruction can wait in queue, be rejected under operating rules, or proceed against daylight overdraft capacity when the Payment System Risk Policy permits it, subject to the net debit cap linked to the capital of the institution and to fees that differ for uncollateralized and collateralized overdrafts. That intraday credit is not a courtesy balance. It is a measured exposure, monitored during the day, priced to discourage routine dependence, and expected to be repaid by incoming funds or by an end of day funding transaction before the account closes.

Receipt changes the legal position at once. When the receiving Reserve Bank credits the master account of the receiving institution, the transfer is final and irrevocable under Regulation J, and Article 4A of the Uniform Commercial Code supplies the wider legal frame for funds transfers, including rules on acceptance, rejection, and the obligations of a beneficiary's bank. The receiving institution then credits its customer or applies the funds as instructed, and its duty to its customer follows the transfer rules and the account agreement rather than any remaining discretion about whether the interbank leg settled. A later dispute over the underlying contract, an invoice, or a fraud allegation travels as a new claim between the parties. It does not peel back the settlement entry.

Two failure modes show why the mechanics matter. In the first, the sending institution has the customer funds but lacks the reserve balance at the moment of submission, so the payment waits while receipts arrive, and a closing or a payroll deadline bears the cost of a liquidity timing problem rather than a solvency problem. In the second, the receiving details contain an error, and the receiving institution must sort a credit intended for one customer from an instruction naming another, applying Article 4A rules on misdescription instead of improvising. In both cases the system protects the integrity of the central ledger first and leaves the commercial consequences to the institutions and customers around it, which is the price and the purpose of final settlement.

## One Account, Many Ledgers: Subaccounts and Respondents

One master account can support an internal structure that outsiders rarely see. An institution organizes its own books into subaccounts and internal ledgers for branches, business lines, affiliates where the law permits, and respondent institutions whose payments it processes. The Reserve Bank sees the master account balance and the instructions the account holder submits. The account holder sees the allocation underneath, deciding which internal balance belongs to which activity and which respondent payment drew on which funds. That division of visibility is deliberate. The central ledger stays simple enough to settle with finality, while the commercial complexity of many customers and many respondents lives one layer down, on the books of the institution that chose to serve them.

The mechanism works by prefunding, by intraday allocation, or by credit judgment, and the holder chooses among those methods respondent by respondent. Under prefunding the respondent keeps funds with the holder in advance, and the holder releases payments only against that collected balance. Under intraday allocation the holder submits payments during the day and reconciles the respondent position as receipts arrive, watching exposure against an internal limit. Under a credit judgment the holder knowingly pays before the respondent funds arrive, extending unsecured or secured intraday credit based on the respondent's capital, history, and collateral. From the Reserve Bank's perspective all three methods produce the same debit to one master account. From the holder's perspective they are three different businesses, with three different loss profiles if a respondent fails during the day.

That hidden layer explains a recurring confusion in access debates. An institution without a master account is not necessarily outside the payment system, and an institution with an account is not necessarily settling only for itself. The respondent settles through the holder's balance, on the holder's timetable, under a private agreement the Reserve Bank does not write. The holder can impose balance requirements, cut off a respondent that breaches a limit, charge fees that recover its own Federal Reserve charges and add a margin, and require information for compliance screening before any payment moves. Each control is rational for the holder and each one is a point where the respondent's access can narrow or end without any central bank decision at all.

Record keeping ties the layers together when something goes wrong. If a payment is duplicated, misdirected, or disputed, investigators reconstruct the chain from the customer instruction to the holder's internal ledger to the master account entry, and each link must match in amount, timing, and reference information. A break between the internal ledger and the master account entry is an operational defect of the holder, not a defect in settlement, because the Reserve Bank settled exactly the instruction it accepted. Institutions therefore reconcile continuously during the day rather than waiting for an end of day statement, comparing queues, confirmations, and balances so that an error surfaces while correction procedures can still reach the parties involved.

The structure also concentrates responsibility. The account holder answers to the Reserve Bank for every instruction submitted under its credentials, including instructions that originate with a respondent's customer several layers away. Screening for illicit finance, sanctions compliance, and fraud therefore cannot stop at the holder's own customers. It must reach into respondent relationships in proportion to their risk, and a holder that cannot see into a respondent's business will either restrict that business or absorb exposure it cannot explain. The master account is one account in law and in settlement, and many ledgers in practice, and both descriptions are accurate at the level where each operates.

## Correspondent Banking, the Older Door Into the System

Correspondent banking is the arrangement that predates direct priced access and still carries institutions that lack an account of their own. A respondent institution holds a balance with a larger correspondent institution, and the correspondent submits payments through its own master account on the respondent's behalf. Before 1980, Federal Reserve services were tied to membership and provided without explicit per item charges, and nonmember institutions reached the system through member correspondents as a matter of structure rather than as an improvised workaround. The correspondent was the door, the toll keeper, and the guide, bundling settlement with credit lines, check collection, securities services, and local knowledge that a smaller institution could not build alone.

The Monetary Control Act of 1980, enacted March 31, 1980 as Title I of the Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act, changed the terms without abolishing the older door. The Federal Reserve had to publish a fee schedule for its services, and those services opened to all depository institutions, member and nonmember, on the same priced terms, while reserve requirements extended to all depository institutions as well. A nonmember could then hold its own account and pay the published price directly. Many institutions still chose a correspondent, because direct access carries fixed costs in technology, staffing, liquidity management, and compliance that can outweigh the fee savings for an institution with modest payment volumes.

A worked comparison shows the trade plainly. A small depository institution sending a limited number of wires each month compares the direct route, with its connection costs, trained backup staff, intraday monitoring, and account review burden, against the correspondent route, with its per item charges, balance requirements, and dependence on the correspondent's cutoff times. The direct route gives control and settlement in the institution's own name. The correspondent route gives scale and a staffed operation on the first day. Neither route changes the money that finally settles, because the correspondent's payment still ends as a debit and a credit between master accounts. What changes is who holds the relationship with the Reserve Bank and who can say no along the way.

That power to say no is the enduring weakness of the older door. A correspondent can raise prices, tighten balance requirements, restrict payment types, or end the relationship when its own risk appetite changes, and the respondent must then find another correspondent or seek its own account under time pressure. Industry language calls the withdrawal of correspondent services de-risking when the respondent's business becomes costly to monitor, and the respondent experiences the same event as exclusion from payments its customers regard as basic. The grievance behind many direct access claims is therefore older than any novel charter. It is the grievance of the institution that can serve its customers only through a rival or a larger neighbor whose interests do not always align with its own.

The correspondent also performs a screening function. Before submitting a payment, the correspondent applies its own compliance review, sanctions screening, and fraud controls to instructions from customers it has never met, and it answers to its Reserve Bank for what passes through its master account. A respondent with weak controls borrows a compliance perimeter along with settlement capacity, and it loses both if the relationship ends.

Correspondent banking endures because it solves real problems of scale, expertise, and credit, and direct access endures as a claim because dependence carries real costs. The master account system holds both arrangements at once, with direct holders settling for themselves and for respondents beneath them, and the boundary between the two groups is where the access argument begins in earnest.

## Who Is Eligible to Hold an Account

Eligibility begins with legal status, not with size, reputation, or a business plan. The Federal Reserve Act of 1913 built the system around depository institutions, and the twelve regional Federal Reserve Banks hold master accounts for institutions in their own districts. An applicant therefore starts by showing what it is in law: a bank, a savings association, a credit union, a branch or agency of a foreign bank, or another entity that a federal statute places inside the eligible class. That showing is documentary before it is financial. The institution produces its charter, names the authority that issued the charter, identifies its primary regulator, and maps its home office and operations to the district of the Reserve Bank that would hold the account. A filing that cannot complete that first map never reaches questions about risk, because the account is a statutory relationship before it is a service relationship.

### Can any bank open a Federal Reserve master account?

No. Eligibility under the Federal Reserve Act and related statutes is only the first gate. A chartered depository institution may apply, but the Reserve Bank in its district reviews legal authority, risk, supervision, and operational readiness, and it may deny an application that fails that review.

The distinction between eligibility and approval does the real work in the system. Eligibility answers a classification question, settled by statute and charter. Approval answers a judgment question, settled by the Reserve Bank after examining whether this particular institution can hold central bank money and use Federal Reserve services without shifting unacceptable risk onto the Reserve Bank, the payment system, or other account holders. The mechanism runs in sequence. The applicant submits its request to its district Reserve Bank. Staff test the legal basis for the request against the charter and the governing statutes. Staff then examine supervision, capital, management, anti money laundering controls, and the operational capacity to connect to services such as the Fedwire Funds Service and FedACH. Each step can end the inquiry, and passage at one step creates no entitlement at the next.

Federal insurance changes the weight of the file without removing the file. An institution insured by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation or the National Credit Union Administration arrives with a federal supervisor already examining its condition, and that record gives the Reserve Bank evidence it did not have to generate itself. An institution without federal insurance and without federal prudential supervision arrives with a thinner external record, so the Reserve Bank must build more of the evidentiary record on its own. Custodia Bank illustrates the far end of that spectrum. Wyoming chartered Custodia in 2020 as a special purpose depository institution under a Wyoming statute enacted in 2019, and Custodia held no federal insurance when it applied to the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City in 2020. The charter made application possible. The charter alone did not make approval follow.

Geography and corporate form add further edges to the gate. A foreign bank reaches the system through a licensed branch or agency in the United States rather than through its home office abroad, because the account must sit inside American supervision and American law. An Edge Act corporation, chartered under the Federal Reserve Act for international banking business, stands inside the eligible class by a different statutory door. In each case the mechanism is the same. The law names a class, the charter places a particular institution inside or outside that class, and the district Reserve Bank then decides whether the institution that qualifies on paper also qualifies in practice. Eligibility opens the conversation. It never finishes it.

## The Charter Question: What Counts as a Bank

Every access dispute eventually becomes an argument about a word. A charter is the legal instrument that turns a company into a bank, and the kind of charter an institution holds determines which supervisor watches it, which activities it may pursue, which insurance fund stands behind its deposits, and how a Reserve Bank reads its application for a master account. A national bank charter from the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency carries federal supervision from birth. A state charter splits the picture, pairing a state chartering authority with either the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation or the Federal Reserve as the federal supervisor, depending on membership and insurance. A credit union charter routes supervision through the National Credit Union Administration. The documents differ, but the function is constant: the charter fixes the rulebook before the first deposit arrives.

The hard cases sit at the edges of the definition, where a state legislature or a federal agency creates a form that performs some banking functions while standing outside the standard supervisory frame. Wyoming created its special purpose depository institution charter by statute in 2019, designing a form for institutions holding digital assets and other deposits without making loans in the conventional pattern and without federal deposit insurance. Industrial loan companies, trust companies with limited powers, and other narrow forms raise the same structural question in different dress. The question is never whether the entity calls itself a bank. The question is which promises the entity makes to depositors, which assets stand behind those promises, and which supervisor has examined both before public money moves.

Julie Andersen Hill, whose scholarship examines Federal Reserve account access and novel bank charters, treats the charter question as the hinge of the access question, because a state can create a new form faster than federal supervisors can build a record on it. The mechanism of the problem is timing. A chartering authority grants the form. The institution then applies for the account that would let the form operate at full scale, pointing to the charter as proof of legitimacy. The Reserve Bank, asked to settle payments for an institution no federal examiner has yet tested through a full supervisory cycle, must decide how much weight a new charter can bear. Peter Conti-Brown, in The Power and Independence of the Federal Reserve (2016), describes the Reserve Banks as hybrid public-private institutions, and that hybrid character sharpens the dilemma, since the deciding institution is neither a simple agency applying a simple rule nor a private counterparty free to refuse any customer.

A charter also fails to answer the question it is most often asked to settle. Holding a charter proves that a government allowed an institution to organize. It does not prove that the institution manages liquidity well, screens customers lawfully, or survives a run on its deposits. Reserve Banks therefore read the charter as the first exhibit rather than the verdict. The charter tells the reviewer where to look: at the chartering authority's standards, at the supervisor's examination history, and at the gap between the powers granted and the safeguards attached. Where the gap is narrow and supervised, the account question stays administrative. Where the gap is wide and unexamined, the charter question becomes the whole case, and the account waits on an answer that chartering alone cannot supply.

## From Membership to Priced Access: The Settlement of 1980

For most of the twentieth century, access to Federal Reserve services ran through membership, and membership carried a price that never appeared on an invoice. Member banks held required reserves at their district Reserve Banks as sterile balances that earned no interest, and in return they received services such as check clearing, wire transfer, and currency supply without explicit per item charges. The arrangement looked free only because the cost was hidden inside the reserve requirement itself. A bank paid by holding idle balances, and the size of the payment rose with the size of its deposit base. Nonmember institutions stood outside that bargain entirely. A nonmember bank or thrift reached the payment system through a member correspondent, paying the correspondent for access that the central bank supplied to the member directly, and accepting the correspondent's terms, hours, and credit judgment as the price of admission.

Inflation in the 1970s broke the bargain by raising its hidden price. As market interest rates climbed, the earnings a bank surrendered by holding noninterest bearing reserves climbed with them, and the incentive to escape the requirement grew stronger than the value of the services membership bought. Institutions shifted deposits into forms and affiliates structured to sit outside reserve requirements, and membership itself began to shrink as banks weighed withdrawal. The mechanism was a slow leak rather than a single failure. Each departure narrowed the base over which the Federal Reserve spread the burden of monetary control, and each shift of deposits beyond the requirement weakened the link between the reserves the Federal Reserve supplied and the money creation it sought to govern. Congress answered with a statute that rebuilt the bargain on explicit terms.

The Monetary Control Act of 1980, Title I of the Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act, enacted March 31, 1980, changed three things at once. First, it extended reserve requirements to all depository institutions, member and nonmember alike, closing the escape route that had drained the system. Second, it required the Federal Reserve to publish a fee schedule for its services, converting an implicit charge buried in idle balances into explicit prices that any user could read and compare. Third, it opened Federal Reserve services to all depository institutions on the same priced terms, so a nonmember institution could buy check clearing or wire transfer from the Reserve Bank directly instead of renting access through a correspondent. Price replaced status as the organizing principle of access to services. The published schedule also let institutions compare the Reserve Bank price against a correspondent price line by line, turning a relationship once governed by custom into a purchase that a treasurer could audit each month.

The settlement of 1980 drew a line that still organizes every later dispute, the line between services and the account itself. The statute priced and opened the services, and priced services presuppose an account through which charges are debited and transfers settle. Yet the Act spoke in the language of services and fees rather than in the language of an unconditional account entitlement, and that drafting choice left a space in which Reserve Bank discretion later grew. An institution after 1980 could point to the fee schedule and demand the published price for a published service. Whether the same institution could demand the master account that made the service usable, as a matter of legal right, remained a separate question, and it stayed separate through the litigation of the following decades. The 1980 settlement democratized the price list. It left the door itself in the hands of the doorkeeper, and the argument over that door defines the modern access fight.

## Before 1980: Par Balances, Membership and the Clearing Problem

The system that the Monetary Control Act replaced had been built to solve a clearing problem that predated the Federal Reserve itself. Before 1913, checks drawn on distant banks often returned less than face value, because collecting banks charged exchange fees to cover the cost and risk of presenting a check across the country and shipping currency back. A check at par meant a check paid at full face value, and par clearance was an achievement rather than a default. The Federal Reserve Act of 1913 gave the new Reserve Banks a clearing role, and the Federal Reserve built a national collection system that pressed banks toward par settlement. The mechanism ran through balances. A member bank held reserve balances at its Reserve Bank, presented checks through the Federal Reserve, and settled the net result by debit and credit to those balances rather than by moving currency for every item.

Membership made that machinery possible, and membership was never universal. National banks had to join the system. State chartered institutions chose whether to join, and many stayed outside, weighing the cost of holding noninterest bearing reserves against the clearing and currency services membership provided. The Federal Reserve could require par clearance from its members as a condition of using its collection system, but nonmember banks that cleared through correspondents could keep charging exchange, and pockets of nonpar banking survived for decades in regions where local banks defended the fee income. The result was a two tier geography of payments. Inside the membership perimeter, checks moved at par through Reserve Bank balances. Outside it, checks moved through chains of correspondents, with fees, delays, and credit exposure accumulating at each link in the chain.

Balances did more than settle checks. A member bank's reserve balance was at once a legal requirement, a settlement asset, and a buffer against the day's outflows, and the Federal Reserve set the requirement as a fraction of the bank's deposits. Because the balance earned no interest, every dollar held to satisfy the requirement carried an opportunity cost equal to the interest that dollar could have earned in the market. In calm decades with low interest rates, that cost stayed small enough to ignore. The accounting still mattered for the clearing system, because settlement finality rested on those balances: when the Reserve Bank debited one member's balance and credited another's, the transfer closed in central bank money, and no further collection step remained. The correspondent chains outside the system could imitate that result only by stacking promises, each bank trusting the next bank's balance at the bank above it.

The pre 1980 order therefore joined three questions that later law would separate. Who had to hold reserves, who could use Federal Reserve services, and who paid what for the privilege were all answered by the single fact of membership. That fusion explains both the stability of the old system and its eventual failure. As long as the implicit price of membership stayed low, fusion was convenient. When inflation raised the price, institutions did not merely complain about a fee; they exited the structure that imposed it, and monetary control leaked away with them. The Monetary Control Act of 1980 unfused the three questions, spreading reserve requirements across all depository institutions and pricing services openly. Understanding the fused system that came before it shows what the 1980 reform actually changed: not the existence of a gate, but the basis on which the gatekeeper charged for passage.

## How an Application Is Judged: The Tiered Review Framework

An application for a master account lands at the Reserve Bank of the applicant's district, and the review that follows is an evidence exercise with a defined sequence. Staff confirm the applicant's legal authority to hold the account, assemble the supervisory record, examine capital and liquidity, test the applicant's compliance with the Bank Secrecy Act and anti money laundering law, and assess whether the applicant can operate the account without imposing undue risk on the Reserve Bank or the payment system. For decades that review proceeded case by case without a published map of its intensity. The Board changed the visibility of the process when it proposed the Guidelines for Evaluating Account and Services Requests in 2021 and adopted them in final form in August 2022, published at 87 Federal Register 51099. The guidelines sorted applicants into three tiers by insurance and supervision, and matched the depth of review to the evidence already available from other supervisors.

The table below settles which applicants face which intensity of review and why.

| Review tier | Applicant profile | Supervision in place | What the review must settle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 | Federally insured institution with federal supervision | Full federal prudential supervision and deposit insurance oversight | Whether streamlined review can rely on the existing supervisory record |
| Tier 2 | Federally insured institution or institution under federal prudential supervision | Federal insurance oversight or federal prudential supervision, with one element absent | Whether the available federal record covers the risks the account would create |
| Tier 3 | Institution neither federally insured nor federally supervised | State supervision without federal insurance or federal prudential supervision | Whether the Reserve Bank can build a sufficient risk record without a federal supervisor |
| No account of one's own | Institution holding no master account and settling through another institution | Supervision attached to the institution itself and to its correspondent | Whether the correspondent relationship carries the settlement risk the account would have held directly |

The mechanism the tiers create is a division of labor among supervisors. In Tier 1, the Reserve Bank leans on examinations already performed by the applicant's primary federal regulator and on the insurance fund's monitoring, so review concentrates on account specific matters such as operational readiness and the applicant's intended use of services. In Tier 2, one federal element is present and one is missing, so the Reserve Bank fills the specific gap the missing element leaves, examining more deeply where no insurer or no federal prudential supervisor has passed judgment. In Tier 3, neither element exists, and the strictest review follows because the Reserve Bank must generate nearly the whole evidentiary record itself, from the soundness of the business model to the quality of compliance controls.

Two features of the framework matter as much as the tiers themselves. The first is that the guidelines describe review rather than entitlement: placement in Tier 1 streamlines scrutiny without promising approval, and placement in Tier 3 intensifies scrutiny without decreeing denial. The second is that the framework made the gate legible. An applicant after August 2022 could read the published guidelines, locate its own tier, and predict the shape of the inquiry it faced, where an applicant a decade earlier faced a discretionary process with no public map. Legibility changed the politics of denial as well as the practice of review, because a denied applicant could now argue from the Board's own published standards, measuring the Reserve Bank's stated reasons against the framework the Board had announced. The guidelines narrowed the distance between discretion and rule without closing it, and that remaining distance is where the hardest cases still live.

## The Six Principles Behind the Review

Tiers organize the intensity of a review. Principles organize its content. The Board's account access guidelines, adopted in final form in August 2022, rest the evaluation on six principles that together define what a Reserve Bank must satisfy itself about before it opens a master account. Stated in full, the set runs as follows: the applicant's legal authority to hold the account; risk to the Federal Reserve Bank; risk to the payment system; risk to the stability of the financial system; effect on the implementation of monetary policy; and the applicant's record on illicit finance. Each principle names a different failure mode, and the review fails the applicant if any one of them fails badly enough, because the principles operate as gates in series rather than as scores averaged together.

Legal authority comes first because it is the only principle that no amount of financial strength can cure. The Reserve Bank examines the charter, the statutes under which the applicant operates, and any limits those instruments place on the applicant's powers. An institution organized for a purpose that its charter does not authorize, or holding itself out for activities beyond its legal powers, fails at this gate regardless of capital. Risk to the Reserve Bank comes second, and it is the most concrete of the six. An account holder that overdraws, fails mid settlement, or entangles the Reserve Bank in litigation over frozen funds imposes direct losses and legal costs on the institution that keeps the ledger. The review therefore tests liquidity, operational reliability, and the legal structure through which the Reserve Bank could protect itself if the account holder collapsed at the wrong hour of the settlement day.

Risk to the payment system widens the lens from the Reserve Bank's own balance sheet to the network the account joins. A master account connects its holder to the Fedwire Funds Service, the Fedwire Securities Service, FedACH, and, after its launch in 2023, FedNow instant payments. A holder with weak controls can inject fraudulent transfers, erroneous files, or failed settlements into channels that other institutions trust precisely because the Federal Reserve operates them. The mechanism of contagion is operational as much as financial: a compromised participant forces counterparties and operators to unwind, delay, or manually repair transfers that the system was designed to close automatically. The fourth principle, risk to the stability of the financial system, asks the harder question whether distress at this applicant could propagate, through concentrated deposit bases, novel asset exposures, or confidence effects, into stress at institutions far beyond the applicant itself.

The last two principles look outward to policy and to law enforcement. Effect on the implementation of monetary policy asks whether the applicant's business model, multiplied across imitators, would change how reserve balances circulate or how the interest on reserve balances rate transmits into market rates, a concern that grows sharpest for models built to hold nothing but reserves. The illicit finance principle examines the applicant's record and controls under the Bank Secrecy Act and anti money laundering law, because an account at the center of the payment system is a powerful instrument for moving value, and a holder that cannot identify its customers or monitor their transactions turns that instrument into a channel the Reserve Bank cannot defend. Read together, the six principles convert a vague question, whether an applicant deserves an account, into six narrower questions that evidence can answer. The conversion does not remove judgment. It disciplines judgment by forcing the Reserve Bank to say which principle failed, and forcing the applicant to answer on that ground.

## Capital, Supervision and the Price of Being Inside

Holding a master account places an institution inside the settlement core of the financial system, and the price of that position is paid in capital, supervision, and continuous compliance rather than in fees alone. Capital comes first because capital is the buffer that absorbs losses before anyone else bears them. A supervised bank holds capital against its assets under standards set by its primary regulator, with the required cushion rising as the risk of the assets rises. The mechanism matters for the account because settlement is unforgiving about timing. An institution that enters the settlement day thinly capitalized can meet its obligations in the morning and fail before the afternoon closes, leaving the Reserve Bank and its counterparties to sort out transfers made against a balance that no longer stands behind them. Adequate capital does not prevent failure. It lengthens the distance between the first loss and the moment the failure reaches the account, and that distance is what supervisors are buying when they set the requirement.

Supervision is the second price, and it is paid continuously rather than once. An account holder with a federal supervisor submits to examinations, files reports of condition, and operates under enforcement tools that can restrict its activities before insolvency arrives. That continuing scrutiny is part of what a Reserve Bank relies on when it streamlines review for a Tier 1 applicant under the guidelines adopted in August 2022. The post-crisis settlement in American banking raised those supervisory expectations across the system, as any [Dodd-Frank banking impact assessment](/2014/01/01/dodd-frank-banking-impact/) records in tracing how the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 expanded examination, stress testing, and resolution planning for supervised institutions. An applicant outside that supervisory perimeter asks the Reserve Bank to accept the account without the examination record the perimeter generates, and the strictness of Tier 3 review is the system's way of pricing that absence.

The third price is operational, and it is the least visible from outside. An account holder must maintain the controls that Federal Reserve services presuppose: personnel screening, transaction monitoring under the Bank Secrecy Act, reconciliation of settlement files, collateral management where daylight credit is used, and compliance with Regulation J and the operating circulars that govern each service. Each control costs money in staff, systems, and audit, and each one must function every business day rather than on examination day alone. The Federal Reserve also prices its services explicitly under the fee schedule tradition that began with the Monetary Control Act of 1980, so the holder pays per item charges for transfers, files, and currency orders on top of the standing cost of being supervised and capitalized.

These three prices explain why the account is valuable and why it is guarded by the same logic. The value of the account is finality: settlement in central bank money that closes a payment without residual credit risk. Finality is worth guarding precisely because every other participant in the system relies on it without inspecting each counterparty transfer by transfer. Capital requirements, supervisory examination, and operational controls are the means by which an institution demonstrates, before it is trusted with finality, that its presence inside the core will not dilute the quality that makes finality credible. An institution that finds the price high is measuring the same thing the Reserve Bank measures from the other side: the distance between holding money that merely promises settlement and holding the account in which settlement actually ends.

## Interest on Reserve Balances and the Economics of Holding the Account

### Do banks earn interest on master account balances?

Yes. The Board of Governors sets an interest on reserve balances rate, and each Federal Reserve Bank credits interest on the balances an institution holds in its master account. The payment turns idle settlement funds into earning assets, so holding central bank money carries a return rather than a pure cost.

For most of the history of the Federal Reserve System, balances held at a Reserve Bank paid nothing. An institution kept only the reserves the law required and the working balance its payments demanded, because every dollar parked at the central bank was a dollar that earned no return anywhere else. The economics changed in two legislative steps. The Financial Services Regulatory Relief Act of 2006 granted the Board authority to pay interest on reserve balances, with a deferred start written into the statute. The Emergency Economic Stabilization Act of 2008 then accelerated that start to October 2008, in the middle of a financial crisis, and the authority moved from a distant reform into an operating tool of monetary policy.

The mechanism is simple in form and powerful in effect. At the close of each day, the Reserve Bank measures the balance standing in the institution's master account. The Board's interest on reserve balances rate applies to that balance, and interest accrues and is credited to the same account. Because the balance itself is a liability of the Federal Reserve Bank, the interest is a payment from the central bank on its own liability, made by enlarging the very balance it owes. No market transaction intervenes, no counterparty must perform, and no settlement risk attaches to the credit.

That design gives the rate a special place in monetary policy. When the Board raises the rate paid on reserve balances, holding reserves becomes more attractive, and an institution weighing a very short loan to another bank compares that loan against a riskless return available at its own Reserve Bank. The rate therefore works as a floor under overnight money market rates, pulling them upward without the Federal Reserve having to drain a single dollar from the system. When the Board lowers the rate, the comparison reverses, reserves become less rewarding to hold, and funds face stronger pressure to move into lending and other uses.

The rate also reshaped how institutions manage the balance itself. A treasurer deciding whether to hold funds in the account overnight or place them in the federal funds market runs a direct comparison between two returns, one paid by a private borrower and one paid by the Federal Reserve Bank on its own liability. Because the central bank return carries no credit risk and no settlement risk, private overnight rates must compensate a lender for both disadvantages or the funds stay in the master account. Through that daily comparison, the rate paid on reserve balances transmits Board policy into the money market without any open market operation taking place.

Interest also changed what a master account is worth to its holder. Before 2008, the account was chiefly a settlement utility, valuable for the payments it completed and costly for the idle balances it required. After 2008, the account became an earning asset in its own right, and the size of the balance an institution chose to hold reflected a portfolio decision as well as a payments need. That shift explains why access disputes carry an economic charge beyond plumbing. An institution shut out of a master account is shut out not only from direct settlement but also from the riskless return the Federal Reserve pays on its own liabilities, a return its chartered competitors collect by right of holding the account.

## Daylight Overdrafts: Credit the Federal Reserve Extends by Day

A payment system that settles in real time creates a timing problem, and daylight overdrafts are the Federal Reserve's answer to it. An institution may need to send a Fedwire payment in the morning before the payments that fund it arrive in the afternoon. If the Reserve Bank refused every payment that exceeded the balance at that moment, transfers would queue, counterparties would wait, and the gridlock in one account would spread across the system. Instead, under the Federal Reserve's Payment System Risk Policy, an institution may run a negative intraday balance, borrowing central bank money for hours rather than days.

The extension of credit follows a controlled sequence. The institution releases a payment larger than its balance, the Reserve Bank processes the transfer because finality cannot wait for funding, and the account moves below zero. Through the day, incoming payments replenish the account, and by the close of business the institution must return the balance to zero or above. The overdraft is therefore self-liquidating in the ordinary course, extinguished by the same flow of payments that made it necessary, and it never becomes an overnight loan.

Control rests on the net debit cap, a ceiling on the intraday negative balance an institution may run, linked to its capital. Capital supplies the measure because it gauges the loss an institution can absorb, and an overdraft ceiling tied to it scales permission to the holder's own strength. An institution with more capital may run a larger intraday position; an institution with thin capital faces a tighter ceiling and must fund payments more nearly in advance. When a payment would breach the cap, the Reserve Bank can reject or delay it, and that refusal is the point at which intraday credit stops being automatic.

Intraday monitoring makes the discipline operational rather than theoretical. The Reserve Bank observes balances as payments post through the day, measures each institution's negative position against its net debit cap, and can act while the day is still running rather than after a loss has settled. An institution approaching its ceiling must choose among concrete remedies: delay outgoing payments until incoming funds arrive, borrow in the federal funds market to replenish the account, or pledge collateral to support a larger position. Each remedy has a cost, and the costs rank themselves in real time, so the holder internalizes the price of intraday credit at the moment the credit is used. Collateral pledged in advance changes the calculation again, because securities already positioned at the Reserve Bank can support an overdraft without a scramble for funding in the middle of the payment day.

Pricing sharpens the discipline. The Payment System Risk Policy charges fees on uncollateralized daylight overdrafts and grants discounts for collateralized ones, so an institution that pledges collateral against its intraday borrowing pays less than one that borrows on its standing alone. The price difference pushes holders toward collateral, and collateral in the Reserve Bank's hands converts an unsecured intraday exposure into a secured one. A cap breach carries its own sequence: the payment waits, the institution is notified, funding must arrive or collateral must be pledged, and only then does the transfer release. That pause, applied at scale, is how the Reserve Bank keeps intraday credit from becoming an invisible overnight exposure shared across the payment system.

The arrangement shows the master account working as more than a ledger. It is a credit relationship measured in hours, governed by capital, priced by risk, and closed out every business day by design.

## The Discount Window Is a Separate Door

A master account and discount window borrowing are separate relationships, and confusing them misreads both. The account is a settlement relationship: the institution holds balances at a Federal Reserve Bank and moves them through the payment services the account opens. Borrowing is a lending relationship: the Reserve Bank extends credit against collateral under its own eligibility rules, for its own reasons, on its own decision. Holding the first relationship confers no entitlement to the second, and an institution can settle payments through its account for decades without ever standing at the discount window.

The separation follows from the different risks each door admits. Settlement risk is intraday and operational, governed by balances, net debit caps, and the Payment System Risk Policy. Lending risk is credit risk across a longer horizon, governed by the quality and value of pledged collateral and by the borrowing institution's condition. A Reserve Bank evaluating a loan asks whether the collateral covers the advance and whether the borrower qualifies as an eligible institution in sound enough condition to borrow. Those questions have no bearing on whether the same institution's morning Fedwire payment should settle, and the account's rules rightly ignore them.

Mechanics keep the doors apart in practice as well. When an institution borrows, the Reserve Bank credits the proceeds to the borrower's master account, because that account is where the institution holds central bank money. The credit uses the account as a destination without merging the two relationships, in the same way that a deposit uses an account without becoming the account itself. Repayment reverses the flow: the account is debited, the collateral is released, and the lending relationship closes while the settlement relationship continues untouched.

A worked sequence shows the separation in motion. An institution facing a temporary shortage of settlement funds first spends the balance it owns, because spending its own central bank money requires no permission from anyone. If the shortage persists, it turns to private markets, where other institutions lend their surplus balances for a price. Only when it seeks credit from the central bank itself does the discount window enter the picture, and at that point a new file opens: the Reserve Bank examines the collateral offered, applies its eligibility rules, and decides whether to lend and on what terms. Approval of the loan then produces a credit to the master account, and refusal leaves the account exactly as it was, fully usable for settlement and entirely without borrowed funds. Three different doors, three different decisions, and only the first of them is the account itself. Eligibility for one says nothing automatic about eligibility for another, and each decision is recorded, collateralized, and reviewed on its own terms by the Reserve Bank that bears the corresponding risk.

The distinction matters most in a crisis, when the two doors are often described as one rescue. An account holder in difficulty still holds its own balances and can spend them to finality, because those balances are its property held at the central bank. What it cannot do is convert account holding into borrowing power by assertion. The discount window lends by decision, against collateral, under rules that stand apart from the ledger, and that separation protects the settlement system from becoming an automatic channel of credit to any institution that merely keeps an account.

## What Happens When an Account Holder Fails

Failure tests every promise a master account makes, because the institution is gone while its balances, its queued obligations, and its counterparties remain. The first question is what the balance is. Funds standing in a master account are the failed institution's asset and the Federal Reserve Bank's liability, central bank money that does not depend on the failed institution's own solvency for its value. A receiver stepping into the institution's place finds that asset intact on the Reserve Bank's books, claims it through the receivership process, and applies it for creditors under the priority rules that govern the failure.

The second question concerns payments already made. Transfers completed through Fedwire before the failure stand, because Fedwire transfers are final and irrevocable when the receiving Reserve Bank credits the receiving institution's master account, under Regulation J and Article 4A of the Uniform Commercial Code. A receiver cannot unwind a settled payment to enlarge the estate, and a counterparty that received final funds keeps them. Finality in life becomes finality in failure, and that continuity is precisely what prevents one institution's collapse from reopening every payment it touched on its last day of business.

Loss absorption runs in a set order before public support enters the picture. Shareholders lose first, then subordinated and loss absorbing instruments take their write down or conversion, and that layer of capital absorbing losses before account balances are reached in a failure is the subject of the [AT1 bond explainer](/2026/10/16/how-at1-bonds-work/). Only after those private layers are exhausted do deposit insurance and resolution funds bear cost. The master account balance itself sits outside that stack: it is not capital, it absorbs no losses, and it remains a central bank liability payable to whoever lawfully succeeds to the failed institution's claim.

The treatment of obligations still in flight follows the same finality logic step by step. A payment instruction sent but not yet settled when the failure occurs has not moved central bank money, so it creates no completed transfer for the receiver to honor or reverse. Funds that other institutions owe the failed holder continue to arrive as credits, swelling the balance the receiver administers. Funds the failed holder owed to others must be claimed through the receivership rather than extracted from the account by self help, because the balance at the Reserve Bank belongs to the estate as a whole and not to whichever creditor reaches the window first. That ordering protects the payment system and the creditor body at the same time: settled payments stay settled, unsettled claims enter an orderly process, and no scramble for the central bank balance can rewrite priorities after the fact. Counterparties planning around a failure can therefore separate their exposure into two clean parts, the payments already final and beyond recall, and the claims still open and subject to the receivership.

What stops at failure is activity, not value. The Reserve Bank ceases to process new payment instructions from an institution that no longer operates, daylight overdraft capacity disappears with the capital that supported the net debit cap, and access to services ends as the receivership takes control. Incoming credits for the estate can still be received and held under the receiver's direction. The account thus closes the way it opened, as a ledger relationship with the central bank: the money in it survives the holder, the permissions around it do not, and the orderly transfer of the balance to a receiver or an acquiring institution completes the last settlement the account performs.

## Custodia and the Discretion Question

Custodia Bank began under another name, Avanti, and took a charter that placed it outside the federal insurance system from the start. Wyoming had enacted a statute in 2019 creating special purpose depository institutions, a charter designed for institutions serving digital asset businesses while holding customer funds in full reserve. Custodia was chartered by Wyoming in 2020 under that statute. It was not federally insured, and no federal agency supervised it as federal law supervises insured banks. In 2020, Custodia applied to the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City for a master account, seeking direct access to Federal Reserve services on the strength of its state charter.

The application waited, and then it failed. On January 27, 2023, the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City denied the application. The denial posed the question that runs through every access dispute in its sharpest form. Federal statutes describe which institutions are eligible to receive Federal Reserve services, and Custodia held a state charter of a kind the Wyoming legislature had built for depository business. Custodia's position treated eligibility as entitlement: an institution inside the statutory class, it argued, could claim an account. The Federal Reserve's position treated eligibility as a threshold that opened a discretionary judgment about risk, supervision, and the safety of the payment system, a judgment the Reserve Bank was entitled to make and to make negatively.

The timing sharpened the contest. Custodia had applied in 2020, before any published framework governed such requests. The Board proposed its account access guidelines in 2021 and adopted them in final form in August 2022 (87 Federal Register 51099), placing institutions that are neither federally insured nor federally supervised in Tier 3, the tier carrying the strictest review. Custodia, uninsured and supervised by Wyoming alone, fell squarely inside that tier, so the denial in January 2023 arrived under a framework that had taken shape while the application was pending. The six principles behind the framework, covering legal authority, risk to the Reserve Bank, risk to the payment system, risk to the stability of the financial system, effect on the implementation of monetary policy, and the applicant's record on illicit finance, supplied the vocabulary of the denial, while the lawsuit asked the prior question whether discretion of that breadth existed at all.

Custodia sued, and the case moved through the federal courts as a contest over statutory meaning. In 2024, the United States District Court for the District of Wyoming ruled for the Federal Reserve, holding that the statutes do not entitle an eligible institution to an account. The court read the governing provisions as granting access to services through accounts the Reserve Banks may provide, not as commanding an account for every institution that fits the eligible class. On October 31, 2025, the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit affirmed that judgment by a divided court, leaving the district court's reading of the statutes in force and the denial standing.

The litigation ended in the Supreme Court without a decision on the law. On November 16, 2026, the Supreme Court denied certiorari in Custodia Bank, Inc. v. Federal Reserve Board of Governors, No. 26-62, without opinion and without a noted dissent. A denial of certiorari decides nothing on the merits and sets no precedent: the Court declined to hear the case, the Tenth Circuit judgment remained the final word between the parties, and the underlying question of statutory entitlement stayed exactly where the lower courts had left it, resolved for Custodia and unresolved as national precedent. Wyoming's charter experiment survived the case; the claim that the charter carried an account with it did not.

## Novel Charters and the Pressure on the Gate

### Why do novel charter banks want a Federal Reserve master account?

A master account gives a novel charter bank settlement in central bank money, direct access to Fedwire and other Federal Reserve services, and interest on reserve balances, all without renting access through a correspondent bank that sets its own price, imposes its own limits, and can end the relationship.

The novel charter is a deliberate legislative invention. A state such as Wyoming, acting through its 2019 special purpose depository institution statute, designs a charter for a business the established system serves badly, then charters institutions that hold funds in full reserve and take no conventional lending risk. The design answers one problem and creates another. The institution can safeguard customer funds under state law, yet every payment it makes must still travel through the national payment system, and that system settles at the Federal Reserve. Without an account of its own, the new institution stands outside the settlement layer its charter never mentions and its customers always need.

Dependence on a correspondent fills the gap at a price. The novel institution holds its operating funds at an established bank, sends payments through that bank's master account, and accepts the correspondent's fees, limits, and risk judgments. The correspondent can restrict volumes, demand balances, or end the relationship when the novel business begins to trouble its own supervisors and insurers. A business built to serve customers the established banks avoid can therefore be extinguished by the very dependence its lack of an account imposes, without any public decision ever being made about whether it should exist.

The tiered review framework adopted in August 2022 in the Board's account access guidelines (87 Federal Register 51099) places such applicants in Tier 3, the strictest review, because they are neither federally insured nor federally supervised. Tier 3 review examines legal authority, risk to the Reserve Bank, risk to the payment system, and the applicant's record on illicit finance with the greatest intensity the framework allows. Julie Andersen Hill has traced how this structure developed: Congress wrote eligibility in broad terms, the Reserve Banks built discretion in practice, and state legislatures then created charters whose business models assume the federal account will follow the state grant. Each side acts within its own authority, and the collision occurs at the account itself. A state can charter an institution in a single legislative session, but it cannot charter settlement in central bank money, because the ledger belongs to the Federal Reserve Banks. A novel institution must therefore persuade a second sovereign system, under a framework written for risk rather than for industrial policy, that its supervision, its reserves, and its controls on illicit finance are strong enough to finish payments beside the largest banks in the country.

The application process itself adds friction by design. The applicant files with the Reserve Bank of its district, supplies evidence of its charter, its supervision, its financial condition, and its controls against illicit finance, and then answers successive rounds of questions as reviewers test each element against the six principles. A gap in any one element slows the whole file, because the framework treats the principles as cumulative conditions rather than as a score to be averaged. For a novel charter whose home state supervision is the only supervision on record, the federal file must carry weight that an insured bank's file shares with its federal supervisors, and building that record takes time the charter itself cannot shorten.

The pressure on the gate is therefore structural rather than personal. Each new charter a state creates produces an institution designed to need the account, supervised by a state alone, and routed by federal guidelines into the longest and hardest review the system runs.

## The Narrow Bank Argument and the Pass-Through Fight

TNB USA Inc., known as The Narrow Bank, pressed the access question from the opposite direction. Where Custodia offered a novel charter and a novel customer base, the narrow bank offered the oldest and simplest balance sheet in banking. Its model, pursued from 2018 in an application to the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, was to take deposits, hold the proceeds in a master account as reserve balances, and pass the interest on reserve balances through to depositors. It proposed to make no loans, hold no securities portfolio of consequence, and take no credit risk beyond the central bank itself. If safety were the criterion for access, the narrow bank argued by its design, no applicant could be safer.

The mechanism deserves attention because it exposes what interest on reserves does to banking structure. A conventional bank earns its margin by transforming deposits into loans and securities that yield more than deposits cost. A narrow bank skips the transformation entirely. Deposits enter, the funds rest at the Federal Reserve earning the rate paid on reserve balances, and that rate, minus a thin spread for operating cost, flows back to depositors. The depositor holds a claim backed by central bank money rather than by a loan book, and the institution's solvency depends on the Federal Reserve's own liability rather than on any borrower's performance.

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York did not act on the application. No approval issued, and no denial issued either, and that inaction became the dispute. TNB USA Inc. sued to compel a decision, and in 2020 a federal district court dismissed the suit on the ground that the dispute was not ripe. Ripeness doctrine asks whether a court faces a concrete decision to review; with no grant and no refusal on record, the court found nothing final to judge, and the application remained in the unresolved state the dismissal preserved.

The objection from the Federal Reserve side, expressed through inaction rather than a stated decision, concerned what a successful narrow bank would do to the rest of the system. Deposits gathered by a narrow bank leave conventional banks and rest at the central bank, shrinking the deposit base that funds private lending. In calm conditions that shift is gradual and competitive; in a panic it can accelerate, because a depositor moving funds to an institution backed entirely by central bank money abandons credit risk altogether. The same pass-through rate that rewards depositors in ordinary times becomes a magnet in a crisis, and the flight it invites lands on institutions least able to lose funding. Whether that systemic cost outweighs the benefit of a perfectly safe deposit is a genuine policy question, and it is the question the New York Fed's silence left unasked in any reviewable form.

The pass-through fight therefore ended without any ruling on whether a narrow bank deserves an account, yet the argument it embodied has not gone away. A model that pays depositors a rate anchored to the central bank's own rate competes directly with conventional deposits, and in stress it offers frightened depositors a visibly safer home, which can pull funds out of ordinary banks at the worst moment. The narrow bank case shows the gate working by silence rather than by judgment: no principle was announced, no precedent was set, and the question of whether the safest possible applicant may finish its own payments stayed open by default.

## How Other Central Banks Draw the Line

Every central bank that operates a settlement system faces the same design choice in a different legal costume. The question is which institutions may hold balances directly on the central bank balance sheet and which institutions must settle through an intermediary that holds such balances. The Bank of England answered that question in a notably open direction in 2017, when it opened direct settlement accounts in its real time gross settlement system to non-bank payment service providers. The Bank of England described the reform as the first of its kind by a major central bank, and the mechanism matters more than the priority claim. A payment firm that holds its own settlement account receives incoming transfers as a credit to that account, pays outgoing transfers as a debit from that account, and manages its intraday liquidity against a balance it controls directly rather than against a balance held for it by a commercial bank that may also compete with it.

The contrast with the Federal Reserve design is instructive because the underlying technology is similar and the gate is different. Both systems settle transfer by transfer in central bank money, and both make settlement final when the central bank ledger records the credit. The Bank of England widened the class of firms that could reach that ledger for sterling payments, subject to authorization and supervision under British law, while the Federal Reserve retained a framework in which eligibility under federal law is the starting point and Reserve Bank discretion governs close cases. Neither design removes risk from the system. A wider gate moves risk management toward collateral rules, liquidity requirements, and operational standards applied to a larger set of direct participants. A narrower gate concentrates settlement in fewer institutions and pushes excluded firms into correspondent arrangements, where their payments depend on the credit and operational decisions of an intermediary.

Other systems illustrate intermediate positions without supplying a single model to copy. The Eurosystem, operated by the European Central Bank and the national central banks of the euro area, grants access through national central banks under harmonized rules tied to supervised credit institution status. The Bank of Canada operates Lynx, its large value transfer system, with direct participation limited to institutions that meet membership, collateral, and risk requirements under Canadian payment law. The Reserve Bank of Australia operates its Reserve Bank Information and Transfer System with exchange settlement accounts held by institutions that satisfy its access policy. Each arrangement draws the line by reference to supervision and function rather than by size alone, and each reserves a judgment to the operator about whether an applicant can settle without endangering other participants.

The comparison sharpens the American dispute rather than settling it. A foreign reform proves that a central bank can admit a new category of participant and continue to operate a stable settlement system when the admission rule is written in advance and the safeguards travel with the account. What the Bank of England reform of 2017 demonstrates most clearly is a procedural point. When the operator publishes the category, the criteria, and the safeguards together, an applicant can organize its charter, capital, and controls around a known standard, and a refusal can be read against that standard. Where the standard remains discretionary and case specific, the same refusal carries a heavier explanatory burden, because outsiders cannot easily tell whether the decision applied a rule or created one.

## What Approval and Denial Each Prove

An approval and a denial each prove less than public argument often claims. Approval of a master account application by a Federal Reserve Bank establishes that the applicant satisfied the reviewing Reserve Bank, under the Board account access guidelines adopted in August 2022, that it held legal authority for the account and that its risk profile fell within the tolerance of the tier assigned to it. The mechanism of review explains the limit of the conclusion. Reviewers examine charters, supervisory arrangements, capital, liquidity, risk controls, anti money laundering programs, and the business model described in the application, and they judge those materials against the six principles stated in the guidelines. A favorable judgment on that record is a judgment about access to settlement services. It is not a certification that the institution will remain sound, that its assets are well chosen, or that its deposits carry federal insurance, and the account itself confers none of those qualities.

Denial carries an equally narrow meaning, and precision on that point protects both sides of the debate. When the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City denied the application of Custodia Bank on January 27, 2023, the denial established that the Reserve Bank was not persuaded that the applicant, a Wyoming special purpose depository institution without federal insurance, could operate the account within the risk tolerances applied to a Tier 3 applicant. A denial under a discretionary framework does not by itself establish that an institution is insolvent, illiquid, dishonestly managed, or incapable of operating safely under a different set of conditions or controls. The distinction follows from the structure of the decision. The Reserve Bank weighs prospective risks to itself, to the payment system, to financial stability, to monetary policy implementation, and from illicit finance, and a concern on any one principle can support denial even when the other principles point in the opposite direction.

The identification problem runs deeper because the same evidence can support either outcome under a discretionary standard. A novel business model presents little operating history, and little operating history can be read either as an absence of demonstrated failure or as an absence of demonstrated control. A concentrated deposit base can be read either as a focused franchise with a clear understanding of its customers or as a funding profile exposed to a single sector. The reviewing Reserve Bank must choose a reading, and the choice, rather than any single fact in the file, often decides the application.

Confusing access decisions with safety judgments distorts the incentives of every participant. If approval is treated as a public endorsement, a Reserve Bank has reason to delay or deny borderline applications in order to avoid the reputational cost of a later failure, and applicants have reason to advertise approval as a guarantee that the account never supplied. If denial is treated as a finding of unsoundness, an excluded institution suffers a market penalty larger than the decision itself imposed, and future applicants have reason to litigate rather than revise and reapply. The cleaner reading treats the master account as infrastructure with conditions. Approval means the conditions were met on the record presented. Denial means the conditions were not met to the satisfaction of the deciding Reserve Bank on the record presented. Either outcome leaves the separate questions of charter validity, deposit safety, and business merit to the authorities and markets charged with deciding them.

## The Case Against the Gatekeeper

The strongest case against discretionary gatekeeping begins with the text and structure of the Monetary Control Act of 1980. Congress required the Federal Reserve to publish a fee schedule for its services and opened those services to all depository institutions, member and nonmember, on the same priced terms. On the reading developed by Julie Andersen Hill, that design replaced a membership privilege with a priced public service. A depository institution that state law has chartered, that federal law places within the class eligible for services, and that pays the published price has, on that reading, a claim to the account rather than a request for a favor. Discretion that turns on an open ended assessment of business model risk rewrites the bargain, because the applicant has satisfied the legislature and the chartering authority and then faces a second, standardless charter decision at the settlement window. The argument gains force from the competitive consequence. A denied fintech oriented bank or special purpose depository institution must buy settlement from a correspondent that may view it as a rival, so denial does not merely delay the applicant. Denial assigns its business to its competitors.

The defense of discretion, in its best form, answers that settlement access is not an ordinary public service because the account places the applicant on the balance sheet of the central bank itself. A Reserve Bank that credits a master account makes payment final in its own liabilities, extends intraday credit through daylight overdrafts under the Payment System Risk Policy, and exposes the payment system to the operational and legal failures of the account holder. Congress, on that reading, priced services without surrendering the ordinary power of a bank to judge the risk of an account relationship, and the tiered guidelines of 2022 supply the discipline that critics demand by sorting applicants according to federal insurance and federal supervision. Tier 1 institutions receive streamlined review because a federal supervisor and an insurer already examine the risks that matter. Tier 3 institutions receive the strictest review because no federal backstop of that kind exists, and a Reserve Bank asked to settle for an unsupervised institution is entitled to examine what no other federal authority has examined. On that account, discretion is not a veto over state chartering. Discretion is the risk function of the settlement operator, exercised at the only point where the risk becomes the central bank liability of the nation.

Both arguments identify a real value, and the collision between them narrows the thesis rather than destroying it. Equal access explains why the gate requires a justification grounded in settlement risk rather than in a preference for familiar business models, and risk explains why the justification cannot be reduced to the production of a charter and the payment of a fee. The narrowed position is therefore exact. A master account remains the point where private money becomes final, and access to that point may be conditioned on safeguards that protect finality itself, including capital, liquidity, supervision, and controls against illicit finance. Access may not, consistent with the design of 1980, be withheld on a ground that the guidelines do not name or on a judgment that a lawful model is undesirable in the abstract. The fight is over who finishes a payment without borrowed permission, and the legitimate gate is the set of conditions that make finishing safe, stated in advance and applied in the same way to familiar and unfamiliar applicants alike.

## Reading an Access Dispute From the Outside

An access dispute reads clearly once the documents are placed in the order in which the decision was made. Start with the charter, because the charter decides the tier. A federally insured institution with federal supervision enters Tier 1 under the Board guidelines adopted in August 2022 and receives streamlined review, while an institution with neither federal insurance nor federal supervision enters Tier 3 and receives the strictest review. Next, read the application record against the six principles rather than against the reputation of the applicant. Legal authority comes first, followed by risk to the Reserve Bank, risk to the payment system, risk to financial stability, effect on monetary policy, and record on illicit finance. A dispute that turns on one principle should not be argued as if it turned on all six, and you can locate the real disagreement by finding the principle on which the Reserve Bank placed the weight of its decision.

The procedural history then tells you how much weight the decision can bear. An administrative denial states the position of one Reserve Bank on one record. A district court judgment, such as the 2024 judgment of the United States District Court for the District of Wyoming in the Custodia litigation, decides the legal question presented on that record. An appellate affirmance, such as the divided decision of the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit on October 31, 2025, settles the law within that circuit unless a higher court intervenes. A denial of certiorari by the Supreme Court, such as the denial on November 16, 2026, in Custodia Bank, Inc. v. Federal Reserve Board of Governors, settles nothing on the merits and should never be quoted as approval of the reasoning below. Separating those levels prevents the most common reading error, in which the last procedural event is mistaken for the strongest legal statement.

Payment mechanics supply a further check on the rhetoric of a dispute. Ask where the applicant's customer funds settle while the dispute proceeds, because the answer reveals the practical stake more reliably than any characterization of the parties. If customer transfers settle through a correspondent master account, the applicant already reaches the payment system and the dispute concerns the price, control, and durability of that reach. If a transfer cannot complete without the account because no correspondent will carry the flow, the dispute concerns market access itself. The distinction also disciplines claims about harm. Loss of direct settlement raises cost and dependence in the first case and threatens the existence of the service in the second, and a careful reader grades the argument accordingly.

Two practical checks complete the reading. First, separate the account question from the insurance question and from the charter question, because a valid Wyoming charter, an absence of federal insurance, and a denied master account can all be true at once without contradiction. Second, ask what condition would change the outcome. A denial grounded in capital can be answered with capital, a denial grounded in an untested control can be answered with examination and time, and a denial grounded in the legal category itself can be answered only by Congress or by a court with authority to construe the statute. That question converts a dispute from a contest of characterizations into a map of the remedy, and it shows whether the gate in a given case operated as a safeguard with terms or as a barrier without them. ## What a Master Account Does Not Do

A master account performs a narrow function, and a large share of public confusion comes from assigning the account powers that belong to other relationships. A master account does not insure deposits. Deposit insurance comes from the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation for insured institutions, within statutory limits, and an uninsured institution gains no insurance by holding an account at a Federal Reserve Bank. A depositor who treats the existence of the account as protection has mistaken settlement access for a guarantee, and the mistake matters because the two protections fail in different ways. Insurance responds to the failure of the institution. Settlement finality responds to the completion of a payment, and a payment can be perfectly final while the institution that received it later fails for reasons that have nothing to do with the transfer.

A master account also does not confer borrowing privileges at the discount window. Account holding and discount window borrowing are separate relationships, governed by separate eligibility and collateral rules, and an institution that settles through its own account may still be unable to borrow, or able to borrow only against acceptable collateral under the terms that govern lending. The account does not charter the institution, license its activities, or approve its business model in the eyes of any other authority. Chartering belongs to the state or federal authority that issued the charter, supervision belongs to the designated supervisors, and consumer protection obligations arise from the statutes that impose them. The Reserve Bank decision on access decides one question, whether the institution may hold balances and use Federal Reserve services directly, and a reader who loads that decision with wider meanings will misread both approvals and denials.

A related misconception treats the account as a source of funds. Balances in a master account belong to the account holder and represent funds the holder has accumulated through deposits, transfers, and other receipts, and the account pays the rate paid on reserve balances set by the Board on those balances under authority that took effect in October 2008. The account creates no funds of its own. An institution that enters a business day with a low balance must fund its payments from receipts, transfers from other accounts, asset sales, or borrowing arranged under separate authority, and the intraday discipline of the Payment System Risk Policy, with its net debit cap linked to capital, governs how far payments may run ahead of funding. Reading the account as a credit facility reverses the relationship.

Nor does the account make an institution too important to fail, exempt it from reserve or liquidity rules that otherwise apply, or guarantee that other banks will deal with it on favorable terms. Correspondent relationships, market funding, and supervisory ratings continue to rest on their own foundations. The account does not even guarantee speed for every payment the institution touches, because retail transfers may travel over FedACH, the FedNow Service launched in 2023, card networks, or private arrangements before any interbank leg reaches a master account for settlement. What the account guarantees is narrower and more valuable. When a transfer is settled through the account under Regulation J and Article 4A, the credit to the receiving master account is final and irrevocable, and the receiving institution holds central bank money rather than a claim on another bank. Confusing that precise guarantee with a general seal of approval inflates the account into a prize. Keeping the guarantee precise restores the account to its real character as regulated infrastructure with a defined legal effect.

## Why the Gate Decides the Shape of American Banking

The gate decides the shape of American banking because settlement is the layer at which a business model becomes viable or remains theoretical. A payments company without direct access can design an elegant application, raise capital, and obtain a state charter, and still depend for the completion of every transfer on a sponsor bank that sets the price, imposes the controls, and can terminate the relationship. The mechanism runs through the settlement leg. Customer instructions accumulate at the sponsor, transfers move between institutions over Fedwire or FedACH, and the balances that make those transfers final sit in the master account of the sponsor rather than in an account of the company whose customers believe they have paid. A change in sponsor terms therefore reaches the end customer as delay, cost, or refusal, even though the customer never chose the sponsor and may never learn its name.

The same dependence appears at the largest scale in public payments. When the Treasury disburses benefits, refunds, and other payments to households, the funds move from the government account at the Federal Reserve into the banking system and then between institutions until the receiving bank credits the household account, a sequence explained in the [government cash payments explainer](/2026/10/03/how-government-cash-payments-work/) and completed, at the interbank stage, by debits and credits across master accounts. No household sees that stage, and no household needs to see it while the stage works. The invisibility is the point. A payment system earns trust by making finality routine, and the institutions admitted to the final stage determine whose products can promise that routine finality in their own name and whose products must borrow the promise from an intermediary.

Consolidation follows from the same mechanism and rarely appears in access debates. When direct settlement is confined to a limited class of institutions, smaller banks and new entrants buy settlement, compliance screening, and liquidity management as a bundle from larger correspondents, and the economics of that bundle reward scale at the correspondent level. A correspondent that settles for hundreds of respondents spreads the fixed cost of its master account operations, its Fedwire connection, and its risk staff across a wide base, while each respondent bears a margin on every transfer. The gate therefore does not merely respond to the shape of American banking. Through the price and control terms of correspondent access, the gate helps produce that shape, and a decision about one applicant carries consequences for the competitive position of every institution that settles through a rival.

Charter innovation presses on the gate for the same reason. Wyoming created its special purpose depository institution charter by statute in 2019, and Custodia Bank, chartered under that statute in 2020, sought a master account in the same year because the charter without the account left the institution dependent on the correspondents it was designed to bypass. TNB USA Inc. pressed a different model from 2018, seeking an account at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York in order to hold balances and pass interest on reserve balances through to depositors, and the absence of a decision left that model untested rather than refuted. In each case the contested asset was not prestige. The contested asset was the ability to complete the last leg of a payment on the books of the central bank, and the outcome determined whether a state charter described a functioning bank or a design awaiting permission. A gate with that effect allocates more than access. The gate allocates the practical freedom to build a bank of a new kind, which is why the criteria at the gate carry constitutional weight in economic life even though the instrument itself is an account ledger maintained by a Reserve Bank.

## Finality Is a Permission as Much as a Technology

The technology of settlement is settled in a way the law of access is not. Regulation J and Article 4A state when a Fedwire transfer becomes final, the Payment System Risk Policy states how intraday credit is capped and priced, and the guidelines of August 2022 state the tiers and principles by which applications are judged. A transfer that satisfies those rules ends the payment obligation it carries, and no later dispute between the sending and receiving customers reopens the interbank credit that completed it. That achievement is mechanical, repeatable, and indifferent to the identity of the account holder. The ledger does not ask whether the institution that owns the credited account is old or new, large or small, conventional or experimental. The ledger records the credit, and the credit is central bank money.

Permission enters before the ledger moves. An institution without a master account never reaches the indifferent stage in its own name, because its transfers settle through the account of another institution whose permission it rents and whose risk judgment it must satisfy in addition to the judgment of the Federal Reserve. Restated plainly, the central claim of the master account debate is that direct holding of reserve balances is the dividing line between issuing a payment instruction and completing a payment, and control over admission to that line is control over which institutions may complete payments on their own authority. That is why the Custodia litigation, ending in the Supreme Court denial of certiorari on November 16, 2026, mattered beyond one Wyoming institution, and why the Bank of England reform of 2017 remains the comparison that clarifies the American choice. One system widened the class that could reach finality under published safeguards. The other retained a discretionary gate and defended that gate as protection for the settlement system itself.

The history of the account supports that reading at each stage. Before 1980, Federal Reserve services were tied to membership and supplied without explicit per item charges, and nonmember institutions reached the system through member correspondents as a matter of design. The Monetary Control Act of 1980 broke that link by pricing services and opening them to depository institutions generally, and the guidelines of 2022 then rebuilt a sorting mechanism inside the opened system, this time sorted by insurance and supervision rather than by membership. Each reform answered the access question of its era, and each reform left a residue of discretion because no legislature has written a rule that decides every future applicant in advance.

A durable settlement of the question would honor both halves of that record. Published criteria, applied in the same form to every tier, would let applicants build toward a known standard and let courts and Congress evaluate denials against the standard the Federal Reserve announced. Safeguards tied to settlement risk would remain firm, because finality in central bank money is worth protecting and an account holder that cannot control its liquidity, its operations, or its illicit finance exposure endangers more than its own customers. Between those commitments, the account returns to its proper scale. A master account is a ledger relationship with legal consequences of the first order, neither a trophy for established institutions nor an entitlement detached from risk, and the legitimacy of the gate depends on whether a denied applicant can point to the condition it failed and a path by which that condition could be met. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is a Federal Reserve master account in simple terms?

A master account is an account that a bank or another eligible depository institution holds directly at one of the twelve regional Federal Reserve Banks created under the Federal Reserve Act of 1913. The institution keeps reserve balances in the account, and those balances are liabilities of the Reserve Bank itself, which means they are central bank money rather than a claim on a commercial bank. Through the account the holder sends and receives payments for itself and for its customers, settles obligations with other institutions, obtains currency and coin, and uses Federal Reserve payment services. When a payment settles through master accounts, the Reserve Bank debits one account and credits another, and that bookkeeping entry completes the payment.

### Q: How is a master account different from a regular bank account?

A regular bank account is a claim on a commercial bank, so its safety depends on that bank honoring its promise to pay and on deposit insurance within its legal limits. A master account is a claim on a Federal Reserve Bank, and its balances are central bank money, the asset in which payments between banks are finally settled. A business or household cannot open one, because access is limited to institutions made eligible by federal statute. The account also works differently in daily use. Instead of checks and consumer transfers, it carries interbank settlement, reserve balances that earn the rate paid on reserve balances, intraday credit under Federal Reserve policy, and direct connections to services such as Fedwire, FedACH, and FedNow, which launched in 2023.

### Q: Which banks are eligible for a Federal Reserve master account?

Eligibility turns on legal status under federal law rather than on size, profitability, or public reputation. Commercial banks, savings banks, savings associations, credit unions, and certain other depository institutions recognized by statute may apply to the Federal Reserve Bank for their district. Branches and agencies of foreign banks operating in the United States can also qualify under the governing statutes. Legal eligibility opens the application process, but it does not by itself guarantee approval. Under the Board account access guidelines, adopted in final form in August 2022, the Reserve Bank examines the applicant's charter, insurance status, supervision, risk controls, and legal authority before deciding whether to open an account, and different applicants face very different depths of review.

### Q: What is the Monetary Control Act of 1980 and why does it matter for account access?

The Monetary Control Act of 1980 is Title I of the Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act, enacted on March 31, 1980. Before that statute, Federal Reserve services were tied closely to membership in the Federal Reserve System and were provided without explicit per item charges, while nonmember institutions often reached the system indirectly through correspondents. The 1980 act changed the bargain in two directions. It required the Federal Reserve to publish a fee schedule and price its services, and it opened those services to all depository institutions, member and nonmember alike, on the same priced terms. It also extended reserve requirements beyond member banks. Because priced access replaced membership as the organizing principle, later disputes over master accounts have argued from the act's equal access design.

### Q: What services does a master account give a bank access to?

A master account is the settlement foundation for the priced services the Federal Reserve offers to depository institutions. Through it a holder uses the Fedwire Funds Service for large value transfers, the Fedwire Securities Service for transfers of securities, FedACH for automated clearing house payments, and check services for paper and image based clearing. The account also supports currency and coin services, through which institutions order and deposit cash with their Reserve Bank, and FedNow instant payments, a service launched in 2023. Each service has its own operating rules, fees, and risk controls, but settlement returns to the same place. Debits and credits generated by the services post to the master account, so the balance in that account is the final record of the institution's position with its Reserve Bank.

### Q: What is Fedwire and how does it settle payments?

Fedwire is the Federal Reserve's real time gross settlement system for large value payments, operated through the Fedwire Funds Service. Gross settlement means each payment settles individually rather than being netted against other payments at the end of a processing cycle. When a sending institution instructs a transfer, its Reserve Bank checks the instruction, debits the sender's master account, and credits the master account of the receiving institution. Under Regulation J and Article 4A, the transfer becomes final and irrevocable when the receiving Reserve Bank credits the receiving institution's master account. Because settlement occurs in central bank money on the Federal Reserve's own books, the receiving bank does not need to wait for a later clearing step or rely on the sending bank's promise after credit is given.

### Q: What is a correspondent bank and how does it substitute for a master account?

A correspondent bank is an institution that holds a master account and provides payment and settlement services to another institution, called a respondent, that either lacks its own account or chooses not to use one for every purpose. The respondent keeps a balance with the correspondent, and the correspondent submits payments through its own master account on the respondent's behalf. Before 1980 this arrangement was the normal route into the Federal Reserve system for nonmember institutions. The substitute carries costs and limits. The respondent depends on the correspondent's willingness to serve it, pays for the service, faces the correspondent's credit and operational risk, and settles in the correspondent's money rather than directly in central bank money. For a novel charter institution denied its own account, correspondent access is therefore a workable route, but not an identical one.

### Q: What are the three tiers in the Federal Reserve account access guidelines?

The Board's account access guidelines, proposed in 2021 and adopted in final form in August 2022 at 87 Federal Register 51099, sort applicants into three tiers based on insurance and supervision. Tier 1 covers federally insured institutions with federal supervision and receives streamlined review. Tier 2 covers institutions that are federally insured or subject to federal prudential supervision and receives intermediate review. Tier 3 covers institutions that are neither federally insured nor federally supervised and receives the strictest review. The tier does not decide the outcome by itself. It sets how deeply the Reserve Bank investigates matters such as legal authority, risk to the Reserve Bank, risk to the payment system, financial stability, monetary policy implementation, and illicit finance controls before granting or denying access.

### Q: Why did Custodia Bank sue the Federal Reserve?

Custodia Bank began under the name Avanti and received a Wyoming charter in 2020 as a special purpose depository institution under a Wyoming statute enacted in 2019. It was not federally insured. In 2020 Custodia applied to the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City for a master account, seeking direct settlement access for a business model built around digital asset customers and payment services. The Kansas City Fed denied the application on January 27, 2023. Custodia sued because it argued that federal law entitled a legally eligible depository institution to receive an account and that prolonged delay followed by denial exceeded the Federal Reserve's lawful discretion. The Federal Reserve answered that eligibility permits consideration without creating an entitlement, and that Custodia's risk profile justified denial. That disagreement over entitlement versus discretion became the center of the litigation.

### Q: What did the Tenth Circuit decide in the Custodia case?

The United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit affirmed judgment for the Federal Reserve on October 31, 2025, by a divided court. The decision upheld the earlier 2024 ruling of the United States District Court for the District of Wyoming, which had held that the governing statutes do not entitle an eligible institution to a master account. In practical terms, the Tenth Circuit accepted that a Reserve Bank may exercise discretion in evaluating an application, even when the applicant satisfies the basic legal definition of an eligible depository institution. The Supreme Court then let that judgment stand by declining to hear the case on November 16, 2026. Because a refusal to grant review endorses nothing and establishes no national rule, the Tenth Circuit decision governs the dispute it decided and leaves the underlying statutory question open for other circuits.

### Q: Can the Federal Reserve deny a master account to a legally eligible bank?

Yes, according to the outcome of the Custodia litigation. Legal eligibility establishes that an institution belongs to a class allowed to apply, but the District Court for the District of Wyoming in 2024 and the Tenth Circuit on October 31, 2025, both rejected the argument that eligibility creates an automatic entitlement to an account. Reserve Banks therefore retain discretion to weigh the applicant's business model, supervision, capital, risk management, and compliance record under the Board guidelines adopted in August 2022. That discretion is not described by the courts as unlimited whim. A denial rests on an administrative record and on reasons tied to legal authority and risk, and an applicant can challenge agency action in court. The Custodia decisions settle, within that circuit, that a court will not convert eligibility alone into a judicial order compelling an account.

### Q: What is the difference between a master account and discount window access?

A master account and discount window borrowing are separate relationships with a Federal Reserve Bank, even though the same institution may have both. The master account holds reserve balances and provides the settlement point for payments and priced Federal Reserve services. Discount window access is a lending relationship under which an eligible institution borrows against pledged collateral, subject to its own eligibility rules, collateral valuation, and credit judgment by the Reserve Bank. Holding an account does not by itself confer borrowing privileges, and approval for borrowing does not follow automatically from approval for settlement services. The distinction matters because public debate sometimes treats an account as a rescue facility. It is not one. An account lets an institution finish payments in central bank money, while discount window credit supplies temporary funds under separate terms designed for liquidity support.

### Q: Why did the Federal Reserve begin paying interest on reserve balances?

Payment of interest on reserve balances began because the Federal Reserve needed a floor under short term interest rates once reserves became abundant. Congress granted the authority in the Financial Services Regulatory Relief Act of 2006 but set a deferred start, and the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act of 2008 moved the start forward to October 2008, in the middle of the financial crisis. With banks holding large balances at the Reserve Banks, a rate paid on those balances gives every holder a reason to refuse overnight loans priced below it, so the policy rate transmits through money markets instead of leaking away. The Board of Governors sets the rate, and earnings on a master account balance therefore track a monetary policy instrument rather than a deposit rate negotiated with a customer.

### Q: What is a daylight overdraft at the Federal Reserve?

A daylight overdraft occurs when an institution's master account balance falls below zero during the business day because payments sent exceed funds received at that moment, even if the account returns to a positive balance by the close. Payment timing makes such intraday credit necessary, because large payments cannot wait for every incoming transfer to arrive first. The Federal Reserve permits daylight overdrafts under its Payment System Risk Policy, subject to a net debit cap linked to the institution's capital. Pricing reinforces the limit. Fees apply to uncollateralized overdrafts, while collateralized overdrafts receive discounts, giving institutions an incentive to pledge collateral and control intraday exposure. The policy balances two goals. It keeps payments flowing smoothly during the day, and it protects the Reserve Bank from carrying unsecured intraday credit for an institution that might fail before the day ends.

### Q: What happens to a master account when a bank fails?

When an account holder fails, the master account does not become an ordinary asset that depositors divide among themselves, and its balance is not a guarantee that customers will be repaid in full. Resolution follows the institution's charter, its primary regulator, and, for insured institutions, the deposit insurance framework. The Reserve Bank protects its own position first through the tools built into the relationship, including collateral pledged for intraday or discount window credit and the finality of payments already settled through Fedwire. Payments completed before failure remain final under Regulation J and Article 4A, which prevents a failure from unwinding the payment chain backward. Capital and other loss absorbing resources stand ahead of account balances in absorbing losses, a point explained further in discussions of bank capital instruments. The account may be closed, transferred, or operated under a receiver or successor institution according to the resolution plan and the Reserve Bank's instructions.

### Q: What is a narrow bank and why did the Federal Reserve resist the model?

A narrow bank is an institution designed to hold deposits and invest the proceeds almost entirely in safe, liquid assets, principally reserve balances at the central bank, rather than making loans. TNB USA Inc., known as The Narrow Bank, sought a master account at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York from 2018 so it could pass interest on reserve balances through to large depositors. The New York Fed did not act on the application, and a federal district court dismissed the bank's suit in 2020 on the ground that the dispute was not ripe. Federal Reserve resistance centered on monetary policy and financial stability concerns. A pass through vehicle offering the policy rate to large depositors could draw funds rapidly from commercial banks during stress, alter the transmission of interest rate policy, and expand central bank intermediation without the lending and supervision framework that accompanies ordinary banking.

### Q: How do central banks in other countries decide who gets a settlement account?

Other central banks draw the access line by statute, institutional category, supervision, and payment system risk, but the exact boundary differs by country. The Bank of England provides the clearest contrast named in Federal Reserve debates. In 2017 the Bank of England opened direct settlement accounts in its real time gross settlement system to non bank payment service providers, becoming the first major central bank to take that step. The reform treated supervised payment firms as direct participants rather than forcing every payment through a commercial bank sponsor. Other systems have kept access narrower, limiting direct accounts mainly to banks and selected financial market infrastructures. The comparison shows that direct access is a policy choice about competition, risk, and the role of commercial banks as gatekeepers, not a technical necessity dictated by settlement technology alone. Each central bank decides which risks it will hold directly and which it will leave inside correspondent relationships.

### Q: What is a special purpose depository institution?

A special purpose depository institution is a bank charter created by Wyoming statute in 2019 for institutions that take deposits and provide payment and custody services without making loans in the ordinary way. The design answers a specific problem: a firm holding customer funds and moving them through the payment system needs a bank charter to hold those funds safely, yet a lending charter brings credit risk the business model does not want. The Wyoming design therefore pairs the deposit and payment powers with a requirement to hold assets in high quality liquid form, so the institution resembles a vault with payment rails rather than a lender. Such an institution is chartered and examined by the state and carries no federal deposit insurance. Custodia Bank, chartered by Wyoming in 2020, is the instance that carried the model into the master account litigation.

### Q: What risks does the Federal Reserve weigh before granting an account?

The Board guidelines adopted in August 2022 organize the inquiry around six principles. The Reserve Bank examines the applicant's legal authority to hold the account, risk to the Federal Reserve Bank, risk to the payment system, risk to the stability of the financial system, effect on the implementation of monetary policy, and the applicant's record on illicit finance. In practice those principles reach capital adequacy, liquidity, governance, operational resilience, cybersecurity, anti money laundering controls, customer concentration, and the quality of supervision behind the charter. Scholar Julie Andersen Hill has made the access framework a subject of sustained legal analysis. A weakness in one area does not automatically answer the application, because reviewers weigh the whole profile. A Tier 3 applicant, neither federally insured nor federally supervised, faces the strictest review precisely because fewer external safeguards stand between its risks and the Reserve Bank.

### Q: How long does a master account application take to decide?

The Board guidelines adopted in August 2022 set no fixed deadline for deciding a master account application, and no standard timetable should be treated as promised. Review time depends on the applicant's tier, the completeness of its filings, the novelty of its charter and business model, and the questions raised by supervision, risk management, and illicit finance controls. A Tier 1 institution with federal insurance and supervision receives streamlined review, while a Tier 3 institution faces the strictest examination and a longer record building process. Observed timelines have ranged from months to years. Custodia Bank applied to the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City in 2020 and received a denial on January 27, 2023, a span of more than two years. That history illustrates the upper range for a contested novel charter application, but it does not create a general rule for ordinary applicants.
