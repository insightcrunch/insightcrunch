Ecosocialism and green capitalism name the two most fully developed answers to a single question, and the question should be stated before either answer is described. Can economic output continue to grow while the material and energy an economy draws from the environment, and the waste it returns there, fall in absolute terms? If the answer is yes, then ecological stabilisation is a problem of prices, rules, and technology inside existing property relations. If the answer is no, stabilisation requires changing who owns production and who decides what gets made. The disagreement is therefore partly empirical. It turns on a body of evidence that both sides cite selectively, and the name given to the contested process is decoupling.

![Ecosocialism vs Green Capitalism, the four questions grid and the decoupling question - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

That framing matters because public argument about these positions rarely reaches the evidence. Advocates trade charges instead. One side is accused of wanting to abolish prosperity, the other of painting an unchanged economy green. Neither charge engages the actual claims. Ecosocialists argue that production organised for exchange, under a compulsion to accumulate, generates an expansionary pressure that regulation can blunt but never remove. Green capitalists, drawing on ecological modernisation theory, argue that the institutions of developed economies have repeatedly absorbed environmental goals through regulation and innovation, and that nothing in the record shows a barrier that better rules and cheaper technology cannot cross. Stated that way, the dispute has parts that evidence can settle and parts it cannot, and separating those parts is the work of the pages that follow.

A comparison article owes its readers two things that advocacy does not. The first is the strongest available version of each position, attributed to the theorists who actually made it, rather than a summary assembled from opponents. The second is a plain statement of where a side's familiar claim fails its own evidence. This article gives the ecological modernisation case at full strength, with its record of improvement accompanying rising income and its account of falling technology costs. It also states a correction that cuts against the Marxist side. The strong ecosocialist claim, that absolute decoupling is impossible in principle, is not supported by the empirical literature. A weaker claim about rate and generality is much better supported. Keeping those two claims apart, rather than letting them travel as one, is the distinction this comparison exists to make, and it recommends neither programme.

## The question underneath the quarrel

Begin with the physical vocabulary, since confusion between two nouns does most of the damage in popular versions of this debate. Output is the monetary value an economy produces, recorded in national accounts and capable of rising because prices change, because services expand, or because goods embody more value in less matter. Throughput is the physical flow underneath that value, the timber, ore, fuel, crops, and water drawn in at one end and the emissions, effluent, and discarded goods leaving at the other. An economy can in principle raise output while lowering throughput, by making things lighter, longer lived, repaired rather than replaced, or by shifting spending toward activities with small material footprints. Whether it can do so fast enough, across enough of its activity, is the whole argument.

### How is throughput different from output?

Throughput counts physical matter and energy moving through an economy, from extraction to disposal, measured in tonnes and joules. Output counts monetary value, which can rise through prices, services, durability, or design while physical flow falls. The two variables answer different questions, and confusing them converts a physical dispute into an accounting illusion on either side.

Growth, in the ordinary usage both programmes inherit, refers to output. When green capitalists say economies can keep growing while becoming cleaner, the claim concerns value rising as physical flow falls. When ecosocialists say growth must end, the claim concerns the physical flow and the accumulation dynamic said to drive it, not necessarily the services, care, learning, or security that people experience as living standards. A large share of the public quarrel consists of one side defending output and the other attacking throughput while both use the word growth. The [guide to ecological Marxism](/2017/07/01/ecological-marxism-complete-guide/) maps the wider field in which this vocabulary developed, and readers who want the conceptual groundwork will find the terms grounded there.

The stakes of the vocabulary become clear once rates enter. Stabilisation, as the physical sciences describe it, is a condition on flows: certain throughputs must fall below thresholds set by the systems receiving them, and must do so within periods those systems dictate. Output carries no such threshold. Nothing in physics limits how much value an economy records. Everything in the dispute therefore hangs on the relationship between the two series over time. If value can rise indefinitely while the physical flow falls at the required pace, the green capitalist programme is coherent and the remaining argument is about instruments. If value growth drags the physical flow with it beyond a point, the ecosocialist diagnosis gains its force. Between those poles sits the evidence, and the evidence is more specific, and more awkward for both camps, than campaigning summaries suggest.

Notice also what the question is not. It is not whether markets or plans are morally preferable, a matter of values on which evidence bears only indirectly. It is not whether capitalism has damaged environments, which no serious participant denies, nor whether planned economies damaged them, which the record examined later in this article makes plain. It is not, finally, a question about degrowth as a programme in its own right. The throughput position taken for its own sake is treated in the companion discussion of [degrowth and Marxism](/2017/09/01/degrowth-and-marxism/), and this comparison touches that proposal only where the two programmes here under review define themselves against it.

## What ecosocialism proposes

Ecosocialism, as a named position, was assembled in the opening decades of the twenty first century from older materials: Marx's remarks on the metabolism between society and nature, the ecological writings recovered by later scholarship, and the accumulated record of environmental damage under both market and planned systems. Its canonical definition can be stated compactly. Ecological stabilisation requires social ownership of the principal means of production and democratic planning of what is produced, because the compulsion to accumulate, which defines capital as a social relation, cannot be regulated away. Production for exchange subordinates every other criterion to the expansion of value. A firm that declines to grow loses ground to rivals that accept the discipline, and a state that restrains its firms too firmly watches investment migrate elsewhere. On this account the expansionary pressure is not a policy error or a failure of conscience. It is structural, and structures are answered by changing them.

Michael Löwy gave the position one of its clearest programmatic statements in Ecosocialism: A Radical Alternative to Capitalist Catastrophe, published in 2015, where the argument runs from the accumulation dynamic to a demand for democratic planning over the major decisions of production and investment. Löwy's version insists that the alternative is not bureaucratic command. The plan, in his formulation, is made by the associated producers and communities themselves, deciding collectively what to produce, what to retire, and what needs take priority when claims conflict. Joel Kovel, in The Enemy of Nature, first published in 2002, pressed a parallel case from a different direction, arguing that capital as a system must expand or die, and that ecological integrity requires production reorganised around use and need rather than valorisation. Ian Angus, in Facing the Anthropocene, published in 2016, carried the argument into the earth system literature, insisting that the timescale set by physical findings leaves no room for gradual market adjustment. Daniel Tanuro's writings on the impossibility of green capitalism develop the thermodynamic and productivist strands of the same case.

Several distinct propositions sit inside that shared programme, and the comparison later in this article grades them separately because their evidential standing differs sharply. One proposition is diagnostic: accumulation generates growth in throughput as a systemic tendency. A second is institutional: regulation inside market economies is subject to capture by the interests it governs and to erosion by competition between jurisdictions. A third is programmatic: only social ownership removes the tendency at its root, because decisions about the scale and composition of production can then be taken directly, as political choices, rather than emerging as the unintended sum of private accumulation decisions. A fourth, often smuggled in alongside the others, is predictive: absolute decoupling of throughput from output is impossible under capitalism in principle. The first three are arguments, open to evidence and counter argument of several kinds. The fourth is a claim about what can happen, and it is the one the empirical literature examines most directly.

The ecosocialist treatment of living standards deserves care, because the most common misreading of the position begins here. Ecosocialists do not, in their programmatic texts, call for generalised austerity. The argument distinguishes between throughput and provision. Output devoted to advertising driven replacement cycles, built in obsolescence, and positional consumption could contract sharply, on their account, while housing, nutrition, health care, education, transport, and leisure improved, because resources released from wasteful production become available for services people actually need. Whether that reallocation would in practice deliver the promised provision is a fair and difficult question, pressed hard in the objections considered later. But the position to be answered is the one its theorists state, and that position promises a different composition of production, not a poorer population. Löwy is explicit that the transformation aims at a society organised around genuine human needs, with expanded free time among its central goods.

Planning, in this literature, is likewise more specific than the word suggests to readers formed by twentieth century command economies. The ecosocialist proposal assigns the largest decisions, the overall scale of throughput, the retirement of destructive branches, the direction of investment, to democratic deliberation, while leaving the detail of production to workplaces and communities. Markets for many consumer goods are not always abolished in these sketches; what is abolished is the sovereignty of accumulation over the aggregate outcome. Critics reply that the sketch has never been operated anywhere, that the information and incentive problems of planning return at every scale, and that the environmental record of states which did plan production comprehensively is the worst evidence the position carries. That record gets its own section in this article, and ecosocialists themselves treat it as a problem to be answered rather than a detail to be waved away. Kovel's distinction between the ecosocialist proposal and the productivism of the historical planned economies is drawn precisely to meet it.

One further element completes the position. Ecosocialists hold that the decisions about what is produced are the decisions that matter ecologically, and that market systems never pose those decisions as questions at all. A market registers demand backed by money; it does not ask whether a branch of production should exist, whether a need is genuine, or whether a throughput budget permits an expansion somewhere else. Planning, whatever its defects, at least makes the composition of output an object of collective choice. This is why the third row of the four questions grid, set out later, may be the deepest of the four: the dispute is not only about whether throughput can fall while output rises, but about who, if anyone, is entitled to decide the shape of an economy, and by what procedure. Green capitalism has an answer to that question too, an answer about dispersed knowledge and revealed preference, and it is given in full in the sections that follow.

## What green capitalism proposes

Green capitalism names the family of positions holding that ecological stabilisation can be achieved within existing property relations, through market mechanisms corrected and steered by public rules. Its theoretical form is ecological modernisation, a body of theory developed from the nineteen eighties onward, principally in northern European sociology, which holds that environmental improvement and economic development are compatible and that the institutions of modern economies can be reformed to deliver both at once. Where an older environmentalism treated industry and ecology as antagonists, ecological modernisation treats environmental criteria as a further stage of modernisation itself, comparable to the incorporation of labour standards or public health into the normal operation of industrial societies during earlier periods. The claim is historical as much as theoretical: it points to domains where exactly that incorporation occurred.

Arthur Mol and Gert Spaargaren, in their programmatic statement of ecological modernisation theory published in 2000, set out the core proposition that ecological considerations can be built into the design of production and consumption as an independent sphere of social organisation, no longer subordinated to economic criteria alone. Joseph Huber's earlier work supplied much of the theory's industrial sociology, emphasising technological innovation and the super industrialisation of production processes as the mechanism by which material intensity falls. Martin Jänicke contributed the political science of the programme, studying how states develop environmental policy capacity and how pioneer regulation in one jurisdiction diffuses through markets to others, as firms standardise on the strictest rule they face. The theory's instruments follow from its diagnosis. If environmental damage is a design failure and an information failure rather than an iron law of accumulation, then carbon pricing, performance standards, subsidy for research and deployment, and disclosure requirements can correct it, and the innovative capacity of competing firms becomes an environmental asset rather than the problem itself.

### What does ecological modernisation claim institutions can do?

Ecological modernisation claims that environmental goals can be institutionalised inside market economies the way labour standards and public health rules were absorbed earlier. Regulation sets binding criteria, pricing makes damage visible in costs, and firms then compete on meeting the criteria cheaply, so improvement becomes a routine output of the system rather than an exception wrested from it.

The innovation argument received its most influential statement from Michael Porter and Claas van der Linde, whose 1995 article in the Journal of Economic Perspectives argued that properly designed environmental regulation can trigger innovation that partly or wholly offsets the cost of compliance, because regulation forces firms to notice inefficiencies, material waste, and product weaknesses that routine competition had left unexamined. The claim, known in the literature as the Porter hypothesis, is narrower than its popular versions. Its strong form, that regulation generally raises competitiveness, remains contested among economists. Its weak form, that well designed rules stimulate innovation in the regulated direction, commands wide support, and it is the weak form that the green capitalist programme actually needs. On that basis the programme predicts a specific pattern in the evidence: regulated pollutants and material intensities should fall as income rises, not despite growth but partly through mechanisms growth finances.

The popular synthesis arrived with Natural Capitalism, published in 1999 by Paul Hawken, Amory Lovins, and L. Hunter Lovins, which assembled the engineering evidence for radical resource productivity, whole system design, and service based business models into a case that profitable environmental improvement was available at large scale inside existing firms. Whatever critics made of its optimism, the book fixed the green capitalist proposition in its recognisable shape: the barrier to stabilisation is not the market form itself but the price signals, rules, and engineering habits that govern it, and each of those is corrigible by policy and competition. A later generation of green growth writing, produced by international economic institutions as well as academics, generalised the claim into a development strategy, arguing that environmental policy properly sequenced raises rather than lowers long run prosperity. The Marxist reader should register the structure of this case before its weaknesses, because its weaknesses are specific, and a critique that misses the structure will miss them too.

### Does green capitalism mean leaving markets alone?

No. Green capitalism is a programme of active steering, not laissez faire. It prescribes carbon pricing, binding performance standards, state funding for research and deployment, and mandatory disclosure, precisely because unpriced damage is treated as a failure the state must correct. Its disagreement with ecosocialism concerns ownership and the location of decisions, not whether public authority should intervene in markets.

Two clarifications protect the comparison from caricature in the opposite direction. Green capitalism does not claim that markets unaided will stabilise the environment; every serious version assigns the state an indispensable role as rule setter, price maker, and funder of early stage technology. Nor does it claim that every environmental problem yields to pricing. Its theorists treat some damages, particularly those involving irreversibility or threshold effects, as matters for prohibition and standard setting rather than tradeable permission. The programme's wager is compositional. An economy's output can shift, under corrected signals, toward activities carrying small throughputs, while the throughput intensive remainder shrinks under price and regulatory pressure, until total physical flow falls even as total value rises. That is a claim about the flexibility of the output structure, and it connects directly to the decoupling evidence, because sectoral composition change is one of the main mechanisms by which measured decoupling has in fact been achieved.

The corresponding ecosocialist rejoinder is also compositional, and it deserves statement here rather than later, so that the empirical sections can be read as an arbitration between two defined positions. If the composition of output shifts by exporting throughput intensive production abroad while importing the goods, measured domestic improvement conceals an unchanged global flow. If efficiency gains lower the cost of a service and demand expands to consume the saving, the rebound consumes the gain. If the sectors that remain, agriculture, construction, heavy industry, freight, resist substitution because their throughputs are intrinsic to their physical function, then composition change exhausts its easy gains and the aggregate curve flattens. Each rejoinder is testable in principle, and the literature has tested all three, with results that distribute credit unevenly between the programmes. That distribution, not a verdict on capitalism in the abstract, is what the next sections assemble.

## Decoupling, the crux of the comparison

Decoupling names the separation of output from throughput over time, and the literature divides it into two claims whose difference the whole dispute turns on. Relative decoupling means throughput rising more slowly than output, so that each unit of value carries less matter and energy than the unit before it, while the total flow still grows. Absolute decoupling means throughput falling while output rises, so the economy becomes smaller in physical terms as it becomes larger in monetary ones. The first is a claim about intensity. The second is a claim about totals, and totals are what receiving systems register. A river does not respond to the emissions intensity of the industry upstream; it responds to the load. The physical sciences' stabilisation conditions are stated in totals, which is why the argument between the programmes is an argument about the second claim wearing the vocabulary of the first in most popular retellings.

### What does relative decoupling establish, and what does it leave open?

Relative decoupling establishes that output can grow faster than throughput, so material intensity per unit of value falls. It leaves open everything that matters for stabilisation, because totals may still rise while intensity falls. A literature full of relative results therefore proves efficiency gains without proving that any physical threshold will be met in time.

The empirical literature, reviewed as a body, supports a layered verdict, and the layers must be kept separate or the comparison collapses into slogan exchange. Relative decoupling of major material and emission flows from output is well documented across many economies and many decades; it is among the most robust findings in environmental economics, and neither programme disputes it, since ecosocialism's case never rested on the claim that intensity cannot fall. Absolute decoupling has also occurred. The empirical literature records a number of wealthy economies in which environmental throughput fell while output grew over sustained periods, and the finding survives the harder accounting test described in the next section, in which goods produced abroad and consumed domestically are counted against the consuming economy rather than the producing one. That is the finding the strong Marxist claim cannot accommodate, and this article states the correction plainly in the section after next.

### Is absolute decoupling possible?

it has occurred. Several wealthy economies have reduced environmental throughput while output grew, including on measures accounting for imported goods. Whether it has occurred fast enough, and whether it generalises across sectors and income levels, is the genuine dispute, rather than whether it is possible at all.

What remains genuinely open, and where the ecosocialist side stands on much firmer ground, are two further questions that the occurrence of absolute decoupling does not settle. The first is rate. Stabilisation conditions are time bound; a flow falling too slowly arrives at the threshold after the threshold has done its work, and the literature's assessments of observed rates against required rates are considerably less comfortable for the green capitalist programme than its assessments of direction. The second is generality. Observed absolute decoupling is concentrated in wealthy economies, in particular sectors, and in particular flows, and it is an open empirical question whether the pattern extends to economies at other income levels building their basic infrastructure, and to the sectors whose throughputs have so far proved stubborn. The [analysis of the climate crisis](/2017/09/15/marxist-analysis-climate-crisis/) in this series sets out the physical side of those rate questions, and readers should hold its framing in mind throughout what follows here: occurrence, rate, and generality are three propositions, not one, and the evidence grades them differently.

A systematic review of the decoupling evidence by Helmut Haberl and colleagues, published in Environmental Research Letters in 2020, illustrates the layered structure precisely because its authors took care to separate the claims. Surveying the literature across resources and emissions, the review found robust evidence of relative decoupling, evidence of absolute decoupling for some flows in some economies, and little evidence that observed absolute decoupling had proceeded at rates sufficient for the stabilisation conditions the physical literature describes, or that it generalised across all material categories at once. That is a finding neither camp's pamphlets quote whole. Green capitalist advocacy cites the occurrence results and stops. Ecosocialist advocacy cites the rate results and stops. The review itself supports the occurrence claim against the impossibility thesis and the rate claim against complacency, which is why this article treats it as the fairest single summary of where the empirical arbitration stands.

The distinction between the claims also disciplines what counts as a fair test going forward. A single economy achieving absolute decoupling in a single flow for a single decade establishes possibility for that flow; it establishes nothing about rates across the whole throughput basket. Conversely, a failure to observe sufficient rates in economies that never adopted the full green capitalist instrument set is weaker evidence against that programme than ecosocialist rhetoric suggests, since the programme's own theorists can reply that their prescription was never actually filled. Symmetrically, the ecosocialist programme has never been operated in its proposed form anywhere, so its performance is likewise untested rather than refuted. Both programmes therefore argue partly from a record and partly from a theory of why the record will extend or fail to extend. The four questions grid, later in this article, exists to keep those two kinds of argument visibly apart.

## The accounting basis decides the result

Every decoupling claim rests on a measurement decision that most summaries never mention, and the decision can reverse the verdict. Production based accounting assigns throughput to the territory where it physically occurs: the emissions of a factory count against the economy hosting the factory, and the material extracted from a mine counts where the mine operates. Consumption based accounting instead charges that throughput to the economy whose final demand caused it: the factory's emissions count against the consumers of its products, wherever they live, and the mine's extraction follows the goods made from its ore through every border they cross. For economies that have shifted their industrial composition toward services while importing manufactured goods, the two ledgers tell different stories. The production ledger shows improvement. The consumption ledger shows how much of that improvement was purchased by relocating the throughput rather than reducing it.

### Why does the accounting basis change the answer?

Production based accounting counts throughput where it physically happens, so an economy that imports its manufactured goods exports its measured burden. Consumption based accounting follows final demand across borders and counts imported goods against the consumer. The same economy can therefore show falling throughput on one ledger and a flat or rising profile on the other.

The research programme associated with Thomas Wiedmann and colleagues, whose material footprint study of nations appeared in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 2015, built the consumption based measure for materials on a global scale, tracing the raw material equivalents embedded in traded goods back to the economies whose consumption they ultimately served. Its central methodological lesson generalises beyond materials. Territorial improvement is a real phenomenon and also an incomplete one, and any decoupling claim that does not state its ledger has not finished stating itself. This is the sixth instance in this series of what might be called the measurement dominance pattern: the choice of accounting convention determines the substantive conclusion, exactly as the choice of valuation convention determines measured profit rates in the literature on the tendency of the rate of profit to fall. In both literatures, partisans select the convention that favours them and present its output as the fact itself. The discipline this article applies is simple to state and laborious to practise: every finding reported here carries its ledger with it.

With that discipline in place, the consumption based literature delivers the result that matters most for the comparison, and it is a result in two parts. The first part vindicates the ecosocialist suspicion in its weaker form: for many economies, consumption based profiles improve more slowly than production based ones, and for some flows the apparent decoupling of the territorial ledger disappears entirely when imports are counted, which means a portion of celebrated green performance is displacement. The second part limits that vindication. The empirical literature also records economies whose consumption based throughput fell in absolute terms over sustained periods while output grew. Displacement, in other words, explains part of the observed decoupling and not all of it. The strong claim that apparent decoupling is nothing but offshoring fails on the consumption based evidence itself, which is the very evidence assembled to test it. A critic who demands the harder ledger must accept the harder ledger's verdict when it arrives, and the verdict is mixed in a specific way: occurrence confirmed, magnitude reduced, rate and generality unresolved.

There is a further measurement subtlety that both programmes exploit when it suits them. Throughput is not one variable but a basket: greenhouse gas emissions, material extraction, land use, water withdrawal, nitrogen flows, and waste generation behave differently, respond to different instruments, and decouple at different rates. Emissions of some regulated pollutants have fallen absolutely in many economies under standard setting regimes, a result the ecological modernisation literature rightly emphasises. Aggregate material footprint has proved far more stubborn, a result the ecosocialist literature rightly emphasises. An article, a speech, or a manifesto that announces decoupling without naming the flow has chosen its conclusion in advance by choosing its basket. The honest unit of analysis is the individual flow, tracked on both ledgers, over a period long enough to separate trend from cycle. Assembled at that resolution, the evidence supports neither triumph nor dismissal, and the sections that follow grade the programmes question by question rather than in the round.

## The impossibility and rate distinction

The namable claim of this article can now be stated in its exact form. The impossibility and rate distinction separates two propositions that ecosocialist rhetoric routinely fuses into one. The impossibility claim holds that absolute decoupling cannot occur under capitalism, because accumulation makes throughput growth a condition of the system's survival. The rate claim holds that absolute decoupling, where it has occurred, has not occurred fast enough, widely enough, or across enough flows to meet stabilisation conditions, and shows no settled tendency to do so. These propositions have different evidence, different defences, and different fates. The first is contradicted by the recorded occurrence of absolute decoupling, including on consumption based measures. The second is supported by the same literature's findings on pace, sectoral concentration, and the concentration of observed success in wealthy economies. The ecosocialist case is weakened by the first claim and strengthened by the second, and an ecosocialism that stated only the second would be harder to refute than the one that insists on both.

Why does the impossibility claim persist, given the evidence against it? One reason is theoretical satisfaction. A claim of impossibility follows neatly from a model in which accumulation compels expansion, and claims that follow neatly from a valued theory acquire protection from the theory's defenders. A second reason is polemical convenience: impossibility ends arguments, while insufficiency begins them, and movements prefer endings. A third is a genuine ambiguity in the evidence's presentation, since production based results were for years reported without their ledgers named, allowing each side to suspect the other's numbers wholesale rather than grading them flow by flow. Whatever the reasons, the cost to the ecosocialist position is real and measurable in argumentative terms. Every debate that opens on impossibility is a debate the ecosocialist loses on the record, and the loss stains the rate claim, which deserved a hearing on its own merits. Separating the claims is therefore not pedantry. It redistributes the burden of proof. On occurrence, the burden now sits with whoever denies the consumption based findings. On rate and generality, it sits with whoever asserts that observed trajectories suffice, or will generalise, without further institutional change.

The correction must be run in the other direction as well, or this article would commit the asymmetry it criticises. Green capitalist rhetoric fuses its own pair of claims. The occurrence claim, that absolute decoupling has happened under market institutions steered by regulation, is supported. The sufficiency claim, that the institutional package responsible will deliver required rates across the whole basket of flows and across economies at every income level, is an extrapolation the record does not yet underwrite, and its advocates rarely mark the seam where demonstration ends and projection begins. Ecological modernisation theory, read carefully, is more cautious than the green growth slogans built on it: Mol and Spaargaren describe an ongoing institutional transformation, not a completed one, and Jänicke's work on policy capacity emphasises how unevenly that capacity is distributed. The strongest version of the green capitalist case is therefore also a rate and generality claim, held as a projection from demonstrated mechanisms, and it should be debated as such. Both programmes, disciplined by the distinction, end up defending claims about the future behaviour of rates. That is a narrower and more honest dispute than the one the slogans describe.

### What does decoupling at a sufficient rate require?

A sufficient rate means absolute throughput falling fast enough, across the basket of flows that matter, to reach stabilisation thresholds within the periods physical systems allow. Occurrence in one flow over one decade does not establish it. Judging sufficiency requires comparing observed trajectories, on both accounting bases, against the trajectories those thresholds imply.

One more refinement completes the distinction, and it concerns what kind of statement the rate claim is. The impossibility claim was a claim about a system's nature, answerable by a single counter instance. The rate claim is a claim about trajectories under specified institutions, answerable only by sustained observation and by judgements about which institutional changes count as the programme itself and which count as departures from it. If a market economy adopts planning instruments extensive enough, at what point has the ecosocialist diagnosis been vindicated rather than the green capitalist prescription refuted? If a democratically planned economy retains markets and accumulation pressures in large domains, whose programme is being tested? These boundary questions are not evasions; they are where the comparison will actually be decided over the coming decades, and the grid in the next section is designed to expose them rather than conceal them. Readers who keep the impossibility and rate distinction in hand will notice that most published exchanges on this subject, from both traditions, spend their force on the settled claim and their rhetoric on the open one.

## The four questions grid

Comparisons of programmes decay into exchanges of examples unless the questions are fixed in advance and both sides are held to all of them. The four questions grid is this article's findable artifact, built to do that holding. Its rows are the four questions on which ecosocialism and green capitalism actually divide. Whether throughput can be parted from output altogether, fast enough and broadly enough to matter. Whether the state can regulate capital sufficiently against capture and competitive erosion. Who decides what is produced, and by what procedure. Whether technology is the primary lever of stabilisation or a subordinate instrument. Its columns give each position's answer, the evidence bearing on that answer, and the observation that would change it. A programme that cannot fill the final column has stopped being a claim about the world and become a loyalty, and the grid is deliberately unkind to both programmes on exactly that point.

| Question | Ecosocialism | Green capitalism | Evidence bearing on it | What would change the answer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Can throughput be decoupled from output absolutely, at sufficient rate and scope? | Only once accumulation no longer governs production; observed decoupling is partial, displaced, or too slow | Yes, under corrected prices, standards, and innovation pressure; occurrence is already recorded | Consumption based studies find absolute decoupling in some wealthy economies, at rates the review literature judges insufficient, concentrated in particular flows | Sustained consumption based decoupling at required rates across the whole flow basket, or its persistent failure under full instrument deployment |
| Can the state regulate capital sufficiently? | Regulation is eroded by capture and by competition between jurisdictions for investment | Regulatory capacity has repeatedly been built and has diffused across markets through standard setting | Historical absorption of labour and health standards; documented capture episodes; uneven policy capacity across states | Regulation surviving competitive pressure at stabilising stringency over decades, or systematic collapse of stringency under relocation threats |
| Who decides what is produced? | The associated producers and communities, through democratic planning of the major decisions | Dispersed buyers and firms, within rules the state sets; no central decision is taken or needed | No operating instance of the ecosocialist procedure; market systems decide composition without ever posing it as a question | A working democratic planning procedure at scale, or a demonstration that composition under markets converges on stabilising output |
| Is technology the primary lever? | Technology helps within limits set by ownership and scale; rebound and throughput growth dominate unaided | Technology, driven by pricing and standards, is the main engine of decoupling | Cost trajectories of low carbon technologies falling faster than official projections; rebound effects documented across many applications | Rebound effects shrinking toward zero under pricing, or rebound consuming gains even where prices bite |

Read the grid row by row and the comparison sharpens. On the first row, the programmes have converged more than their rhetoric admits: serious writing on both sides accepts occurrence and disputes rate and generality, which is why the impossibility and rate distinction matters before the grid is even consulted. On the second row, the dispute is genuinely institutional and only partly empirical, because the evidence contains both successful absorption of standards and documented capture, and the weighting of those episodes is where the argument lives. On the third row the division is deepest, because it concerns not what will happen but what kind of decision the composition of production is: a political question to be answered deliberately, or an emergent outcome that no one should pretend to author. On the fourth row, the evidence is surprisingly concrete, since cost trajectories and rebound measurements are among the best developed parts of the empirical literature, and both sides quote the half of it that suits them.

The grid also serves students directly. Examiners reward answers that can state what observation would settle a dispute, because that is the mark of a claim understood rather than recited. Each row's final column supplies that discipline for this topic, and the exam oriented sections near the end of this article return to it. Teachers, for their part, can set the grid as an exercise: take any published exchange between the programmes, assign every argument to a row, and note which rows the exchange never touches. In practice the untouched rows predict where the exchange's conclusion was loaded. A debate confined to the first row is a debate about evidence, and can be won on evidence. A debate confined to the third row is a debate about political authority, and should be conducted as one, openly, rather than disguised as a quarrel over measurements.

One caution governs the grid's use. Its cells compress positions that their holders state with qualifications, and compression is a cost this article accepts for the sake of comparability. Löwy's planning is democratic and decentralised in intention, not a Gosplan restored; Mol's modernisation is an institutional transformation still under way, not a claim that existing policy suffices unchanged. The cells describe the logical centre of each position, the answer its premises generate, and the full statements given in the earlier sections should be read as the controlling text where a cell seems to flatten them. Compression is also why the grid names evidence in general terms: the flow specific, ledger specific findings assembled in the previous sections are the substance the cells summarise, and a reader who wants the verdict on any cell should return to those findings rather than trusting the summary.

## Regulation and the growth imperative

The second row of the grid deserves a section of its own, because it is where the ecosocialist diagnosis does its most serious work and where the green capitalist reply is most often understated by its opponents. State the ecosocialist argument at full strength first. Firms operate under competitive compulsion: each must accumulate or be accumulated, expand or be displaced. A state that regulates its firms stringently raises their costs relative to firms elsewhere, and capital, being mobile, responds by relocating production, threatening relocation, or capturing the regulatory process itself through lobbying, revolving doors, and the structural dependence of governments on private investment for employment and revenue. Regulation therefore faces a ratchet problem. Its stringency is capped by the exit options of the regulated, and its enforcement decays as attention moves on. On this account the historical record of environmental law shows real gains within a ceiling, and the ceiling is set by the growth imperative itself. The question of how far a state can discipline capital is an old one in this tradition, and the general theory behind it is treated in the series discussion of [Marxist state theory](/2013/04/01/marxist-state-theory-today/), which this section applies rather than repeats.

### Why do ecosocialists expect regulation to erode?

Because the regulated hold exit options the regulator lacks. Firms can relocate production, threaten relocation to discipline legislatures, or capture the agencies meant to govern them, while treasuries depend on the investment of private firms for jobs and receipts. Ecosocialists read environmental law's history as gains achieved beneath a ceiling that this asymmetry imposes and periodically reasserts.

The green capitalist answer, at full strength, begins by denying that the historical record shows a ceiling where ecosocialists place it. Labour standards, workplace safety, public health rules, and the abolition of the most destructive industrial practices were all imposed on unwilling capital, all predicted to destroy competitiveness, and all absorbed; several became sources of advantage once diffusion standardised them, which is Jänicke's documented mechanism of pioneer regulation spreading through integrated markets. Exit, moreover, is not free: firms are embedded in workforces, infrastructures, legal systems, and consumer markets they cannot costlessly abandon, and the largest markets can set de facto global standards because producers standardise on the strictest rule rather than maintaining parallel product lines. Capture is real but variable, and its variation is itself evidence that institutional design matters, which is the green capitalist premise. On this reading the ceiling ecosocialists describe is a tendency, contested case by case, not a structural constant, and the empirical question is the balance of the episodes rather than the logic of the model.

Weighing those cases requires a distinction the polemics on both sides blur. Regulation of a flow at the point of production, where a substitute technology exists and monitoring is cheap, has repeatedly succeeded at stabilising stringency. Regulation of the aggregate scale of throughput, where no single firm's compliance solves the problem and every jurisdiction fears first mover disadvantage, is the hard case, and it is largely untested at the stringency stabilisation would require. The ecosocialist argument is strongest exactly there, at the aggregate level, and the green capitalist record is strongest at the level of the individual flow. This is the same pattern the decoupling evidence showed, recurrence of form across the grid's first two rows: occurrence and flow level success on one side, rate, scope, and aggregate level doubt on the other. A reader who sees the pattern will also see why the dispute survives every new study. Each study arrives at one level, and each side publicises the level it owns.

### What does the growth imperative predict about pricing alone?

Pricing alone, on the ecosocialist prediction, changes the composition of output at the margin while aggregate throughput keeps rising, because the revenue firms earn is reinvested under competitive compulsion and efficiency gains lower costs that expansion then consumes. The prediction is testable: priced flows should show intensity gains outpacing total reductions, which is broadly the documented pattern.

Carbon pricing sits at the centre of this argument because it is the green capitalist instrument par excellence and the ecosocialist target of choice. Its defenders describe a mechanism of genuine elegance: a single price signal propagates through millions of decisions no planner could enumerate, rewarding every actor who finds a cheaper way to abate and penalising every actor who does not, continuously and without further legislation. Its critics answer that elegance is purchased by abstraction from politics. The price level is set by the same legislatures subject to the pressures described earlier, and observed price levels in operating schemes have generally been set by what incumbents could bear rather than by what stabilisation requires. Defenders reply that this is an argument about political will, available against any instrument including planning, whose budgets would be set by the same politics. The exchange is instructive because it ends in symmetry: each side's instrument fails in the other's model for the same reason, the resistance of organised interests, and each side's programme therefore contains an implicit theory of how that resistance is overcome. Green capitalism trusts diffusion, innovation rents, and the falling cost of compliance. Ecosocialism trusts a change in who holds power over production. Neither trust is an empirical finding.

## Who decides what is produced

The third row of the grid is the one least amenable to evidence, and the article treats it as a dispute about decision procedures rather than disguising it as one about outcomes. Begin with the ecosocialist claim in its most precise form. In a market economy, the composition of production is decided by no one. It emerges from the interaction of purchasing power and expected profit, which means it is decided, in effect, by the existing distribution of income and by the accumulation requirements of firms. Needs without money behind them do not register. Goods are produced because they can be sold at a margin, not because a community judged them worth their throughput. For ecosocialists this is the ecological core of the matter, deeper than any question of regulation: a society that never asks what should be produced, in aggregate and in kind, cannot be surprised when the unasked question is answered by expansion. Democratic planning is proposed as the procedure that poses the question, with throughput budgets and investment priorities settled by deliberation among those who will live with the results.

### Can a price signal decide what an economy should make?

A price signal coordinates decisions; it does not deliberate about them. Pricing can make a damaging good expensive and a cleaner substitute cheap, and that is a genuine power. What pricing cannot do is pose the prior question of whether an entire line of production ought to exist at its present scale, because markets register demand rather than judge it.

The green capitalist answer to the third row is not a shrug but a rival theory of decision quality, and it should be heard in its strongest form. Dispersed decisions aggregate knowledge that no planning body can assemble: knowledge of local conditions, of substitutability, of what consumers will accept and what engineers can deliver, held in fragments by millions of actors and coordinated by prices into a composition of output that, whatever its defects, responds continuously to correction. The planning alternative must gather that knowledge through administrative channels that distort it, and must then enforce its conclusions against the preferences it overrode, which is why the historical planning systems generated queues, hoarding, falsified reporting, and vast informal economies alongside their official outputs. On this account the absence of a central decider is not the market's ecological defect but its epistemic virtue, and the proper place for collective judgement is the setting of rules and prices within which dispersed decisions occur. Ecosocialists answer that the knowledge argument proves too much: it would acquit any emergent outcome, including the throughput trajectory the physical literature condemns, and a procedure that cannot in principle conclude that a profitable branch should contract has built its conclusion into its method.

Notice that both arguments, pressed to their limit, become arguments about legitimacy as much as about information. The ecosocialist asks who is entitled to shape the material life of a society and answers: those who live in it, through procedures in which each counts for one. The green capitalist asks the same question and answers: no one is entitled to shape it wholesale, because the entitlement itself is the danger, and legitimate collective action stops at setting the terms on which free decisions proceed. Environmental outcomes enter both cases, but the cases would survive a change in the environmental evidence, which is the operational test of whether a dispute is empirical at all. A student who can identify which parts of this controversy would dissolve if the decoupling literature changed its verdict, and which parts would stand untouched, understands the controversy better than one who has memorised both programmes entire. The parallel with the older argument between reform and revolution inside the workers movement is instructive, and the series comparison of [Marxism and social democracy](/2019/11/15/marxism-vs-social-democracy/) traces the same procedural divide in its earlier form: whether a system's outcomes can be corrected by governing its rules, or only by changing who holds power within it.

The decision question has a temporal dimension both programmes handle awkwardly. Throughput decisions bind the future: infrastructure built for one composition of output lasts for decades and locks in the flows it was designed to carry, a phenomenon the literature calls carbon lock in or, more generally, infrastructural inertia. Markets discount the future at rates set by finance, which systematically undervalues consequences arriving beyond the horizon of returns. Planning, in principle, can take the long view, since its horizon is set politically. In practice, the historical planned systems discounted the future at least as brutally, driven by output targets and political cycles of their own, and the green capitalist can fairly ask why a democratic plan would prove more patient than an elected legislature operating market rules, given that both answer to publics with short horizons. Ecosocialists reply that democratic planning at least makes the horizon an explicit object of decision, arguable in the open, rather than an artefact of discount rates nobody chose. The exchange ends, as the pricing exchange ended, in a symmetry that honest comparison should report rather than resolve: both procedures contain a mechanism for myopia, and each side's case depends on its own mechanism being the more corrigible one.

## Technology, lever or alibi

The fourth row of the grid concerns the role each programme assigns to technical change, and here the empirical literature is unusually concrete, which makes the selectivity of both camps unusually visible. The green capitalist case rests on a pattern its theorists predicted and events confirmed: the cost of low carbon technologies fell, under competitive conditions of deployment and incremental innovation, faster than official projections and expert consensus expected. Successive forecasts by energy agencies and planning bodies underestimated the speed at which the relevant technologies cheapened as production scaled, learning accumulated, and supply chains matured. For the ecological modernisation argument this is the Porter mechanism operating at the level of whole industries: policy created demand, demand created scale, scale created cost reductions that no committee had scheduled, and the reductions then relaxed the political constraint on further policy. The episode is the strongest single exhibit in the green capitalist brief, because it shows the market mechanism doing precisely what the theory said it would do, in the sector where the ecosocialist model predicted incumbent resistance would prevail.

### How does each side treat technical change?

Green capitalism treats technology as the primary lever, expecting pricing and standards to steer innovation toward low throughput production faster than planners can schedule. Ecosocialism treats technology as a subordinate instrument whose gains are real but conditional, since ownership and scale decide whether efficiency is banked as reduced throughput or spent as expanded output.

The ecosocialist answer begins by conceding the cost trajectories and then changing the variable under discussion. Cheap low carbon supply addresses the carbon intensity of energy; it does not by itself address the scale of energy and material demand, and the literature on rebound effects documents the mechanism by which efficiency is converted into expansion. When a technology lowers the cost of a service, demand for the service grows, and part or all of the expected saving is consumed by the growth, a pattern observed across lighting, transport, heating, and computation since the nineteenth century observation, associated with William Stanley Jevons and his 1865 study of coal, that more efficient steam engines increased rather than decreased total coal consumption. Rebound rarely consumes the whole gain in the studied cases, and ecosocialist rhetoric that treats it as total overstates the literature as badly as green rhetoric that ignores it. The disciplined statement is that efficiency is necessary and insufficient: it lowers the throughput cost of a given composition of output while leaving the growth of that composition untouched, and the programmes divide over whether pricing and standards can govern the composition effect or only social decision can.

### Which sectors have resisted substitution?

The literature's stubborn cases share a structure: sectors where throughput is intrinsic to the physical service itself. Agriculture must move nitrogen and water at scale; construction embodies bulk materials in long lived stock; freight moves mass across distance; heavy industry transforms ores and fuels chemically. Substitution thins these flows at the margin without offering the clean replacement that electricity generation found.

Sectoral asymmetry explains a pattern in the decoupling record that aggregate arguments obscure. The flows that decoupled absolutely and rapidly are concentrated where a functional substitute existed at comparable cost: one way of generating a unit of electricity or delivering a unit of light could replace another while the service itself continued unchanged. The flows that resisted are those where the service is the throughput, where feeding people, housing them, and moving goods are material processes before they are economic ones. Green capitalists respond that the frontier moves: processes once thought intrinsic have been redesigned before, and the history of substitution is a history of confident intrinsic claims expiring. Ecosocialists respond that each expiry took decades the stabilisation timetable may not contain, returning the argument to rate. The reader will by now recognise the gravitational pull of this dispute: every concrete question, examined honestly, migrates toward the rate and generality claims, and every slogan migrates away from them toward impossibility or complacency. Resisting that second migration is most of what intellectual honesty consists in here.

There is also a distributional question inside the technology argument that both programmes raise against each other and neither resolves. Innovation under market direction follows purchasing power, so the technologies developed first are those serving consumers who can pay, and the throughput problems of poorer populations, clean cooking, water, basic mobility, wait at the back of a queue ordered by margin. Planning could in principle order the queue by need, and ecosocialists press the point. The planning record, however, ordered its queues by political priority and military requirement with results examined in the next section, and green capitalists press that point in return. The fair conclusion is uncomfortable for both: the direction of technical change is governed by whoever sets the incentives, both systems set them imperfectly, and the argument between the programmes on this row is finally an argument about which imperfection has the better correction mechanism attached to it. Markets correct through entry and competition when signals permit; plans correct through deliberation when institutions permit. The conditional clauses are doing the work, and they are empirical.

## The record of planned production

No section of this comparison carries more weight against the ecosocialist programme, and none should be softened. The states that planned production comprehensively in the twentieth century compiled environmental records worse than those of the market economies they competed with: rivers diverted and seas shrunk by irrigation gigantism, industrial regions poisoned by plants answerable to output targets rather than to any public, forests cleared and soils exhausted under plans that treated nature as a storehouse to be emptied for accumulation by the state. The mechanisms were not accidental. Where production targets are set politically and managers are judged on fulfilling them, environmental costs are the easiest costs to externalise, because no price registers them and no independent public can organise around them. Where the press, the courts, and scientific institutions answer to the same authority that owns the polluting plant, the feedback channels through which damage becomes a political problem are severed by design. The comprehensive account is given in the series article on the [environmental record of the socialist states](/2017/11/01/socialist-states-environmental-record/), and its findings stand behind every paragraph of this section.

### What did the planned economies record environmentally?

The planned economies recorded environmental damage exceeding that of their market competitors across the standard dimensions: energy and material intensity per unit of output far higher, pollution loads heavier, land and water degradation more extensive, and correction slower because no independent press, court, or scientific establishment could force the owning state to answer for its own enterprises.

Ecosocialists do not, in their serious literature, deny this record; they dispute its relevance, and the dispute deserves exact statement. The historical planned economies, Kovel and Löwy both argue, were not instances of the ecosocialist programme but of its opposite in the respects that matter: productivist to the core, committed to outpacing capitalist output in heavy industry, governed by bureaucracies insulated from the populations bearing the costs, and organised around accumulation by the state rather than around democratically chosen need. A programme whose defining features are democratic decision and the subordination of production to deliberated purposes cannot be refuted, they argue, by regimes that had neither. The reply has logical force and a visible limit. It protects the ideal by distinguishing it from every attempt, and a distinction available for every failure eventually empties a programme of content, since nothing that could happen would count against it. Green capitalists press exactly there: the knowledge and incentive problems that wrecked the historical plans are not features of those regimes' bad luck but of planning as such, and the burden of showing that democratic planning escapes them lies on its proponents, who have no operating instance to exhibit.

The honest grading of this exchange assigns costs to both sides. The ecosocialist distinction between the programme and the historical regimes is genuine: the programmes really do differ in their stated decision procedures, and treating a bureaucratic command economy as a trial of democratic planning is a category error of the same kind as treating an unregulated market as a trial of ecological modernisation. But the green capitalist counter also lands, because a programme insulated against all historical evidence has traded testability for purity, and the grid's final column asks of ecosocialism what observation would change its answer on the decision row. A fair ecosocialist answer exists: the failure of a democratically constituted planning procedure, operated with free information and real throughput budgets, to control its flows would count decisively. That no such procedure has yet operated is a fact about history. Whether it is also a fact about possibility is the open question the record cannot close, and comparison should leave it open rather than pretend the twentieth century settled it in either direction.

One further asymmetry deserves naming, because partisans of both programmes exploit its absence. Market economies generated their environmental improvements partly by mechanisms ecosocialists identify correctly: independent courts, a free scientific establishment, organised publics able to make damage politically expensive, and, less nobly, the relocation of dirty production beyond their own borders. Planned economies lacked the corrective institutions and kept the dirt at home, which flattered the territorial ledgers of their competitors. The comparison of records is therefore not a clean experiment in ownership. It compares systems differing in ownership and in feedback institutions simultaneously, and the green capitalist claim that the market economies' advantage derived from markets alone assigns to property relations an effect that partly belongs to pluralism. Ecological modernisation theory, to its credit, builds the institutional point into its own account: its claim is that modern societies develop ecological rationality across several spheres at once, markets, states, sciences, and publics together. A green capitalism faithful to its own theory should therefore argue from the package, not from ownership alone, and an ecosocialism faithful to its theory should specify which feedback institutions its planning preserves. Neither always does, and the reader now has the means to notice.

## The strongest objection, stated and answered

The strongest objection to the ecosocialist programme is the green capitalist case at full strength, and it should be stated as its theorists state it, not as its opponents summarise it. Environmental improvement has historically accompanied rising income: as economies grew wealthier, the flows most dangerous to immediate health, urban air pollution, waterborne contamination, lead, sulphur, were regulated down absolutely while output climbed, a trajectory the literature discusses under the heading of the environmental Kuznets curve and its critics, and which occurred through the ordinary institutions of market societies rather than against them. The cost of low carbon technology fell faster under competitive conditions of deployment than planners and official forecasters predicted, which suggests that the innovative mechanism the programme relies on is not a hope but a demonstrated pattern. And the states that planned production, the only large scale alternative ever operated, recorded worse environmental outcomes than the market economies of the same period, on the dimensions both sides accept. If ownership were the variable that mattered, the record would run the other way. It runs the way it runs, and a theory of why capitalism cannot stabilise must explain why the stabilisations that did occur occurred there.

The answer available to ecosocialism, stated with equal care, begins by sorting the objection's exhibits by sector and flow. The historical improvements cluster where substitution was commercially available and the damage was local, visible, and borne by populations with political voice: a scrubber, a fuel switch, a catalytic converter, a treatment plant. The flows that define the stabilisation problem are global, cumulative, and borne disproportionately by the voiceless and the unborn, and in those flows the record shows relative decoupling, absolute decoupling in favoured ledgers and favoured economies, and a pace of reduction that systematic reviews find wanting. The technology cost exhibit is real but proves a conditional: where policy created a market and the service was substitutable, competition delivered. It does not prove the unconditional claim that the mechanism generalises to sectors where the service is the throughput. The planned economy exhibit is the strongest of the three, and the ecosocialist answer to it, the distinction between bureaucratic productivism and democratic planning, is a defence of a programme never yet operated, which honest ecosocialists, Löwy among them, acknowledge is a serious problem for their case rather than a debating point to be dismissed. Conceding that, the answer returns to its strongest ground: the objection demonstrates that managed capitalism can improve, which nobody here denies, and the dispute is whether improvement converges on stabilisation at the required rate and scope, which the objection's own exhibits, graded flow by flow, do not yet show.

Symmetry requires the strongest objection in the other direction to receive the same treatment, briefly. Against green capitalism, the ecosocialist's best case is not impossibility, which fails, but the conjunction of the rate findings, the generality gap, the capture and relocation mechanisms, and the observation that the programme's sufficiency claim rests on extrapolating its best sectors to its worst. The green capitalist's answer, given at full strength, is that extrapolation from demonstrated mechanisms is how every programme argues, that the ecosocialist alternative extrapolates from no demonstrated mechanism at all, and that the burden asymmetry matters: one side must defend a trajectory, the other must defend a procedure that has never existed in its specified form. That answer is also strong, and it identifies the real shape of the remaining dispute. Both programmes are wagers on the future behaviour of institutions. One wagers that correction mechanisms inside market societies will extend faster than thresholds arrive. The other wagers that a decision procedure never yet operated would outperform them. Evidence disciplines both wagers without settling either, and a comparison that pretended otherwise would be serving its readers a conclusion the literature does not contain.

## Misreadings this comparison corrects

Four misreadings circulate so reliably that correcting them is worth a section, since each one manufactures agreement or disagreement where the programmes themselves have neither. The first holds that decoupling has not occurred anywhere, a claim repeated in ecosocialist popular writing and false on the consumption based evidence: it has occurred, in a number of wealthy economies, across sustained periods, in important flows. Believing otherwise leaves the ecosocialist case hostage to a finding its own preferred ledger refutes. The second misreading holds that relative decoupling is sufficient, a claim implicit in green advocacy that quotes intensity gains as if they were stabilisation: what receiving systems register is the total load, and an intensity gain inside a rising total is a slower approach to the same threshold, not a retreat from it. The third holds that production based and consumption based accounting give the same answer, which the footprint literature refutes directly, and which flatters whichever economy does the importing. The fourth pair concerns the programmes' intentions. Green capitalism does not mean doing nothing; it is an interventionist programme of pricing, standards, subsidy, and disclosure. Ecosocialism does not require reduced living standards; its texts promise a different composition of production, with provision in housing, health, learning, and leisure maintained or expanded while wasteful throughput contracts. A reader may doubt either promise. Doubting a promise differs from misstating it, and the doubt lands harder when the statement was fair.

A fifth misreading, subtler than the others, treats this dispute as a scientific controversy. It is not, and the distinction protects both science and politics. The physical findings about thresholds, rates, and system responses are the province of the relevant sciences, are attributed to those sciences throughout this article, and are not adjudicated here, because a comparison of political programmes has no standing to vote on them. The dispute between ecosocialism and green capitalism begins where the findings end: it concerns which institutions will deliver what the findings require, and that is a question about political economy, evidence of a different kind, and values, conducted legitimately only when its scientific premises are held steady and stated as premises. Polemic that recruits science as a factional badge, on either side, corrupts both enterprises. The ecosocialist who implies that physics has refuted markets, and the green capitalist who implies that economics has refuted planning, are making the same error in mirror image, and the error is the first thing a careful reader should refuse.

## A short history of the two programmes

The programmes have genealogies, and knowing them prevents a common error: treating either position as a slogan invented for the present argument rather than a developed body of thought with internal debates. Ecological modernisation came first as a self conscious theory. Joseph Huber's work in the early nineteen eighties argued that industrial society could pass through an ecological phase of modernisation as it had passed through earlier phases, with environmental criteria becoming a design principle of production rather than an external complaint against it. Dutch and German scholars built the research programme through the following decade: Gert Spaargaren's studies of consumption, Martin Jänicke's comparative work on environmental policy capacity, and eventually the systematic statement by Mol and Spaargaren at the turn of the century. The theory was from the beginning an intervention against two opponents at once: the degrowth and limits traditions, which it accused of ignoring institutional learning, and the older industrial establishment, which it accused of treating environmental demand as a cost rather than a modernising signal.

Ecosocialism's founding documents are later and more explicitly Marxist. Joel Kovel and Michael Löwy published an ecosocialist manifesto in 2001, setting out the claim that the ecological crisis is rooted in the capital relation itself and that the alternative must be a democratically planned production oriented to use values and ecological repair. A subsequent international declaration, adopted at Belém in 2009, broadened the current, bringing in indigenous movements, peasant organisations, and thinkers from the global South for whom the ecological argument and the anti colonial argument were never separate. The current's intellectual sources run in several directions at once: the recovery of Marx's ecological writings by scholars such as John Bellamy Foster, whose work on the metabolic rift reconstructed the ecological dimension of the critique of political economy; the earlier green Marxism of writers like Andre Gorz, who had argued from the nineteen seventies that productivism itself, and not only its capitalist form, threatened both ecology and freedom; and the ecofeminist and social reproduction literatures this series treats elsewhere. Readers who want the field's full genealogy will find it in the ecological Marxism guide linked earlier in this article, and in the thinker profiles the series publishes alongside this comparison.

The two genealogies explain a temperamental difference that surfaces in every exchange. Ecological modernisation was written largely by scholars advising or observing governments, and it reads like it: institutional, incremental, interested in instruments, confident that systems learn. Ecosocialism was written largely by movement intellectuals, and it reads like that: diagnostic, structural, interested in power, sceptical that systems learn what their incentives forbid. Neither temperament is a refutation of the other, but each predicts its holder's characteristic blind spot. The modernisation literature chronically underweights the political economy of resistance, treating capture as a design flaw rather than a strategy. The ecosocialist literature chronically underweights administrative reality, treating the plan as a procedure that deliberation will somehow staff, inform, and discipline. A reader equipped with both genealogies can predict, for almost any new contribution to the debate, which evidence it will handle carefully and which it will hurry past, and that predictive equipment is worth more in practice than a verdict on either programme's truth.

The historical sequence matters for one more reason. Ecological modernisation's claims were framed when territorial emissions in wealthy economies were the standard evidence, and its founding exhibits were the regulated pollutants of the postwar industrial cleanups. Ecosocialism's claims were framed after consumption based accounting and the earth system literature had complicated that evidence, and its founding exhibits are the global and stubborn flows. Each programme was, in part, an accurate reading of the evidence available at its formation, generalised beyond its warrant. The comparison this article conducts is possible only because the subsequent literature enlarged both evidence bases until they overlapped, forcing the dispute onto the shared ground of ledgers, rates, and sectors where it can actually be graded. That is a recent achievement, it is incomplete, and the arguments of the founding texts should be read as claims on that shared ground rather than as scriptures exempt from it.

## The curve, the claim, and the critics

One exhibit does so much work in green capitalist argument that it deserves direct examination: the proposition, associated with the environmental Kuznets curve literature of the early nineteen nineties, that environmental damage first rises and then falls as income grows, tracing an inverted U. The hypothesis took its name by analogy with Simon Kuznets's conjecture about inequality, and its early econometric support came from local air pollutants, whose concentrations in wealthy cities had indeed fallen from mid century peaks while incomes multiplied. For green capitalism the curve supplies a reassuring grammar: growth is not the disease but the cure's funding mechanism, since wealth generates both the demand for environmental quality and the surplus that pays for cleanup. For critics the curve is the most dangerous exhibit in the whole debate, precisely because it is accurate where it is accurate.

### What does the income and environment curve actually show?

The curve holds for some local, flow pollutants with available substitutes and visible damage, whose concentrations fell as wealthy economies grew. It does not hold reliably for cumulative, global, or stock pollutants, for material throughput in aggregate, or on consumption based measures. Its domain is a list of favourable cases, not a law of development.

Sorting the curve's domain is the analytical work. The favourable cases share features identified in the earlier sections: the damage is local and perceptible, a substitute or an end of pipe technology exists, the affected population has voice, and the cost of abatement is small relative to the wealth available. The unfavourable cases invert every feature: damage is distant or deferred, no substitute exists at scale, the affected are unborn or disenfranchised, and abatement requires restructuring the flow rather than filtering it. A curve estimated on the first class of cases and invoked over the second commits an elementary error of extrapolation, and the empirical literature's verdict, as the curve's own more careful proponents acknowledge, is that the relationship is a contingent outcome of regulation and composition change rather than an automatic fruit of income. Where policy did not act, income alone did not clean. That concession, properly weighed, moves the curve from the green capitalist column into shared territory: it becomes evidence that wealthy market societies can regulate when their institutions choose, which both programmes can explain, and the argument returns to the conditions under which they choose.

Ecosocialists add a geographical objection that the consumption based literature partly sustains. The descending limb of the curve, in several estimated cases, coincides with the relocation of dirty production to economies on the ascending limb, so that the curve for the world does not descend as the national curves do. The objection does not dissolve the genuine improvements, which include real technological diffusion to the receiving economies, whose own intensities typically fall as production modernises there. But it forbids the simplest reading, in which growth as such cleans the environment of the economy growing. The disciplined summary runs as follows. Income growth creates capacities: fiscal surplus, technical capability, institutional attention. Whether those capacities are applied to throughput is a political and institutional outcome, varying by flow, by ledger, and by the organisation of the affected. Green capitalism is the wager that the application can be made general and sufficient through its instruments. Ecosocialism is the wager that it cannot, because the capacities are generated by a process whose expansion continuously recreates the problem at a larger scale. The curve, read carefully, is evidence about capacities. The dispute is about the wager, and it remains open.

## Consumption, needs, and manufactured demand

Both programmes must eventually say something about the demand side of the economy, and both find it uncomfortable territory. Ecosocialism inherits from the Marxist tradition a critique of manufactured want: the argument, associated in its classic form with John Kenneth Galbraith's account of the dependence effect and developed by Marxists into a theory of the sales effort, that a large share of consumption in wealthy economies is not the satisfaction of independently formed needs but the output of an apparatus of advertising and product design whose function is to keep accumulation moving. If wants are manufactured by the production system, then consumer sovereignty, the green capitalist's answer to the decision question, is compromised at its root: the decider is itself a product of the process it is supposed to govern. Ecosocialist writing on this point converges with the social reproduction literature elsewhere in this series, since the labour of maintaining households is what manufactured convenience consumption claims to save, and the saving is often an illusion of accounting.

Green capitalists answer on two levels, one dismissive and one serious. The dismissive answer notes that the manufactured want argument has been available for a century, that consumers are not the passive material it describes, and that the same scepticism applied to political messaging would dissolve the electorate ecosocialists hope will deliberate their plans. The serious answer concedes the phenomenon and relocates it: advertising, product design, and the whole apparatus of demand formation are themselves regulable activities inside the programme's instrument set, and a framework that can price carbon can also restrict marketing, mandate durability, and require repairability, as several regulated markets already do for other categories of harm. The ecosocialist rejoinder targets the scale relation: demand management at the margin trims the apparatus while the accumulation dynamic rebuilds it, because firms that restrain their own sales effort lose share to firms that do not, and the regulator faces the same competitive erosion described in the regulation section. Once again the exchange terminates in the master dispute, and recognising the recurrence is itself instructive: nearly every subordinate argument in this controversy is the accumulation claim or the institutional learning claim wearing local dress.

### Can demand itself be a policy variable?

Each programme answers yes in practice and no in slogan. Green capitalism officially defers to consumer preference yet regulates advertising, durability, and product standards, all of which shape demand. Ecosocialism officially proposes deliberated needs yet must persuade majorities to deliberate against habits its own analysis calls manufactured. Both therefore govern demand while describing themselves as doing something else.

The deeper question under the demand argument is what a need is, and here the Marxist tradition carries resources neither programme has fully used. Marx's own treatment distinguishes the historically developed standard of life, which expands with civilisation and is legitimate in its expansion, from the artificially stimulated wants of a system that must sell. On that distinction, ecosocialism need not preach asceticism: the critique of manufactured demand defends real need satisfaction against its simulation, and the good life in the ecosocialist sketch is materially richer in time, security, and public provision than the consumption treadmill it replaces. Green capitalists can appropriate the same distinction from their side, arguing that well designed markets with honest information serve the historically developed needs better than queues served the citizens of planned economies, whose suppressed consumption wants were real and whose frustration with them contributed to those systems' collapse. The demand section of the dispute thus ends where the decision section ended: two theories of how human wants should be formed and registered, each pointing at the other's failure mode, neither able to settle the question from the decoupling evidence that governs the rest of the comparison.

## Distribution between economies

The generality question has a human face, and this section gives it one. Observed absolute decoupling is a phenomenon of wealthy economies whose basic infrastructures, housing stocks, transport networks, and industrial plants were built during the high throughput era and are now being operated, maintained, and gradually substituted. Economies at lower income levels face the building itself: the dwellings, grids, roads, ports, schools, and hospitals that wealthy populations take as given, and building them is throughput intensive in ways no known technology avoids. A global stabilisation path therefore cannot consist of every economy replicating the wealthy trajectory at a discount. It requires the wealthy economies to decouple fast enough, and to transfer technology and finance generously enough, that the building economies can reach provision on a smaller throughput budget than their predecessors needed. Whether either programme can deliver that combination is the sharpest practical form of the generality question, and both programmes know it, which is why both devote their most strained passages to it.

### What do the two programmes owe poorer economies?

Both programmes accept that economies still building basic infrastructure cannot be asked to freeze provision at present levels, and both therefore require wealthy economies to contract throughput fastest and to finance cleaner building elsewhere. They divide over the mechanism: transfers negotiated between states inside a market order, or a planned reallocation that treats the global throughput budget as a single deliberated object.

The green capitalist answer runs through development economics. Growth in poorer economies is not optional, because the alternative to rising output there is not stable contentment but continued deprivation, and any programme that reads as a ceiling on the poor world's development will be refused, rightly, by its governments and peoples. Green growth strategy therefore proposes leapfrogging: the building economies adopt the low throughput technologies whose costs wealthy deployment drove down, skipping the dirtiest stages as they skipped fixed telephone networks for mobile ones. The mechanism is the same cost trajectory celebrated earlier, now doing distributional work. Ecosocialists answer that leapfrogging rhetoric understates the materiality of construction, that the transferred technologies arrive embedded in trade and finance relations that extract as they assist, and that the historical pattern of wealthy economies stabilising their own ledgers partly by relocating throughput makes their tuition suspect. The empirical core of the rejoinder is the consumption based evidence itself: part of the wealthy world's decoupling was the poor world's coupling, entered in a different ledger.

Grading this exchange by the article's discipline, the ecosocialist point about ledgers stands, and the green capitalist point about refusal also stands, and together they define the actual constraint set within which any workable stabilisation must be designed. The wealthy economies must decouple on the consumption ledger, at rates beyond anything yet sustained, while the building economies couple partially and then decouple earlier and lower than their predecessors did, with the difference financed substantially by the wealthy. Neither programme's literature, examined fairly, demonstrates a political mechanism adequate to that schedule; green capitalism trusts climate finance negotiated between sovereign states with divergent interests, and ecosocialism trusts a solidarity between movements that has not yet governed anywhere. The comparison's honest output on this row is therefore a challenge addressed to both: the distributional schedule is derivable from the physical findings and the ledgers, and the programme that first shows an institution capable of executing it will have answered the generality question in the only form in which the answer matters.

## Work, livelihoods, and the transition

Every programme for restructuring production is also, whether it says so or not, a programme about jobs, and the employment argument has decided more real world contests between these positions than the decoupling literature has. Green capitalism's employment case is straightforward and empirically grounded in the observed pattern of environmental policy: regulation and deployment create work in installation, retrofitting, manufacturing of new equipment, monitoring, and services, and the net employment effect of the historical cleanup programmes in wealthy economies was small in aggregate, positive in the affected sectors, and nothing like the mass job destruction their opponents forecast. The mechanism is the composition shift again, now read through labour rather than throughput: spending moves between activities, and labour moves with it, provided the transition is paced and the receiving sectors are hiring where the releasing sectors are firing. On this account the politics of transition are manageable with ordinary instruments: notice periods, retraining support, regional investment, and the simple passage of generational turnover in declining trades.

Ecosocialism's employment case operates on a different variable: time. Where green capitalism redistributes a given volume of work, ecosocialist writing proposes to reduce and share it, treating shorter working time as one of the principal dividends of a production system no longer compelled to expand. The argument has a long pedigree inside the Marxist tradition, which regarded the reduction of necessary labour as the precondition of freedom, and Löwy's programme gives expanded free time a central place among the goods the transition is for. Combined with the shift of resources toward labour intensive services, care, education, repair, restoration, whose throughput per unit of employment is low, the ecosocialist sketch claims to solve the employment problem by redefining it: work less, share the work that remains, and count the released time as income in the currency that matters most. Green capitalists reply that working time reduction on that scale is a consumption choice disguised as a structural one, that electorates have consistently preferred income to leisure at the margin when offered the trade through markets, and that a plan which requires them to prefer otherwise has assumed its conclusion.

The honest comparative point is that employment functions in this dispute as a stress test for each programme's theory of politics. The green capitalist mechanism requires displaced workers to believe that the receiving economy will actually receive them, a belief that several deindustrialised regions, whose losses were attributed to trade rather than environmental policy, have been given little reason to hold, and the political backlash from those regions now constrains environmental ambition in ways the ecosocialist capture argument predicts under a different name. The ecosocialist mechanism requires a majority to vote for a restructuring whose benefits arrive as time and security rather than as wages, a preference that has never been demonstrated at the ballot box in the required form. Each programme's transition politics is therefore its weakest engineering, and the literature of both camps, when it turns from diagnosis to coalition building, becomes noticeably thinner than the decoupling chapters that precede it. A reader assessing the programmes as practical proposals, rather than as accounts of the world, should weight this section heavily: the best analysis loses to the best coalition, and neither analysis has yet produced its coalition.

One more employment argument deserves separation, because both sides use it dishonestly. The claim that a programme will create some large number of jobs, or destroy some large number, treats employment as a pile to be added to or subtracted from, when the evidence of every previous transition is that employment is a flow through changing occupations, with the pain concentrated in specific places, trades, and age groups rather than spread across the aggregate. Green jobs rhetoric and jobs versus environment rhetoric are the same fallacy in opposite colours. What the evidence actually supports is narrower and more useful: transitions governed with long notice, place based investment, and income insurance impose modest and temporary costs on identifiable groups, while transitions imposed suddenly on communities with no alternative employer impose permanent ones. That finding is programme neutral. It indicts the implementation habits of governments more than the content of either programme here, and it returns the reader, once more, to institutional capacity, the variable that the grid's second row identified and that no slogan in this dispute can substitute for.

## Who funds the transition

Investment is where programmes meet their budget constraints, and the financing question separates the two positions as cleanly as the decision question does. The green capitalist mechanism for funding the transition is redirection: the vast flow of private investment, steered by carbon pricing, disclosure rules, and public de risking, turns toward low throughput assets because the rules have changed the expected returns. Public money plays a catalytic role, absorbing early stage risk, funding research whose returns cannot be appropriated, and building the infrastructures, grids, transit, charging networks, that private actors will use but not supply. The theory of the state embedded here is of a referee and first mover, not an owner: it changes the gradient of the field and lets the players' own energy do the rest. The empirical support is the observed responsiveness of investment to price and policy signals, which is real and rapid when policy is credible, and equally rapid in reverse when credibility collapses, a symmetry the green capitalist literature handles by emphasising policy durability as a design goal.

The ecosocialist mechanism is direction: investment is too consequential to be left to expected returns, because the composition of the capital stock built in the next decades determines the throughput profile of the economies that inherit it, and a redirection that still requires every project to clear a private return hurdle will systematically underbuild the unprofitable necessities, flood defences, ecosystem restoration, public transit in poor districts, insulation of rented housing whose owners cannot capture the savings. Social ownership of finance, or at minimum a public investment authority with a mandate stated in physical terms, is proposed so that the throughput budget can govern the investment budget directly. John Maynard Keynes, no Marxist, reached for a similar instrument under the pressure of a different emergency when he wrote of the socialisation of investment, and ecosocialists cite the precedent to show that directing investment is a governable technique rather than a utopian leap. Green capitalists answer that directed investment has its own systematic bias, toward the visible, the monumental, and the politically connected, and that the historical record of state investment banks and five year plans exhibits it abundantly.

Underneath the mechanism dispute sits a shared embarrassment that comparison should expose. Both programmes require investment rates in the transition sectors well above what any economy has yet sustained voluntarily, maintained across electoral cycles and commodity price swings, in the face of organised opposition from the owners of the capital stock being retired. Green capitalism's answer to the political problem is to make the transition profitable enough to recruit its own defenders, a strategy with genuine historical successes and a visible failure mode when the profits depend on subsidies a later legislature can repeal. Ecosocialism's answer is to change the ownership so the opposition loses its veto, a strategy whose political precondition, a majority for expropriation, is at least as demanding as the green programme's requirement of durable cross party consensus on pricing. Neither answer is derivable from the decoupling literature, and both programmes would be improved by stating their political preconditions as plainly as they state their instruments. The reader who has followed the comparison this far will recognise the pattern for the last time: at every level of concreteness, the dispute resolves into rival claims about how institutions behave under pressure, and the evidence, honestly assembled, instructs the judgement without replacing it.

## Scale, population, and an identity both sides use

A durable analytical frame, older than either programme, helps organise the dispute's variables, and both camps reach for it when they want to sound precise. The frame is the identity associated with Paul Ehrlich and John Holdren, which decomposes environmental impact into three multiplicative factors: population, affluence or consumption per person, and the impact per unit of consumption delivered by the prevailing technology. The identity is an accounting truth, not a theory, and its usefulness lies in forcing any programme to declare which factors it proposes to move. Green capitalism declares for the third factor almost exclusively: technology, broadly understood to include composition of output, is to fall fast enough to offset growth in the second, while the first is treated as outside legitimate policy altogether. Ecosocialism declares for the second and third together, and its critics sometimes accuse it of smuggling the first in behind them, an accusation its programmatic texts explicitly reject, since the tradition's founders treated population control politics with hostility and its current documents repeat the rejection.

The identity also clarifies why the rate dispute has the structure it does. Stabilisation requires the product of the three factors to fall at a specified pace. If population and affluence both rise, the technology factor must fall faster than their combined growth, year after year, across the whole economy rather than in favoured sectors. The historical record shows the technology factor falling reliably for intensity measures, which is relative decoupling in the identity's language, and falling in absolute terms only where composition change helped it, in the wealthy, service shifted economies of the consumption based studies. The identity cannot say whether the required pace is achievable; only the empirical literature speaks to that, and its answer was graded in the decoupling sections. What the identity contributes is a check on rhetoric. A programme claiming stabilisation through technology alone is claiming a specific arithmetic: that the third factor can fall indefinitely at rates exceeding the growth of the other two. A programme claiming stabilisation through demand restraint is claiming that the second factor can be governed politically at acceptable cost. Both claims are now visible as claims, with their burdens attached, which is all a frame can do and all this one is asked to do.

Population deserves one paragraph of direct treatment, because insinuation does damage here that analysis can prevent. Neither programme in this comparison proposes population policy as an instrument of stabilisation. Green capitalism excludes the factor on liberal grounds: reproductive choice is not a policy variable in its framework. Ecosocialism excludes it on grounds internal to its tradition: the Marxist critique of Malthus, running from Marx's own polemics to the present literature, holds that population pressure is a function of social organisation rather than a natural constant, and that Malthusian framings have historically functioned to blame the poor for scarcities produced by distribution. Where the programmes differ is in their reading of the factor's trajectory: both rely, in their projections, on the demographic transition's observed pattern of fertility falling with development and security, which makes the first factor a slow variable that eventually stabilises in most models. A reader who encounters population control argued as a component of either programme is reading an opponent's caricature or a fringe text, and the distinction matters because the caricature is among the most effective recruiting tools each camp uses against the other.

## The weightless economy and its discontents

Composition change, the mechanism doing quiet work throughout this comparison, has its own literature and its own overclaim, and examining the overclaim tests both programmes once more. The overclaim, popularised in the nineteen nineties under labels like the weightless or dematerialised economy, held that advanced economies were graduating from matter altogether: value increasingly embodied in software, design, finance, entertainment, and knowledge, activities whose direct throughput is negligible, so that growth and throughput would part company as a matter of structural evolution rather than policy effort. The claim drew strength from genuine statistics about the falling material intensity of output and from the visible shift of employment into services. It survives in diluted form inside green growth writing, where the services transition is treated as a tailwind the programme can rely on.

The discontents begin with the ledger. Services are not weightless; they are weight displaced. The office worker's output rests on buildings, devices, transport, food systems, and energy supplied from material sectors whose throughputs appear in other industries' accounts and, frequently, in other economies' territories. Consumption based material footprint studies, the Wiedmann programme among them, found that the material requirements of wealthy consumption rose or held even as territorial extraction measures improved, which is the weightless thesis failing its own test on the honest ledger. A second objection is internal to services themselves: many of the largest service sectors, health, education, care, are subject to the cost dynamics the economist William Baumol identified, in which labour intensive services grow more expensive relative to goods because their productivity grows slowly, and their material support systems, hospitals, campuses, care facilities, are substantial construction and energy loads. A third objection is dynamic: savings from dematerialised consumption are spent, and the spending lands somewhere in the material economy, the rebound mechanism wearing sectoral dress.

Ecosocialists cite this literature as a refutation of green capitalism, and it is not quite that, for a reason the article's discipline makes visible. The weightless thesis in its strong form is a journalistic simplification that ecological modernisation theory never needed: Mol's and Jänicke's claims concern institutional capacity to impose criteria, not a spontaneous evaporation of matter from economic life. The failure of the strong weightless claim refutes the complacent version of green growth, the version that treats decoupling as a trend to be ridden rather than a result to be engineered, and that version deserves refuting. What survives is the modest and defensible proposition that composition is a real variable, that output structures differ enormously in throughput per unit of value, and that policy can push composition further than drift has taken it. How much further, and whether far enough, is the rate and generality question in sectoral form. The pattern should now feel familiar enough to name outright: at every level of analysis, from the global identity down to the individual industry, the evidence supports the possibility claims of the green programme and the insufficiency warnings of the ecosocialist one, and the programmes' own rhetorics each suppress the half of that sentence they dislike.

## Energy as the favourable case

Take the sector where the green capitalist mechanism has performed best, and examine it without either camp's commentary track, because its internal structure teaches what success requires. Electricity supply presented every favourable condition the earlier analysis identified. The service, delivered energy at a socket, is perfectly standardised, so substitution among generation sources leaves the consumed product unchanged. Multiple substitute technologies existed at varying maturity, so policy induced competition among them rather than betting on one. Costs were dominated by capital and engineering, the domains where learning curves and scale reliably bite. Monitoring was trivial, since generation is metered at few points. And the incumbents, though powerful, faced entrants whose product was identical and increasingly cheaper, a configuration in which incumbent resistance spends itself defending a shrinking margin. Under those conditions, pricing, standards, and deployment subsidy produced the cost trajectories and the absolute reductions in the sector's emissions intensity that the green literature celebrates, and in several wealthy economies absolute reductions in the sector's total emissions alongside rising output and rising electricity consumption itself.

Now list what the ecosocialist reading adds, granting every fact. The sector's success was purchased by public action at every step: research funding across decades, deployment mandates, guaranteed prices in the early schemes, grid investment by public or regulated utilities, and planning decisions about networks that no spot market generated. The market mechanism operated inside a scaffold of collective decisions thick enough that describing the outcome as capitalism unaided going green is a misdescription the careful ecological modernisation theorists themselves avoid. The ecosocialist draws the moral that the scaffold, not the market, did the decisive work, and that extending the scaffold to sectors lacking the favourable conditions is a polite name for the planning they propose openly. The green capitalist draws the opposite moral: the scaffold is exactly their programme, pricing and standards and subsidy are what green capitalism means, and the demand that the scaffold be renamed or extended to ownership is a change of subject. Both morals are available because the case genuinely exhibits both mechanisms in harness, which is why partisans quote it constantly and why it settles nothing by itself. Cases settle disputes when their conditions discriminate between theories. This one discriminates only against the two extreme caricatures, markets unaided and plans omnipotent, that neither serious programme holds.

The sector also disciplines the rate discussion with a sobering observation. Even here, with every favourable condition present and policy applied over decades, the transition of a single sector's supply remains incomplete in every economy that has attempted it, and the build out of the low throughput alternative is itself a large throughput event: mining, manufacture, construction, and land transformation on a scale that registers in the global material accounts. The literature calls the general phenomenon the transition's own footprint, and it imposes an arithmetic on all programmes alike. Rates of reduction in operating flows must be achieved while investment flows temporarily rise, so the early decades of any successful path show worse aggregate numbers than the later ones, and a judge sampling the record too early will convict every programme of failure. Ecosocialists use this to explain away slow aggregate progress under market regimes; green capitalists use it to promise inflection. The observation itself belongs to neither: it belongs to the physics of replacing a capital stock, and it warns the reader of this comparison that the evidence now available may systematically understate every programme's eventual performance, including that of the programme whose instruments happen to be in force. Judgement deferred is not judgement abandoned, but the deferral has a rational basis here that the polemics of both camps rarely concede.

## The built environment as the hard case

Set against energy the sector that presents the opposite configuration, and the comparison's sectoral logic completes itself. The built environment, the stock of dwellings, workplaces, roads, and infrastructures within which life is conducted, has the longest lived capital in the economy, the most dispersed ownership, the least standardised product, and a throughput profile dominated by bulk materials, cement, steel, aggregates, timber, whose production processes are chemically and physically constrained in ways generation never was. A building erected now commits its occupants to a heating, cooling, and transport pattern for generations, and the flow consequences of the existing stock dwarf those of new construction in any given year, so that even perfect standards for new building move the aggregate slowly. Retrofit of the standing stock is the sector's real frontier, and retrofit is labour intensive, site specific, finance constrained, and entangled with the tenure structure of housing, since the party who would pay for insulation is frequently not the party who would save on heating. Every favourable condition from the energy case is absent or inverted.

The programmes' treatments of this case are diagnostic of their deeper commitments. Green capitalism reaches for its instruments and finds them blunted: pricing carbon raises the cost of cement and heating fuel, which is the intended signal, but the actors who must respond are millions of owners facing credit constraints, split incentives, and a shortage of the skilled labour the response requires, so the price signal arrives at decision makers least able to act on it. The programme's honest wing answers with the complementary instruments, public retrofit programmes, building codes with ratcheting standards, finance facilities that solve the split incentive, and in doing so concedes that the sector needs direction of a thickness approaching the ecosocialist description. Ecosocialism reaches for planning and finds its own blunting: the sector's decisions are irreducibly local and particular, the knowledge required is distributed among occupants and trades, and a democratic plan for the retrofit of a housing stock is a procedure nobody has designed, let alone operated, whose administrative demands dwarf those of the energy transition. The ecosocialist honest wing answers with municipal and cooperative delivery vehicles, community land trusts, and workplace organisation of the building trades, conceding in turn that its planning here means federated local initiative rather than central allocation. Observe what has happened: at the hard case, each programme's practice migrates toward the other's theory. The grid's clean rows blur at the sectoral frontier, and the remaining difference is which direction of migration each side trusts when its own instruments run out.

Agrifood systems extend the lesson in a third direction, since there the throughput is not a by product of the service but its substance: feeding a population means moving water, nitrogen, phosphorus, and biomass at scales set by biology, and the efficiencies available are bounded by the metabolism of crops and animals rather than by engineering ingenuity alone. The decoupling literature's findings for agriculture are correspondingly the least encouraging in the basket, with intensity gains real but absolute flows stubborn, and with the largest reductions in wealthy economies again entangled with trade, as feed and food imports carry foreign land and water in their ledgers. Both programmes have agrifood chapters thinner than their energy chapters, and for the same reason: the sector resists the master mechanisms of each. Pricing meets the political third rail of food costs. Planning meets the biological variability that wrecked collectivised agriculture's yields wherever it was imposed at scale. The sector therefore stands in this comparison as a standing rebuke to general theory, a reminder that stabilisation will be won or lost in places where the available instruments are few, slow, and contested, and that the aggregate curves both programmes argue over are sums of such places.

## Objections each programme makes to itself

A comparison gains credibility when it reports the criticisms each camp's own members press hardest, since internal critics cannot be accused of caricature and rarely pull their punches. Inside the Marxist tradition, ecosocialism faces a productivist objection from fellow Marxists who regard the ecological turn as a concession to a Malthusian mood alien to the tradition's confidence in developed productive forces. On this internal view, scarcity is historically produced by distribution rather than by nature, the limits literature mistakes relative barriers for absolute ones, and a socialism that begins by preaching restraint has surrendered the promise, abundance through collective mastery of production, that made the tradition a force. Ecosocialists answer that the recovered Marx, the Marx of the metabolic rift and the critique of the robbery of the soil, never held the crude productivism attributed to him, and that the abundance the tradition promised was always an abundance of free time and developed capacities rather than of throughput. The exchange is genuinely intra mural and unresolved, and it matters for the comparison because it shows the impossibility claim is not even the settled position of the broader tradition ecosocialism speaks for: plenty of Marxists reject it on textual grounds before the decoupling evidence is consulted.

Ecosocialism also faces an internal objection about agency. The programme requires a political subject capable of winning power and then of exercising it through democratic planning, and its own theorists, examining the weakness and fragmentation of the movements that would have to carry the transition, sometimes write as if the analysis has outrun the politics by a distance they cannot close. Kovel's later writing grows notably more pessimistic on this point, and the ecosocialist literature's recurring turn toward prefigurative and municipal strategies can be read as an implicit concession that the road to the programme does not run through the conquest of national state power in the classical manner. Green capitalists face a mirror image objection from within their own broad camp. Empirical sociologists sympathetic to modernisation, Richard York and Eugene Rosa prominent among them, subjected ecological modernisation theory to tests its founders had not performed and reported that the theory's claims about the decoupling effects of modernisation's institutions were weakly supported at the level of national aggregates: the factors the theory celebrated did not reliably predict the outcomes it promised. Their critique, published within environmental sociology rather than against it, forced a second generation of the theory toward more conditional and mechanism specific claims, which is precisely the disciplined version this article has been expounding. A programme whose own sociologists administer its hardest examination is healthier than its pamphlets suggest.

The internal objections converge on a shared admission that comparison should register as the closest thing to a joint finding this dispute has produced. Both programmes, in their self critical moments, concede that their master variable underdetermines their conclusions. Ownership does not by itself fix throughput, as the planned record proves to ecosocialists. Modernisation fares no better when its own aggregate tests are applied. What each side then adds is a mechanism story, capture and accumulation on one side, institutional learning and innovation on the other, and the mechanism stories are where the evidence is thinnest and the theorising most creative. A student who locates the argument at the level of mechanisms, and demands of every contribution which mechanism it evidences and how, will find the literature sorting itself with unusual clarity: the strong papers on both sides are mechanism papers, studies of how a regulation survived or died, how a technology's costs actually fell, how a planning procedure actually allocated, and the weak ones are the restatements of allegiance that fill the journals between them.

## Wartime mobilisation, the precedent both sides raid

When polemic needs a proof that economies can change at stabilisation speed, both camps reach for the same exhibit: the conversion of industrial economies to war production in the nineteen forties. The episode is raided so regularly that its actual lessons are worth separating from its rhetorical uses. The conversions were fast beyond peacetime precedent: consumer durable industries retooled for armaments within months, materials were allocated by administrative priority, consumption was rationed, labour was directed, and output of the prioritised category multiplied while civilian throughput fell. Ecosocialists cite the episode as existence proof for planning: when a society decides collectively what matters, the composition of production turns out to be a choice, executable at speed by administrative means, and the claim that economies cannot be steered is refuted by the steering everyone can see. Green capitalists cite the same episode for the opposite moral: the conversion was executed overwhelmingly by private firms under contract, using market capacities for engineering, logistics, and improvisation that no ministry possessed, with the state's role confined to demand, finance, and priority setting, which is ecological modernisation's division of labour in uniform.

The disciplined reading refuses both morals their simplicity. The wartime economies were hybrids under emergency discipline: planning of priorities and allocation, markets and firms as the executing machinery, rationing as the demand side instrument, and a suspension of the ordinary politics of consumption that no peacetime programme in this comparison proposes to replicate. What the episode actually demonstrates is narrower and more valuable than either moral. It demonstrates that the composition of production is far more plastic, at far greater speed, than peacetime debate assumes, and that the binding constraints in ordinary times are political and distributive rather than technical. Both programmes can claim that finding, and both do. It also demonstrates, in its aftermath, the reversibility of directed change: the converted economies converted back with equal speed when the emergency ended, and the consumption suppressed by rationing returned expanded. A transition intended to be permanent cannot borrow the emergency's instruments without answering the question the emergency never faced, which is how the new composition is to be legitimised and defended across generations of ordinary politics. Neither programme's literature answers that question well. The green programme assumes profitability will defend the new composition once built; the ecosocialist assumes democratic commitment will. The wartime record supports the shared premise about plasticity and is silent, pointedly, on both assumptions.

There is a final, quieter lesson in the precedent. The wartime states knew, in physical units, what they were doing: their planning was conducted in tonnages, capacities, and shipping space, with monetary accounts subordinate to material balances. The ecosocialist tradition's interest in physical planning descends partly from that experience, transmitted through the material balances methods of the planned economies, while the green tradition's confidence that monetary signals suffice descends from the postwar reaction against physical controls. The decoupling dispute, at this depth, is also a dispute about the proper units of economic knowledge, whether a society steering its throughput must count in tonnes and joules directly or can trust prices to carry the information. The accounting sections of this article have already shown that prices do not carry it transparently, since the ledger choice reverses findings. A state that wants to govern throughput must therefore build the physical statistical apparatus whichever programme it follows, and the construction of that apparatus, unglamorous and undisputed, may be the most consequential practical step either camp could take, precisely because both need it and neither can campaign on it.

## What would settle the dispute

Arguments that cannot say what would settle them are not finished arguments, and this section holds both programmes to the standard the four questions grid announced. Settlement will not come from a single study, because the dispute's components sit at different levels, but the components can be listed with the observations that would grade each of them, and listing them is a service to every reader who will meet this controversy again in less careful hands. The occurrence component is already settled, by the consumption based literature, in favour of possibility. Any future contribution that reopens it by citing territorial ledgers alone should be read as uninformed or as advocacy, and the same verdict applies to contributions that cite occurrence as if it settled the questions that remain open. Scholarship's centre of gravity has moved, and the comparison's first duty is to move its readers to where the centre now sits.

The rate component would be graded by a specified comparison: observed trajectories of the major flows, on consumption based accounting, held against the trajectories that stabilisation conditions imply, sustained over periods long enough to exclude the transition's investment hump and the business cycle. The review literature performs exactly this comparison and returns the insufficiency verdict described earlier, which stands, on the present evidence, as the best supported substantive claim in the entire dispute. What would change it is equally specifiable: a run of years in which wealthy economies, deploying the green instrument set at the stringency its own theorists recommend, held consumption based reductions at or beyond the required pace across the majority of the flow basket, including the stubborn sectors. Partial versions of that observation, a sector here, a flow there, extend the occurrence finding without touching the rate verdict, and advocates who present them as touching it should be corrected by their own side's standards. Conversely, the rate verdict against green capitalism would harden into something like refutation if the full instrument set, applied at recommended stringency over a long period, repeatedly failed to move the stubborn flows, since at that point the programme's reply that its prescription was never filled would cease to be available.

### Why is scope a separate question from rate?

Rate asks how fast throughput falls where it is falling; scope asks across how much of the economy, and how many economies, the falling extends. The two vary independently. A steep reduction confined to electricity in wealthy economies can coexist with a flat global total, which is why assessments that report one while implying the other mislead.

### What would change the verdict on generality?

Generality would be established by building economies reaching modern provision at markedly lower throughput than their predecessors used, on consumption based measures, with the difference traceable to transferred technology and finance rather than to postponed development. It would be damaged by infrastructure built on the old pattern at the old intensities across another generation.

The institutional components admit no single observation, and honesty requires saying why. Whether regulation can hold stringency against competitive pressure for the decades stabilisation requires is a question about the future behaviour of political systems, and evidence arrives as episodes, each contestable, each generalisable only by argument. Whether democratic planning of the ecosocialist type can operate at national scale is a question about an unbuilt procedure, and evidence cannot arrive until someone builds it, which is itself a political event outside the literature's control. These components will therefore be settled, if they are settled, historically rather than scientifically: by attempts, partial adoptions, hybrids neither camp forecast, and the slow accumulation of cases like the sectoral ones this article examined, where practice migrates across the theoretical frontier in both directions. The comparison's closing advice to its readers is to distrust any participant who reports the dispute as already settled in either direction, and to ask of every new contribution the three questions this article has applied throughout. Which claim is being made, occurrence, rate, or generality? On which ledger, and for which flows? And what observation, stated in advance, would its author accept as evidence against it? A literature that answered those questions routinely would need fewer manifestos.

## Reading the primary texts

Comparisons mediate, and mediation has costs this section exists to repay, by sending readers to the sources with instructions for reading them against each other. Begin with the ecosocialist programme in Löwy's Ecosocialism, the manifesto volume of 2015, read for the architecture of the argument rather than the rhetoric of its subtitle: note how the accumulation claim is established, how democratic planning is specified, and how the historical record of the planned economies is handled, since the handling is the programme's stress point and Löwy, to his credit, does not hide it. Read Kovel's The Enemy of Nature alongside for the tradition's more philosophical register, where the enemy is named as a system of production rather than a set of policies, and where the constructive chapters, on production for use and on the transformation of needs, show both the ambition and the vagueness that the decision row of the grid records. Angus's Facing the Anthropocene then supplies the movement's encounter with the earth system literature, valuable because it accepts the physical findings at full strength and draws the political conclusions its author believes follow, letting the reader test the inferences against the findings directly.

For the other programme, the foundational reading is Mol and Spaargaren's statement of ecological modernisation theory, best read with attention to its sociological machinery: the claim is about the differentiation of an ecological sphere within modern societies, and it is more subtle, and more falsifiable, than the green growth slogans derived from it. Porter and van der Linde's article on regulation and innovation should be read in the original rather than in summary, because the distance between what it claims, that well designed regulation can trigger offsetting innovation, and what is claimed for it, that regulation pays for itself, is the distance between a research programme and a sales pitch, and students are examined on exactly such distances. Jänicke's comparative studies of environmental policy capacity supply the programme's empirical spine, the documented diffusion of pioneer standards through integrated markets, and they repay reading as a corrective to both camps: to ecosocialists, because the diffusion is real; to green capitalists, because the capacity that produces it is unevenly distributed in ways their aggregate claims glide over. Hawken, Lovins, and Lovins's Natural Capitalism can then be read as the programme's popular synthesis, its engineering optimism intact, its institutional thinness equally visible.

Reading across the frontier, three habits repay cultivation. Track the ledger: whenever a text reports improvement, ask which accounting basis and which flows, and notice how rarely the answer is volunteered. Track the modifier: absolute or relative, occurring or sufficient, somewhere or everywhere; the entire dispute lives in modifiers, and authors reveal their honesty in whether they supply them. Track the concession: the strongest texts on both sides contain a paragraph their own pamphleteers would cut, the ecosocialist acknowledgement that planning's record is a problem, the moderniser's acknowledgement that capacity is uneven and capture real, and those paragraphs are where a comparison like this one is built from. A reader who finishes the primary texts with those habits will not need this article again, which is the proper ambition of any guide, and will be equipped to grade the next decade of the dispute as it arrives, study by study and sector by sector, rather than allegiance by allegiance.

## Cross-examination: each programme answers the other's best question

Structured comparison reaches its limit when the programmes stop describing themselves and start interrogating each other, and this section stages that interrogation fairly, posing to each side the single hardest question the other side owns, with the best available answer and the residue the answer leaves. The ecosocialist's hardest question for green capitalism runs as follows. Your instruments have been available, in substantially their present form, for decades, and their deployment at stabilising stringency has been urged by your own theorists throughout that period. If the institutional learning your theory predicts were operating as advertised, why does the record show deployment everywhere lagging the theory's own prescriptions, stringency everywhere set by incumbent tolerance, and the required rates nowhere achieved? Either the theory's account of political capacity is wrong, or the system's resistance is structural, and the second possibility is the ecosocialist diagnosis arriving by the green programme's own evidence.

The best green capitalist answer distinguishes between a theory of possibility and a timetable. Ecological modernisation claims that modern institutions can absorb ecological criteria; it never promised they would do so on a schedule set by physical thresholds, and the lag between capacity and deployment is the normal condition of institutional change, visible in every previous absorption from factory safety to food standards, each of which lagged its scientific warrant by generations before becoming unremarkable. The answer adds a counter observation: the direction of travel under deployment, partial as it is, has been uniformly toward the programme's predictions, occurrence of decoupling included, whereas the ecosocialist procedure has no deployments from which a direction could even be read. The residue is genuine. The answer concedes that green capitalism cannot promise rates, only mechanisms, and a reader who regards the physical timetable as non negotiable will find that concession decisive against it. The programme's honesty consists in making the concession where its serious theorists do; its vulnerability is that the concession, once made, transfers the argument to politics, where the ecosocialist's structural account of why deployment lags is a standing rival explanation that fits the same facts.

Now the reverse interrogation. The green capitalist's hardest question for ecosocialism runs as follows. Your programme's decision procedure, democratic planning of production and investment, has never operated, and every historical approximation to planning at scale produced throughput intensities worse than the market systems of its day, coercion in place of the deliberation you specify, and environmental feedback channels severed by the same authority that owned the damage. What feature of your specification prevents the recurrence, and how would anyone know, before the experiment is run on a whole society, whether the feature works? The question is not rhetorical; it asks for the programme's falsification conditions on its central institution.

The best ecosocialist answer proceeds by specifying the differences and accepting the burden they create. The historical plans were set by bureaucracies accountable upward, under productivist goals, with information monopolised by the centre; the proposed procedure inverts each term, with goals set by deliberation among those affected, throughput budgets as its binding physical constraint rather than output targets, information published as a condition of legitimacy, and the plan's own environmental accounting independent of the planning authority. The answer then makes its concession with a clarity its pamphlets avoid: none of this has been demonstrated, the specification is a design rather than a record, and the programme asks to be trusted on the strength of its diagnosis of the rival's failures plus the coherence of its design, which is a weaker epistemic position than its rhetoric usually admits. The residue is the mirror of the other side's. Ecosocialism cannot promise outcomes, only a procedure whose rationale is that the questions markets never pose would at last be posed. A reader who weighs demonstrated mechanisms against undemonstrated procedures will grade the programmes accordingly; a reader who weighs the rate findings against the procedure's absence will grade them the other way; and the comparison, having brought both readers to the same evidence, has done its work.

## How to read a decoupling claim

Because this dispute will reach most readers through headlines rather than review articles, a short practical method belongs in the comparison, stated once and applicable everywhere. When a decoupling claim arrives, in a speech, a report, a manifesto, or a news story, six checks grade it in under a minute. First, the ledger: does the claim rest on production based or consumption based accounting, and does the text say? Silence here is the single most reliable marker of advocacy. Second, the modifier: is the decoupling relative or absolute, and over what period? Intensity gains described in the vocabulary of totals are the dispute's most common counterfeit. Third, the flow: which throughputs, named specifically? A claim about carbon offered as a claim about material footprint, or the reverse, has changed the subject while keeping the applause. Fourth, the denominator's geography: which economies, at which income levels, and are the building economies in the sample or only the built ones? Fifth, the rate comparison: is the observed pace set against the pace stabilisation requires, or against the weaker standard of its own past? Sixth, the counterfactual discipline: are gains credited to a programme's instruments, and could the same period's trade shifts, price shocks, or recessions explain them instead? A claim surviving all six checks deserves belief. In the present literature, claims surviving the first three are already a minority, and that scarcity, rather than any camp's wickedness, is why the dispute persists in its degraded public form long after its scholarly form has narrowed to rate and generality.

Apply the method once, illustratively, to the two canonical slogans. Green growth is working, as a headline, fails at the first check when its evidence is territorial, at the third when its flows are unnamed, and at the fifth when its rates go unexamined; repaired through all six checks, it emerges as the defensible and far less thrilling statement that some wealthy economies have absolutely decoupled some flows on both ledgers at rates below those required. Decoupling is a myth, as the counter headline, fails at the second check when its evidence is intensity, at the fourth when its sample quietly includes the whole world to dilute the wealthy economies' recorded occurrence, and at the first when it refuses the consumption based studies their actual finding; repaired, it emerges as the defensible and equally unthrilling statement that decoupling at sufficient rate and generality is unproven. Between the repaired statements there remains a real dispute, about projections, institutions, and the burden of proof. Between the slogans there is only noise, and the commercial and political incentives of public argument guarantee the noise a larger audience. This article has been written, deliberately, at the level of the repaired statements, and readers who carry the six checks away will find they have acquired the article's whole method in portable form.

## The affluent frontier: where wealthy throughput concentrates

As wealthy economies' aggregate ledgers improve, the composition of their remaining throughput changes, and the change poses a problem each programme must answer in its own vocabulary. The residual flows concentrate increasingly in consumption categories associated with affluence itself: large dwellings under occupied, frequent aviation, meat heavy diets, fast replaced consumer goods, private vehicles oversized for their loads. These are not the throughputs of subsistence or of industrial necessity. They are discretionary in the aggregate and fiercely defended in the particular, because they are woven into the status order and daily convenience of the populations whose governments must legislate their contraction. The empirical literature on consumption based footprints by income group, stated qualitatively as this article's policy requires, finds steep gradients: the footprints of the highest income households exceed those of the lowest by large multiples within the same economy, and the global affluent, wherever resident, account for a disproportionate share of the flows that must fall fastest. Stabilisation arithmetic therefore runs through the consumption of the comfortable, a fact both programmes know and handle with different degrees of candour.

Ecosocialism handles it with the most candour and the least electoral prospect. Its framework names the pattern directly: high throughput consumption by the affluent is the material form of a distribution of power, its contraction is a distributional act, and no pricing scheme that leaves the distribution untouched will contract it sufficiently, since the affluent can absorb prices that exclude the poor long before the prices bite on the behaviour targeted. Hence the programme's preference for quantitative instruments aimed at the top of the distribution, caps, rationing by allowance, prohibition of the most throughput intensive luxuries, of the kind its wartime precedents operated across whole populations. The political difficulty is inscribed in the proposal: the affluent are also the politically weighty, in every democracy and most other systems, and a programme whose first visible act is the contraction of their consumption must win an election, or a revolution, on that prospectus. Green capitalism handles the same arithmetic with instruments chosen to spare it that confrontation: prices set to bite gently and rise gradually, efficiency standards that deliver the same services at lower throughput, and the hope that status competition itself can be redirected toward low throughput goods, as it partly has been in the market for conspicuously sustainable products. The ecosocialist verdict on that hope is that it mistakes the margin for the mass: status goods signal by their cost and rarity, and a low throughput status economy is a contradiction the affluent will route around.

Grading the exchange by evidence rather than by sympathy, the ecosocialist diagnosis of pricing's distributional limit is supported by the structure of demand at the top, where price elasticities for the relevant luxuries are low and the income available to absorb increases is high. The green capitalist reply that standards can do what prices cannot, banning the inefficient vehicle or the uninsulated building outright regardless of the buyer's wealth, is also supported, and it reveals again the programme's practical drift toward the quantitative instruments its theory treats as secondary. What neither programme has produced is evidence that the affluent frontier has ever, anywhere, been contracted at stabilisation pace by consent in peacetime. The wartime precedent contracted it by emergency powers over whole populations. Every other precedent is a story of the frontier expanding more slowly. A reader who believes that observation is the crux of the entire dispute has this article's blessing to think so: it is the rate claim and the capture claim fused into a single observable, located precisely where the two programmes' stories about power diverge most sharply.

## Who does the research

The technology argument has an institutional basement both programmes prefer not to tour, because the tour embarrasses both tour guides. The low carbon technologies whose cost curves vindicate the green mechanism were not conjured by markets from private initiative alone. Their scientific foundations were laid in publicly funded research, their early development subsidised by states, their demonstration projects underwritten by public money, and their deployment, as the energy section noted, scaffolded by mandates and guaranteed prices. The economist Mariana Mazzucato's work on the entrepreneurial state documented the pattern for the signature technologies of the digital economy and argued its general form: the public sector funds the high risk, early stage research whose returns are unappropriable, and private actors commercialise the results, capturing returns the public investment made possible. Green capitalists absorb the finding by enlarging their definition of the programme, public research funding was always among ecological modernisation's instruments, and the state as funder is no concession. Ecosocialists absorb it from the other side, arguing that a system requiring collective provision of its knowledge base, collective absorption of its research risk, and collective construction of its deployment scaffold has conceded the substance of social direction while retaining private appropriation of the result, an arrangement they characterise as planning for the costs and markets for the profits.

The symmetric embarrassment awaits ecosocialism in the same basement. The knowledge system its planning would rely on, earth system science, ecological economics, the footprint accounting programmes used throughout this article, is itself a product of the market societies' public institutions, universities, research councils, and statistical offices operating inside the rival programme's world, and its findings are available to ecosocialism precisely because those societies funded inquiry whose conclusions threaten their own organisation. A planned economy of the historical type, which subordinated its sciences to the plan's requirements, produced Lysenko as its emblem and falsified harvest statistics as its accounting. The ecosocialist programme therefore presupposes, for its own evidence base, institutions of free inquiry that its historical predecessors abolished and its current documents promise to preserve. Whether the promise would hold under the pressures of a plan failing to meet its throughput budgets is a question the programme's literature treats briefly and its critics treat as settled in the negative, and the fair verdict is that the question is open in exactly the way the planning procedure itself is open: as a design commitment untested against adversity. Both programmes, then, stand on knowledge institutions neither created nor can guarantee, and the comparison's final institutional lesson is that the survival of independent science may matter more to stabilisation than the ownership question this article has spent its length comparing, a conclusion each camp will find deflating in its own way and which the evidence assembled here supports without embarrassment.

## Fossil capital and the discipline of labour

A historical argument from within the Marxist camp complicates both programmes' favourite stories, and comparison is obliged to include it because it changes what the energy transition's difficulty consists in. Andreas Malm's Fossil Capital, published in 2016, reconstructed the original adoption of steam power in the British cotton industry, the founding energy transition of the industrial age, and argued against the standard explanation. Coal fired steam was not adopted because it was cheaper or more productive than the water power it displaced; on the early evidence it was neither. It was adopted because it freed manufacturers from the geographical and collective constraints of water sites, from dependence on flows they could not command and on workforces concentrated where flow and fall dictated. Fossil energy, on this account, entered history as an instrument of labour discipline and spatial freedom for capital, and the property has adhered to it ever since: stock based, transportable, available on demand, indifferent to season and place, and therefore uniquely suited to an economy organised around the command of labour time.

The argument matters to this comparison because it explains the incumbent resistance both programmes must overcome as something more specific than lobbying power. The fossil economy is not merely a set of firms defending margins; it is an energy basis selected, historically, for properties that align with the organisation of production itself, and substituting flow based and site bound alternatives reorganises more than the fuel supply. It reorganises the temporal and spatial freedoms on which two centuries of managerial practice were built. Green capitalism's reply exists and is not negligible: the cost and performance trajectories of the alternatives have now inverted the original calculus in sector after sector, whatever the origins were, and a system adopted for control can be abandoned for cheapness when cheapness changes sides, as the energy section's evidence shows beginning to happen. Ecosocialists answer that the pace of abandonment, measured against the timetable, is exactly what the discipline thesis predicts: the transition proceeds at the speed compatible with the preservation of the relations it should be disrupting, which is the capture argument written in energy history. Malm's own conclusion, that nothing short of a rupture with the property relations of fossil capital will move at the required speed, places him at the ecosocialist end of the dispute, and his book is best read as the rate claim's most sophisticated historical brief, the claim that the observed pace of transition is not an accident of policy but a property of the system doing the transitioning.

Set against it the modernisation reading of the same history deserves one paragraph, because it is the reply a careful green theorist actually gives. Transitions between energy bases have occurred repeatedly inside market societies, wood to coal, coal toward oil, town gas to natural gas, each resisted by incumbents whose properties were equally woven into the organisation of production, each completed when substitutes won on service and cost under rules the state maintained. The origins of an energy basis in labour discipline, on this reading, explain the preferences of its first adopters and nothing about the constraints on its successors, whose preferences are formed by the same cost signals that ended every previous basis. History, once more, supplies both camps with a genuine pattern and neither with a law: the record contains fast transitions and slow ones, state led and market led ones, and the variable that best predicts the fast ones, across the cases, is the presence of public authority willing to impose the costs of change on the incumbents of the old basis, which is an observation both programmes can house and neither can monopolise.

## Unequal exchange and the world ledger

The accounting argument has a theoretical cousin that gives the ecosocialist suspicion its most developed scholarly form, and the comparison should name it. The literature on ecologically unequal exchange, descending from the dependency tradition through the work of Alf Hornborg and the world systems analysts, argues that trade between economies at different levels of development systematically transfers embodied materials, energy, land, and labour time from the poorer party to the richer, while the monetary accounts record the exchange as balanced or as development assistance flowing the other way. The mechanism is the ledger problem at civilisational scale: prices register scarcity and purchasing power, not physical content, so an hour of labour or a hectare of land embodied in exports commands a fraction of the physical return embodied in the imports it pays for. On this analysis, wealthy economies' environmental improvement and poor economies' environmental load are not parallel national stories but two entries in a single account, and the wealthy party's decoupling is partly the system working as designed.

Green capitalism's engagement with this literature is thinner than its engagement with the footprint studies, partly because the unequal exchange framework questions not a policy but the trade order the programme treats as the vehicle of development. The replies that exist run through the development evidence: participation in world trade, whatever its physical asymmetries, has accompanied the fastest reductions in poverty in the historical record, and the economies that withdrew from exchange in the name of self reliance stagnated materially and ecologically together. Ecosocialists counter that the poverty reductions were driven substantially by state directed strategies inside the trading economies rather than by exchange as such, returning the argument to the state's role for the last time in this article. The comparison's grading discipline applies here as elsewhere: the physical transfer findings of the unequal exchange literature are documented and survive the ledger checks; the inference that exchange itself, rather than the terms and institutions governing it, is the operative cause is contested within the literature's own camp; and the programmatic conclusion, that stabilisation requires the wealthy economies to contract their consumption based throughput in absolute terms rather than merely green it, is one the footprint evidence supports regardless of which side of the exchange debate a reader takes. That conclusion, notably, is now stated inside mainstream assessment literature as well as in ecosocialist manifestos, in the language of consumption based responsibility, and the migration of a claim from the margin of a tradition into the centre of the evidence is itself a datum about how this dispute evolves: not by conversion, but by the steady annexation of each side's best findings into common ground, leaving the irreconcilable remainder smaller, harder, and more honestly visible with each decade.

## Two theories of history underneath the evidence

Quarrels this durable are never only about their stated subject, and a comparison that has graded the evidence owes its readers a short account of the deeper commitments that keep the dispute alive when the evidence narrows. Ecological modernisation rests, at bottom, on a theory of modernity: societies organised around science, markets, and plural institutions possess a reflexive capacity, an ability to register their own side effects and build counter institutions against them, and history's direction, though neither smooth nor guaranteed, runs toward the widening of that capacity. The environmental crisis, in this telling, is modernity confronting an unmodernised residue, the treatment of nature as a free storehouse, and the cure is more and better modernisation rather than less. Ecosocialism rests on the rival theory this series exists to expound: history moves through modes of production whose internal requirements set the terms on which every other problem is addressed, the accumulation requirement of the capitalist mode systematically overrides registered side effects whenever the two conflict at the level of the whole system, and the resolution of the crisis therefore passes through a change of mode rather than through the refinement of the existing one. Each theory reads the same environmental record as a chapter in its own narrative, one as evidence of learning under way, the other as evidence of a ceiling repeatedly struck, and the record, being genuinely mixed, confirms both readings often enough to sustain them.

Naming the meta level disciplines the evidence sections retroactively. It explains why occurrence findings, which this article treated as settling a claim, settle so little in the wild: for the modernisation theorist, occurrence is the narrative confirming itself and needs no emphasis; for the historical materialist, occurrence within the favoured economies is a detail the narrative already budgets for, through displacement and the hierarchy of the world system, and the rate findings are where the narrative lives. It also explains the programmes' characteristic temporal moods. Modernisation writes in the tense of the trend, projecting absorption forward. Ecosocialism writes in the tense of the threshold, projecting collision forward. Both tenses are legitimate responses to a record that contains real absorption and real thresholds, and the reader's own judgement about which tense the next decades will vindicate is, in the end, the reader's judgement about the theories of history, informed by but not derivable from the decoupling literature. A comparison that pretended the evidence could substitute for that judgement would be dishonest in the precise way this series exists to avoid.

The meta level also clarifies what each programme would count as its own historical vindication, and stating the vindication conditions is the final service the grid's logic can perform. Ecological modernisation is vindicated if, within the lifetime of its current adherents, the consumption based ledgers of the wealthy economies show sustained reductions at required pace across the flow basket while output and provision rise, achieved by instruments recognisably within its set, without a transfer of production's ownership. Ecosocialism is vindicated if that outcome repeatedly fails under full deployment of those instruments, and if the blockage is traceable, case by case, to the mechanisms its theory names, capture, relocation, rebound, and the political untouchability of affluent consumption, rather than to accident or incompetence. Note that the vindication conditions are asymmetrically demanding in an instructive way: the modernisation condition requires an event, the ecosocialist condition requires a pattern of failures plus a mechanism audit, and events are easier to exhibit than audits are to complete. Note also that both conditions could fail together, leaving a world that stabilises through hybrids neither theory predicted, municipal, cooperative, statist, and market instruments intermixed under emergency pressure, which the wartime precedent makes historically plausible. The theories would then be graded as all grand theories finally are, by the fertility of the questions they taught their successors to ask, and on that grading both have already earned more than their slogans suggest.

## What the comparison leaves standing

Gather the results, since a comparison article should end its analytical work with the state of the question in one place. The impossibility claim falls: absolute decoupling has occurred, on both ledgers, in a number of wealthy economies, and ecosocialist argument that continues to assert it damages the tradition's credibility on the claims that matter. The sufficiency claim also falls, in its slogan form: nothing in the record yet shows the green instrument set delivering required rates across the flow basket and across income levels, and green capitalist argument that proceeds as if occurrence were sufficiency damages its credibility symmetrically. What stands, after both slogans are cleared, is a dispute with three live components. The rate component, where the present evidence favours the ecosocialist warning and the burden sits on green capitalism to exhibit trajectories rather than mechanisms. The generality component, where the evidence is thinnest and the distributional schedule described earlier defines the test both programmes must pass. The institutional component, where capture and competitive erosion face institutional learning and diffusion, where the evidence is episodic, and where the deepest division, who decides what is produced, is a question of political theory that no dataset will close.

That is a narrower dispute than the slogans advertise, and narrowing it is not a small achievement, because the narrowed version can actually be worked on: by researchers who know which component their study addresses, by students who know which claims an examiner will reward them for separating, by citizens who know which questions to put to any programme that asks for their allegiance. Those two instruments, the grid and the distinction, are what this article leaves behind for that work. Neither tool recommends a programme. Both were built by taking the programmes seriously enough to grade their claims one at a time, which is the only respect that matters in print, and the reader who has followed the grading can now supply the recommendation, or withhold it, from a position the advocates on both sides will have to reckon with. The sections that remain are addressed to specific readers and their uses of the topic: the student preparing an answer, the teacher preparing a seminar, and the researcher or journalist preparing to work on the dispute itself.

## Prices versus quantities: the instrument theorists' contribution

Economists inside the green tradition have given the dispute one of its few genuinely formal results, and it cuts in a direction neither camp's slogans predict. Martin Weitzman's celebrated analysis of prices versus quantities, published in 1974, asked which instrument serves better under uncertainty: fixing a price and letting quantities adjust, or fixing a quantity and letting prices adjust. The answer depends on the shapes of the cost and damage curves. Where the damage from overshooting rises steeply, as it does near a threshold, quantity instruments are safer, because a price set too low permits a catastrophic overshoot, while a quantity cap bounds the damage directly and lets the price find its level. The result is pure theory, belonging to no camp, and its implication for climate style problems is plain: where thresholds dominate, caps are the prudent instrument, and the elegant price mechanism carries a tail risk its advocates rarely price in. Carbon taxation's partisans reply with the symmetric point about cost explosions under rigid caps and with hybrid designs, caps with price collars, that import quantity safety into price systems, and the instrument literature has largely converged on hybrids as the technically literate answer.

Watch what each programme does with the formal result, since the handling is diagnostic. Green capitalism absorbs the hybrid gladly, since a cap with a collar is still a market instrument and the programme's identity survives the amendment; the absorption is genuine and shows the programme's capacity to metabolise its own theory's corrections. Ecosocialism reaches past the result to the quantity side's deeper implication: a cap is a physical budget imposed politically, which is planning's elementary form, and the more seriously thresholds are taken, the more the instrument set converges on administered quantities, throughput budgets, and allocation decisions, until the market is operating inside a plan's parameters and the remaining dispute concerns only how much of the interior the plan should govern. That reading is also genuine, and the two readings together reveal the instrument debate as a gradient along which actual regimes are already distributed, with neither pole occupied by any real economy. The comparison's consistent finding reasserts itself in the formal register: the poles are theories, the world is a gradient, and the interesting questions concern movement along it, which mechanisms push regimes toward quantity discipline, which push them back toward price deference, and whose theory predicts the observed movements. On that question the capture literature and the diffusion literature, the ecosocialist and modernisation spines respectively, remain the live competitors, and the instrument theorists, having clarified the stakes, hand the dispute back to the political economists who own it.

## The firm and the workforce

Programmes are executed, or resisted, inside workplaces, and a comparison that stays at the level of systems misses the level at which the transition will actually be negotiated. The workforce of a throughput intensive industry occupies a position neither programme's theory fully digests. Its members hold the skills the transition needs and bear the losses the transition imposes; their organisations have historically defended the industries that employ them against environmental regulation, earning the enmity of environmentalists, and have simultaneously been the only force inside those industries capable of imposing safety and restraint on management, earning a more complicated verdict from history. Ecosocialism claims this workforce as its natural constituency and proposes to convert the defence of jobs into a defence of provision under new ownership, with the plan guaranteeing livelihoods through the conversion. The claim's weakness is the historical record of workers' organisations choosing the industry over the atmosphere whenever the choice was posed as immediate, a choice the programme's theory attributes to the absence of credible guarantees and its critics attribute to interests no guarantee dissolves.

Green capitalism addresses the same workforce through the market for its skills, expecting scarcity of retrofit, installation, and engineering labour to bid its price up and pull workers across without political negotiation, a mechanism that has operated in the deployment booms the energy section described. Its weakness is symmetrical: the mechanism works for the mobile and the young, strands the older worker whose skills are plant specific, and concentrates its failures geographically in the single industry towns whose politics then poison the national programme. Both programmes, examined at the level of the firm, thus reduce to rival answers to a question of credibility: whose promise to the incumbent workforce will be believed, the plan's guarantee or the market's pull? The evidence of past transitions suggests belief follows the presence of institutions that have kept such promises before, local, specific, and boring institutions, funds, compacts, conversion agencies, rather than the grand design of either programme. A comparison written in a series devoted to a tradition that began with the organisation of producers should perhaps end its analytical work on exactly that note of scale: the dispute will be settled, where it is settled, in negotiations most of whose participants have never heard the programmes' names, and the theories will be graded afterwards by historians looking for the mechanisms the negotiators actually used.

## If each side is wrong: the asymmetry of error

Decision under uncertainty is completed by pricing the errors, and the comparison gains a final analytical section by asking what follows if each programme is adopted and its central claim proves false. Suppose green capitalism is adopted generally and the ecosocialist rate warning is right. The instruments deploy, occurrence extends, intensities fall, and the aggregate trajectories still miss the stabilisation thresholds at the pace the physical literature requires. The error is discovered through the ledgers themselves, which is the good news: the failure arrives as data, in consumption based accounts, over years, legible to any government willing to read it. The bad news is temporal. The capital stock built during the decades of error embodies the wrong composition, the thresholds approached during those decades do not recede while policy corrects itself, and the correction, when it comes, must be correspondingly more abrupt than the path not taken, with the political system that presided over the error the least credible agent of its remedy. The error's cost is therefore convex: it grows faster than the delay, because the stock accumulates and the thresholds are stocks too.

Suppose instead the ecosocialist programme is adopted and the green claim about planning proves right in its essentials. The decision procedure is built, the budgets are set, and the knowledge and incentive problems assert themselves: reported figures diverge from physical reality, allocation queues form, the composition of output reflects the planners' information rather than the population's needs, and throughput itself, deprived of honest feedback, is mismeasured by the very apparatus meant to govern it, as the historical planned economies mismeasured theirs. The discovery problem inverts the first case. Under the market programme, error arrives as data because the accounting is independent of the programme's success. Under the plan, the accounting is an organ of the programme, and its corruption is both a symptom of failure and the obstacle to detecting it, which is the deepest structural lesson of the twentieth century record and the one ecosocialism's specifications of independent audit and published information are designed, self consciously, to answer. Whether design can defeat the structural pressure is the unbuilt procedure's largest single unknown, larger even than its allocative efficiency, and this article flags it as the question a serious reader should put first to any ecosocialist text from now on.

The asymmetry between the two errors is real but does not point where partisans expect. The market error is legible and late; the planning error is illegible and, in the historical cases, durable, since the systems that made it also controlled the means of acknowledging it. A risk ordering built on detectability favours the market programme: better a failure one can see. A risk ordering built on the physical thresholds favours urgency over detectability: a visible failure arriving once the threshold's damage is complete is not obviously preferable to a gamble on the procedure that takes the threshold as its binding constraint from the first day. The two orderings encode the dispute's final irreducible difference, which is a difference in what each tradition has learned to fear most from its reading of the twentieth century: the one fears unaccountable power, the other fears unaccountable process, and each can recite the other's catastrophe from memory. Comparison cannot dissolve that difference, and an article that claimed to would forfeit the trust this one has tried to earn. What comparison can do, and has done, is strip from both fears the factual overclaims that inflate them, leaving each reader's judgement smaller, better informed, and unmistakably their own.

## Two end states, described soberly

Abstract programmes become assessable when their destinations are described as places to live, and a short disciplined sketch of each end state, drawn from the programmes' own texts and stripped of both utopia and dystopia, completes the comparison's descriptive work. In the green capitalist end state, the surface of economic life is familiar: firms, markets, wages, prices, brands, and the whole apparatus of consumer choice persist, while the material basis underneath them has been transformed. Energy arrives from low throughput sources as a matter of infrastructure rather than virtue. Goods are durable because standards and service models made durability profitable; repair is a trade again because design for disassembly is mandated and parts are guaranteed. Prices carry the costs that the nineteenth and twentieth centuries externalised, so the cheapest option and the cleanest option coincide far more often than they diverge, and the state's hand is visible mainly at the boundaries: the cap, the standard, the disclosure that lets a buyer see a product's ledger. Living standards, measured as provision, are higher than at the start; measured as throughput, lower; and the system's characteristic anxiety is the one its critics predicted, that the composition of demand, still sovereign, keeps generating new throughput frontiers faster than the instruments retire the old ones, so the end state is not an equilibrium but a permanently contested frontier patrolled by regulators.

In the ecosocialist end state, the surface differs at the points the programme cares about. The large investment decisions, the energy system, the transport network, the housing programme, the retirement schedule of destructive industries, are taken through deliberative institutions whose proceedings and physical accounts are public, and the throughput budget is a constitutional fact of economic life, debated as budgets are debated now. Markets persist in the programme's more careful versions for much of consumer production, but they operate inside the budget, and the firms within them are owned socially, cooperatively, or municipally in varying mixes the texts argue about among themselves. Working time is shorter, provision in the collective services is broader, and the advertising apparatus has contracted to information because the accumulation it served no longer governs. The system's characteristic anxiety is the mirror of the other's: that the deliberative institutions harden into a new bureaucracy, that the budget becomes a target to be gamed, that the population's wants, formed by a century of the old system, strain against the plan's composition until the strain becomes the politics. Each sketch, notice, ends in the anxiety its rival predicts, because each programme's own honest literature ends there too. The destinations are neither heaven nor hell in either telling; they are two distributions of power and risk, described in the same units, between which the evidence informs a choice it cannot make.

## The budget as a physical frame

One scientific framing device has migrated from the physical literature into the centre of this dispute, and both programmes now conduct their arguments inside it, so the comparison should state it cleanly and attribute it correctly. The device is the budget: for certain cumulative pollutants, the physical sciences find that the eventual magnitude of the effect tracks the total accumulated quantity rather than the rate at any moment, so that stabilisation corresponds to a finite cumulative budget of emissions, spendable quickly or slowly but not exceedable without consequence. The finding belongs to the climate science literature and is reported here as that literature's finding, not restated as this article's own physics. Its rhetorical effect on the dispute has been enormous, because a budget converts every question of pace into a question of arithmetic. A trajectory is no longer good or bad in itself; it is a schedule for spending a finite stock, and schedules can be summed and compared. The rate claim, which this article has treated as the ecosocialist case's strong form, is the budget framing translated into polemic: not that the spending cannot slow, but that the present schedule exhausts the stock before the slowing suffices.

Notice what the budget does to the green programme's favourite evidence. Occurrence of absolute decoupling, under a budget, is necessary and radically incomplete as a defence, since a flow can be falling and still be spending the stock too fast, which is why the review literature's insufficiency verdict has the budget's logic behind it. Notice equally what the budget does to the ecosocialist programme's favourite rhetoric. A finite stock to be allocated across economies, sectors, and generations is a planning object of the purest kind, and the ecosocialist who wields the budget inherits its implications: allocation by deliberation at exactly the scales where the programme's decision procedure is least specified and its historical predecessors performed worst. The budget also disciplines the distributional section's schedule, since the claims of building economies are claims on the same finite stock the wealthy economies have largely spent, a fact the ecosocialist literature states as historical responsibility and the green literature answers with the finance and technology transfers discussed earlier. Neither programme escapes the frame, and the frame's neutrality is worth remarking: it was produced by physics, it serves whoever argues carefully within it, and its migration into the centre of a dispute between political programmes is the clearest instance in this whole controversy of a scientific finding restructuring a political argument without adjudicating it, exactly as the scientific claims policy governing this cluster prescribes.

## Greenwashing, and what the accusation actually tests

No word in the public version of this dispute does more work with less precision than greenwashing, and the comparison owes the term a short examination, because the accusation, properly specified, is a testable claim that belongs to neither camp. In its disciplined use, greenwashing names a discrepancy between an actor's communicated environmental performance and its measured performance on a stated ledger: the firm whose advertising celebrates a relative intensity gain while its absolute consumption based footprint rises, the product line marketed on a single favourable flow while the basket deteriorates, the commitment announced for a date beyond every responsible manager's tenure with no instrument attached. So specified, the accusation is an audit, and its targets are as often found among the customers of green rhetoric as among its authors. The term's polemical use is something else: a verdict on the green capitalist programme as such, asserting that improvement inside market relations is definitionally cosmetic. That use begs the question this article has spent its length grading, since the ledgers record real absolute reductions alongside the cosmetics, and a vocabulary that cannot distinguish them is a vocabulary designed to prevent grading.

The symmetry completes itself when the ecosocialist side's own version is specified. A programme can be washed too: the plan whose published figures describe a throughput its territory never achieved, the cooperative or municipal enterprise whose social ownership is advertised while its material accounts go unexamined, the historical regimes whose constitutions proclaimed nature's protection while their ministries poisoned it. Call it planwashing if a label helps; the phenomenon is the same discrepancy between communicated and measured performance, wearing the other programme's colours. The lesson the comparison draws is methodological rather than moral. Both programmes generate legitimating rhetoric as a by product of existing, and the rhetoric is not the programme. The reader's defence is the one this article has practised throughout: demand the ledger, the modifier, the flow, and the rate, of a ministry as of a manufacturer, and let the discrepancy, where it appears, be charged to the actor who produced it rather than to the theory in whose vocabulary the actor was speaking. Theories are responsible for their mechanisms. Actors are responsible for their accounts. Confusing the two responsibilities is the final misreading this comparison corrects, and it is the one on which all public argument about these programmes most reliably founders.

## What Marx contributes to a dispute he never saw

Both programmes claim descent from Marx while disagreeing about the environment, and a series on Marxism should state, briefly and exactly, what the founding texts supply to a controversy their author could not have imagined. The ecological materials are real. The analysis of the metabolic relation between human production and the natural cycles it draws on, the account of capitalist agriculture exhausting the soil while concentrating populations in towns, the insistence that the producers of wealth are labour and the earth together, these are documented in Capital and the notebooks, and the recovery scholarship of recent decades, Foster's work chief among it, established their presence beyond serious dispute. Equally documented are the counter materials: passages celebrating the development of the productive forces as history's engine, treating nature's domination as progress, and framing communism as the resolution of scarcity through abundance of production. The ecosocialist reading elevates the first set into a system and treats the second as period residue. Rival Marxists elevate the second and treat the first as metaphor. The textual dispute is genuine, and this article does not settle it, since its bearing on the comparison is smaller than either camp advertises.

The bearing is smaller because neither programme's fate turns on exegesis. Green capitalism does not need Marx to have been a productivist; its mechanisms stand or fall on the modernisation evidence. Ecosocialism does not need Marx to have been an ecologist; its diagnosis stands or falls on the accumulation argument and the ledgers. What the descent claims actually supply is authority within a tradition, valuable for recruiting adherents and for settling internal precedence, and the history of Marxist controversy warns against mistaking them for evidence about the world. A reader meeting the claim that Marx settles this dispute, from either direction, should apply the article's standing method: ask which empirical claim is being advanced under the authority's cover, and grade that claim on the ledgers. The founder's genuine contribution to the comparison is methodological rather than doctrinal, and it is the contribution this article has used from its first section: the insistence that production, what is made, how, by whom, and under whose decision, is the level at which a society's relation to nature is determined, beneath the prices and rhetoric that float on it. Both programmes, at their best, honour that insistence. The green theorist who traces a flow to the production decision that generated it, and the ecosocialist who traces a decision to the property relation that framed it, are practising the same discipline on successive links of one chain.

## Pioneer economies and the diffusion wager

The generality question has an optimistic variant that deserves its own hearing before the personas speak, because it is the green programme's most concrete answer to the charge that its successes are local luxuries. The variant begins from Jänicke's documented pattern: environmental policy innovation originates disproportionately in a small set of pioneer economies and industries, where capacity, wealth, and organised publics coincide, and then diffuses outward through trade, as exporters standardise on the pioneer's requirements, through emulation, as regulators borrow working designs, and through the multinational firm's preference for single global standards. On this account the concentration of decoupling in wealthy economies is not the programme's embarrassment but its mechanism: the wealthy are the laboratory, and the laboratory's results propagate at falling cost to economies that could never have afforded the experiments. The cost trajectories of the low carbon technologies are the pattern's showcase, since the deployment subsidies of the pioneer phase, paid by wealthy consumers and taxpayers, purchased the learning from which every later adopter now benefits at a fraction of the pioneers' price.

The ecosocialist grading of the diffusion wager is the comparison's now familiar double judgement. As a description of how techniques travel, the pattern is documented and this article has relied on it. As an answer to generality, it has three limits its advocates understate. Diffusion transmits products and standards faster than it transmits the state capacity that generated them, and the receiving economies' regulatory weakness is part of why production relocated there in the first place, so the same integration that spreads the pioneer's standards spreads the pioneer's outsourced throughput, and the net direction is an empirical question the consumption ledgers, not the diffusion literature, must answer. Second, diffusion is slowest exactly where the pioneer model is weakest, in the stubborn sectors of construction, agriculture, and freight, whose local and physical character resists standardisation. Third, the laboratory metaphor conceals a timetable problem: the pioneers took decades over their experiments, and the budget frame allows the rest of the world no such leisure if the aggregate schedule is to hold. The wager's honest statement is therefore conditional: generality will be achieved if diffusion, financed transfer, and capacity building run faster than the building economies' throughput locks in. Every clause of that condition is a policy programme in itself, none is guaranteed by the mechanism, and the ecosocialist is entitled to observe that executing the conditions at the required speed is, once again, a description of direction from the centre, whatever name it travels under. The green theorist is equally entitled to answer that direction through markets and treaties among states is the only direction that has ever actually been executed at world scale. The exchange is the dispute, entire, in miniature, and it is a fitting place for the analytical sections to end.

## Who owns the numbers

Every finding quoted in this comparison descends from a statistical infrastructure that neither programme built and both depend on, and the infrastructure deserves a section, because the dispute's future will be decided as much in its ledgers as in its legislatures. Consumption based accounting is not a measurement anyone makes directly. It is a construction: multi region input output models that trace the production chains of traded goods across the world's economies, built by research groups from national statistical returns of varying quality, reconciled by methods whose assumptions move the results. The footprint databases on which the decoupling verdicts rest are maintained by academic consortia, updated irregularly, and revised in ways that rewrite the apparent past. None of this makes the findings worthless; the constructions are the best available, their error structures are studied by their own builders, and the layered verdict this article reported survives the revisions. But it does mean the dispute's central evidence is an artefact of institutions, funded largely by the wealthy economies whose performance the ledgers grade, and a reader is entitled to ask the ecosocialist question about them too: what are the incentives of the measurers, and would the numbers survive measurers with different ones?

The historical comparison is instructive and cuts unexpectedly. The planned economies' statistics were an organ of the plan, and they lied in the plan's favour as a matter of institutional routine, which is why the environmental record of those systems had to be reconstructed after their fall from physical evidence and foreign observation. The market economies' statistics are produced by offices with professional independence, audited by academic replication, and contradicted in public by rival constructions whenever the stakes justify the effort; the footprint literature itself is largely a product of that adversarial ecology, built by researchers whose careers advanced by showing that the official territorial ledgers flattered their sponsors. The asymmetry is the green world's strongest institutional argument, and it is not about markets: it is about pluralism, the same variable the record section identified beneath the ownership comparison. Ecosocialism's specifications, independent audit, published accounts, free scientific institutions, are attempts to secure that pluralism inside a planned framework, and their adequacy is the unbuilt procedure's test once more. What comparison can add is a grading of the present infrastructure against both programmes' needs. For adjudicating decoupling claims, the infrastructure is adequate and improving; the ledgers exist, the methods are public, the revisions are argued in the open. For governing a transition, by budgets and caps as the instrument section contemplated, the infrastructure is thinner than either programme admits: the constructions arrive years late, at national rather than sectoral or firm level, with revisions large enough to move a year's verdict, and a state that proposed to steer by them in real time would be steering by a wake. Both programmes therefore require, as a precondition neither campaigns on, a statistical build out comparable in seriousness to the one that constructed the national income accounts in the last century. The reader who wants a practical conclusion from this entire comparison could do worse than that one.

## The household frontier

The last site of the dispute is the smallest, and the one where its abstractions become decisions a person can recognise. A large share of wealthy economies' consumption based throughput is attributable, in the footprint constructions, to household demand: the heated and cooled dwelling, the vehicle and the journeys it makes, the diet, the replaced devices and furnishings, the flights. Programmes address this demand from their characteristic directions. Green capitalism addresses it through the prices and standards attached to each purchase, trusting that a household facing honest prices and efficient options will assemble a low throughput life without ever being asked to want one. Ecosocialism addresses it through provision, arguing that the household's throughput is largely a function of the systems it is locked into, the distant workplace, the unwalkable district, the absent transit, the rented dwelling it may not insulate, so that the honest unit of intervention is the system rather than the shopper, and moralising at households for the throughputs their infrastructures impose is the individualised evasion the tradition exists to refuse.

The literature on social reproduction, treated elsewhere in this series, adds the dimension both programmes habitually miss: the household is a workplace, its low throughput options are frequently labour intensive, cooking from ingredients, repairing, caring, sharing, and the labour they require is performed disproportionately by women, inside time budgets already exhausted by paid work. A transition that lowers throughput by re domesticating labour without shortening paid working time or socialising care is a transfer of the transition's cost onto the least powerful, executed in the vocabulary of virtue. Ecosocialism's stronger texts see the trap, since their working time and collective provision planks are designed to close it, and the connection between the ecological programme and the feminist one is load bearing in the tradition this cluster of the series examines. Green capitalism's instruments are blind to the trap by design, since a price cannot ask whose evening the repair will consume, and its defenders answer that blindness is the price of a system that does not presume to organise private life, an answer whose force depends on how much organisation the reader believes the status quo already performs through its infrastructures and advertising. The household, in short, recapitulates the entire comparison at kitchen scale: prices against provision, choice against lock in, the sovereign consumer against the situated one, and a cost that someone will bear, distributed by whoever wins. A dispute that can be recognised at that scale has been understood, and the reader who can conduct it there, in both vocabularies, without reaching for a slogan, is ready for the personas and the questions that close this article.

## Clocks that do not agree

Underneath every section of this comparison runs a disagreement about time, and making it explicit explains the dispute's emotional register better than any doctrinal summary. The physical systems at issue operate on their own clocks: cumulative budgets spent at a rate, infrastructures that lock in flows for generations, ecosystems whose responses lag their causes by decades. Political systems operate on shorter ones: electoral cycles, budget years, the tenure of ministers and chief executives, the quarterly horizon of the firms whose investment decisions the transition needs. Market mechanisms inherit the short clocks, since prices are formed by actors discounting at commercial rates, and the ecosocialist argument can be restated as the claim that a civilisation steered by short clocks will systematically arrive late at long problems, whatever its intentions. Planning proposes to install a long clock by constitutional means: budgets and plans whose horizons are set by the physical problem rather than by the political calendar, defended by institutions designed to outlast the temptations of the cycle. The proposal's difficulty is equally temporal: the long clock must be wound by short clock politics, a founding generation must bind its successors, and the history of such bindings, from constitutional fiscal rules to independent central banks, shows they hold exactly as long as the coalition behind them does.

The green programme's temporal answer is to change the payoffs rather than the clocks. If low throughput investment is made profitable early and durably, the short clocks begin working for the transition instead of against it: the executive's quarter, the minister's cycle, and the firm's depreciation schedule all pull toward the new capital stock, and the long problem is solved by a million short decisions whose incentives were aligned in advance. The record gives this answer real support wherever the alignment was achieved, and gives its limit in the same places: alignment has repeatedly been reversed by a single change of government or a single collapse in the price of the old fuels, and the new capital stock's defenders, though real, have proved weaker than its opponents at the moments of reversal. Ecosocialism reads those moments as the structural point asserting itself. Modernisation theory reads them as design failures, correctable by better entrenchment of the kind independent agencies and treaty commitments supply. The reader will recognise, in this final form of the exchange, that the dispute has narrowed to a single empirical wager about institutions under stress, which is perhaps the most precise statement of the whole controversy this article can offer: whether correction mechanisms built inside market democracies can be made durable enough to outrun physical deadlines, or whether durability itself requires the change of ownership and decision procedure the rival programme proposes. No study answers that. Only the coming decades will, and they will answer it in the ledgers this article has taught its readers to check.

## A worked example: grading one exchange

Methods are best fixed by a single application, so take a specimen exchange of the kind journals and broadcasters stage weekly, with names withheld because the specimen is a type rather than an event. The ecosocialist speaker opens: decoupling is a mirage manufactured by creative accounting; wealthy economies offshore their industries and import their footprints; nothing in the record shows throughput falling in any sense that matters. The green speaker answers: emissions and materials intensity have fallen for decades across the wealthy world while output doubled; the technologies of the transition get cheaper every year under competition; and the alternative on offer ran the dirtiest economies in modern history. The exchange closes, as staged exchanges do, with both speakers unshaken and the audience's prior allegiances confirmed. Grade it with the article's instruments and every sentence sorts itself. The mirage claim fails the occurrence test on the consumption based evidence, as the accounting section showed, and the speaker's own preferred ledger refutes the strong form of the sentence even as it supports the weaker displacement point buried inside it. The mirage claim's salvageable content is a rate claim, and the speaker would have won the round by making it. The green answer's first clause trades on intensity where totals are the question, fails the modifier check, and its second clause, though true of the energy sector, generalises a favourable case across the basket the sectoral sections warned against. Its third clause is a true statement about historical planning deployed as if it tested a democratic procedure that has never operated, which the record section graded as a category error with a genuine burden of proof hiding inside it. Both speakers, finally, omitted the only questions that would have advanced the matter: at what rate, on which ledger, across which flows, and what observation would change your mind?

Now stage the repaired exchange, to show what the dispute sounds like when conducted at the level of its evidence. The ecosocialist speaker opens with the rate claim in its strong form: consumption based trajectories in the wealthy economies are falling too slowly against the budget arithmetic, the stubborn sectors show no mechanism yet that closes their gap, and the generality problem is untouched by any deployment to date. The green speaker concedes the rate findings as stated and answers at the level of mechanism and trajectory: the sectors that have received the full instrument set at sustained stringency are the sectors where the trajectories bend, the set has never been deployed whole for a generation anywhere, and the fair test of the programme is its performance under its own prescription rather than under the diluted versions politics has so far supplied. The ecosocialist replies that the dilution is the datum: a prescription that politics reliably dilutes has its dilution inside its evidence, and the programme must grade itself by its deployable form. The green speaker answers that the same standard convicts the rival procedure in advance, since its deployable form under real politics is the bureaucracy its texts disown. At this point the exchange has reached the genuine floor of the dispute, the institutional wager named in the previous section, and it has done so in four speeches instead of never. Nothing has been settled, and everything has been clarified, which is the realistic ambition of public argument on a question of this structure. The reader who can run the unrepaired exchange through the grid, locate each failure, and hear the repaired version's floor being reached, possesses the comparison in usable form, and the personas that follow are addressed to putting that possession to work.

## The first decade of either programme

Programmes are usually compared as destinations; comparing their openings is fairer, because the opening is where most programmes in history have died. The green programme's first decade, as its own strategists describe it, is a sequencing exercise in credibility. Prices and standards must be announced with trajectories long enough for investors to trust and gentle enough for incumbents to survive the first cycle, then tightened on a legislated schedule that future governments inherit as a commitment rather than a choice. Subsidy for research and deployment front loads the visible benefits, jobs, cheaper services, cleaner local air, so that the coalition defending the programme assembles before the costs concentrate. The decade's characteristic failure, visible in several real attempts its literature studies without naming here, is the reversed sequence: costs imposed first on dispersed households, benefits promised later, and a politics of backlash that repeals the schedule before the learning curves bend. The programme's theorists know the failure mode by heart, which is why the modern literature of green growth reads increasingly like a manual of political sequencing wearing economic dress.

The ecosocialist first decade is a construction exercise of a different order. Before its mechanisms can operate, the decision procedure itself must be built: the physical accounting apparatus described earlier, the deliberative institutions that will set budgets, the forms of social ownership through which investment will be directed, and the guarantees, of livelihood, of information, of audit, that distinguish the design from its historical predecessors. Each element is a precondition of the others, and the decade's characteristic failure is the emergency shortcut: a government elected on the programme, facing the old economy's resistance and the new institutions' absence, reaches for command methods to keep its promises, and discovers that the shortcuts are habit forming and the promised institutions are postponed past the point of return. The programme's serious literature circles this failure mode with a wariness its pamphlets lack, debating transitional institutions, workers' control as a school of administration, municipal experiments as prototypes, with an honesty that deserves acknowledgment from its critics. Set the two openings side by side and the comparison's final structural symmetry appears. The green programme's opening risk is repeal: the old order votes it back. The ecosocialist programme's opening risk is substitution: the new order's emergency form replaces the design. Both risks are instances of the same law, that transitions are governed by the politics they occur in rather than by the texts they descend from, and a reader grading the programmes as practical proposals should grade the openings, where the law operates, more heavily than the destinations, where it is politely assumed away.

## Words that smuggle verdicts

Close the analytical work with a short audit of the dispute's vocabulary, since several of its hardest working words carry concealed verdicts and a reader alerted to them reads everything else in this article more safely. Clean and dirty, applied to energy and industry, import a moral hygiene into a physical account: a source is clean whose ledgered flows are low, on a stated basket and basis, and the adjective should never be allowed to substitute for the ledger. Green, applied to a product, a fund, a government, or a growth path, is the dispute's master smuggler, since it certifies the conclusion the comparison exists to test; this article has used it only inside the programmes' own names, and readers should notice how rarely careful writers in either tradition use it of their own claims. Efficiency sounds like a virtue beyond argument and is a ratio instead, whose numerator and denominator must both be asked after: an efficient process that expands the scale of its own application can raise the totals the ratio was meant to reduce, which is the rebound finding in lexical form. Sustainable, the era's most exhausted adjective, means nothing until a flow, a rate, and a period are supplied. Natural fares worst of all in Marxist company, where its use as a standard of judgement contradicts the tradition's own teaching that the human relation to nature is always socially mediated, and ecosocialist texts are at their weakest exactly where they forget it.

The audit cuts the other way as well, into the critical vocabulary. Exploitation and accumulation are terms of art inside the Marxist analysis, with definitions a reader can check, and they smuggle only when exported into general argument as if their definitions were common ground. Degrowth's own label performs the reverse smuggle, naming a programme by the variable it expects to fall and thereby conceding the headline to its opponents, a self inflicted wound its authors debate among themselves. Even decoupling, this article's disciplined term, smuggles when its modifiers are dropped, which is why the repair work of the middle sections consisted mostly of restoring modifiers that rhetoric had stripped. The reader's rule is simple and portable. Whenever a word in this dispute makes an argument feel finished, ask what measurement, definition, or decision the word is standing in for, and do the unfinished work it was concealing. That rule, plus the ledger, the modifier, and the concession, is the whole critical apparatus the comparison has built, and it transfers intact to every adjacent controversy in this series, from the value theory of the early clusters to the state theory of the middle ones.

## The late Marx and the newest turn of the argument

The newest development inside the ecosocialist camp changes the comparison's balance in ways neither programme's founders scripted, and a guide published in this series should register it. Kohei Saito, a Japanese scholar whose archival work on Marx's natural scientific notebooks extended the recovery project Foster began, argued in Marx in the Anthropocene, published in 2020, that the late Marx of the notebooks and the Russian correspondence moved beyond the productivism of his middle period toward a vision of post capitalist society organised around stationary material throughput, communal regulation of the metabolism with nature, and the wealth of free time: in Saito's phrase, a degrowth communism. The argument matters inside Marxism because it relocates the tradition's ecological warrant from scattered passages to the founder's final research trajectory, and it matters to this comparison because it sharpens ecosocialism's demand side beyond anything Löwy or Kovel required: not merely a different composition of production under social decision, but a deliberate renunciation of further aggregate throughput growth in the wealthy economies as a matter of principle, defended as Marx's own conclusion.

The reception inside the tradition has been divided in instructive ways. Sympathetic readers treat Saito's archival case as the recovery project's completion and his programme as ecosocialism with its textual foundations finally poured. Critical Marxists answer on two fronts: the exegesis, where the notebooks' openness is held to support several conclusions and Saito's is judged the most convenient rather than the most compelled, and the economics, where a principled renunciation of growth is said to abandon the developing world's claims and the tradition's own analysis of how capitalism, for all its destructiveness, built the productive capacity any successor must inherit. Green capitalism's theorists watch the quarrel with an interest they rarely confess, since the Saito turn relieves them of their easiest target: an ecosocialism that promises abundance through planning was always vulnerable on the record, whereas an ecosocialism that renounces throughput growth openly is a clearer opponent, one whose disagreement with the modernisation programme is finally total and therefore finally honest. The comparison's grading applies to the new turn as to the old claims. Textually, the notebooks support a Marx more ecologically serious and less productivist than the tradition's mid century image, and Saito's scholarship has shifted the burden on that question. Programmatically, the renunciation thesis inherits every open problem this article has catalogued, the unbuilt procedure, the unpriced politics, the generality schedule, plus one of its own: the wealthy electorate asked to ratify a stationary throughput as a permanent condition rather than a transitional discipline. It is the ecosocialist programme with the comfort removed, and readers should respect it for exactly that, whatever they conclude about its prospects.

## Why the comparison refuses to close

A word, before the personas, about the refusal this article has practised from its opening section, since readers formed by advocacy literature may mistake it for indecision. The refusal to recommend either programme is not a hedge but a finding: the dispute's empirical components, graded honestly, leave its institutional wager open, and an article in an educational series serves its readers by mapping the openness exactly rather than by annexing their judgement. There is also a disciplinary reason internal to the Marxist tradition itself, which this series has honoured in every cluster: the tradition's own method distinguishes between the analysis of a system, where rigour is possible and conclusions can be earned, and the advocacy of a successor, where the conclusions are wagers dressed in the analysis's authority whenever the distinction is blurred. Marx's refusal to write recipes for the cook shops of the future, as he put it in the afterword to Capital, was the founder's version of the same discipline, and the ecosocialist and modernisation literatures are each at their best when they observe it and at their worst when they borrow certainty across the line. The four questions grid, the impossibility and rate distinction, the six checks for a decoupling claim, the vocabulary audit: these are instruments for readers who will meet this dispute again in worse company than this article, and instruments outlast verdicts. The verdict each reader reaches with them is that reader's own, which is where, in a comparison between programmes that both claim to speak for the future, it properly belongs.

## On being wrong in public

A dispute's health can be measured by what its participants do when the evidence moves, and this one offers a final, revealing test in its two camps' handling of unwelcome findings. When the consumption based literature confirmed absolute decoupling in the very economies where ecosocialist argument expected pure displacement, the serious ecosocialist response was absorption under protest: the finding was accepted, the emphasis moved to rate and generality, and the impossibility formulation began its slow retreat from the tradition's careful texts, surviving mainly in its slogans. When the review literature returned its insufficiency verdict on rates, the serious modernisation response had the same shape: the finding was accepted, the emphasis moved to deployment and design, and the sufficiency formulation began its own retreat from the careful texts toward the slogans, where it too survives. The symmetry is the healthiest fact in this entire comparison. Both traditions contain an inner circle that grades itself against the evidence and an outer ring that performs certainty for recruitment, and the distance between circle and ring, in each case, is the distance this article has exploited throughout, by citing the careful texts against the slogans of their own side. Readers can perform the same operation on any future contribution, from either camp, in one step: ask whether the author is writing as the inner circle or the outer ring, a judgement the modifiers, the ledgers, and the concessions will announce within a page.

The operation generalises beyond this topic, and students of the series will recognise it from earlier clusters: wherever a tradition argues with itself across generations, the careful stratum is where its knowledge lives, and the slogans are where its recruitment lives, and confusing the strata is the beginner's characteristic error in reading any school of thought. Marxism's own history is the standing exhibit, since the distance between the founder's texts and the orthodoxies erected on them was the founding problem of half the traditions this series has examined. The ecological dispute is young enough that its strata have not yet hardened into separate churches, and a reader meeting it now has the advantage, rare in intellectual history, of watching the sorting happen in real time, study by study, concession by concession. Keeping the ledgers straight while it happens is a small, learnable discipline, and it is the whole of what this article has asked.

## Three readers in office

Abstract programmes land differently at different desks, and a short translation for three office holders shows the comparison's practical grain. A minister reading it finds the sequencing problem wearing her diary: the instruments exist, the trajectories can be legislated, and the binding constraint is the inheritance problem, whether a schedule announced in her term survives her successors, which is why the modern green literature she is briefed from speaks so insistently of commitment devices, independent authorities, and benefits front loaded into the first parliament. A mayor finds the dispute arriving as the built environment section in person: a housing stock to retrofit with borrowed money and borrowed labour, a transit decision that will lock flows for generations, and two programmes whose national arguments resolve, at his desk, into the same short list of projects competing for the same constrained budget, to be defended in the same public meeting. A manager of a firm in a stubborn sector finds both programmes describing her constraints from outside: the price signals that reach her are real but insufficient against her sector's physics, the planning alternative would reach her as a budget she did not set, and her actual decisions, about plant, product, and process, are made in the seam between the two, in the hybrid territory this article has mapped at every level. The three readers share a discovery the programmes' manifestos rarely advertise: at the point of execution the dispute is not between two systems but over the proportions of one mixture, and the argument worth having, in the ministry, the town hall, and the boardroom alike, is the argument about proportions, conducted flow by flow and ledger by ledger, which is the argument this comparison has tried to equip.

## The commons: the institution neither programme owns

Between the market and the plan, the two poles of this comparison, sits a third institutional tradition that neither programme generated and both have learned to cite, and its existence complicates the comparison's geometry in a productive way. Elinor Ostrom's research programme, summarised in Governing the Commons, published in 1990, documented communities across the world governing shared resources, fisheries, irrigation systems, forests, grazing lands, through neither private property nor state command but through crafted local institutions: boundaries defined, rules fitted to local conditions, monitors accountable to the users, sanctions graduated, and the whole arrangement nested, at larger scales, inside wider jurisdictions that recognised its authority. The findings unsettled the era's governing parable, in which shared resources were doomed without privatisation or Leviathan, by exhibiting the excluded middle in working order. Ostrom herself resisted every attempt to recruit the work as a panacea, insisting that the design principles were diagnostics of durability rather than recipes, and that scale was the standing problem: the documented successes govern resources whose users can know one another's conduct, while the atmosphere, this dispute's largest commons, assembles billions of users who cannot.

Each programme's absorption of the commons literature reveals its reflexes. Green capitalism reaches for the parts that resemble its own instruments: defined rights, monitored use, priced access, and treats Ostrom as a theorist of institutional design within a broadly market order. Ecosocialism reaches for the parts that resemble its own hopes: collective self government of production by its users, decisions taken by those who bear their consequences, and treats Ostrom as evidence that the democratic governance of material life is a human capacity with a record, not a speculation. Both reachings are partly justified and partly theft, since the commons tradition is genuinely distinct: it trusts neither the price system nor the central plan but the rule crafting of situated communities, and its findings about monitoring, graduated sanction, and the fatal effects of externally imposed blueprints embarrass the administrative habits of both camps. For this comparison the tradition's service is to mark the geometry as incomplete. The four questions grid asks who decides what is produced, and its two columns give the programmes' answers; the commons literature documents a third family of answers, federated, polycentric, and local, whose performance at the scale of whole economies is as untested as the ecosocialist procedure and whose performance at the scale of the resources it has actually governed is better documented than either programme's. A stabilisation path assembled historically, rather than deduced, would likely draw on all three families in proportions no theorist now predicts, and the reader who keeps the third column in mind will read the next decade's experiments with better questions than the partisans of either pole can supply.

The commons tradition also returns a quiet verdict on the dispute's oldest sub argument, the knowledge problem, since its documented communities solve, at their scale, the problem both programmes claim the other cannot: they generate the local knowledge a central plan would lack, through the users' own monitoring, and they enforce the restraint a price system would outsource, through sanctions the users themselves impose. Scale remains the unbridged gap, and honesty requires leaving it unbridged in print: no one has operated atmospheric governance on commons principles, and the federated designs proposed for it are, like the ecosocialist procedure, drawings rather than buildings. But a comparison that began with two programmes ends more truthfully with three institutional families on the table, and the reader's judgement, which this article has refused to pre empt throughout, is correspondingly better supplied.

## Maintenance: the transition nobody photographs

Both programmes speak the language of building: new plant, new grids, new industries, new institutions. The physical transition, examined at ground level, speaks a different language, that of maintenance, and the discrepancy between the two vocabularies conceals a sector where the comparison's categories behave unexpectedly. Most of the throughput an economy will ever commit is already embodied in its standing stock: the buildings to be insulated rather than replaced, the vehicles to be kept in service or retired early, the industrial plant to be retrofitted, repurposed, or scrapped, the soils and water systems to be repaired from earlier damage. Decisions about the stock are less photogenic than decisions about new construction and more consequential in aggregate, because the stock is enormous, its turnover is slow, and every choice between repair and replacement is a choice between the throughput of making and the throughput of keeping. A transition strategy is, in large part, a maintenance strategy with better publicity.

The programmes meet the stock from their usual directions and are each surprised by it. Green capitalism's price mechanism handles replacement decisions fluently, since a carbon price tilts every repair or replace calculation at the margin, but it handles the stock's collective dimensions badly: the decision to maintain a district's fabric, a railway's unfashionable branch, a water system's unfashionable margin of safety, has no single buyer, and maintenance deferred is invisible in the accounts until it fails, a bias the accounting literature calls the catastrophe of the unglamorous and which afflicts market and plan alike. Ecosocialism's deliberative machinery is well shaped for the collective dimensions, since a community can vote to maintain what no market would, yet it meets the stock's sheer granularity as its planning met the housing retrofit: millions of particular judgements about particular objects, each requiring local knowledge of condition and use, each trivial alone and decisive in sum. The historical planned economies were notoriously poor maintainers, their incentive systems rewarding new construction that could be inaugurated over repair that could only be continued, and the bias is a standing warning that ownership does not cure the glamour problem. The market economies' maintenance record is better where property is long held and worse where assets are traded, which is a finding about time horizons rather than about ownership, and it returns the comparison to the clocks section's variable once more.

There is a cultural sediment under the maintenance question that both programmes inherit and neither has fully examined. Industrial modernity taught its populations to read the new as progress and the kept as failure, an aesthetic the advertising system monetised into the replacement cycles the consumption section described. A stabilised economy inverts the aesthetic: durability becomes the visible virtue, repair a skilled and honoured trade, the patina of the maintained object a sign of competence rather than of poverty. Green capitalism expects the inversion to arrive through prices and fashion, and can point to the markets where it has begun, the premium now attached to the repairable and the long guaranteed. Ecosocialism expects it to arrive through the changed purposes of production itself, since goods made under a throughput budget advertise their longevity as their makers' pride. Either way, the inversion is a change in what populations admire, and changes of that kind have historically been slower than any technology curve and faster than any plan, arriving through the accumulated prestige of examples rather than through argument. The comparison notes the point because it marks the boundary of what programmes, of any kind, can do: they can arrange incentives and decisions, and the culture that makes their arrangements feel normal or absurd forms elsewhere, in practices their texts can bless but not command. A reader who finishes this article persuaded that the boundary matters has understood something about both programmes that their authors, in their different ways, are reluctant to say.

## Sufficiency, the third word in a two word dispute

Policy discussion in the wealthy economies has quietly acquired a third term alongside the two this article has compared, and mapping it clarifies both programmes by showing what each leaves out. Sufficiency names the strategy of reducing demand for throughput intensive services themselves, not by deprivation but by arranging provision so that less throughput delivers the same or better outcomes: housing located where daily life needs no car, goods designed to be shared rather than owned in idle duplicate, working patterns that substitute time for commuting, dietary norms that treat the most intensive foods as occasion rather than staple. The concept developed in European policy research as a complement to efficiency and consistency, the three strategies of a well known German taxonomy, and it has migrated into assessment literature because the arithmetic of the other strategies kept failing to close the gap the budget frame describes. Efficiency cheapens each unit of service; sufficiency asks how many units the service, properly arranged, actually requires.

Watch how each programme receives the newcomer, since the reception exposes their instincts. Green capitalism absorbs sufficiency awkwardly. Demand reduction sits uneasily inside a framework whose legitimacy rests on consumer sovereignty, yet its instruments reach sufficiency obliquely whenever a standard removes a wasteful option or a price makes the intensive choice expensive enough to reconsider, and its urban and product policies often pursue sufficiency outcomes while declining the word. Ecosocialism absorbs sufficiency warmly in principle, since deliberated needs and the critique of manufactured demand are its native territory, yet faces the reciprocal awkwardness: sufficiency administered from above is indistinguishable, to the citizen experiencing it, from rationing imposed by the bureaucracy the programme promises not to rebuild, and its literature's insistence that sufficiency be chosen rather than assigned returns it to the persuasion problem every programme in this article has met at the door of the affluent household. The concept's real contribution may be diagnostic rather than programmatic. It identifies the residual that neither efficiency nor ownership touches directly, the volume of demand itself, and it forces both programmes to say, in their own vocabularies, whether that volume is a datum to be served or a variable to be governed. This article has shown both of them governing it in practice while describing themselves otherwise, and sufficiency is the name of the place where that shared secret is kept.

## Risk, insurance, and the market as a sensor

One mechanism belongs to neither programme's manifesto and yet does steady work in the actual transition, and a comparison attentive to mechanisms should record it before closing. Insurance and finance price risk continuously, and as the physical consequences of a destabilised climate have moved from projection to claims experience, the pricing of risk has begun transmitting information that no ministry issued and no manifesto persuaded anyone of: premiums rising or cover withdrawn in exposed districts, lenders repricing long lived assets in vulnerable locations, investors demanding disclosure of physical exposure as a condition of capital. Green capitalists claim the mechanism as their worldview in miniature, prices aggregating dispersed knowledge faster than deliberation can, and there is force in the claim: a premium is a forecast nobody has to believe in politically, revised annually, enforced by the simple refusal to write the policy. Ecosocialists read the same mechanism as evidence of the system's limits in their purest form, since risk pricing protects balance sheets rather than places: it withdraws cover from the exposed, raises the cost of shelter for the poor, and prices the abandonment of districts and coastlines with the same neutrality it prices their defence, leaving the collective decision, defended or abandoned, exactly where it always was, waiting for a politics.

The mechanism also illustrates, in a single sector, the article's recurring finding about hybridity. The insurance signal operates only inside a scaffold of public decisions: flood maps drawn by public science, building codes that determine what may be insured where, public reinsurance absorbing the tail risks private capital refuses, and courts deciding whether disclosure duties exist. Remove the scaffold and the sensor goes blind or predatory; remove the sensor and the scaffold plans in ignorance of the prices its decisions create. Programmes that promise purity, in either direction, misdescribe the machinery already running, and the reader who has followed the comparison through its sectors will by now expect exactly that: energy, buildings, food, finance, each one a harness of market signal and collective decision in proportions the theories argue about and the practice keeps renegotiating. The dispute between ecosocialism and green capitalism is real, and its stakes are the largest a political economy can carry. But it is conducted, on the evidence assembled here, across a frontier that practice crosses daily in both directions, and the programmes are best understood not as descriptions of two worlds but as rival theories of which direction across that frontier leads, in the end, to a stabilised one.

## For students and exam candidates

Build your answer on the impossibility and rate distinction, because it converts a slogan exchange into three gradeable claims: occurrence, rate, and generality. State the occurrence finding first, with its ledger: absolute decoupling has happened in several wealthy economies on consumption based as well as territorial measures, so any answer asserting impossibility loses marks against the evidence. Then argue rate and generality, where the serious dispute lives, citing the review literature's insufficiency verdict and the concentration of success in wealthy economies and favourable sectors. Structure the comparison with the four questions grid, one paragraph per row, and give each programme its strongest theorist: Löwy and Kovel against Mol, Spaargaren, and Porter. The series treatment of [ecological Marxism exam answers](/2017/11/15/ecological-marxism-exam-answers/) shows the expected format for this cluster, and the grid itself is worth keeping as a working card: the [Marxism study notes on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) include a companion card laying out the four questions with the evidence and falsification columns, designed to be reviewed the night before an examination. Close every answer by stating what observation would change your verdict; examiners reward the sentence, and it costs you nothing.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

This comparison is the clearest available teaching case for a general lesson: the strength of a claim depends on its precise formulation, and movements habitually defend the strong formulation of a claim whose evidence supports only the weak one. Set the impossibility and rate distinction as the seminar's spine. In the first hour, have students extract from one ecosocialist and one green text every empirical claim made, and sort them into occurrence, rate, and generality before any evaluation is permitted; the sorting alone dissolves most of the staged disagreement. In the second hour, assign the four questions grid as group work, requiring each group to fill the falsification column for the position it privately opposes, which forces the steelmanning this article practised. The accounting material teaches a transferable skill: give the class one decoupling headline and the six checks from the section on reading a claim, and let them discover how few published claims survive the first three. Finish with the cross examination exercise, each side posing the other's hardest question, and grade the questions rather than the answers; the ability to state what would settle a dispute is the transferable outcome, and it outlasts the topic.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Your professional exposure in this dispute is the accounting basis, and the working rule is absolute: never publish a decoupling claim, in either direction, without naming its ledger, its flows, and its period. The territorial figures in official releases describe where throughput occurs; the consumption based constructions describe whose demand drives it; the two diverge most in exactly the wealthy, service heavy economies whose performance is the story, so a report built on the convenient release will invert the finding a footprint study supports. When a source asserts that decoupling is achieved or that it is a myth, the follow up questions write themselves from the six checks: absolute or relative, which flows, which economies, at what rate against the budget arithmetic, and what would the source accept as disproof. Historians will find the underworked seam of the literature in the mechanism studies this article kept citing: how specific regulations survived or died, how specific technology costs actually fell, how the statistical constructions themselves were built and revised. The aggregate quarrel is overpopulated; the mechanisms are where reputations remain to be made, on both sides of a dispute that will be graded by the ledgers for decades.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between ecosocialism and green capitalism?

Ecosocialism holds that ecological stabilisation requires social ownership and democratic planning of production, because the compulsion to accumulate cannot be regulated away. Green capitalism holds that market mechanisms, pricing, regulation, and technological innovation can achieve stabilisation within existing property relations. The difference is therefore not whether the state should act, since both programmes assign it a large role, but where the decisive power sits: with deliberative bodies deciding what is produced, or with dispersed market actors operating inside rules the state sets. The empirical dispute between them reduces to decoupling, its rate, and its generality.

### Q: What is ecological modernisation?

Ecological modernisation is the body of theory assembled from the nineteen eighties onward by Joseph Huber, Arthur Mol, Gert Spaargaren, Martin Jänicke, and their colleagues, holding that prosperity and environmental improvement can advance together because the institutions of a market economy are capable of being rebuilt to deliver the two jointly. It treats ecological criteria as modernisation's next stage, comparable to the earlier absorption of labour standards and public health, and it supplies green capitalism with its theoretical form. Its toolkit follows from that diagnosis: prices that charge for carbon, binding standards of performance, state support for research and for getting new plant built, and compulsory disclosure, with innovation by competing firms as the engine of improvement.

### Q: Is absolute decoupling possible?

Yes, on the evidence of the empirical literature. Absolute decoupling, in which environmental throughput falls while output grows, has occurred in several prosperous economies over long stretches, with the finding holding up under consumption based accounting, the stricter test that charges an economy for the goods its residents consume from factories overseas. The stronger thesis, that such separation is impossible in principle within capitalism, is a staple of ecosocialist rhetoric and is not borne out. What remains open, and genuinely disputed, is whether observed decoupling has proceeded at a sufficient rate and whether the pattern extends beyond the sectors and income bands where it was recorded.

### Q: Has decoupling actually happened anywhere?

Relative decoupling, in which throughput rises more slowly than output, is documented thoroughly and over long periods in economy after economy. Absolute decoupling has also happened: the literature records wealthy economies whose throughput fell in absolute terms while output grew, including on consumption based measures tracing traded goods back to the households and firms whose purchases ultimately occasioned them. Two qualifications define the honest version of the finding. The success is concentrated in particular economies, sectors, and flows, with material footprint more stubborn than emissions of regulated pollutants. And part of the territorial improvement in importing economies reflects relocation of production rather than reduction, which consumption based accounting exposes and partly, not wholly, explains.

### Q: What is ecosocialism?

Ecosocialism is the position, given programmatic form by Joel Kovel and Michael Löwy in the opening years of the twenty first century and developed by writers such as Ian Angus and Daniel Tanuro, that the ecological crisis grows out of the capital relation itself. Because production for exchange under competitive pressure must accumulate, throughput expansion is a systemic tendency rather than a policy error, and regulation inside such a system is worn down by capture and by jurisdictions competing against one another for mobile investment. The remedy proposed is collective ownership of the main productive assets, with the big choices about production settled through democratic planning, oriented to use values, genuine needs, and expanded free time rather than to accumulation.

### Q: Can regulation restrain the growth imperative?

The evidence supports a divided answer. Regulation has repeatedly restrained specific flows at the point of production, imposing labour, health, and environmental standards on unwilling firms and holding them across decades, which is the green capitalist record at its strongest. Governing the sheer scale of throughput is another matter: there the compliance of any one firm settles nothing, each government hesitates to move first for fear of watching investment depart, and the experiment has barely been tried at the stringency stabilisation would demand. Ecosocialists argue the asymmetry is structural: the regulated hold exit options, and treasuries lean on privately directed investment for jobs and tax receipts. Green capitalists answer that capacity varies with institutional design, and that variation itself proves the point.

### Q: Who decides what gets produced in each model?

In the green capitalist model, no one decides the composition of production directly. It arises from consumer demand, expressed through purchasing power, acting together with firms' investment decisions, inside rules, prices, and standards the state sets. The model's defenders treat that dispersion as an epistemic virtue, since prices aggregate knowledge no planning body can assemble. In the ecosocialist model, the large choices, how much throughput in total, which harmful industries to wind down, where investment should flow, are settled democratically by the producers and communities who live with the results, with detail left to workplaces. The dispute is therefore about decision procedures as much as outcomes, and it is the least empirical row of the comparison.

### Q: What is the strongest case for green capitalism?

The strongest case, as its theorists state it, rests on three exhibits. First, the historical pairing of enrichment with cleanup: as incomes rose in the wealthy economies, the pollutants that regulators targeted fell outright, and they fell through the ordinary machinery of market societies rather than against it. Second, the behaviour of technology costs once deployment began in earnest, which outran every official projection and vindicated the programme's central wager that competition, given a price and a standard to aim at, innovates faster than committees schedule. Third, the comparative record of the planned states, whose environmental performance trailed that of their market rivals on the measures neither side disputes. Together the exhibits argue that green capitalism's mechanisms are not conjectures but demonstrated patterns, while the rival programme's central procedure remains entirely untested.

### Q: What is the strongest case for ecosocialism?

The strongest case is not the claim that decoupling is impossible, which the evidence contradicts, but a conjunction of better supported claims. Absolute decoupling, where recorded, has moved too slowly, on the review literature's assessment, to satisfy stabilisation requirements, and it is concentrated in wealthy economies and favourable sectors. Part of the wealthy record is displacement, as consumption based accounting shows. Regulation faces documented capture and relocation pressures, and the sectors where throughput is intrinsic to the service, agriculture, construction, freight, heavy industry, have resisted the substitution that powered the favourable cases. On that evidence, the system's correction mechanisms, however real, show no settled tendency to arrive in time.

### Q: What evidence would settle this dispute?

Different components of the dispute settle differently. Occurrence is already settled, in favour of possibility, by the consumption based studies. The rate component would be graded by sustained comparison of consumption based trajectories, across the basket of major flows, against the trajectories stabilisation conditions imply; a long run of wealthy economies meeting required rates under the green instrument set deployed at recommended stringency would change the present verdict, as would repeated failure under that deployment. The generality component turns on economies still building their infrastructure arriving at modern levels of provision while spending far less throughput than the pioneers spent. The institutional components, capture against learning, and the workability of democratic planning, can only be settled historically, by attempts and their audited results.

### Q: How does the impossibility and rate distinction grade a decoupling claim?

The distinction separates two propositions that rhetoric fuses. The impossibility claim says such separation cannot happen while capital rules production; a single recorded instance on honest accounting refutes it, and several exist. The rate claim says the separation, where recorded, has run too slowly, covered too little of the economy, and touched too few of the flows that matter to satisfy stabilisation conditions; that is a claim about trajectories, graded by comparing observed consumption based rates against required ones. Grading any decoupling claim therefore begins by asking which proposition it is. A claim of occurrence answers only the first, and a claim of insufficiency only the second, and an argument that wins on one while implying the other has committed the fusion the distinction exists to prevent.

### Q: What should a student separate between relative and absolute decoupling?

A student should separate the variable, the direction, and the test. Relative decoupling concerns intensity: throughput per unit of output falls while the total may still rise. Absolute decoupling concerns the total: throughput falls while output grows, which is the only form that answers to stabilisation conditions, since receiving systems register loads rather than intensities. The test then divides again, by ledger, because absolute decoupling on a production based account may vanish on a consumption based one, and by sufficiency, because an absolute fall can still be too slow. An examination answer that keeps intensity, total, ledger, and rate in four separate compartments will outscore one that treats decoupling as a single fact to be asserted or denied.

### Q: Why does the accounting basis decide the decoupling result?

Because the two bases assign the same physical flows to different economies. The production basis counts throughput at the place where it physically occurs, so an economy that closes its factories and imports its goods records an improvement. The consumption basis instead follows final demand, charging the factory's throughput to the people who ultimately consume what it makes, in whatever economy they reside, so the importing economy keeps its burden on the ledger. For economies that shifted toward services while importing manufactures, the verdict can reverse between the bases. Part of the celebrated decoupling of wealthy economies is displacement, measurable only on the consumption basis; part survives it, which is why the basis must be named before any decoupling claim can be graded at all.

### Q: How does the four questions grid order the two programmes?

The grid takes the comparison apart into the four issues where the programmes genuinely part company, and it refuses to let either side answer only its favourite. First comes the physical issue, whether flows can fall outright while value rises, quickly and broadly enough to count. Then the institutional issue, whether public authority can hold capital to account despite lobbying and the threat of exit. Then the procedural issue, where the authority to shape output is located. Last comes the standing of technology, engine or instrument. For every row the grid demands three things side by side: what each programme answers, what the record shows, and what new observation would force a revision. Used well, it turns a comparison into a grading exercise: a programme unable to name the observation that would change its answer on a row has stopped making a claim about the world on that row.

### Q: What separates ecological modernisation from ecosocialism as programmes?

Three separations matter. The diagnosis differs: ecological modernisation treats environmental damage as a design and information failure within institutions capable of learning, while ecosocialism treats it as the expression of an accumulation dynamic that no design correction reaches. The remedy differs accordingly: pricing, standards, subsidy, and disclosure steering dispersed decisions, against social ownership directing the major decisions through democratic planning. The theory of history differs underneath both: modernisation as a widening reflexive capacity, against modes of production whose requirements override registered side effects. The programmes can converge on individual instruments, and in the stubborn sectors their practices visibly migrate toward each other, but their accounts of why the crisis exists, and therefore of what would end it, remain opposed.
