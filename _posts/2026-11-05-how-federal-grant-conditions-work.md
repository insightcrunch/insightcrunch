## The Bargain at the Center of Federal Grants

Conditions on federal grants work as the terms of a voluntary exchange in which Congress offers money for a defined public purpose and a state, by accepting the money, accepts the obligations that Congress wrote into the statute authorizing the program. The structure sounds simple, and the phrase federal grant conditions describes nothing more exotic than those obligations, yet disputes over the structure fill a large share of modern federalism litigation because the money is rarely small and the obligations are rarely confined to bookkeeping. A highway program carries construction standards and labor rules. An education program carries reporting duties and civil rights commitments. A health program carries eligibility rules that reshape budgets far beyond the federal contribution itself. Each condition therefore raises two distinct questions that courts keep separate even when litigants blend them together. The first question concerns authorship and clarity, namely whether Congress imposed the disputed obligation at all and whether Congress said so in language a state could understand before signing on. The second question concerns freedom, namely whether the financial stakes grew so large that acceptance stopped being a choice and became compulsion in substance.

The thesis that organizes the doctrine follows directly from that separation. A federal grant condition is lawful only when it survives two separate audits, because Congress, not an agency, must have spoken the condition clearly in the statute, and the bargain must leave the state a real choice, which is why grant fights are usually decided on statutory authority before constitutional coercion is ever reached. The order matters. A court that finds no statutory condition has no occasion to weigh coercion, and a court that finds a clear condition within a modest program has little reason to question voluntariness. Most litigation ends at the first audit for a practical reason as well, since statutory questions have textual answers that judges can verify, while coercion questions require judgments about budget pressure that courts approach with caution.

The bargain framing also explains why states seldom win by arguing that a condition feels burdensome after acceptance. The doctrine protects the moment of decision rather than the comfort of performance. A state that could read the terms, price the terms, and refuse the funds received the protection the law promises, even when performance later proved expensive or politically awkward. Conversely, a state that accepted funds under one set of written terms holds a strong claim when federal officials later announce a different set and threaten repayment or termination. The wrong in that setting is not hardship alone. The wrong is alteration of a deal after one side performed in reliance on the original text.

None of this means the federal government holds unlimited power to purchase policy outcomes that direct regulation could not compel. The spending power is broad, and courts apply several of its limits with notable deference, but the limits exist and each one guards a different value. Clarity guards fair notice. Relatedness guards the connection between a national expenditure and a national purpose. The bar on independent constitutional violations guards rights that no bargain may surrender. The coercion limit guards the voluntariness that makes the bargain metaphor honest rather than decorative. A condition that fails any one limit falls, however worthy the underlying program may be.

## The Constitutional Source: One Clause and Two Theories of Spending

The spending power rests on a single sentence in Article I, Section 8, Clause 1, which grants Congress the power to lay and collect taxes, duties, imposts, and excises, to pay the debts and provide for the common defense and general welfare of the United States. The text joins taxing and spending in one clause, and that joinder produced the foundational disagreement of spending doctrine. One reading treated the general welfare phrase as a limit, so that Congress could tax and spend only in service of the other enumerated powers listed later in the same section. The other reading treated the phrase as a separate grant, so that Congress could tax and spend for any purpose that served the general welfare, even when no other enumerated power reached the subject directly. The disagreement was never academic in the narrow sense, because the narrow reading would have confined national fiscal policy to a catalog written for an agrarian republic, while the broad reading allowed Congress to address industrial, educational, and public health problems through fiscal means long before courts accepted comparable regulatory reach.

James Madison defended the narrower reading, arguing that the general welfare language took its meaning from the enumerated powers that followed and that an independent reading would convert a government of defined powers into a government of general legislative authority. Alexander Hamilton defended the broader reading, arguing that the power to appropriate money for the general welfare stood on its own footing and was limited by the requirement of generality rather than by the later enumeration. The dispute persisted through the nineteenth century without a definitive judicial resolution, largely because federal spending remained modest and because Congress often tied appropriations to subjects, such as armies, roads, and the postal service, that other clauses plainly covered.

The resolution came in United States v. Butler (1936), which adopted the Hamiltonian reading and held that the spending power is not confined by the direct grants of legislative power found elsewhere in the Constitution. That holding gave Congress a fiscal instrument of remarkable breadth. Yet the same decision struck down the Agricultural Adjustment Act program before the Court on a different ground, reasoning that the processing tax and benefit payments invaded powers reserved to the states under the Tenth Amendment by regulating agricultural production. The pairing of a broad power holding with an invalidating judgment captures the transitional character of the era. The Court accepted Hamilton on the scope of spending while still policing the boundary between fiscal inducement and direct control through a reserved powers lens that later decisions abandoned.

Helvering v. Davis (1937) then supplied the deference that made the broad power workable. Upholding the Social Security old age benefits program, the Court held that judicial review of the general welfare judgment belongs largely to Congress, and that courts should disturb a congressional choice only when the choice appeared arbitrary on its face. The decision shifted the practical center of spending law. Once the power existed independently and its purpose element received deference, future contests moved away from the existence of the power and toward the conditions attached to its exercise. That movement explains the modern shape of the field. Litigants rarely deny that Congress may spend for a broad national purpose. Litigants instead contest who wrote a condition, how clearly the condition was written, whether the condition belongs to the program, and whether the price of refusal left any genuine option. Clause 1 supplies the authority. The later doctrine supplies the discipline.

## Why Courts Treat Spending Legislation as a Contract

The contract analogy entered modern doctrine through Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman (1981), a dispute over a federal statute that announced findings and a bill of rights for persons with developmental disabilities in programs receiving federal assistance. Pennsylvania officials resisted the claim that the statute imposed binding obligations, and the Court agreed that the provisions at issue stated congressional hopes rather than enforceable conditions. The reasoning mattered more than the outcome. The Court explained that legislation enacted under the spending power operates much like a contract, in that a state accepts federal funds subject to conditions in return for the funds, and that legitimacy of the arrangement rests on the state exercising its choice knowingly, aware of the conditions and accepting them voluntarily. Because the exchange rests on informed assent, Congress must speak with a clear voice when it intends to impose a condition, so that a state can understand the consequence of participation before the state commits its budget and its institutions.

The analogy does real analytical work in three directions. First, it allocates the burden of clarity to the drafter with the greater power, which is Congress. A state reviewing a grant statute reads the text as an offer, and ambiguities in an offer do not silently expand the obligations of the party accepting it. Second, the analogy fixes the relevant moment at acceptance. Conditions disclosed in the statute before acceptance bind the recipient, while conditions announced afterward resemble a unilateral modification and receive skeptical treatment. Third, the analogy shapes remedies. Contract thinking directs attention to the terms the parties actually made, to the expectations those terms created, and to the difference between enforcing a promised performance and imposing a penalty that no party contemplated at formation.

The analogy also has limits that courts acknowledge without abandoning the framework. A state is not a consumer signing a standard form, and Congress is not a private seller bound by every interpretive habit of commercial law. Statutes carry public purposes, administrative machinery, and amendment procedures that no private contract replicates. Later legislatures may revise programs prospectively, and agencies administer conditions across dozens of recipients whose circumstances differ. The contract model therefore functions as a discipline on interpretation rather than as a complete theory of intergovernmental relations. Courts borrow the principles that protect notice, reliance, and voluntary assent, and courts leave aside doctrines, such as excuse or market damages, that would fit public programs poorly.

Cummings v. Premier Rehab Keller P.L.L.C. (2022) demonstrated how seriously the Court takes the remedial side of the analogy. The case asked whether emotional distress damages were available under federal antidiscrimination statutes enacted through the spending power, including Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act. The Court held that such damages were unavailable because a funding recipient, at the moment of acceptance, would not have understood emotional distress liability as a traditional contract remedy for breach, and the governing statutes gave no clear notice of that exposure. The holding extends the logic of the earlier case from the existence of a condition to the consequences of violating one. Notice must cover not only the duty but also the liability that follows from breach, at least where the liability departs from the ordinary contract measure. For grant administrators and state budget officers, the combined teaching is concrete. The statute defines the deal, the deal defines the duties, and familiar contract principles define the exposure, unless Congress writes a different and unmistakable rule into the text that recipients accept.

## Element One: The Condition Must Come From Congress, Not From an Agency

The first audit in any grant dispute asks a question of authorship. A condition binds a state only when Congress imposed the condition, because the spending power belongs to Congress and the bargain that courts enforce is the bargain that the statute offered. Agencies administer spending programs, often with deep expertise and broad delegated discretion over details, but administration differs from legislation. An agency may fill gaps that a statute leaves open, prescribe forms and timetables that a statute authorizes, and interpret ambiguous language within the bounds that the statute sets. An agency may not invent a substantive obligation that the statute omits, attach a funding penalty that the statute does not contemplate, or convert guidance written for convenience into a condition whose violation ends a program. The distinction protects states, and it also protects agencies, since an agency that acts within a genuine delegation can point to the statute as the source of authority when a recipient objects.

### Can a federal agency add conditions to a grant that Congress did not write?

No. An agency may administer a condition that Congress enacted and may explain how an enacted condition applies, but the agency may not create a new obligation, expand an enacted obligation beyond the statutory text, or attach a funding consequence that the statute does not authorize.

In practice, the audit begins with the statutory text and moves outward only as far as the text permits. Counsel examines the authorizing statute, the appropriations language, and any cross-referenced provisions that the statute incorporates, and counsel asks whether the disputed requirement appears there or follows from language that a reasonable recipient would read as imposing it. Program regulations receive attention next, with a critical sorting step. A regulation that restates or implements a statutory condition carries the authority of the statute behind it. A regulation that announces a preference, a best practice, or an expectation without statutory anchoring carries only persuasive weight, and a state that declines the preference has not breached the grant. Guidance documents, dear colleague letters, frequently asked question sheets, and grant announcement boilerplate sit still further from the statute, and experienced recipients treat each document as a claim about authority rather than as authority itself. The question in every instance remains the same, namely which enactment by Congress authorized the requirement and the consequence.

A useful comparison appears in questions about who authorized a federal money structure, such as [how the universal service fund works](/2026/10/18/how-universal-service-fund-works/), where the analysis starts with the statute that created the mechanism rather than with the agency that runs it. The interpretive backdrop sharpened with Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo (2024), which overruled the framework that had directed courts to defer to reasonable agency interpretations of ambiguous statutes. After that decision, courts exercise independent judgment in deciding whether an agency acted within statutory authority, and agency views receive respect proportional to their persuasive power rather than obedience owed to a delegate. For grant conditions, the shift reinforces the first audit. An agency reading that expands a condition must now persuade a court that the reading is the best reading of the statute, not merely a permissible one. States gained a stronger argument against creative conditions, while agencies retained every power that Congress conferred in genuine delegations. The audit therefore ends where it began, with the enacted text as the measure of the bargain.

## Element Two: The Condition Must Be Stated Unambiguously

Authorship alone does not complete the first audit, because a condition that Congress wrote in language no recipient could decode fails the bargain test as surely as a condition that an agency invented. The clear statement rule associated with Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman (1981) holds that Congress must impose a funding condition unambiguously, so that a state can exercise its choice knowingly, cognizant of the consequences of participation. The rule resembles other clear statement doctrines that protect federalism values, yet its justification is distinctive. The justification is fairness inside a voluntary exchange rather than suspicion of federal power in general. A state official deciding whether to accept funds must be able to read the statute and identify the obligations, the standards of performance, and the exposure that accompanies failure. Language that announces a general policy, expresses a congressional preference, or directs an agency to pursue a goal does not satisfy the standard unless the language also notifies recipients that compliance is the price of the money.

The rule operates with more severity than ordinary statutory interpretation, and the severity is deliberate. In an ordinary case, a court gathers meaning from text, structure, purpose, and history, and a plausible reading often suffices. Under the clear statement rule for spending conditions, plausibility does not suffice. A recipient is not required to guess correctly among competing readings, to anticipate the interpretation an agency might announce years later, or to treat a hortatory provision as a binding covenant because a court might later discern a conditional intent. The burden sits with Congress to remove doubt at enactment, when removal costs little, rather than with states to insure against doubt through litigation, when the cost is high.

Arlington Central School District Board of Education v. Murphy (2006) shows the rule applied to a remedial detail with significant money at stake. Parents who prevailed in proceedings under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act sought reimbursement for expert fees as part of their costs, pointing to a provision that allowed courts to award reasonable attorneys fees as part of the costs to a prevailing parent. The Court held that the statute did not provide the clear notice required to condition state participation on liability for expert fees, because the text mentioned attorneys fees and said nothing about experts, and a state reading the statute would not understand expert fees to be included. The decision turned away an interpretation that many readers might have considered natural as a matter of policy, since expert testimony often drives the outcome of special education disputes and its cost burdens families unevenly. Doctrine, however, asked a different question. The question was whether Congress had told states, in unmistakable terms, that acceptance of the funds carried that liability. Silence on the specific item answered the question.

The practical reach of the unambiguity element extends beyond litigation into drafting and acceptance decisions. Federal drafters now append explicit conditions, definitions, and remedies sections to grant statutes, often stating that a requirement applies as a condition of receipt in so many words. State officials, in turn, read grant statutes with attention to the difference between mandatory condition language and aspirational language, and state officials document their reading at acceptance, since that contemporaneous understanding frames any later dispute. Few conditions fail in published decisions, because Congress learned to write clearly, but the rule shapes every negotiation that precedes enactment and every compliance manual that follows it.

## Element Three: The Spending Must Serve the General Welfare

The third element in the conventional ordering is also the element on which courts intervene least, and understanding why requires a return to the settlement reached during the twentieth century. Once United States v. Butler (1936) located the spending power in the Hamiltonian reading of Clause 1, the general welfare requirement remained as the textual limit attached to that power. Congress may spend only for the general welfare, not for purely private advantage or for a purpose so parochial that no national characterization fits. In principle, the requirement could have supported searching judicial review of appropriations, with courts weighing whether a bridge, a research program, or a subsidy served the nation or merely a district. In practice, the Supreme Court declined that role almost immediately, and the declination has held through every subsequent shift in spending doctrine. The element therefore operates as a background commitment of the system, real in theory and almost never decisive in litigation between a state and the federal government over grant conditions.

Helvering v. Davis (1937) supplied the governing attitude in the Social Security litigation, holding that the general welfare judgment rests primarily with Congress and that courts should intervene only when a congressional choice lacks any plausible foundation. The reasoning drew on institutional competence. Legislators hold hearings, weigh regional and sectoral effects, and answer to voters for the aggregate pattern of expenditure, while courts encounter a program through a single record framed by parties with adverse interests. A judge equipped to decide whether a statute violates a right is poorly equipped to rank national needs or to declare that assistance for one group serves the general welfare while assistance for another group does not. The deference also reflects the generality concept itself, which concerns the character of the purpose rather than the wisdom of the expenditure. A program may be inefficient, generous to a fault, or targeted at a problem that better tools could address, and the program still pursues a purpose of national scope that the clause requires.

Several consequences follow from the near dormancy of this element, and grant litigants ignore them at their peril. A state challenging a condition rarely gains anything by attacking the welfare basis of the underlying program, because courts treat that attack as an invitation to second guess appropriations politics. The stronger challenges lie elsewhere, in clarity, relatedness, independent constitutional bars, and coercion, where judicially manageable standards exist. Keeping the elements separate prevents a common briefing error in which strength on the easiest element disguises weakness on the element that will actually decide the case.

The element retains one genuine function beyond its symbolic place in the framework. It marks the outer boundary of the spending power in cases involving expenditures that lack any public purpose characterization at all, such as a transfer designed solely to enrich private parties with no articulated public benefit. Courts and commentators agree that such an expenditure would present a different problem from an unwise subsidy, even if litigation reaching that boundary is rare to the point of near invisibility. The rarity itself instructs. Congress drafts spending legislation with purpose statements, findings, and programmatic structures that articulate a public rationale, and that drafting habit keeps programs comfortably inside the boundary. For the law of grant conditions, then, the general welfare element functions less as a weapon for challengers and more as a reminder of the constitutional premise beneath the entire structure, namely that federal money moves for national purposes, and that the conditions attached to the money derive their legitimacy from those purposes rather than from federal power standing alone.

## Element Four: The Condition Must Be Related to the Program It Funds

The relatedness element, often called germaneness, requires that a condition on federal funds bear a relationship to the federal interest in the particular program that the funds support. The requirement sounds modest, and courts apply the requirement with restraint, yet the requirement performs work that no other element performs. Clarity ensures that a state understood the deal. Relatedness ensures that the deal concerns the program being funded rather than any policy that Congress wishes to advance by leveraging money appropriated for another subject. Without such a limit, a popular and generously funded program could become a vehicle for conditions across the entire range of state governance, and the formal voluntariness of acceptance would mask a practical power to purchase compliance on subjects that share nothing with the expenditure. The element therefore protects the structure of program specific bargaining. Each grant stands as an exchange about its own subject, and conditions must belong to that exchange in a way that a reviewing court can articulate.

The standard of relationship is deliberately loose. A condition need not be indispensable to the program, and Congress need not prove that the condition represents the least restrictive means of achieving a program goal. The connection must be genuine rather than contrived, and courts look for a plausible account of how the condition protects the expenditure, advances the program purpose, or safeguards the integrity of the funds.

Sabri v. United States (2004) illustrates the strength of the federal interest at the core of the element. The case involved a federal prosecution for bribing an agent of an organization receiving federal program benefits, and the defense argued that the statute exceeded congressional power because the particular bribe had no demonstrated connection to federal funds. The Court rejected the argument, reasoning that the statute served the federal interest in protecting program funds from corruption and that Congress could act on the reality that money is fungible, so that corruption anywhere inside a recipient organization threatens the integrity of the federal investment. Although the case arose in a criminal setting rather than in a state challenge to a grant condition, its reasoning informs relatedness analysis throughout spending law. Courts accept that Congress may guard an expenditure against diversion and fraud without tracing individual dollars, and that acceptance widens the zone of conditions that count as related. A condition aimed at the honesty, solvency, or administrative competence of the recipient will almost always clear the element, whatever program supplies the funds.

The element acquired its named place in the modern framework through South Dakota v. Dole (1987), the decision that organized the limits on conditional spending into the sequence that courts and commentators still follow. That decision upheld a condition connecting highway funds to state drinking age policy, and its treatment of relatedness, pressure, and independent constitutional bars deserves separate and detailed attention as the synthesis of everything the earlier cases built. For the present element, the significance lies in the naming itself. Relatedness moved from an assumption embedded in program design to an articulated limit that litigants must plead and courts must answer, even when the answer favors the government. A state that can show no genuine connection between a condition and the funded program holds a real argument, distinct from complaints about clarity or coercion, and Congress drafting a novel condition must be ready to state the connection in terms of the program rather than in terms of general policy ambition.

## Element Five: No Condition May Require a State to Surrender an Independent Right

The fifth element draws a line that the other elements do not reach. A grant can rest on a valid source of congressional power, speak with complete clarity, serve the general welfare, and relate closely to the program it funds, and still fail because the price attached to the money asks the recipient to give up a right that the Constitution protects independently of the spending decision. The condition, in that setting, does not merely define the terms of a federal program. It purchases the surrender of a protection that the government could not take away by direct command, and the doctrine treats that purchase as a constitutional problem in its own right.

Kathleen M. Sullivan gave the doctrine its most systematic scholarly treatment in Unconstitutional Conditions, published in the Harvard Law Review in 1989. Sullivan showed that the problem recurs across fields that rarely speak to one another, from speech and religion to property and procedural protection, and that the cases resist any single mechanical formula. The recurring insight is distributional as well as doctrinal. A condition can burden a right indirectly in ways that escape the scrutiny a direct prohibition would attract, and the burden often falls hardest on recipients with the fewest alternatives to federal money.
The clearest illustration in the spending context remains Federal Communications Commission v. League of Women Voters (1984). A federal statute barred any noncommercial educational broadcasting station that received federal funds from engaging in editorializing, even when the station used entirely private money for the editorial and even when the editorial appeared outside the federally supported activity. The Supreme Court struck the condition down. The reasoning turned on reach. Congress could define the contours of the program it chose to fund, and a station remained free to decline the money, but the statute did not stop at the boundary of the funded program. It silenced speech the station financed on its own, in a forum the station itself maintained, and that extension beyond the program converted a funding definition into a restriction on protected expression.

The distinction between defining a program and reaching beyond it does much of the work in this area, and it explains why element five so often decides cases that the earlier elements leave open. A condition that governs how grant money is spent, what records the recipient keeps, or what standards the funded activity must meet operates inside the bargain the recipient accepted. A condition that governs what the recipient may say with separate funds, whom the recipient may associate with outside the project, or what procedures the recipient must abandon in unrelated proceedings operates on rights the bargain never legitimately included. Courts examining that second kind of condition ask whether the government has achieved indirectly, through leverage over funding, a result that direct regulation would have forbidden.

The element also protects the structure of the choice itself. If surrender of the right were treated as freely consented to in every case, no condition would ever fail, because every recipient could in theory refuse the money. The doctrine rejects that purely formal answer. Consent given under financial pressure still counts as consent for many conditions, which is why element six addresses coercion separately, but consent does not cure a condition whose object is the right itself. Where the burdened activity lies outside the program and enjoys independent constitutional protection, the government must justify the condition as something more than an efficient use of its purchasing power. Failing that justification, the condition falls even though the grant, the purpose, and the statement of terms were all in order.

## Element Six: Persuasion Must Stop Short of Compulsion

The sixth element guards the voluntariness that makes the entire conditional spending framework intelligible. The framework rests on an analogy to contract. Congress offers money on stated terms, a state weighs the offer against the obligations, and acceptance creates duties that courts can enforce precisely because the state chose them. That logic collapses if the offer is one the state cannot realistically refuse. At that point the condition no longer resembles a bargain between separate sovereigns. It resembles a command delivered through the treasury, and the federal structure that conditional spending is supposed to respect has been bypassed rather than honored.

Steward Machine Company v. Davis (1937) drew that line and, on the facts before the Court, refused to find that it had been crossed. The case concerned the federal unemployment tax scheme, which credited employers for contributions paid into a state unemployment fund that met federal standards, so that a state declining to establish such a fund left its employers paying the full federal tax with no corresponding local benefit. The pressure was real and the Court did not pretend otherwise. Every tax and every grant exerts some influence on state choices, and influence alone could not be the test, because a holding that pressure equals compulsion would have disabled the spending power altogether. The Court upheld the scheme, reasoning that the states retained a genuine choice and that motive or temptation, however strong, is not the same as coercion.

The opinion nevertheless preserved the boundary it declined to enforce on those facts. Justice Cardozo, writing for the majority, treated the difference between inducement and compulsion as a difference in kind that a future case might present in sharper form. Later decisions repeated the reservation in nearly ritual fashion. Case after case upheld the challenged condition while stating that at some point pressure turns into compulsion, and that a condition crossing that point would exceed the spending power. The repetition mattered even when no condition failed under it. The reservation told Congress that the voluntariness element had content, told states that an extreme case could be brought, and told lower courts to attend to the scale of the threatened loss rather than to the label Congress placed on the program.

Measuring that scale requires attention to proportion and to reliance. A condition that risks a small fraction of one program budget leaves a state with room to decline, to raise replacement revenue, or to restructure the affected service. A condition that risks a large share of a state budget, built up over decades of cooperative administration and woven into obligations the state owes to hospitals, schools, or beneficiaries, presents a different question. The coercion inquiry therefore examines the amount at stake, the age and entrenchment of the program, and whether the new condition attaches to money the state already depends on or only to money offered for the first time.

That focus keeps element six narrow without making it empty. Ordinary political pressure, unfavorable publicity, and the loss of a competitive advantage do not amount to compulsion, and states remain responsible for hard choices that federal offers routinely force upon them. The element intervenes only when the financial stake grows so large, relative to the state and to the program, that the formal option of refusal ceases to describe the real situation. Section thirteen returns to the case in which that threshold was finally held to have been crossed. Until that case arrived, element six functioned mainly as a stated limit awaiting facts extreme enough to activate it, and its long dormancy shaped how lawyers argued and how Congress drafted.

## How the Tests Were Built Between 1936 and 1987

The framework did not arrive as a framework. Between 1936 and 1987 the Supreme Court decided a sequence of cases, each prompted by a different institutional problem, and each leaving behind a piece of doctrine that later opinions assembled into a single structure. Reading the sequence chronologically shows why the elements take the shape they do. Each step answered a failure mode that the preceding arrangement had exposed, and each step preserved the central settlement of the New Deal era, namely that Congress may spend and condition broadly while states retain protections that sound in clarity, voluntariness, and independent rights.

United States v. Butler (1936) supplied the foundation and, paradoxically, the first invalidation. The Court adopted the broader Hamiltonian reading of the spending power, under which Congress may tax and spend for the general welfare beyond the subjects enumerated elsewhere in the Constitution, and then struck down the agricultural processing scheme before it on Tenth Amendment grounds. Institutionally, the decision separated two questions that earlier argument had fused. The scope of the power was one question, and the permissible means of exercising it against state regulatory domains was another. That separation made the later doctrine possible, because every subsequent case could concede the breadth of the power while still contesting the condition attached to its exercise.

Steward Machine Company v. Davis (1937), decided one year later, shifted the practical center of gravity. By upholding the unemployment compensation scheme, the Court signaled that cooperative federalism financed through the treasury would be a durable feature of national government rather than an emergency improvisation. States learned that participation, not resistance, would be the normal posture toward federal programs, administrators learned to draft conditions as the terms of participation, and Congress learned that the grant instrument could achieve coordination that direct regulation of state unemployment systems could not have achieved. The coercion reservation in the opinion marked the outer boundary of that new normal without disturbing it.

Oklahoma v. Civil Service Commission (1947) extended the logic from program design to the conduct of state personnel. The federal Hatch Act restricted political activity by state employees whose work was financed in part with federal funds, and Oklahoma challenged the condition after a state highway commissioner faced removal for partisan activity. The Court upheld the condition. The step mattered because the condition reached a matter, the political conduct of a state official, that lay close to the core of state self government, and the Court still treated the arrangement as a bargain the state had accepted by taking the funds.
Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman (1981) added the discipline that rapid program growth had made necessary. By the late twentieth century, federal grants carried dense layers of obligations, and disputes increasingly turned on whether a state had actually agreed to the duty a federal agency or a private plaintiff sought to enforce. The Court held that conditions on federal funds must be stated unambiguously, so that a state accepts them knowingly, on the model of a contract whose terms both parties can read in advance. The institutional effect fell on drafting practice, as Congress and the agencies learned to write obligations into the statute itself rather than rely on implication.

By 1987 the pieces were in place for assembly. The power was broad, the voluntariness limit was stated, conditions could reach program administration, and clarity was mandatory. What the doctrine still lacked was a single opinion gathering the limits into one list and applying them to a concrete condition. That opinion arrived in the drinking age litigation considered next, and its list became the standard citation for the whole field.

## South Dakota v. Dole and the Drinking Age Bargain

South Dakota v. Dole (1987) is the case lawyers cite first in this field, not because its facts were extreme but because its opinion organized the doctrine into a form that briefs, statutes, and later judgments could all use. The dispute began with the National Minimum Drinking Age Act of 1984, through which Congress directed the Secretary of Transportation to withhold a percentage of federal highway funds from any state that permitted the purchase or public possession of alcoholic beverages by persons younger than 21. The withholding operated under 23 U.S.C. Section 158. A state with a lower drinking age stood to lose five percent of its highway allocation in the first affected year, with the share rising thereafter. South Dakota, which allowed 19-year-olds to purchase beer with low alcohol content, challenged the condition as an exercise of power Congress did not hold.

Chief Justice Rehnquist, writing for the majority, treated the case as an occasion for synthesis. The opinion gathered the limits developed across the preceding half century and listed five of them in sequence. The spending must serve the general welfare, a judgment to which courts defer substantially. Any condition must be stated unambiguously, so that states can exercise their choice knowingly. The condition must relate to the federal interest in the particular program. The condition must not require the recipient to surrender an independent constitutional right. And the financial inducement must not pass the point at which pressure turns into compulsion. Each entry traced to earlier authority, and the opinion derived much of its force from showing that settled propositions, placed side by side, disposed of the case.

Application followed quickly. The general welfare judgment belonged to Congress. The withholding provision stated its terms plainly enough that no state could misunderstand the consequence of a lower drinking age. Relatedness received the fullest discussion. The Court reasoned that the condition bore directly on safe interstate travel, a purpose at the heart of the highway program, because drivers younger than 21 moved across state lines, drank in states with lower ages, and returned on the highways the federal funds built and maintained. The condition therefore regulated the same evil the program addressed. No independent constitutional provision barred the bargain, and the financial stake, five percent of one category of federal funds, amounted to relatively mild encouragement rather than compulsion. South Dakota remained free to keep its drinking age and pay the stated price.

Justice O'Connor, in dissent, rejected the decision at the relatedness step and at the level of federal structure beneath it. In that view, the condition was not a rule for how highway money should be spent but a regulation of the sale and possession of alcohol, a subject the Twenty-first Amendment reserves substantially to the states. A condition so aimed either related to the program only at a level of generality that would let Congress regulate any subject touching travel, or it amounted to an assertion that Congress could purchase control over a core state power that direct legislation could not reach. The dissent also warned that the majority formulation invited Congress to attach ever broader policy conditions to large funding streams, confident that a court applying deferential relatedness review would sustain them.

The case settled the governing test while leaving its hardest applications for later. Its five percent figure became a benchmark against which larger threats would be measured, its relatedness reasoning became the passage critics cite when a condition reaches far from its program, and its dissent became the starting point for arguments that a formally valid condition can still displace the balance the federal structure protects.

## Six Tests Every Federal Grant Condition Must Pass

The table settles which audit a challenged condition failed, so that a dispute about a large program can be reduced to the single element on which the condition actually founders.

| The test | What it asks | Leading authority | What failure looks like |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Source in Congress | Whether an enumerated power or the spending power authorizes the grant and its terms | United States v. Butler (1936) | A condition resting on no constitutional source at all |
| Clarity of statement | Whether the recipient could read the obligation plainly before accepting the funds | Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman (1981) | A duty discovered in implication after the money was spent |
| General welfare purpose | Whether the spending serves a broad public purpose as Congress judged that purpose | Helvering v. Davis (1937) | An appropriation serving only a private or parochial end |
| Relatedness to the program | Whether the condition connects to the federal interest in the funded program | South Dakota v. Dole (1987) | A condition governing conduct the program never touches |
| Respect for independent rights | Whether compliance requires surrender of a separately protected constitutional right | Federal Communications Commission v. League of Women Voters (1984) | A funding term silencing speech financed with private money |
| Voluntariness of the bargain | Whether the state retained a real choice to decline the offer | National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012) | A threat so large that refusal would wreck a state budget |

Reading the table well means resisting the habit of treating the six rows as interchangeable expressions of judicial discomfort with a condition. Each row assigns the defect to a different institutional actor and points toward a different remedy. A failure of clarity is a drafting failure, and Congress can cure it by writing the obligation into the statute in unmistakable language before the next funding cycle. A failure of relatedness is a design failure, and the cure is structural rather than verbal, because no amount of plain language makes an unrelated condition germane to its program. A failure on independent rights cannot be cured by either technique, since the defect lies in what the condition demands rather than in how clearly or how closely it demands it. The table therefore does practical work before any court is involved.

Use on a real dispute begins with sequence. The source and clarity rows come first because they can be resolved from the statute book without contestable judgments about policy, and a condition that fails either row never requires a court to reach the harder questions. The general welfare and relatedness rows follow, with the understanding that the first invites deference and the second invites argument about the fit between a condition and the program it rides on, so the quality of the record connecting condition to purpose often decides the outcome. The final two rows carry the greatest consequence and demand the fullest factual development. A rights claim requires identification of the protected activity, the funds that finance it, and the distance between that activity and the program. A voluntariness claim requires budget figures, reliance history, and a concrete account of what refusal would cost the state in services and obligations. A challenger who builds the record in that order presents distinct holdings rather than a general appeal to federalism.

## The Medicaid Expansion and the First Finding of Coercion

The coercion element produced statements of principle and no invalidations until National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012) changed that record. The case concerned the Affordable Care Act and its expansion of Medicaid, the joint federal and state program that finances health care for persons with low incomes. The statute extended eligibility to a broad new category of adults and offered federal payment covering nearly the whole cost of the expansion population at first, declining to a still generous share in later years. The difficulty lay in the penalty for refusal. A state that declined the expansion risked losing not only the new expansion funds but every dollar of federal funding for the Medicaid program the state already operated.

That structure transformed the litigation. 26 states challenged the expansion, and readers tracing the case through [the NFIB v. Sebelius explainer](/2011/05/15/nfib-v-sebelius-explained/) can follow how the coercion question moved from a supporting argument to the ground on which the expansion was ultimately reshaped. Medicaid had grown since 1965 into one of the largest items in every state budget, financed jointly but administered by the states, with hospitals, nursing facilities, physicians, and millions of beneficiaries arranged around its rules. Withdrawal of all federal Medicaid money would have removed a substantial share, in many states more than 10 percent of the entire state budget, from a system the state could not replace from its own revenue on any realistic timetable.

### How much money can the federal government threaten to withhold before a condition becomes coercion?

Chief Justice Roberts treated a threatened loss exceeding 10 percent of a typical state budget as crossing the line, especially where the funds supported a long established program on which states, providers, and beneficiaries had built settled arrangements over decades of continuous participation.

Chief Justice Roberts supplied the controlling analysis. No single opinion commanded a majority on every part of the reasoning, but his conclusion on the remedy, joined in its result by enough colleagues to govern, held that the expansion as written crossed the line from encouragement to coercion. The reasoning distinguished the original Medicaid enactment, in which states had joined a new program on stated terms, from a transformation of that program enforced by threatening the funds attached to the old one. A state facing loss of its entire Medicaid grant had no real choice. Five percent of highway funds had been mild encouragement. The whole of Medicaid was, in the phrase the opinion made famous in paraphrase, a financial threat of an entirely different order, aimed at a program states had depended on for generations.

The joint dissent, written by Justices Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Alito, reached the same conclusion on coercion by a different route and would have gone further in consequence. Agreement between the controlling opinion and the joint dissent on the existence of coercion, despite sharp disagreement about remedy and about the rest of the statute, gave the coercion holding unusual weight. Seven justices in total accepted that the threatened withdrawal crossed the constitutional line, even though they divided over what followed from that conclusion.

The remedy the Court adopted made the expansion optional in practice. The Secretary of Health and Human Services could not enforce the expansion by withdrawing existing Medicaid funds from a state that declined it, while the offer of expansion funds on the statutory terms remained available to any state that chose to accept. Doctrinally, the case converted element six from a reservation recited in opinions into an operative limit, and it fixed the considerations, scale, reliance, entrenchment, and the attachment of new terms to old money, that later coercion claims must address.

## The Anti-Commandeering Boundary Standing Beside the Spending Power

Beside the spending power stands a boundary that explains why the conditional grant carries so much weight in the federal system. Congress may not commandeer the machinery of state government. It may not order a state legislature to enact a law, order state executive officers to administer a federal program, or achieve the same result by prohibiting state action in a way that leaves the state no legislative choice of its own. The prohibition is categorical rather than balancing based, and it applies however worthy the federal objective may be. Because the prohibition closes the direct route, the indirect route of the funded bargain, in which a state consents to federal terms in exchange for federal money, becomes the principal instrument of cooperative national policy.

New York v. United States (1992) established the modern statement of the rule. Federal legislation addressing radioactive waste disposal pressed states to take title to waste generated within their borders if they failed to provide disposal capacity by a statutory deadline. The Court struck down the take title provision. Congress could encourage state action through funding incentives, and it could regulate private actors directly under its enumerated powers, but it could not compel a state to legislate according to federal instructions or to assume liability as the instrument of a federal policy. The opinion grounded the rule in accountability. When Congress governs through state officials, voters cannot tell which government made the decision that burdens them, and the political safeguards of federalism lose the clear lines of responsibility on which they depend.

Printz v. United States (1997) extended the boundary from state legislatures to state executive officers. The Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act required local chief law enforcement officers to conduct background checks on prospective handgun purchasers during an interim period before a federal system took over. The Court held the requirement invalid. The federal government may not impress state officers into federal service, even temporarily and even for a task of modest burden, because the Constitution divides authority between two governments that remain accountable to their own electorates and because early practice offered no precedent for conscription of state executives. Together, New York v. United States (1992) and Printz v. United States (1997) closed both halves of state government, legislative and executive, to direct federal direction.

Murphy v. National Collegiate Athletic Association (2018) confirmed that the boundary runs in both directions. A federal statute made it unlawful for a state to authorize sports gambling, and the Court held the provision invalid on the ground that forbidding a state legislature from repealing its own prohibition is a command to the legislature just as an order to enact one would be. The form of the federal instruction did not matter. A direction to maintain a law and a direction to pass a law both dictate the content of state legislation, and both fall within the prohibition.

The relationship between that boundary and the spending doctrine is one of mutual definition. Anti-commandeering doctrine keeps the grant a bargain by forbidding Congress from taking by order what it has not obtained by consent, and spending doctrine polices the bargain itself through the clarity, relatedness, rights, and voluntariness elements, so that consent extracted by overwhelming financial pressure does not become commandeering under another name. Each body of law protects the same structural value from a different direction. A federal system in which states remain genuine governments, rather than administrative districts of the national one, requires both that orders be forbidden and that offers remain offers.

## Formula Grants and Discretionary Grants Produce Different Lawsuits

Federal money reaches states through several distinct structures, and the structure of a program shapes the lawsuits that later challenge its conditions. A categorical grant funds a narrow purpose defined by statute, and the recipient may spend the money only inside that purpose. A block grant consolidates several categorical programs into a broader funding stream, giving the recipient wider discretion over allocation while keeping a general subject boundary in place. Within and across those categories, Congress chooses between two allocation methods that carry very different legal consequences. A formula grant distributes money according to a calculation written into law, so every eligible recipient receives a share determined by the statutory factors rather than by an agency decision among competing applicants. A project grant, often called a discretionary grant, is awarded through competition, with agency officials reviewing applications and selecting recipients under published criteria.

The Edward Byrne Memorial Justice Assistance Grant program illustrates the formula model in its classic form. Its authorizing statute directs the Bureau of Justice Assistance to allocate funds among states and local governments resting on population and violent crime statistics, with minimum allocations and adjustments specified by Congress itself. The agency role in such a program is largely administrative, involving verification of eligibility, calculation of shares, and oversight of compliance with the terms Congress attached. Because the entitlement to a calculated share flows from the statute, a recipient that is denied funds or burdened with an added requirement can frame the dispute as a question about what Congress authorized, and the record for that dispute is the statutory text and the formula itself.

A discretionary program builds a different record and invites a different lawsuit. Competitive selection requires the agency to announce priorities, score applications, and justify choices among applicants that cannot all be funded. That process gives the agency lawful room to set programmatic emphases inside the boundaries of the statute, because Congress designed the program around agency judgment. A condition attached to a discretionary award may therefore be defended as an exercise of the very discretion the statute conferred, and a challenger must show that the condition exceeds that delegated space or conflicts with another legal limit. By contrast, a condition attached to a formula award faces a harder defense, since the agency cannot easily point to a selection function that the condition helps it perform. When the statute already decided who receives money and in what amount, an added condition looks less like program administration and more like new legislation written by an agency.

The difference also affects timing, standing, and remedy. A formula recipient can often challenge a condition before any money is withheld, because its share is calculable and the condition applies by rule rather than by an individual award decision. A disappointed applicant for a discretionary grant, by contrast, may struggle to show entitlement to funds it was never selected to receive, and courts often treat selection disputes as challenges to scoring or procedure rather than to the condition itself. The identity of the plaintiff matters as well, since states that administer formula programs year after year build budgets, staffing plans, and subaward systems around predictable shares. Disruption to a hoped for competitive award presents a more speculative injury, and litigation tends to focus instead on whether the competition followed its own announced rules. For those reasons, formula design narrows the range of plausible agency conditions, while discretionary design widens it, and lawsuits in each setting test different questions even when the underlying condition sounds identical.

## How an Agency Condition Is Built, Announced and Challenged

An agency condition rarely appears from nowhere, and its path from internal decision to public obligation determines how a court later reviews it. Most federal grant programs operate under standard terms and conditions, a body of general requirements covering financial management, reporting, audit access, nondiscrimination, and remedies for noncompliance. Program specific conditions sit on top of that foundation. An agency may draw them from the authorizing statute, from appropriations language, from regulations adopted through rulemaking, or from guidance documents and award notices that interpret existing authority. The source matters because a condition that merely repeats a statute carries the force of Congress behind it, while a condition that appears only in guidance must trace its authority to some delegation that a court can identify and test.

Announcement takes several forms, each with different legal weight. A regulation published after notice and comment rulemaking under the Administrative Procedure Act creates a legislative rule that binds recipients and the agency alike. A funding opportunity announcement describes the terms of a particular competition or award cycle and invites applications on those terms. An award document then memorializes the bargain for a specific recipient, listing the amount, the period of performance, and the special conditions attached. Litigation over a condition therefore often begins with a classification dispute about which kind of document created the obligation and whether the agency followed the procedure that kind of document requires.

The Administrative Procedure Act supplies the frame for many of those disputes without supplying the whole answer. A challenger may argue that a condition is a legislative rule adopted without notice and comment, that it exceeds statutory authority, or that it is arbitrary and capricious because the agency failed to explain its reasoning or to consider reliance interests built up under earlier terms. The agency response typically defends the condition as an interpretation of its organic act, as a condition of voluntary participation, or as a term the recipient accepted when it signed the award. Courts sort those arguments by examining the statutory delegation, the administrative record, and the clarity with which the condition was stated before the recipient committed itself.

Standing decides who may bring that challenge at all. A state that receives a grant directly has the clearest position, since a condition alters the terms on which money already allocated or expected will arrive, and compliance itself imposes administrative costs. A subrecipient, such as a city, county, or nonprofit organization funded through a state pass through award, faces a harder path, because its legal relationship runs through the state and the federal condition may reach it indirectly. Courts examine whether the subrecipient suffers its own injury, whether that injury traces to the federal action rather than to a state choice, and whether a judgment would redress it. Associations and local governments that administer programs on the ground often join litigation alongside states to supply that concrete operational record.

Preliminary relief shapes the practical outcome long before a final judgment. A recipient that files suit may ask for a preliminary injunction or a stay that freezes the condition while the case proceeds, preserving existing funding flows and compliance systems in the interim. Courts weigh likelihood of success, likelihood of irreparable harm, the balance of equities, and the public interest. Because grant cycles run on fixed calendars, a ruling on preliminary relief often decides whether a condition ever takes effect for a given award year, even when the merits remain contested for years afterward.

## The Immigration Conditions Litigation as a Worked Example

The immigration conditions litigation offers a dated and fully attributed example of the formula grant dispute in operation, with courts reaching opposite conclusions on closely related facts. From 2017, the Department of Justice attached immigration cooperation conditions to Edward Byrne Memorial Justice Assistance Grant awards. The conditions addressed notice to federal authorities about the release of certain persons from local custody, access for federal officers to detention facilities, and compliance with a federal statute governing information sharing about immigration status.

In City of Chicago v. Sessions (2018), the United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit considered a challenge brought by a city that received Byrne funds through the formula. The court held that the Attorney General lacked statutory authority for two of the conditions, reasoning from the text of the authorizing statute that Congress had specified the requirements attached to the program and had not delegated a general power to add new ones. The panel treated the formula structure as central to that conclusion, since a program that distributes funds by calculation leaves little implied space for an official to reshape eligibility through added terms. The Seventh Circuit majority rejected the federal reading for the two conditions before it, and the decision left the conditions unenforceable within the scope of its judgment while related litigation continued elsewhere.

A different court examined similar conditions and reached the opposite result. In New York v. Department of Justice (2020), the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit upheld conditions of the same family against a challenge brought by states and a city. That court read the statutory grants of authority to the Attorney General more broadly, finding delegation in provisions that assigned the official responsibilities connected to the program and its administration. The federal argument in that litigation emphasized that the Attorney General held delegated authority to set reasonable terms for the awards and that cooperation conditions served federal law enforcement purposes by coordinating local custody decisions with federal enforcement responsibilities. The Second Circuit accepted the federal reading, and the two appellate decisions stood in acknowledged conflict, a circuit split that left the law dependent on geography for recipients in different parts of the country.

The dispute returned to court in a later generation of cases concerning renewed conditions. On November 4, 2026, Chief Judge John J. McConnell Jr. of the United States District Court for the District of Rhode Island ruled from the bench in State of New Jersey v. United States Department of Justice, No. 1:25-cv-00404, permanently enjoining immigration conditions on Byrne grants and on juvenile justice grants for the plaintiff states. The ruling rested on the ground that Congress, in designing those formula programs, had not authorized the conditions the Department imposed. The states in that litigation pressed the argument that conditions must come from Congress and must relate to the program Congress created, and that an agency administering a formula holds a ministerial role bounded by the statute. The federal side pressed the counterargument that the Attorney General held delegated authority over the programs and that cooperation conditions served federal law enforcement purposes within that authority. The district court adopted the position advanced by the plaintiff states for the programs before it, and its injunction barred enforcement of the conditions against those states. The sequence across 2017, 2018, 2020, and 2026 displays the same statutory question producing different answers in different courts, with formula design at the center of each analysis.

## Conditions That Define a Program and Conditions That Ride On It

Spending power doctrine draws a boundary between a condition that defines the program Congress funded and a condition that uses funding as leverage over conduct outside that program. The distinction decides many cases because Congress may set the contours of its own spending with wide latitude, while a condition that reaches beyond the funded activity begins to regulate behavior that the recipient did not bargain to surrender. Courts approach the boundary by asking what activity the money supports, what the condition governs, and whether the two occupy the same field of conduct. A condition that specifies how grant funds may be spent, who may deliver the funded service, or what the funded project may say as a project sits on the program defining side of the line. A condition that demands a policy commitment, an organizational position, or conduct in other operations financed by other money sits on the leveraging side, and it attracts stricter judicial attention.

Rust v. Sullivan (1991) supplies the classic example of a condition upheld as program defining. The case concerned federal family planning funding and regulations that restricted abortion related counseling and referral within the funded project. Challengers argued that the restrictions burdened speech and professional judgment beyond the reach of the funding statute. The Supreme Court upheld the regulations in 1991 on the reasoning that Congress had defined the scope of the program it created, and that a recipient remained free to engage in the restricted activities outside the funded project with separate funds and separate facilities. The decision treated the condition as a definition of what the federal money purchased rather than as a penalty on conduct elsewhere. Both readings of the decision agreed on the governing frame, which asked whether the condition governed the funded activity itself.

Agency for International Development v. Alliance for Open Society International (2013) marks the other side of the boundary. The condition at issue required organizations receiving federal funds to combat HIV and AIDS abroad to adopt a policy explicitly opposing prostitution and sex trafficking. The Supreme Court in 2013 held the condition unconstitutional as applied, reasoning that it compelled recipients to profess a belief beyond the scope of the federal program and regulated their activities outside the funded projects, including activities financed with private money. The government had defended the requirement as a means of ensuring that federal funds advanced a consistent message and did not undermine the program. The Court answered that the condition did not merely define the funded program but leveraged funding to control speech and association beyond it, crossing the line the earlier funding cases had drawn.

The line between the two decisions is practical as well as doctrinal, and agencies test it whenever a new condition is drafted. A condition written to govern the use of the funds, the content of the funded service, or the qualifications of those delivering it resembles the restriction sustained in 1991. A condition written to govern the recipient as an organization, its other programs, its public positions, or its operations under other funding streams resembles the requirement struck down in 2013. Drafting choices about severability, separate accounting, and the geographic and temporal reach of a condition therefore carry constitutional significance. Recipients deciding whether to comply or to sue examine the same geography, mapping each obligation onto either the funded project or the wider institution. Courts then decide the case on that map, and the map often matters more than the subject matter, since the same policy goal can survive when written as a program definition and fail when written as a condition riding on the program from outside.

## Matching Requirements and Maintenance of Effort: The Quiet Conditions

Some of the most consequential conditions in federal grant law attract the least litigation because they operate through arithmetic rather than prohibition. A matching requirement obliges a recipient to contribute its own resources alongside federal funds, at a rate set by statute or regulation. A program funded at a 75 percent federal share requires the recipient to supply the remaining 25 percent, in cash or, where the statute permits, in kind contributions such as staff time, facilities, or donated services. The match commits state money in advance, aligns the recipient budget with the federal purpose, and gives the recipient a financial stake in the cost of the project. Matching rates vary with the wealth of the recipient in some programs, with federal shares rising in poorer states according to formulas that use per capita income, so the same national program asks different fiscal contributions from different states.

Maintenance of effort clauses work in the opposite direction, guarding the baseline rather than adding to it. Such a clause requires a recipient to sustain its own spending on the funded activity at or above a specified level, often a prior year amount or a percentage of a reference period. The purpose is substitution control. Without the clause, a state could accept federal money for a service it already funded, cut its own spending by the same amount, and redirect the freed resources elsewhere, leaving total effort unchanged while federal funds quietly replaced state funds. Federal law describes that outcome as supplanting, and many statutes contain explicit anti-supplanting rules that prohibit the use of federal money in place of nonfederal money that would otherwise have been spent. Auditors test for supplanting by comparing spending baselines across years and by examining whether positions, contracts, or services shifted funding sources without a genuine expansion of effort.

These conditions shape budgets deeply because they bind decisions far from the grant itself. A match obligation competes with every other claim on state revenue, and a legislature accepting a new program accepts a recurring commitment that persists as long as participation continues. A maintenance of effort clause constrains a legislature during a downturn, when revenue falls and officials search for reductions, because spending in the protected category cannot fall below the floor without risking the federal award. The clause therefore functions as a ratchet inside the state budget process, preserving the funded function at the expense of unprotected functions that absorb deeper cuts. Program managers plan around those floors years in advance, and budget officers treat the protected baselines as fixed costs comparable to debt service, even though no bondholder holds a claim.

Litigation over these conditions stays rare for structural reasons. The obligations are stated numerically in statutes and award documents, so disputes tend to present accounting questions rather than constitutional ones, and accounting questions settle through audit resolution, disallowance, and negotiated repayment rather than through published opinions. A recipient that falls short of a match usually faces a proportionate reduction in federal payment, a remedy both sides can calculate without a judge. Ambiguity, when it arises, concerns valuation of in kind contributions or selection of the correct baseline year, and agencies resolve much of that ambiguity through guidance and single audit findings. The absence of courtroom drama does not signal absence of effect. Matching and maintenance of effort conditions decide which services a state can shrink, which it must defend, and how much of its own revenue a federal program quietly commands, and they do that work year after year without ever becoming the subject of a headline case.

## What a Court Can Do to an Unlawful Condition

A court that finds a grant condition unlawful holds a set of remedies that differ in reach, and the choice among them shapes what happens to the program after judgment. The mildest outcome severs the condition and leaves the program standing. Severance treats the offending term as an appendage that can be removed without disturbing the statutory structure, so funding continues under the remaining terms and recipients keep the awards they expected. Courts favor that outcome when the statute functions coherently without the condition and when Congress would have preferred the program without the term to no program at all. The alternative, invalidating a larger portion of the scheme, appears when the condition is woven into eligibility or funding calculations so tightly that removal would rewrite the program rather than repair it. Agencies and recipients both argue severability with that consequence in mind, since the recipient that wins on the merits may still lose its funding if the remedy dismantles the award.

### What happens to a grant program when a court strikes down one of its conditions?

In most cases the program continues operating under its remaining statutory terms, with funding flowing to recipients without the invalid condition attached, unless the condition is so central to the statutory design that a court concludes Congress would not have enacted the program without it.

Injunctive relief adds a question of scope to the question of severance. An injunction may run only to the plaintiff states that brought the case, leaving the condition enforceable against recipients that did not sue, or it may extend more broadly when the court finds a wider remedy necessary. Scope disputes carry practical weight because a state that stayed out of litigation may face the same condition its neighboring state escaped, producing different terms for the same national program across state lines. Courts weigh the principle that relief should fit the plaintiffs before the court against the administrative difficulty of running one program under two sets of rules. The bench ruling of November 4, 2026, in Rhode Island illustrated the narrower pattern, since the permanent injunction there protected the plaintiff states in that action. Appellate review can widen or narrow that scope, and agencies must decide during any appeal whether to enforce a condition against nonparties while its validity remains unsettled.

Money remedies follow a different logic, shaped by the contract analogy that governs spending legislation. In Cummings v. Premier Rehab Keller P.L.L.C. (2022), the Supreme Court held that emotional distress damages are not recoverable under certain federal antidiscrimination statutes enacted under the spending power, reasoning that a funding recipient receives notice of available remedies through the contract like character of the legislation and that such damages fall outside the remedies traditionally available for breach of contract. The decision limits what a private plaintiff can recover even when a violation is established, and it reinforces the notice principle that conditions and consequences must be stated clearly enough for a recipient to accept them knowingly. For governments, the more common financial consequences run through administrative channels rather than damages actions. An agency that finds funds spent in violation of a valid condition may issue a disallowance, demanding repayment of the questioned amount, and may offset the sum against future payments. Recipients can appeal disallowances through agency procedures and then to court, contesting both the interpretation of the condition and the calculation of the amount. Those administrative remedies continue to operate regardless of constitutional litigation, and a recipient can win the constitutional case while still answering for unrelated compliance findings from the same award period.

## How States Price a Bargain They Would Rather Refuse

A state deciding whether to accept a federal grant performs a calculation that extends well past the award amount printed in the announcement. The calculation includes the value of the funds, the cost of the match, the administrative burden of compliance and reporting, the policy commitments embedded in the conditions, and the cost of leaving the program after years of participation. That final element, the exit cost, grows with time. A program accepted as a modest pilot in one decade becomes, by a later decade, the funding base for staff positions, provider networks, eligibility systems, and local subawards that thousands of residents rely on. Federal funds build institutions around themselves, and the institutions persist as obligations even if the federal share shrinks or the conditions change. State officials describe that accumulation as fiscal dependence, and it develops through ordinary budgeting rather than through any single dramatic decision.

Exit costs bite hardest when a program funds a large share of a state budget function. A small grant supporting a discrete project can be declined or surrendered with limited disruption, because the function it supports is small or separately financed. A program that pays for a substantial fraction of a core service cannot be exited without either replacing the money from state revenue or cutting the service itself, and both options carry political and human costs that officials weigh against the objectionable condition. The legal freedom to refuse remains intact throughout that analysis, since no statute compels a state to participate. The practical menu, however, narrows as dependence deepens, and a condition that would have triggered refusal at the start of a program may be absorbed decades later because refusal has become the most expensive option on the table.

Medicaid presents the extreme case of that pattern. The National Association of State Budget Officers, in its state expenditure reports, has consistently identified Medicaid as the largest single element of state spending when federal and state funds are combined, a position the program has held across many reporting cycles. The program finances health coverage and long term services for tens of millions of residents and supports hospital and clinic revenue in every state. A state contemplating withdrawal faces the loss of federal contributions measured in the billions of dollars, alongside the collapse of provider arrangements built on the expectation of payment. No state has withdrawn, and state challenges to Medicaid conditions have therefore taken the form of litigation over particular terms rather than threats of exit. The 2012 litigation over the Medicaid expansion, decided in National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012), turned on that dependence, with the Court treating the threatened loss of existing funds as a pressure distinct in kind from an ordinary funding inducement.

The distinction between a narrow choice and a coerced choice is where doctrine and budgeting meet without merging. Courts reserve the coercion label for circumstances in which the financial pressure leaves a state with no real option, a threshold the case law sets high and applies rarely. Budget officers work with a wider definition of constraint, since a choice can dominate a budget, distort tax policy, and foreclose other priorities while remaining legally voluntary. Both descriptions can hold at once for the same program. A state may possess a legally free choice that is fiscally narrow, meaning the right to refuse exists on paper and the cost of refusal governs in practice. Grant conditions acquire much of their force from that gap, because the federal government drafts terms for recipients whose dependence it understands, and states evaluate each new term against exit costs accumulated over generations of participation rather than against the award alone.

## Conditional Money in Other Federations

Canada offers the closest comparison because its provinces guard social policy with a jealousy that matches or exceeds the jealousy of American states. The Canadian constitution assigns hospitals, education, and much of welfare administration to the provinces, while the federal Parliament in Ottawa raises revenue on a scale the provinces cannot match. Federal transfers therefore fill the gap, and the question of whether Ottawa may attach conditions to that money has never received a clean judicial answer. The spending power rests on inference from the federal power to spend public funds, and courts have tended to avoid a definitive ruling on its outer limits. In practice, conditions travel through negotiated agreements and through federal statutes that set national standards for provincial programs, most visibly in health care, where federal money supports provincial insurance systems that meet stated criteria on universality, portability, and public administration.

Quebec supplies the sharpest objection within that arrangement. Successive Quebec governments have argued that federal conditions in provincial fields amount to indirect regulation of matters the constitution reserved to the provinces, and that a richer central government can reshape provincial priorities without ever passing a law a court could strike down. The objection is structural rather than partisan, because it survives changes in governing party and rests on the division of powers itself.

The Social Union Framework Agreement of 1999 represents the political settlement that followed decades of that friction. Signed by the federal government and every province except Quebec, the agreement committed Ottawa to notice, consultation, and dispute procedures before launching new social initiatives funded through transfers, and it recognized provincial flexibility in program design. The agreement created no enforceable rights in court, and its force came from political expectation rather than from doctrine. Its history shows a federation choosing negotiation over adjudication, managing the spending power through intergovernmental process because no institution held an accepted legal formula for where legitimate conditions ended and intrusion began.

Germany shows a different institutional answer to the same pressure. The Basic Law divides responsibilities between the federation and the Länder, and it historically assigned many administrative tasks to the Länder while concentrating taxing capacity at the center. Joint tasks emerged as a formal device through which the federation co-financed defined regional and sectoral projects, including university construction, regional economic improvement, and agricultural structure, under framework plans negotiated with the Länder. Equalization transfers then redistribute revenue among the Länder themselves and from the federation, so that poorer regions can sustain services comparable to richer ones. The German design writes cooperation into the constitution instead of leaving it to contested implication, and later reforms tightened the boundaries after critics warned that joint financing blurred accountability, because voters could not tell which level of government deserved credit or blame for a shared program.

The European Union extends the pattern beyond a single state. Cohesion funding directs large transfers toward less developed regions, conditioned on programming documents, audit systems, and compliance with Union rules, with the stated aim of narrowing economic gaps across member states. The rule-of-law conditionality regulation adopted in 2020 added a harder edge, permitting the Union to suspend budget funds where breaches of rule-of-law principles affected the sound management of Union money. The comparison shows the American design in relief. The United States leaves conditional spending largely to case-by-case doctrine built from disputes, while Canada leans on political agreement, Germany on constitutional text, and the Union on regulation tied to budget integrity. Each system confronts the same fact that money moves power across levels of government, and each chooses a different instrument to discipline that movement.

## Variation Across Programs: Highways, Education and Health

The spending doctrine reads as a single set of rules, yet its pressure changes with the structure of each program, because a condition attached to a discrete project grant behaves differently from a condition attached to a vast entitlement that states administer for millions of residents. Highway funding illustrates the lighter end of the spectrum. Federal aid for road construction has flowed to states since the early twentieth century, always with standards on design, safety, and maintenance, and states accepted the arrangement because the grants funded discrete projects with identifiable costs. The condition that reached the Supreme Court in South Dakota v. Dole (1987) withheld five percent of certain highway funds from states that permitted the purchase of alcohol by persons under 21 years of age. The sum at stake was small relative to the state budget, the condition connected to safe travel on the funded roads, and the state retained a practical ability to decline. The case became the canonical statement of the doctrine precisely because the facts presented the condition in its mildest and most defensible form.

Elementary and secondary education occupies the middle ground, where money is smaller than in health care but conditions run far deeper into daily administration. The Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965 directed federal funds toward schools serving low-income children, and over successive reauthorizations Congress layered requirements on testing, reporting, teacher standards, and the use of funds to supplement rather than replace state and local spending. Each condition arrived in statutory text of considerable density, and states accepted the funds because the money served constituencies with strong claims on public support. The disputes in education therefore tend to concern statutory meaning and administrative authority, such as whether a federal agency may demand a particular accountability system or waive requirements for some states and not others, rather than whether Congress may condition the money at all.

Health care, through Medicaid, presents the extreme case that forced the coercion question into the open. Created in 1965 as a cooperative program for medical assistance to low-income persons, Medicaid grew into one of the largest items in state budgets, with federal contributions commonly exceeding half of total program spending and reaching far higher shares in poorer states. States built provider networks, eligibility systems, and long-term care arrangements around the federal contribution over decades, so a threat to withdraw all existing funds for refusal of a new expansion carried consequences unlike any discrete grant. In National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012), the expansion of eligibility under the Affordable Care Act was backed by the loss of all federal Medicaid funding for a noncompliant state, and a majority of the Supreme Court treated that threat as compulsion rather than choice. The scale of the money, the entrenchment of the existing program, and the size of the threatened loss together converted a doctrinal footnote into a holding.

The variation across the three fields exposes the logic that runs beneath the formal tests. Relatedness is easier to show for a drinking age condition tied to highway safety than for a social condition tied to a general grant. Notice matters most in education, where dense statutory conditions change through reauthorization and regulation, and where states plan around requirements years in advance. Coercion matters most where exit is ruinous, and exit becomes ruinous when a program grows from a supplement into a foundation of state finance. Courts therefore reach different questions in different fields even while reciting the same framework, because the facts of each program decide which element of the framework carries real weight.

## The Complication: The Clear Statement Rule Does the Real Work

An honest account of conditional spending has to confront the possibility that the celebrated limits on the power do almost none of the deciding, and that outcomes turn instead on narrower rules about notice and statutory authority. Lynn A. Baker pressed that argument in Conditional Federal Spending After Lopez (1995), writing as a critic of the doctrine's permissiveness. Baker contended that the limits announced in South Dakota v. Dole (1987) were so deferential that Congress could satisfy them in nearly every realistic case, because the general welfare inquiry invites judicial abstention, the relatedness inquiry accepts attenuated connections, and the independent constitutional bar adds nothing beyond rights that would constrain Congress in any event. On that reading, the framework functions as a ritual recitation, and the conditions that survive or fall do so for reasons the framework barely mentions.

The Pennhurst doctrine supplies the first of those real reasons. In Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman (1981), the Supreme Court held that conditions on federal funds must be stated unambiguously, so that a state accepting money knows the obligations it assumes and can weigh them as a party to a bargain would. Baker, again as a critic of the doctrine's permissiveness, treated that clear statement rule as the instrument with genuine bite, because agencies and Congress sometimes impose expectations through vague language, guidance documents, or later reinterpretation, and a court enforcing Pennhurst can reject a condition the state had no fair chance to read.

Statutory authority supplies the second reason. Many funding disputes never raise a constitutional question because the agency administering a program has demanded something the governing statute does not authorize, or has read a condition more broadly than the text permits. Courts resolve those cases on ordinary principles of administrative law, asking whether the agency acted within the delegation Congress actually made.

Justice O'Connor made a related objection from within the Dole decision itself. Her dissent argued that the majority's relatedness requirement, properly applied, should have defeated the drinking age condition, because the condition regulated conduct that the Twenty-First Amendment left substantially to the states and because a requirement must be directly related to the purpose of the expenditure rather than to a general problem the expenditure also touches. Her position accepted conditional spending while insisting that relatedness impose a genuine fit between the federal interest in the program and the demand placed on the state. Without that fit, she warned, Congress could use any grant as leverage over any reserved subject, and the enumeration of federal powers would lose practical meaning at the point where money replaced legislation.

The strongest reply begins with National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012), where the coercion limit that Baker's generation of commentary treated as theoretical actually decided the fate of a major federal initiative. Seven justices concluded that threatening all existing Medicaid funds crossed the line from inducement into compulsion, and the expansion survived only as a genuine option states could refuse without losing the underlying program. The reply also defends the Pennhurst rule on its own terms rather than treating its importance as an embarrassment. A notice rule that protects states from surprise conditions is a deliberate federalism safeguard, because the states' protection in a spending bargain lies precisely in knowing the terms before acceptance and in holding the federal government to the terms announced. Baker remains a critic of the doctrine's permissiveness, and the critique correctly identifies where most cases are won and lost, but the existence of a working notice rule and a demonstrated coercion backstop describes a system with layered protections rather than a hollow one.

## What Is Settled and What Remains Open

Certain propositions in conditional spending law command broad agreement across courts, commentators, and the governments that litigate these cases. Congress holds the spending power under the Constitution, and that power extends beyond the subjects Congress may regulate directly, which is the premise that makes conditional grants possible in fields such as education and social welfare. Conditions attached to federal funds require a clear statement, so a state accepting money can identify the obligations that accompany it, a rule anchored in Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman (1981) and applied whenever ambiguous language is said to impose a major duty. States may refuse federal money, and refusal, however costly in fiscal terms, remains a legally available act that no federal officer may override by treating acceptance as compulsory.

Other propositions remain genuinely open, and the open questions cluster where doctrine meets the scale of modern programs. No court has stated a numeric coercion threshold, expressing the line between inducement and compulsion as a percentage of a state budget or of program funding, and the holding in National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012) deliberately rested on the extreme character of the threatened loss rather than on a general formula. Lower courts therefore decide coercion claims by analogy to that extreme case, which leaves most threatened losses, being smaller, in a zone where prediction is difficult and where litigants argue from structure and reliance rather than from a test. Whether the size of a threatened program, the duration of state dependence, or the availability of replacement revenue should count, and in what combination, awaits a case that presses the question with facts strong enough to force an answer.

### Can a state simply refuse federal grant money?

Yes. A state may decline a federal grant and forgo the funds attached to it, and no court order compels acceptance. Refusal carries budgetary and political costs because programs built on the money lose support, but the legal choice remains with the state government that voters elect to decide.

Agency authority presents a second open field. Federal agencies administer most grant programs, and disputes recur over whether an agency may attach or expand conditions on formula grants that Congress distributed by statutory formula, or whether such conditions require clearer congressional authorization. Courts in different circuits have answered differently depending on the statute, the condition, and the deference owed to the administering agency, and changes in general administrative law doctrine have unsettled settlements that once seemed stable. A condition announced through guidance rather than through regulation raises the sharpest version of the problem, because guidance can change administrative practice across every state at once while escaping the procedures that accompany formal rulemaking, and recipients must then decide whether compliance or litigation carries the greater risk.

A third open question concerns remedy rather than right. Multi-state challenges to funding conditions now arrive with dozens of states on one or both sides, and courts disagree over the proper scope of injunctions in such cases, including whether relief should run nationwide or only to the states before the court. The answer shapes strategy as much as substance, because a nationwide order can suspend a condition everywhere on the strength of one record, while party-limited relief can produce a patchwork in which a federal program operates under different rules in different states.

## Reading a Funding Dispute in the Order Courts Decide It

Courts decide funding disputes in a sequence that disciplined reading can reproduce, and the sequence matters because most cases end before the largest constitutional questions are reached. The first inquiry asks whether statutory authority supports the condition at all. A reader begins with the statute that created the program, looking for the language that authorizes the condition, the officer empowered to impose it, and the funds to which it attaches. If the condition appears in guidance, in a funding announcement, or in an agency letter, the inquiry sharpens, because the question becomes whether Congress delegated power to create that obligation or whether the agency supplied its own.

The second inquiry concerns clarity of statement. Under the rule of Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman (1981), a condition binds a state only if Congress spoke unambiguously, and the reader therefore asks whether a state official at the time of acceptance could have identified the obligation and estimated its cost. Ambiguity is assessed from the perspective of the accepting government rather than from the ingenuity of later advocacy, and conditions discovered in cross-references, in definitions sections, or in the interaction of separate statutes face skeptical examination. You can test clarity with a simple discipline at this stage, asking whether the obligation could be underlined in the text the state received, because a condition that cannot be located cannot fairly be enforced against a government that never saw it in identifiable form.

Relatedness and individual rights form the third inquiry, and the two operate differently despite sharing a stage. Relatedness, drawn from South Dakota v. Dole (1987), asks whether the condition connects to the federal interest in the particular program, and courts apply it with deference that makes outright failure uncommon, though a condition aimed at conduct with no program connection remains vulnerable. The rights inquiry is stricter, because a condition that pressures a state or its beneficiaries to surrender a constitutional right faces the same scrutiny that would apply to direct regulation, and no deference to the spending power dilutes the analysis.

Constitutional coercion comes last, and its position in the sequence is itself instructive. The claim asks whether the financial pressure on the state has grown so severe that choice has become compulsion, the line crossed in National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012) when all existing Medicaid funds stood behind a new expansion. Courts reach that question reluctantly, because it requires judgment about fiscal magnitude and political reality rather than about text, and because a holding of coercion limits Congress in terms that later cases must administer. A reader should therefore treat coercion as the argument of last resort in analysis even when it is the argument of first resort in rhetoric, examining reliance, proportion, and the difference between losing new money and losing an entrenched program on which residents depend.

The avoidance instinct explains the ordering across all four stages. Courts prefer grounds that decide the case narrowly, respect the text Congress wrote, and leave the political branches room to correct errors through amendment, and each earlier stage offers such a ground while the later stages do not. A statutory ruling invites Congress to rewrite the statute. A clarity ruling invites Congress to restate the condition. A coercion ruling instructs Congress that a category of bargain is closed. The sequence is therefore not a formality but a map of judicial restraint, and the reader who follows it in order will usually locate the point at which the dispute will actually be decided, which is the point at which preparation and argument deserve the heaviest investment.

## Why the Bargain Endures Despite Every Challenge

Conditional spending survives every doctrinal challenge and every political complaint because the fiscal structure beneath it makes the bargain nearly impossible for either side to abandon. Vertical fiscal imbalance names the core condition. The federal government commands the broadest and most productive tax bases, above all the income tax, and can borrow through downturns that shrink revenue at every level of government. States operate under balanced budget requirements in their constitutions or statutes, with narrow exceptions and limited borrowing room for operating expenses, so a recession forces state cuts or tax increases at the moment residents most need public services. Federal grants smooth that asymmetry, transferring resources from the level of government that can sustain deficits to the levels that cannot, and countercyclical aid in downturns has repeatedly been the fastest available instrument for supporting state services without state tax shocks.

The imbalance also explains why states accept conditions they dislike. A governor who refuses federal funds does not reduce the federal taxes residents pay, so refusal leaves the state's taxpayers financing programs in other states while their own schools, roads, and hospitals go without support. Political competitors make that arithmetic vivid in every election cycle, and the official who declined money on principle must explain empty budgets to voters who received nothing in exchange for the gesture. Acceptance, by contrast, spreads both credit and blame in ways that suit officials at each level. Federal legislators claim credit for funding popular services across the nation. State officials claim credit for delivering those services and for adapting them to local needs, and each level can attribute unpopular conditions to the other, with state leaders blaming federal mandates and federal leaders pointing to the voluntary character of state acceptance.

Program constituencies lock the arrangement in place over time. Hospitals plan around Medicaid revenue, universities around research and student aid, highway departments around federal construction shares, and school districts around federal supplements for disadvantaged students. Each constituency defends its stream with an intensity that no abstract commitment to state autonomy can match, and legislators who question a program's conditions learn that the beneficiaries of the program experience the conditions as a technicality and the funding as a necessity. Reform proposals consequently aim at the margins, adjusting match rates, easing a reporting burden, or converting a categorical grant into a broader block, rather than at the existence of conditional funding itself. Even administrations elected on platforms of devolution have generally administered the same grant structure with different conditions attached, which confirms that the argument concerns the terms of the bargain rather than its survival.

The federal side has parallel reasons to persist. Conditions let Congress pursue national priorities in fields where direct regulation faces constitutional limits or political resistance, and the grant instrument carries those priorities into thousands of state and local agencies without building a federal delivery apparatus. Accountability for federal money also demands conditions of some kind, because an unconditional transfer would leave Congress answerable for waste it had no mechanism to prevent, and audit, reporting, and maintenance requirements are the ordinary instruments through which any funder protects a contribution. The result is a stable equilibrium in which each side complains about the other and neither walks away, sustained by revenue on one side and by service obligations on the other. By contrast, direct payment to citizens, explained in accounts of [how government cash payments work](/2026/10/03/how-government-cash-payments-work/), is the route Washington takes when it wants no state intermediary at all.

## The Structure That Decides the Next Dispute

Every funding dispute that reaches a court will be decided, before any judge weighs its politics, by a structure of text, reliance, and fiscal scale that the preceding analysis has traced across programs and across federations. The thesis of the article can be restated in fresh terms as follows. A federal condition holds when Congress has written the bargain in language a state could price before accepting, and when the price of refusal, however steep, stops short of confiscating a program the state can no longer abandon without wrecking services its residents depend upon. Language and magnitude do the deciding. A condition stated vaguely fails regardless of the importance of its purpose, because a government cannot consent to terms it cannot read. A condition stated perfectly fails when the threatened loss is so large and so entrenched that acceptance records submission rather than agreement, because a signature extracted by ruin is not a bargain in any sense the Constitution should honor.

That structure explains the pattern of outcomes better than any account organized around judicial sympathy for states or for national power. Statutory authority decides the cases where an agency reached beyond its delegation. Clear statement decides the cases where Congress or an agency expected states to discover obligations by implication. Relatedness and rights police the boundaries of fit and of individual liberty, each intervening in the small set of cases where a condition wanders from its program or presses on protected conduct. Coercion intervenes last and least often, reserved for the configuration in which an existing program of enormous scale is held hostage to a new demand, as in National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012). The rarity of intervention at each successive stage is not evidence that the stages lack force, because governments draft, negotiate, and settle in the shadow of the stages they expect to lose, and most federalism in the spending field happens in that shadow rather than in reported decisions.

The one thing a reader should carry away is the discipline of asking, in order, who wrote the condition, where the accepting state could have read it, and what refusal would actually cost. Those three questions, asked in that sequence, locate nearly every funding dispute within the structure and predict the ground on which a court will resolve it, whether the program concerns highways, classrooms, hospital wards, or a field not yet drawn into the grant system. They also expose weak arguments quickly, because a complaint that skips the statutory text to reach for constitutional rhetoric, or a defense that celebrates state consent without examining the cost of refusal, has omitted the stage at which the case will turn. Governments that internalize the sequence write clearer statutes, administer conditions more carefully, and litigate more honestly, since each side can see the decisive question before positions harden around the wrong one.

The structural implication reaches beyond litigation strategy. Conditional spending is the mechanism through which the American federation adjusts itself without formal amendment, renegotiating the practical boundary between national and state responsibility one program at a time, in budget language rather than in constitutional text. That quiet renegotiation will continue because vertical fiscal imbalance will continue, and each new condition, each refusal, and each judicial response will add another term to a settlement that no single generation drafted and no single decision can finish. The durability of the arrangement therefore depends on maintaining the two disciplines that keep a bargain a bargain, intelligible terms and a survivable right to say no, against the constant pressure of urgent purposes and large sums. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is a condition on a federal grant?
A condition on a federal grant is a requirement that a recipient must satisfy in order to receive funds, keep funds, or remain eligible for further payments under a federal assistance program. Congress attaches the requirement in the statute that authorizes the program, and federal agencies often restate or elaborate the requirement in regulations and in the grant agreement itself. Typical conditions address how money may be spent, what reports must be filed, what accounting standards must be followed, and what related policies the recipient must adopt. A recipient accepts the conditions by applying for and taking the money. Because acceptance is formally voluntary, the condition operates through the spending power rather than through a direct order, and the usual remedy for breach is loss, suspension, or repayment of funds rather than a criminal penalty.

### Q: Where does Congress get the power to attach conditions to federal grants?
The power comes from the Spending Clause of the Constitution, which authorizes Congress to lay and collect taxes and to pay the debts and provide for the common defense and general welfare of the United States. In United States v. Butler (1936), the Supreme Court adopted the view that the spending power is not confined to the other enumerated powers, so Congress may spend for purposes beyond those subjects it may regulate directly. Conditions are the mechanism by which Congress directs that broad spending power toward chosen purposes. Because a state or other recipient remains free to decline the offer, the Court has treated conditional spending as an exercise in inducement rather than regulation, although later cases imposed outer limits on how far that inducement may go before it becomes compulsion.

### Q: What did South Dakota v. Dole decide?
South Dakota v. Dole (1987) upheld a federal statute that directed the Secretary of Transportation to withhold a percentage of federal highway funds from any state whose laws permitted purchase or public possession of alcohol by persons under twenty-one years of age. The Court held that the condition was a valid exercise of the spending power. The decision set out the governing framework: the spending must serve the general welfare, the condition must be stated unambiguously, the condition must be related to the federal interest in the program, and the condition must not require the recipient to violate an independent constitutional bar. The Court also observed in passing that a financial inducement might become so coercive that it crossed into compulsion, a suggestion that later litigation developed into a separate limit.

### Q: What is the Pennhurst clear statement rule?
The Pennhurst clear statement rule comes from Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman (1981), in which the Supreme Court held that legislation enacted under the spending power is much in the nature of a contract, so a state accepts the terms only when it does so voluntarily and knowingly. For that acceptance to be knowing, Congress must impose each condition unambiguously, enabling the state to exercise its choice knowingly while aware of the consequences of participation. A condition stated in vague or aspirational language does not bind the recipient, because the recipient could not have understood the obligation at the moment of acceptance. Federal agencies cannot cure the defect later by announcing an interpretation that the statutory text never made clear. The rule therefore places the burden of clarity on Congress at the time the program is enacted.

### Q: When does a federal grant condition become unconstitutional coercion?
A grant condition becomes unconstitutional coercion when the financial pressure leaves the recipient with no real choice except acceptance, so that the offer operates in substance as a command. The Supreme Court acknowledged the possibility in South Dakota v. Dole (1987) but found no coercion in the loss of five percent of highway funds. The limit acquired force in National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012), where a threatened loss of all existing Medicaid funding, amounting to more than 10 percent of a typical state budget, was treated as compulsion rather than encouragement. Courts assessing coercion weigh the size of the threatened loss relative to the recipient budget, whether the funds at risk belong to a longstanding program the recipient already depends upon, and whether the condition transforms that program rather than adjusting it at the margin.

### Q: What did NFIB v. Sebelius hold about Medicaid funding conditions?
National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012) held that Congress could offer states additional funds to expand Medicaid eligibility, but could not threaten to withdraw all existing Medicaid funds from a state that declined the expansion. Seven justices concluded that the threatened loss, exceeding 10 percent of an average state budget, crossed the line from encouragement into coercion and violated the spending power limits recognized since South Dakota v. Dole (1987). The remedy left the expansion in place as an option while barring the Secretary from enforcing the withdrawal penalty against a nonparticipating state. The decision marked the first time the Supreme Court invalidated a spending condition on coercion grounds. It did not disturb ordinary conditions attached to new funds, and it left intact the Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman (1981) requirement that any condition be stated clearly.

### Q: How is a grant condition different from a direct federal mandate?
A grant condition requires action only from a recipient that has accepted federal funds, and the sanction for refusal is ordinarily the loss of those funds. A direct federal mandate binds its subjects whether or not any money changes hands, and it rests on a regulatory power such as the commerce power rather than the spending power. The distinction matters most for states. Under New York v. United States (1992) and later anti-commandeering decisions, Congress may not order a state legislature to enact a law or compel state officials to administer a federal program. Congress may, however, offer money on terms that encourage the same result, because the state retains a formal choice. That difference explains why conditional spending is often used where direct regulation of state governments would be constitutionally doubtful or politically unavailable.

### Q: What does it mean for a grant condition to be germane?
Germaneness means that a grant condition must be related to the federal interest in the particular program or project being funded. The requirement comes from South Dakota v. Dole (1987), where the Court found the condition satisfied because the drinking age condition bore directly on safe interstate travel, the central purpose of federal highway spending. The Court applied the test deferentially and did not demand a tight fit between the condition and every dollar spent. A condition wholly unrelated to the program purpose would fail, because Congress would then be using money appropriated for one object to purchase compliance on an unrelated subject. In practice, litigants rarely win on germaneness alone, since courts accept broad accounts of program purpose, yet the requirement still disciplines drafting by forcing Congress to articulate a plausible connection between the money and the demand attached to the money.

### Q: Why did every state raise its drinking age to twenty-one?
Every state raised its drinking age to twenty-one because Congress used highway funding as leverage rather than attempting a direct national drinking age, which would have raised serious questions about federal power over a subject governed mainly by the states and protected in part by the Twenty-first Amendment. The National Minimum Drinking Age Act of 1984 directed withholding of a share of federal highway funds from states that allowed purchase or public possession of alcohol below that age. South Dakota challenged the arrangement, and South Dakota v. Dole (1987) upheld it as valid conditional spending, with only five percent of highway funds at stake under the version before the Court. Faced with a certain and recurring loss of road money on one side and a policy change on the other, each state amended its law, and the national standard was achieved without a federal mandate.

### Q: What is the difference between a formula grant and a discretionary grant?
A formula grant distributes funds according to a statutory formula, so every eligible recipient that satisfies the program conditions receives an amount determined by factors such as population, poverty counts, or prior spending set out in the authorizing law. Medicaid and federal highway apportionments follow this pattern. A discretionary grant, by contrast, is awarded through a competitive or administrative selection process in which the agency evaluates applications and chooses recipients within the limits Congress has set. The distinction affects predictability and control. Formula funding gives states a stable entitlement-like stream and makes threatened withdrawal a powerful source of leverage, as National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012) illustrated. Discretionary funding gives the agency greater influence over project design, but each award is smaller, shorter in duration, and less likely to create the dependence that supports a coercion claim.

### Q: Who can challenge a federal grant condition in court?
A state or other recipient that faces loss of funds for rejecting or breaching a condition has standing to challenge that condition, because the threatened loss is a concrete injury traceable to the federal requirement. States brought the leading cases, including South Dakota v. Dole (1987) and National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012). Private parties face a harder path. A person benefited by a spending program may sue only when Congress has created an enforceable individual right in clear statutory language, a standard tightened by later decisions following Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman (1981). Competitors, taxpayers, and members of the public who object to a condition in general terms usually lack standing, because a generalized grievance about government policy does not satisfy the case or controversy requirement of Article III.

### Q: What is the unconstitutional conditions doctrine?
The unconstitutional conditions doctrine holds that the government may not condition a benefit, including grant funding, on the surrender of a constitutional right, even though the government has no obligation to provide the benefit at all. A state cannot be required to abandon free speech protections, religious liberty, or other independent constitutional guarantees as the price of federal money. South Dakota v. Dole (1987) recognized this limit as an independent constitutional bar separate from the general welfare, clarity, and germaneness requirements. The doctrine is deliberately narrow in spending cases, because nearly every condition restricts how a recipient would otherwise act, and courts distinguish between defining the scope of a funded program and leveraging funds to control conduct outside that program. A condition that governs the use of the money itself is generally permissible, while a condition that reaches beyond the program to suppress protected activity invites invalidation.

### Q: Why do courts describe federal spending legislation as a contract?
Courts describe federal spending legislation as a contract because the arrangement has the structure of an offer and an acceptance: Congress offers funds on stated terms, and the recipient accepts the terms by taking the funds with knowledge of them. Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman (1981) gave the analogy its canonical form and drew from it the clear statement rule protecting recipients against obligations they could not have understood. The analogy also shapes remedies and interpretation. Conditions are read according to the text the recipient accepted, agencies cannot add major new obligations after acceptance without fresh notice, and disputes are framed as questions about what the bargain actually contained. The analogy has limits, since Congress retains power to amend programs prospectively, but it remains the organizing metaphor for conditional spending doctrine and the reason clarity at enactment matters so much.

### Q: Can the federal government reclaim grant money that was spent wrongly?
Yes, the federal government may reclaim funds that were spent outside the terms of the grant, withheld in error, or obtained through false statements, subject to the procedures in the governing statute and the grant agreement. Agencies pursue recovery through audit findings, disallowance decisions, offsets against later payments, and debt collection, and the recipient ordinarily receives notice and an opportunity to contest the amount. Serious misuse may also produce liability under the False Claims Act when a recipient knowingly presents a false claim for payment, and criminal penalties apply to theft or fraud involving federal program funds. The contract framing from Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman (1981) cuts both ways here: recovery is strongest where the violated condition was stated unambiguously in the bargain, and weakest where the government relies on an obligation the recipient had no clear chance to understand.

### Q: What is a matching requirement in a federal grant?
A matching requirement obliges the recipient to contribute a stated share of program costs from nonfederal sources, in cash or sometimes in kind, as a condition of receiving the federal share. Medicaid illustrates the structure at large scale, with the federal contribution calculated as a percentage of state spending that varies by state under a statutory formula. Matching serves several purposes at once. It stretches federal appropriations across more total activity, it tests whether the recipient values the program enough to commit its own resources, and it gives the recipient a financial stake that discourages waste. Matching also shapes state budgets in lasting ways, because a generous federal match makes spending in the matched category cheaper for the state than spending elsewhere. Critics of matching requirements note that poorer jurisdictions may struggle to raise the nonfederal share and therefore draw down less federal help.

### Q: What does maintenance of effort mean in a federal grant program?
Maintenance of effort means that a recipient must sustain its own spending or activity in the funded area at or above a specified baseline, usually a prior year level or a stated percentage of it, instead of substituting federal money for funds it would have spent anyway. The condition protects the federal purpose: without it, a grant intended to expand a service could simply free the recipient budget for unrelated uses while total effort in the target area stayed flat. Baselines, measurement periods, and waiver rules vary by program, and some statutes allow adjustments for economic downturns or enrollment shifts. Enforcement typically works through reporting, audit comparison against the baseline, and reduction or repayment of federal funds when effort falls short without justification. Recipients therefore treat maintenance of effort clauses as continuing obligations that survive each budget cycle rather than as one-time promises made at application.

### Q: Do federal grant conditions bind cities and counties as well as states?
Federal grant conditions bind whichever entity accepts the funds, so cities, counties, school districts, universities, hospitals, and nonprofit organizations are all bound when each one takes federal assistance on stated terms. Many local recipients receive federal money indirectly as subrecipients through a state, in which case the conditions flow down through the state agreement and the local entity answers both to the state and, through audit and enforcement channels, to the federal agency. The constitutional analysis differs by recipient, however. The coercion and anti-commandeering limits developed in cases such as New York v. United States (1992) protect states as sovereigns within the federal structure. A municipality, which state law creates and may restructure, generally cannot claim the same sovereign protection, although the clear statement principle from Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman (1981) still guards every recipient against unstated obligations.

### Q: What happens when a grant recipient accepts the money and ignores a condition?
When a recipient accepts funds and ignores a condition, the federal agency may suspend payments, terminate the award, disallow the improper costs, and demand repayment of amounts already spent in violation, following the notice and hearing procedures that the statute, regulations, and grant terms provide. Repeated or deliberate noncompliance can lead to debarment, which excludes the recipient from further federal awards for a stated period. A recipient that disputes the agency reading may pursue administrative review and then judicial review, arguing for example that the condition was never stated with the clarity Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman (1981) requires, or that the agency interpretation exceeds the statute. What the government generally cannot do is punish noncompliance with conditions that were ambiguous at acceptance, retroactively invented, or unrelated to the funded program under the germaneness limit in South Dakota v. Dole (1987).

### Q: What is a block grant and how do its conditions differ from a categorical grant?
A block grant consolidates funding for a broad purpose, such as community development or social services, and gives the recipient wide discretion over detailed spending choices within that purpose. A categorical grant funds a narrowly defined project or activity and carries detailed conditions on eligibility, services, reporting, and use of funds. The conditional structure therefore differs in density rather than in kind. Block grants impose fewer and more general conditions, rely more heavily on broad outcome reporting and audit, and shift policy judgment toward state and local administrators. Categorical grants impose many specific conditions and preserve greater federal control over design. Congress chooses between the forms according to how much uniformity the program requires. Both forms remain subject to the same constitutional limits, including clear statement under Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman (1981) and the coercion limit applied in National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012).

### Q: Why do states accept federal conditions that their officials oppose?
States accept opposed conditions because refusal carries a concrete fiscal cost while acceptance preserves services that residents expect and that state budgets cannot easily replace. Federal funds often support a large share of highway construction, health coverage, and education spending, so declining an award means either raising state taxes, cutting the service, or watching residents pay federal taxes that finance benefits in other states. National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012) showed that even the most contested expansion drew participation from states whose officials had litigated against it, once the funding was structured as a genuine option. The contract logic of Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman (1981) also matters: officials may calculate that a clear condition, however unwelcome, is a known price, whereas litigation is slow and uncertain. Acceptance therefore reflects budget arithmetic and political accountability more than agreement with the policy.
