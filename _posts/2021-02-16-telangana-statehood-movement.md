The Telangana statehood movement is the longest sustained renegotiation of a founding settlement in the history of the Indian republic. It opened with a hunger strike beside Khammam railway station in January 1969 and closed on the morning of 2 June 2014, when a state demanded across two generations came into legal existence, with Hyderabad as its capital and the leader of its second phase as its first Chief Minister. Between those two dates lies a record written not in manifestos alone but in agreements signed and breached, in a Supreme Court judgment that reversed a region's safeguards, in a party founded at a single meeting in Hyderabad, in an eleven-day fast that forced the Union Home Minister to speak at half past eleven at night, and in a statute passed by voice vote through a silenced chamber. Read as a whole, the movement is best understood as citizen-renegotiation: organised citizens returning, again and again, to a bargain struck in 1956, testing each of its clauses against the record of its performance, and refusing to accept the answer that the bargain had already been honoured.

That reading has rivals, and this article puts them on the record before judging between them. The linguistic-state reading treats Telangana as an unfinished item of the 1956 settlement, a region merged into a Telugu-speaking state whose internal asymmetry the merger papers never resolved. The regional-political reading, stated most carefully in K. Srinivasulu's Caste, Class and Social Articulation in Andhra Pradesh (2002), treats the demand as an instrument of parties, castes and elites competing inside a state whose politics the merger had made. The citizen-renegotiation reading, defended here, does not deny either frame its evidence. It insists that the movement's own sequence, its organisations, fasts, strikes, electoral vehicles and documentary demands, constitutes an operation of a distinct kind: citizens using every instrument the Constitution leaves open, and several it leaves merely tolerated, to force the renegotiation of a settlement whose written safeguards had failed in their hands. The framework is the one set out in [the movements-as-citizen-renegotiation framework](/2021/01/09/indian-movements-citizen-renegotiation/), and this article applies it to the hardest test the framework faces, a demand that took forty-five years and two distinct phases to succeed.

## The Merger of 1956 and the Safeguards Written for Telangana

Andhra Pradesh came into being on 1 November 1956 under [the States Reorganisation Act of 1956](/2019/02/10/states-reorganisation-act-1956-india/), joining the Telugu-speaking districts of the former Hyderabad State, the region called Telangana, to Andhra State, which had been carved out of Madras in 1953 after the fast and death of Potti Sriramulu. The merger had been doubted by the very commission that proposed the linguistic map. The States Reorganisation Commission, chaired by Fazl Ali and reporting in 1955, recorded the apprehensions of Telangana's leaders and entertained the option of keeping Hyderabad as a separate state for a period before any union, a caution that [the constitutional politics of linguistic reorganisation](/2019/01/19/linguistic-reorganisation-constitutional-politics/) preserves in full. The Congress leadership overruled the caution, and the price of Telangana's consent was a written bargain, the Gentlemen's Agreement, signed in Delhi on 20 February 1956 by leaders of both regions, among them Burgula Ramakrishna Rao and K. V. Ranga Reddy for Hyderabad State and Bezawada Gopala Reddy and Neelam Sanjeeva Reddy for Andhra State.

### What did the Gentlemen's Agreement of 1956 promise Telangana?

Signed on 20 February 1956 in Delhi, the agreement promised Telangana a regional council with a say over its advancement and land sales, continuation of the Mulki rules on public employment, spending of Telangana revenues inside Telangana, expanded education, and a share of cabinet posts proportionate to population.

The agreement's operative clauses were concrete. A Telangana Regional Council would oversee the region's advancement, and the sale of agricultural land in Telangana would remain subject to its control. The Mulki rules, the residence-based employment code inherited from Hyderabad State, would continue in force so that posts in Telangana's administration went to persons with fifteen years of residence in the region. Telangana's revenue surplus would be spent inside Telangana. Educational facilities would expand. Cabinet places would follow the rough proportion of population, and where the Chief Minister came from one region, the Deputy Chief Minister would come from the other. The Mulki rules themselves were older than the republic: they descended from a firman of the Nizam of Hyderabad issued in 1919, which had defined a mulki as a person born in the state or resident there for fifteen years, and the States Reorganisation Act carried them forward as law in force.

The safeguard machinery began operating almost at once, and so did the grievances about its operation. The Telangana Regional Committee, constituted in 1958 under the Constitution's Article 371, gave the region's legislators a forum, but Telangana members complained through the 1960s that its recommendations were advisory where the agreement had implied authority. Appointments to government posts drew the sharpest complaint. By 1968, movement accounts and the employees' associations put the number of non-local appointees holding Telangana posts in the tens of thousands, a figure the state government disputed in method but never entirely in substance. A committee under Justice V. Bhargava, appointed in 1969 to audit Telangana's finances, computed an unspent Telangana surplus running into tens of crores of rupees across the first decade of the merged state, money the agreement had earmarked for the region itself. The movement's first phase was built on documents of exactly this kind: the agreement's text, the appointment registers, and the audited accounts. Whatever else 1969 was, it was a quarrel over whether a written bargain had been kept.

## The Safeguard Decade: Erosion in Operation, 1956 to 1968

The grievance that exploded in 1969 had a prehistory inside Hyderabad State itself. In 1952, four years before the merger, students in Hyderabad and Warangal rose in the non-Mulki agitation against the appointment of officials from outside the state to Hyderabad's services, and police firing on student processions in Hyderabad city killed demonstrators whose names the region's movement literature still keeps. The agitation taught two generations a paired lesson that 1969 would repeat at scale: employment was the nerve of regional politics in the former Nizam's dominions, and the state would shoot to defend its appointment registers. The Mulki rules survived that agitation because they were the law of the state; the Gentlemen's Agreement's achievement in 1956 was to carry that law across the merger, converting a princely firman into a clause of the republic's federal settlement.

What followed was a decade of operation in which every safeguard developed the defect its critics had predicted. The Telangana Regional Committee, working from 1958, examined budgets and schemes and recorded its objections in reports that the state cabinet was free to note and set aside; its members complained, on the record of its own proceedings, that the committee could expose a diversion but could not prevent one. The land safeguard fared no better in the movement's accounting: sales of agricultural land in the region to buyers from the coastal districts proceeded through registrations the committee could question but not void. The employment safeguard generated the decade's most corrosive literature, the employees' lists. Telangana's government employees' associations compiled, office by office, the names of appointees they held to be non-Mulkis holding posts reserved under the rules, and the compilation habit became the movement's characteristic method, an audit conducted by the audited. By the mid-1960s the demand had acquired its enduring triad, water, funds and jobs, because each noun corresponded to a file: irrigation projects sanctioned for the region and deferred in execution, the surplus computed and unspent, the registers compiled and unanswered. When the safeguards movement of 1968 organised students and employees specifically around the enforcement of the 1956 clauses, it was not inventing a politics; it was scheduling one.

The Union government's interventions in the crisis year show the same documentary grammar from the other side. The Eight-Point Plan of 12 April 1969, announced by Indira Gandhi after talks with leaders of both regions, promised a high-powered committee to determine the Telangana surplus, application of that surplus to the region, and strengthened safeguards; a Five-Point Formula followed in November 1969, amending the Mulki arrangements for the twin cities; and the Committee on Telangana Surpluses under Justice V. Bhargava sat through 1969 computing the region's unspent balances from the state's own accounts. Each instrument conceded the movement's premise, that the bargain's performance could be measured, while refusing the movement's conclusion, that measured failure entitled the region to leave. The citizen-renegotiation reading takes the concession seriously: by 1969 both sides had accepted that the settlement was a contract with auditable terms. The quarrel was over the remedy for breach, and it would remain the quarrel for forty-five years, through committees whose very appointment conceded the audit and whose reports were then shelved by the conceding party.

## Feature One: The First Movement and the Year 1969

The first movement began, as the second would forty years later, with a fast. On 6 January 1969, a young man named Ravindranath began an indefinite hunger strike near Khammam railway station, protesting the recruitment of non-local candidates to posts at the Paloncha thermal power project in defiance of the Mulki rules. Students at Osmania University in Hyderabad took the demand up within days, reframing a complaint about one project's appointments as a charge that the safeguards of 1956 had been violated as a system. Government employees joined through their associations, and by the middle of January 1969 the ten districts of the region were in a rolling strike of classrooms, offices and courts. Chief Minister Kasu Brahmananda Reddy convened an all-party meeting, and on 19 January 1969 the All-Party Accord promised the transfer of non-local employees out of Telangana posts and the application of Telangana's surplus to Telangana. The accord calmed Hyderabad for a matter of days. Students who had tasted organisation refused to disperse on a promise, and the agitation resumed with a harder demand: not the enforcement of safeguards inside a merged state, but a separate Telangana state.

The state's answer was force. Through the spring and summer of 1969, police fired on student processions in Hyderabad, Warangal, Nizamabad and other towns. The figure carried by the movement's record, and repeated in the Telangana Rashtra Samithi's later submission to the Srikrishna Committee, is 369 dead, the overwhelming majority of them students killed in police firing; official statements of the period acknowledged a large loss of life without converging on the movement's total, and the gap between the two counts is part of the documentary record rather than a detail to be averaged away. The memorial raised at Gun Park in Hyderabad, opposite the Assembly, commemorates the dead of 1969 by name and number as the movement keeps them. Indira Gandhi's government in Delhi tried the instruments of concession in sequence. Her Eight-Point Plan of 12 April 1969 promised accelerated advancement of the region, a high-powered committee on the Telangana surplus and continued safeguards, and it satisfied neither the students on the streets nor the leaders assembling a political vehicle for the demand.

That vehicle was the Telangana Praja Samithi, formed in the early months of 1969 and led from May of that year by Marri Chenna Reddy, a former minister who gave the students' demand a party, a symbol and a candidate list. Its significance for the citizen-renegotiation reading is precise: the movement converted street mobilisation into an electoral instrument within two years, and it chose as its programme the single clause of separation. When Indira Gandhi called the mid-term Lok Sabha election of March 1971, the Samithi fought the Telangana seats on that clause alone.

### How did the Telangana Praja Samithi perform in the 1971 general election?

The Samithi contested the March 1971 Lok Sabha election on the single demand of a separate state and won ten of the fourteen seats allotted to the Telangana districts, a sweep built almost entirely on the student and employee networks assembled during the 1969 agitation across the region.

Ten of fourteen seats was a verdict no reading of the period can explain away: a party two years old, without money or machine, had taken the region's parliamentary representation on one promise. Yet the verdict produced no state. Indira Gandhi, returned to office nationally on the Garibi Hatao platform and fresh from the December 1971 war, declined separation and negotiated instead with the Samithi's leadership. In September 1971 Chenna Reddy merged the Telangana Praja Samithi into the Congress, accepting assurances on safeguards in place of statehood, and on 30 September 1971 P. V. Narasimha Rao, a leader from Karimnagar district, became the first Chief Minister of Andhra Pradesh drawn from Telangana. Students who had buried their classmates read the merger as a sale of the mandate, and the bitterness of that reading matters to everything that followed: when the second movement's leaders were accused, decades later, of preparing a similar compromise, the accusation drew its force from 1971. The first phase of citizen-renegotiation had demonstrated the pattern the whole movement would repeat: a written safeguard, an audited breach, a mobilisation, a concession offered below the demand, and a settlement whose adequacy the next generation would test again.

## Feature Two: The Mulki Judgment, Jai Andhra and the Six-Point Formula

The settlement of 1971 rested on the Mulki rules, and the rules were in court. A full bench of the Andhra Pradesh High Court had held the rules invalid, and on 3 October 1972 a five-judge bench of the Supreme Court, presided over by Chief Justice S. M. Sikri, reversed that holding in The Director of Industries and Commerce, Government of Andhra Pradesh v. V. Venkata Reddy, reported at AIR 1973 SC 827. The Court held the Mulki rules to be law in force, preserved by Article 35(b) of the Constitution and carried through the reorganisation of 1956, so that the fifteen-year residence qualification for Telangana posts stood. For Telangana's employees the judgment was a vindication. For the coastal districts it was a provocation, and within a day the counter-movement had a name: Jai Andhra. Students in Vijayawada, Guntur, Visakhapatnam and the towns of Rayalaseema demanded a separate Andhra state, freed of the Mulki restrictions that, in their stated case, locked Andhra's educated young men out of the capital their region had helped to build.

The counter-movement's sequence was rapid and it was costly. Gouthu Latchanna organised the Andhra Praja Front in October 1972; on 12 December 1972 a Jaitra Yatra rally filled Vijayawada; ministers from the coastal districts resigned in succession through December, beginning with Batthina Subba Rao and extending to the Deputy Chief Minister, B. V. Subba Reddy; Kakani Venkataratnam, the veteran leader from Tenali who had become the agitation's most respected figure, died in December 1972 while the movement was at its height. Police firing on Jai Andhra processions killed protesters in the coastal towns, and by January 1973 P. V. Narasimha Rao had resigned the chief ministership and President's rule was in force. The symmetry is the analytical point this feature contributes to the citizen-renegotiation reading: the same instrument, mass mobilisation against a safeguard settlement, was operated within a single year by both regions, and the Union government learned that every concession to one region's renegotiation reopened the other's.

Delhi's answer was a new written bargain, drafted to replace the old one's failed clauses. The Six-Point Formula, announced on 21 September 1973 and amplified on 22 October 1973, was presented to Congress legislators of both regions by K. C. Pant, Minister of State for Home Affairs, and accepted by both.

### What did the Six-Point Formula of September 1973 settle?

Announced on 21 September 1973, the formula traded the Mulki rules and the Telangana Regional Committee for state-wide local preference in recruitment, local cadres, an administrative tribunal, educational preference, a planning board for backward areas, and a constitutional amendment giving the scheme permanent legal form.

Parliament gave the formula constitutional form through the Thirty-Second Amendment, in force from 1974, which inserted Article 371D into the Constitution and empowered the President to organise local cadres and prescribe local preference across the whole state. The Mulki Rules Repeal Act closed the older code, the Telangana Regional Committee was abolished, and the Presidential Order of 1975, the Andhra Pradesh Public Employment (Organisation of Local Cadres and Regulation of Direct Recruitment) Order, divided the state into zones and promised each zone's candidates the bulk of its non-gazetted posts. The citizen-renegotiation reading does not treat 1973 as a defeat pure and simple. It treats it as the movement's first full cycle: the bargain of 1956 had been renegotiated into a bargain of 1973, broader in geography and weaker in guarantee, because it exchanged a region's enforceable rules for a whole state's administrative discretion. The Telangana complaint of the following decades was that the exchange itself was then breached, through appointments made in violation of the Presidential Order, a complaint the state government itself institutionalised by appointing the Girglani Commission in 2001 to examine violations of that order. The pattern of 1956 to 1969 had repeated at a slower tempo: text, breach, audit, and a accumulating refusal to accept the audit's shelving.

## The Quiet Decades: Stalemate from 1973 to 2001

Between the settlement of 1973 and the founding of the Telangana Rashtra Samithi in 2001, the statehood demand survived without a vehicle, and its survival is itself evidence in the argument between the readings. The regional-political reading treats the quiet decades as proof that the demand was always an instrument, picked up when parties needed it and set down when they did not. The record supports a colder interpretation. The demand's keepers in the quiet years were not parties but institutions of memory: the Telangana Information Trust, founded in 1986 by veterans and writers of 1969 to document the first movement; study circles and cultural associations that kept the martyrs' lists, the surplus audits and the Mulki litigation in circulation; and forums such as the Telangana Jana Sabha of the late 1990s, which restated the demand in the language of regional deprivation at a moment when no party would carry it. When three new states were created in November 2000, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh and Uttarakhand, none of them preceded by a mobilisation on the Telangana scale, the disparity was itself an argument the second movement would use: the Union, it said, granted states where no one had died for them and withheld one where hundreds had.

The state's politics, meanwhile, ran on other tracks. Marri Chenna Reddy served again as Chief Minister from 1978, within the Congress system he had rejoined. N. T. Rama Rao founded the Telugu Desam Party in March 1982 on the platform of Telugu self-respect and swept the 1983 election, a result his party read as proof that the unified Telugu state carried an emotional mandate of its own, a claim the united-state case would repeat for thirty years. Governments of both Congress and Telugu Desam complexion administered the Presidential Order, and the employees' associations kept their own ledgers of its violations, culminating in the demand for an official audit that produced the Girglani Commission. Inside the Telugu Desam, a legislator from Siddipet named K. Chandrashekar Rao climbed the ladder the system offered: elected to the Assembly in 1985, a minister for transport in N. Chandrababu Naidu's cabinet, and Deputy Speaker of the Assembly from 2000. His career is the hinge between the quiet decades and the second phase, because he carried into a new party the one asset the first movement had lacked, a leader who treated statehood not as a bargaining counter inside an existing party but as the whole programme of a party of his own.

## The Order in Operation: Zones, Cadres and the Long Breach, 1975 to 2000

The settlement of 1973 to 1975 deserves a slower reading than either movement's literature usually grants it, because its machinery defined the terrain on which the second phase would fight. The Presidential Order of 1975 organised the state's public employment into local cadres, district, zonal and state-wide, and defined the local candidate by four consecutive years of study or residence in the area, a definition that replaced the Mulki code's fifteen years and applied the same local principle to every region of the state. Six zones covered the state, the fifth and sixth embracing the ten Telangana districts with Hyderabad in the sixth. Non-gazetted posts in the district and zonal cadres were to go, in their great bulk, to candidates local to the cadre; an administrative tribunal for service grievances, promised by the formula's fourth point, was constituted to hear the disputes the new scheme would generate. The Regional Committee disappeared, and with it the one institution whose sole business had been Telangana; in its place stood planning boards for backward areas across the state, on the formula's theory that backwardness, not region, was the legitimate object of protection.

The movement's critique of the settlement, developed across the following twenty-five years, fastened on the exchange at its centre. The Mulki rules had been Telangana's property, enforceable by Telangana's employees in Telangana's posts; the Order's local cadres were a general administrative scheme, administered by the same state government whose appointments the rules had restrained, and revisable by the same hands. Every subsequent audit therefore read as a sequel: the Girglani Commission's eventual findings of large-scale violation, the unexecuted repatriations of Government Order 610, the tribunal's accumulating docket. Defenders of the unified state answered the critique on its merits and their answer belongs in this record at its strongest: the zonal scheme delivered local employment across all three regions, ended the anomaly of a law that classed an Indian citizen as a foreigner in his own state for service purposes, and coincided with decades in which Telangana's per capita indicators converged with the state's average on several measures the Srikrishna Committee would later tabulate. The committee's report of 2010 held both propositions at once, finding the region backward on irrigation and some services yet integrated in the state's economy, which is why its authors could recommend a council in the spirit of 1956 and call the recommendation workable. The movement read the same tables and drew the opposite inference, that integration without enforceable safeguards was the name of the disease. Between those two readings of one set of tables, the politics of the next decade arranged itself.

## The Water Question: Irrigation as the Movement's Material Core

Of the triad's three nouns, water carried the heaviest material charge, because a river cannot be audited into existence and a deferred canal is visible from every dry village it was drawn to serve. Telangana sits on the Deccan plateau in the upper reaches of the Krishna and the Godavari, and its historic irrigation was tank-based, a lattice of rain-fed reservoirs that the Nizam's engineers had extended and the merged state's plans repeatedly promised to modernise. The movement's water argument, stated in its submissions across both phases, ran as follows. The great dams of the undivided state stood disproportionately in or for the coastal plain: Nagarjuna Sagar, completed in 1967 across the Krishna between Nalgonda in Telangana and the coastal districts, sent the larger part of its command down the canals of the delta, while the projects designed for Telangana's own reaches, the Srisailam left bank works, the Jurala project on the Krishna in Mahbubnagar, the lift schemes of the drought districts, waited decades between sanction and water. The Godavari, which drains the region's north, flowed, in the movement's phrase, past Telangana to the sea, its utilisation by the region held to a fraction of the state's, while the Polavaram project on that river promised the coast irrigation and power at the price of submerging villages in Telangana's Khammam district. The Bachawat Tribunal's award of 1976 had allocated the Krishna's waters to the undivided state as a whole, leaving the division between regions an internal matter, and internal was precisely the arena in which, the movement argued, the region always lost.

The unified state's answer, given in its own submissions to the Srikrishna Committee, contested the accounting without conceding the principle. Plateau irrigation is costlier per acre than delta irrigation; lift schemes consume power the state had to generate; several Telangana projects were delayed by inter-state disputes, forest clearances and the geology of their own alignments, not by coastal conspiracy; and the state's aggregate investment in the region's irrigation rose steeply in the 2000s under governments of both parties. The Srikrishna report's water chapters tabulated utilisations and expenditures that both sides mined, finding regional imbalance in the historical stock of projects and recent convergence in the flow of investment, and recommended a technically competent water management board for the state as part of its preferred option. The Reorganisation Act answered the argument in the only way a statute can, by institutionalising the quarrel: management boards for both rivers, a national project at Polavaram executed by the Centre, and an apex council of the two Chief Ministers under the Union's chairmanship. That the boards' jurisdiction remained unnotified for years after 2014, and that water became the first durable dispute of the successor states, belongs to the bifurcation's governance record rather than the movement's; the movement's own verdict was simpler, and it had been delivered in the triad's order of words for half a century: neellu first, because water is the one promise a government cannot print.

## Feature Three: The Telangana Rashtra Samithi and the Second Movement

On 27 April 2001, at Jala Drushyam in Hyderabad, K. Chandrashekar Rao resigned the Deputy Speakership and his membership of the Telugu Desam Party and founded the Telangana Rashtra Samithi, a party whose constitution carried a single-point agenda: a separate state of Telangana with Hyderabad as its capital. The founding was a renegotiating act in the strict sense. Rao's stated case, set out in his resignation and in the party's founding addresses, was that every existing party had signed Telangana's bargains and every government had breached them, so the demand required a vehicle that could not trade it away without dissolving itself. The design answered 1971 directly: the Samithi had been absorbable because it had a programme beyond its programme, and its leader could be paid in office; the new party's single point made absorption visible as betrayal. Within weeks the party contested local body elections across the region and won enough zilla and mandal parishad seats to demonstrate that the demand of the quiet decades had an electorate waiting for it.

The design was tested sooner than its founder can have expected. In the elections of April and May 2004, the Telangana Rashtra Samithi fought in alliance with the Congress, and it won five Lok Sabha seats and twenty-six Assembly seats, a bloc large enough to enter both governments and too small to command either. The United Progressive Alliance's Common Minimum Programme, adopted on 27 May 2004, carried the sentence the party had bargained for: the government would consider the demand for a separate Telangana state, at an appropriate time, after due consultations and consensus. In January 2005 the Alliance constituted a three-member sub-committee under Pranab Mukherjee to examine the demand. The sub-committee consulted parties for eighteen months and produced no recommendation, since consensus, defined as the agreement of the state's own divided legislature, was the one commodity the process was guaranteed never to yield. The party's ministers resigned from the state cabinet in July 2005, and the logic of the single point completed itself at the Centre in the following year.

### Why did the Telangana Rashtra Samithi leave the United Progressive Alliance?

The party's five MPs had entered the alliance on a written promise, carried in the Common Minimum Programme of May 2004, that Telangana would be considered after consultations. When the Pranab Mukherjee sub-committee drifted without a report, K. Chandrashekar Rao resigned his ministry in August 2006 and later his seat.

Rao resigned as Union Minister for Labour and Employment on 23 August 2006, and in September 2006 he resigned the Karimnagar Lok Sabha seat itself, contesting the December 2006 by-election as a referendum on the delay and winning it by a majority exceeding two lakh votes. The sequence is a small masterpiece of the citizen-renegotiation method, and the movement's own literature treats it as such: the leader converted an office the alliance had given him into evidence about the alliance, surrendering the office to prove the bargain had failed. The years 2007 and 2008 repeated the manoeuvre at a discount, with waves of resignations by the party's legislators and a round of by-elections in May 2008 that returned a diminished party; in the 2009 general election the Samithi fought inside the Telugu Desam-led Grand Alliance and fell to two Lok Sabha seats and ten Assembly seats, its worst result since founding. By the autumn of 2009, the regional-political reading appeared vindicated and the obituaries of the second movement were being drafted in Hyderabad's newspapers. What rescued the demand was not a party calculation at all. It was the death of Chief Minister Y. S. Rajasekhara Reddy in a helicopter crash on 2 September 2009, which removed the strongest living opponent of bifurcation from the state's politics, and the decision of a written-off leader to stake his life on the demand eleven weeks later.

## The Second Movement's Public: Employees, Campuses and Song

The second phase differed from the first in the breadth of the public it organised, and the difference is measurable in the movement's own institutions. The employees came first in organisational weight. The Telangana Non-Gazetted Officers' association and the wider employees' federations carried the Presidential Order's violation ledgers as their institutional memory; their leader Swami Goud and the employees' joint committees gave the Joint Action Committee its strike capacity, since a general strike called by associations that staff the secretariat, the schools, the transport depots and the Singareni collieries is a fact, not a metaphor. The Singareni miners of the Godavari valley coal belt struck with the rest in 2011, and the stoppage of coal production converted a regional agitation into an item in the Union government's energy arithmetic within weeks. The lawyers of the district courts organised their own action committees and boycotted courts; the teachers closed the schools; the road transport crews parked the buses. A movement that can enumerate its participants by profession, as this one could through its constituent committees, has passed the test that separates a crowd from a citizenry in negotiation.

The campuses supplied the second phase with its shock troops and its martyrs, as Osmania University had supplied the first phase. The Osmania students' joint action committee of December 2009, and the parallel committees at Kakatiya University in Warangal and the region's other universities, organised the relay hunger strikes and campus closures that kept the movement's temperature high between the party's electoral defeats. The movement's dead in this phase were disproportionately students and unemployed young men who killed themselves for the demand, many leaving notes that named statehood as the cause; the Samithi and the Joint Action Committee compiled their names into a martyrology that movement compilations carry above twelve hundred for the years between 2001 and 2014, while the state government's own statements acknowledged a smaller toll, and the contest over the count became part of the contest over the movement's meaning. This article records both figures in their holders' hands, as the charter of its stance requires: a movement that counts its dead as evidence in a negotiation, and a state that counts them as a law-and-order statistic, are describing the same corpses in incommensurable grammars, and the historian's duty is to keep the grammars distinct.

Women's organisations formed a fourth column of the second phase's public, and the movement's literature is explicit about the debt. Women's joint action committees in the districts organised relay fasts and kitchen-supported pickets that kept the employees' strikes fed and lawful for weeks; the Bathukamma festival, in which women carry flower stacks to the village tanks in honour of the goddess, was celebrated in the movement years as a declaration of regional identity, its songs carrying the statehood argument into courtyards that party meetings rarely reached. The Samithi's opponents in the unified camp noted, accurately, that the movement's leadership remained overwhelmingly male, and the movement's own women leaders made the same observation in its forums, an interior dispute that outlasted the victory and belongs to the record of the demand's social interior examined below.

The third institution was cultural, and the second phase built it deliberately. The balladeer Gaddar, whose songs had carried the revolutionary movement of an earlier generation, lent his voice to the statehood platform; the Dhoom Dhaam troupes organised by Rasamayi Balakishan from 2002 carried the argument village to village in folk forms, itemising water, funds and jobs in verse; the festivals of Bathukamma and Bonalu were celebrated as assertions of a distinct regional culture against the unified state's official calendar. The cultural work answered the linguistic frame's deepest difficulty. Telangana and Andhra share a language, and the unified state's defenders argued from 1956 onward that one language made one people. The movement's cultural apparatus replied that a people is made by its dialects, its festivals, its food and its remembered injuries, and that Telangana Telugu, stigmatised in the unified state's cinema and secretariat as a rustic dialect, was the speech of a distinct political community. When statues of the unified state's cultural pantheon went into Hussain Sagar in March 2011, the act announced that the renegotiation had reached the level of symbols. A demand that has acquired its own martyrs, its own festivals and its own songs is no longer a clause in a party's manifesto; it is a settlement's rival, organised and waiting.

## Feature Four: The Eleven Days of 2009 and the Committee Years

On 29 November 2009, K. Chandrashekar Rao set out from Karimnagar for Rangadham Pally in Siddipet district to begin a fast unto death for a separate state. Police arrested him on the road and lodged him in Khammam sub-jail, where he began the fast in custody; as his condition deteriorated he was moved to the Nizam's Institute of Medical Sciences in Hyderabad, refusing food from a hospital bed while Osmania University's students formed a joint action committee and made the campus the headquarters of an uprising. The fast's first martyr was not the fasting man. Dasoju Srikanth Chary, an Osmania student, set himself on fire at LB Nagar in Hyderabad in the fast's opening days and died on 4 December 2009, and his death converted a leader's protest into a region's mobilisation: employees, lawyers, teachers, cultural troupes and village committees joined in a rising that participants and historians of the movement alike compare to 1969, with the campuses again at its centre. On 7 December 2009, Chief Minister K. Rosaiah convened an all-party meeting at which the state's major parties recorded their assent to a separate Telangana state, and two nights later, at about half past eleven on 9 December 2009, Union Home Minister P. Chidambaram read the sentence the movement had spent forty years extracting: the process of forming the state of Telangana would be initiated, and an appropriate resolution would be moved in the state Assembly. Rao broke his fast of eleven days with lime juice offered by Professor K. Jayashankar, the movement's senior ideologue.

The announcement governed India for fourteen days. In the coastal districts and Rayalaseema, the Samaikyandhra movement rose against it with a speed matching the Telangana rising it answered: legislators of every party from the two regions tendered resignations in such numbers that the state's government faced collapse, students shut the universities of the coast, and the united-state case, dormant since 1973, acquired a street army of its own. On 23 December 2009, Chidambaram read a second statement: the situation had altered, and the government would hold wide-ranging consultations with all political parties and groups before proceeding. The process announced a fortnight earlier was paused. In Telangana the pause was read, predictably and not unreasonably, as 1971 in modern dress, a bargain withdrawn at the moment of performance. The movement's answer was organisational rather than rhetorical. On 24 December 2009 the Telangana Joint Action Committee was formed under the convenership of Professor M. Kodandaram, binding the Samithi, the employees' associations, the students' committees and dozens of caste, cultural and professional bodies into a single command that could call a strike and be obeyed across ten districts. It is the Joint Action Committee, more than any party, that embodies the citizen-renegotiation claim in the second phase: a parliament of the movement, standing outside the legislature whose arithmetic had failed it.

Delhi's instrument of delay was a committee. On 3 February 2010 the Union government constituted a five-member committee under Justice B. N. Srikrishna, a retired judge of the Supreme Court, to examine the situation in Andhra Pradesh and the demands for separation and unity alike. The committee toured the state, received thousands of representations, and submitted its report to the Home Ministry on 30 December 2010; the report was released on 6 January 2011. Its six options ranged from the status quo, which it found untenable, through bifurcations with Hyderabad as a union territory or as part of a Rayala-Telangana state, to its fifth option, bifurcation on the existing boundaries with Hyderabad as Telangana's capital, which it called the second best course, and its sixth option, a united state governed with a statutory and empowered Telangana Regional Council holding adequate funds, functions and functionaries, which, at page 454 of the report, it recommended as the most workable way forward in the spirit of the Gentlemen's Agreement of 1956. A confidential eighth chapter on law and order and the management of the announcement, submitted in a sealed cover, became public through court proceedings in 2011 and damaged the report's standing in Telangana beyond repair: a committee that advised the government on handling the movement's reaction had, in the movement's reading, confessed that consultation was a technique of control.

The report settled nothing, and the movement escalated on its own calendar. On 10 March 2011 the Million March brought Hyderabad to a halt and ended with statues of Telugu cultural figures on Tank Bund thrown into Hussain Sagar lake, an act of iconoclasm that announced the movement's quarrel was with the unified state's very pantheon. From 13 September to 24 October 2011, the Sakala Janula Samme, the general strike of all sections, shut government offices, schools, transport and the Singareni collieries for forty-two days, organised by the Joint Action Committee through the employees' unions. On 30 September 2012 the Telangana March, named Sagaraharam for its necklace formation around the lake, defied prohibitory orders across the city. Each action followed the citizen-renegotiation grammar the first phase had written: an audited grievance, a deadline, a non-violent mass instrument, and a documentary demand addressed to a named authority. When the end came, it came through party arithmetic in Delhi, but the arithmetic had been manufactured in Telangana's streets.

## The Committee Years in the Districts, 2010 to 2012

The years between the Srikrishna report and the Congress Working Committee's resolution were not a waiting room; they were the phase in which the movement occupied every institution the state offered and measured each one's refusal. The electoral instrument spoke first. When the committee's terms of reference were announced in February 2010, twelve legislators from the region resigned their Assembly seats in protest at yet another examination of a demand they held to be decided; in the by-elections of July 2010 the Samithi and its informal ally, the Bharatiya Janata Party, won all twelve seats, eleven and one respectively, on the statehood plank alone, in contests the Congress and the Telugu Desam largely declined to fight at full strength. The verdict's meaning was not lost on Delhi: within the region, an election fought on the demand returned the demand's party, whatever the same voters did in general elections fought on other questions. In July 2011 the Congress members of Parliament from Telangana resigned their seats en masse over the delay since the report, an act that put the Union government's own backbenchers in the movement's column for a season and demonstrated the demand's capture of the ruling party's regional wing, the development the regional-political reading must explain and cannot easily: an instrument does not usually resign from the hand that wields it.

The street instruments matured in the same years. The Sadak Bandh of 21 March 2013 closed the national highway through the region for a day; the Chalo Assembly call of June 2013 brought the movement to the legislature's gates; the employees' unions rehearsed in 2011's general strike the stoppage that Seemandhra's employees would mirror in 2013 on the other side. The political parties of the state, meanwhile, performed the era's characteristic ritual, the letter: the Telugu Desam had written to the Pranab Mukherjee sub-committee in 2008 recording its support for a separate Telangana state, a letter its leadership thereafter qualified as the state's opinion changed around it, and the movement preserved the letter as it preserved every document, an exhibit in the audit. The YSR Congress, founded in 2011, equivocated on the question through its rise and paid for the equivocation in the region at the 2014 count. By the winter of 2012 the geometry was fixed and visible to every calculator in Delhi: within Telangana, no party could win the region while opposing the state; within Seemandhra, no party could win it while supporting the division; and the Congress, holding both halves of the contradiction, would eventually have to choose which half to lose. It chose, on 30 July 2013, to lose the half it had already lost in December 2009, when its own announcement first taught the state that the division was a matter of timing rather than principle. The committee years are the citizen-renegotiation framework's strongest exhibit after 1969 itself: a movement with no office, no majority and no army kept a question on the national agenda for three years by occupying, in rotation, the ballot, the secretariat, the campus, the highway and the ruling party's own benches, until the forum of decision concluded that refusal cost more than concession.

## The Fast as an Instrument: Three Hunger Strikes and a Grammar of Coercion

The movement's signature instrument was the hunger strike, and its grammar had been written in the same soil two generations before the movement began. Potti Sriramulu, a Gandhian from the coastal districts, fasted at Madras from 19 October 1952 for a separate Andhra state and died on the fifty-eighth night of his fast, 15 December 1952; the riots that followed his death forced Jawaharlal Nehru to announce Andhra State within the week. The precedent was not lost on either side of the later quarrel. A Telugu fast had created the first linguistic state, and the fast thereafter stood in the region's political memory as the instrument that Delhi could not indefinitely refuse: a citizen's body, publicly wasting, converting an administrative question into a moral emergency with a deadline set by physiology. Ravindranath's fast at Khammam in January 1969 invoked the precedent deliberately, and the students who carried his demand into the streets understood themselves to be operating an instrument with a known yield.

K. Chandrashekar Rao's fast of November and December 2009 was the precedent's most calculated use, and analysing it as theatre alone misses its design. The fast was announced after the Samithi's worst electoral result, when the demand's market price stood at its lowest; it was begun in defiance of arrest, conducted in a jail and a hospital under continuous medical bulletin; and it was paired with an organisation, the Joint Action Committee formed in its aftermath, capable of converting the fast's moral capital into strikes before the capital could cool. Its yield, the announcement of 9 December 2009, arrived on the eleventh day; its reversal, on 23 December, taught the movement the precedent's limit, that a fast can extract a sentence but cannot hold a government to the sentence once the faster eats. The movement's subsequent method, the forty-two-day general strike, the million march, the siege of the Assembly's calendar, can be read as the search for an instrument with the fast's urgency and a longer half-life. The comparative point, within this pillar's wider record, is that the Indian state's negotiators have a practised repertoire for every instrument except the one that couples a wasting body to an organised citizenry: concessions can be scheduled, committees appointed, reports commissioned, but the combination of Srikanth Chary's pyre and the employees' strike registers in the same fortnight left Delhi, in December 2009, with no instrument except the sentence it spoke and then withdrew. The withdrawal, more than the fast, is the second phase's founding event, since everything built after it, the Committee, the strikes, the refusal of every formula, was built on the premise that the sentence had been dishonoured. A state that wishes its bargains believed must perform them; the movement's final demand, separation itself, was the price of a performance twice deferred.

## Feature Five: From the Resolution of July 2013 to the State of June 2014

On 30 July 2013, the Congress Working Committee resolved unanimously to request the Union government to form a separate state of Telangana from the ten districts of the region, with Hyderabad as the common capital of both successor states for ten years. The resolution's movers stated their reasoning plainly: Digvijay Singh, the general secretary in charge of the state, presented the decision as the fulfilment of a long-standing demand, and the committee constituted under A. K. Antony was appointed the same evening to hear the anxieties of the party's coastal and Rayalaseema legislators. The Samaikyandhra movement answered at a scale exceeding 2009: government employees of the two regions struck work from August into October 2013, teachers closed schools, and the state's Chief Minister, N. Kiran Kumar Reddy, a Congressman from Rayalaseema, conducted a public campaign against his own party's decision, arguing on the Assembly's floor and outside it that bifurcation would poison water sharing, revive left-wing extremism and strand the residents of Hyderabad who had arrived from the coast. The Union Cabinet approved the bifurcation on 3 October 2013, set a Group of Ministers to draft the statute, and the President referred the draft bill to the state legislature in December, as Article 3 of the Constitution requires, for views Parliament is free to disregard. On 30 January 2014 the Andhra Pradesh Assembly returned its view: a resolution moved by the Chief Minister rejecting the bill outright, carried by voice vote. Under [the federal architecture of the Constitution](/2019/01/03/federalism-indian-constitution-explained/), the rejection was an opinion, not a veto, and Delhi proceeded.

The drafting process that followed the July resolution deserves its own record, since the statute's clauses were the movement's last battlefield. The Union Cabinet's decision of 3 October 2013 constituted a Group of Ministers, chaired by Home Minister Sushilkumar Shinde, to work out the bill's provisions, with Rural Development Minister Jairam Ramesh playing a central drafting role and the committee under A. K. Antony hearing the apprehensions of the coastal and Rayalaseema leadership in parallel. The draft travelled to Hyderabad in December and returned at the end of January with the Assembly's rejection and thousands of amendments proposed by its members appended, every one of which Parliament was entitled to disregard and did. When the Cabinet approved the final bill on 7 February 2014, the document that emerged carried the negotiation's whole history in its clauses: the ten-year common capital the July resolution had promised, the Governor's special responsibility that Seemandhra's negotiators had demanded as their settlers' shield and Telangana's negotiators had resisted as a Union foothold, the river boards neither side trusted the other to staff, and the Polavaram clause that made the Centre the executor of the coast's largest project on a river rising in the new state's complaint. The 2014 election results completed the ledger. In Telangana's one hundred and nineteen Assembly seats the Samithi took sixty-three, the Congress twenty-one, the Telugu Desam fifteen, the Bharatiya Janata Party nine and the Majlis seven; in the region's seventeen Lok Sabha seats the Samithi took eleven. A party founded thirteen years earlier at a veteran's house, on a clause every established party had signed and shelved, held the new state entire.

The parliamentary endgame compressed the movement's whole history into a fortnight. The Union Cabinet cleared the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Bill on 7 February 2014. On 13 February 2014, as Home Minister Sushilkumar Shinde rose to introduce it in the Lok Sabha, Lagadapati Rajagopal, a Congress member from Vijayawada expelled from his party days earlier for defying the decision, discharged pepper spray inside the chamber; sixteen members were suspended in the disorder that followed, and the image of legislators fleeing the House in tears became the counter-movement's last emblem. On 18 February 2014 the Lok Sabha passed the bill by voice vote, in proceedings whose live telecast was interrupted: the Lok Sabha secretariat attributed the break to a technical failure, while opposition members alleged a deliberate blackout, and the dispute over the interruption entered the record alongside the vote itself. The Rajya Sabha passed the bill on 20 February 2014 after Prime Minister Manmohan Singh gave the residuary state a set of assurances on the floor, covering special category status, a financial package and the Polavaram project as a national work. The President gave assent on 1 March 2014. Elections to the undivided state's assembly and to the Lok Sabha were held across April and May 2014, and the Telangana Rashtra Samithi won sixty-three of the new state's one hundred and nineteen Assembly seats and eleven of its seventeen Lok Sabha seats. On 2 June 2014, the appointed day fixed by the statute, Telangana came into existence as the twenty-ninth state of the Union, and K. Chandrashekar Rao took oath as its first Chief Minister.

The six months between assent and the appointed day ran under a constitutional shadow that completed the symmetry of 1969 and 1973. Kiran Kumar Reddy resigned the chief ministership on 19 February 2014, the day after the Lok Sabha vote, and formed a party of his own to contest the coming election on the united platform; the undivided state passed under President's rule on 1 March 2014, the day the Reorganisation Act received assent, so that Andhra Pradesh spent its final three months governed from Delhi, as its predecessor crisis of 1973 had also been governed. The general election of April and May 2014 was thus fought in a state that had legally ceased to have a future, its voters choosing two assemblies in one ballot. In the residuary state the Telugu Desam, in alliance with the Bharatiya Janata Party, won the mandate to build a new capital and a new administration; in Telangana the Samithi's sixty-three seats converted the movement's command structure into the state's first government without an intervening day. The speed of that conversion is the fifth feature's concluding evidence: a movement that has spent thirteen years auditing a state is unusually prepared to staff one.

The statute the movement had won repays the slow reading its negotiators gave it; the full text, with its ten schedules of institutions to be divided, opens for clause-by-clause study in an [office file viewer for Excel, DOCX and PPTX files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), and its architecture is the fifth feature's substance. It created Telangana with ten districts and a residuary Andhra Pradesh with thirteen. Hyderabad would serve as the common capital of both states for a period not exceeding ten years, and Section 8 of the Act laid on the common Governor a special responsibility for the security of life, liberty and property of all residents of the common capital area, discharging it with the assistance of two advisors appointed by the Union government. It constituted management boards for the Krishna and Godavari rivers, declared the Polavaram irrigation project a national project to be executed by the Centre, and divided assets, liabilities and All India Services cadres by formula and by schedule. The common-capital term ran its statutory course and closed on 1 June 2024, from which date Hyderabad stood as the capital of Telangana alone.

### What did the Union government promise the residuary state in February 2014?

Speaking in the Rajya Sabha on 20 February 2014, Prime Minister Manmohan Singh assured the residuary state special category status for five years, a special financial package for its backward districts, central execution of the Polavaram project as a national work, and assistance in building a new capital.

## Hyderabad: The City Both Movements Claimed

Every clause of the quarrel converged on one city. Hyderabad was Telangana's capital by geography and history, the seat of the Nizam's state and of Hyderabad State after 1948, and it was simultaneously, in the united case's telling, the joint creation of the merged state's six decades, built up with coastal capital, coastal enterprise and the labour of migrants from every district. The city's economy generated a share of the undivided state's revenues out of all proportion to the region's population, a fact each side deployed in opposite directions: for the Telangana argument, the surplus extracted from the city's growth while the districts around it waited for water; for the united argument, proof that the city's wealth was a common asset that separation would confiscate. The Srikrishna Committee's options were, at bottom, five answers to the Hyderabad question and one refusal to answer it, since every bifurcation variant differed chiefly in the city's assignment, to Telangana, to a union territory, or to an enlarged metropolitan territory stitched to the coast. The option the committee preferred avoided the question by avoiding bifurcation. The statute of 2014 answered it with a decade of shared custody, a common Governor charged with the security of every resident, and a date after which the sharing would end.

Placed against the series' wider map, the Telangana question tests the frameworks its neighbouring pillars built. The basic structure doctrine, whose career as [the most consequential doctrine of the Constitution](/2020/07/28/basic-structure-most-consequential-doctrine/) frames the judiciary pillar, never governed the case: state creation under Article 3 is among the Constitution's most purely political powers, and the movement's whole history unfolded in legislatures, cabinets and streets rather than courtrooms, the Mulki litigation of 1972 being the exception that proved the rule. The case belongs instead to the domestic politics of decisions that present themselves as technical, the same grammar this series has traced in [the domestic politics of non-alignment](/2020/09/21/non-alignment-indian-domestic-politics/), where a foreign-policy posture was fought as a domestic settlement, and in [the 1991 reforms read as politics](/2020/11/15/1991-reform-as-politics-reanalysed/), where an economic programme's clauses were contested as a distribution of power. It also belongs to the long renegotiation of the founding tensions themselves, the centre against the regions, the written promise against the administered reality, which [the Modi-era settlement of the three tensions](/2019/07/03/2014-inflection-election-reanalysed/) inherited in 2014 with Telangana as its first accomplished fact: a state created by the old system's methods, in the old system's last parliament, weeks before the electorate changed the system.

## The Company Telangana Kept: Statehood Demands in the Same Decades

Telangana's claim ripened in a republic that was redrawing its internal map on other principles elsewhere, and the comparisons isolate what was distinctive in the Telangana method. The three states of November 2000, Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand and Jharkhand, were created by parliamentary consensus after movements of varying depth, none of which had been required to prove its case across four decades of audit; Uttarakhand's movement had its own martyrs, at Rampur Tiraha in 1994, yet its state arrived as part of a package the ruling party found convenient. Vidarbha's demand, older than Telangana's and resting on a parallel documentary base (the same Fazl Ali Commission had recommended a separate Vidarbha, and the Nagpur Pact of 1953 had written safeguards for it into Maharashtra's merger that its holders also charged with breach), remained ungranted through the same period, lacking a single-point party willing to stake its existence on the demand and a capital city whose possession could force the question. The Gorkhaland agitation of 2007 to 2011, led by Bimal Gurung's Gorkha Janmukti Morcha, won an autonomous council in 2011 and stalled there, its territory too small and its leverage over the state too slight to raise the price of refusal. The Bodoland settlement, renegotiated through accords in 1993, 2003 and 2020 after an armed phase Telangana never entered, shows the alternative grammar in which Delhi negotiates with guns before it negotiates with audits.

The pattern across the cases fits the framework this pillar defends. Delhi concedes statehood where the cost of refusal, computed in parliamentary arithmetic, administrative paralysis and moral spectacle, exceeds the cost of concession, computed in the anger of the region that loses territory or status. Telangana's movement is the only one in the set that raised both sides of that ledger deliberately: it maximised the cost of refusal through the fast, the strike and the electoral verdicts of 2010, and it lowered the cost of concession by accepting, in the end, a shared capital for ten years, a residuary package for Seemandhra and the loss of any claim on the institutions the schedules assigned to the successor state. The Vidarbha demand raised neither cost; the Gorkhaland demand raised the first and could not offer the second. One route was expressly closed to Telangana throughout: the demand, heard in every phase, for a second States Reorganisation Commission to examine statehood claims together was never granted, and the movement treated the refusal as confirmation that commissions are appointed to dissipate demands whose justice the appointer does not dispute, a reading the Srikrishna Committee's fate did nothing to soften. Denied the commission route, the movement built its own evidentiary record and carried it to the forum that remained, which is why the Telangana file, alone among the statehood files of its generation, reads as a prosecution dossier rather than a petition. Read comparatively, Telangana in 2014 is not the republic finally honouring a linguistic logic or a party finally collecting a debt. It is the republic paying, at the negotiated price, a citizenry that had spent forty-five years itemising the bill.

## Article 3 and the Irrelevance of the Assembly's No

The movement's constitutional position was stronger than its opponents' rhetoric allowed, and the statute's passage proved it. Article 3 of the Constitution empowers Parliament to form a new state by separation of territory, requiring only that the bill be introduced on the President's recommendation and that the President refer it to the affected state legislature for its views within a period he fixes. The Supreme Court settled the clause's meaning two generations before Telangana tested it. In the advisory opinion In re Berubari Union (1960), the Court distinguished cession of territory to a foreign power, which Article 3 cannot do, from internal reorganisation, which it can; and in Babulal Parate v. State of Bombay (1960) the Court held that the reference to the state legislature exists to elicit views, not to obtain consent, and that Parliament remains free to act on its own judgment after the period expires. The federalism of Indian state-making is, by the Constitution's own design, a federalism of consultation rather than concurrence, a design [the comparative study of state settlements](/2019/12/21/comparative-indian-state-political-settlements/) in this series traces across every reorganisation since 1956.

The events of December 2013 to February 2014 were the doctrine's most dramatic application. The President referred the bill to the Andhra Pradesh legislature in December 2013; the legislature debated it for weeks in an atmosphere of siege, with members of the two regions addressing different republics from the same floor; and on 30 January 2014 it returned the bill with a resolution, moved by the Chief Minister himself, rejecting it. In the creations of 2000 the parent assemblies had assented, and commentators on the 2013 proceedings warned that a rejection would confront Parliament with an unprecedented choice. Parliament made the choice the doctrine prescribed: it treated the rejection as a view, noted it, and proceeded, making Telangana the clearest demonstration in the republic's history that a state in India is created by the Union's decision and by nothing else. The movement understood this before the lawyers finished saying it. Its whole method, from 2009 onward, had been addressed past Hyderabad to Delhi, to the only forum whose arithmetic could grant the demand, and its Delhi-facing instruments, the fast in a hospital under national cameras, the letters extracted from national parties, the resignations of Union ministers and members of Parliament, were the method's working parts. The Samaikyandhra movement, by contrast, spent its strength in the forum the Constitution had made advisory, winning the Assembly's resolution and losing the state. The asymmetry is the coldest sentence this article contains, and the record permits no softer one.

## Three Readings of One Movement

The linguistic-state reading holds the strongest documentary card of the three: the Fazl Ali Commission's own hesitation in 1955, which recorded that Telangana's leaders feared absorption and that a separate Hyderabad state had a serious case. On this reading, 2014 corrected 1956 at a distance of fifty-eight years, and the movement is a footnote to a commission's minority instinct. The reading fails on the movement's own evidence. Telangana's demand after 1969 was never framed by its holders as the completion of linguistic reorganisation; the region already shared a language with the state it sought to leave, which is precisely why the linguistic frame cannot carry the case. The movement's slogans named water, funds and jobs, neellu, nidhulu, niyamakalu, a triad of distributive grievances inside one language group. A frame that cannot state the movement's own stated reasons cannot explain its forty-five years.

The regional-political reading, in K. Srinivasulu's account of 2002 and in the essays of M. Bhaskaran Nair on the post-2009 trajectory, holds the strongest sociological card: the movements of both phases were led by identifiable regional elites, staffed by particular caste and class blocs, and harvested by parties, the Samithi in 1971 and the Samithi's successor in 2014, at the exact points where party advantage opened. Gautam Pingle's The Fall and Rise of Telangana (2014), written by a participant-scholar who served the second movement, complicates the reading from inside, documenting a mobilisation whose depth in the districts repeatedly escaped the intentions of its conveners. The citizen-renegotiation reading accepts the sociology and disputes the inference. That elites lead and parties harvest is the condition of every Indian movement, including the ones this series has traced from [the JP movement's total revolution](/2021/01/14/jp-movement-total-revolution/) to [the Narmada valley's long refusal](/2021/01/25/narmada-bachao-andolan-reanalysed/); the question is what the mobilisation operationally did. In this case it returned, in 1969, in 1973, in 2004, in 2009 and in 2013, to the written terms of a bargain, audited their performance, and extracted a revised instrument each time the audit failed. The counter-case deserves its strongest statement before the verdict.

The counter-movement's case had deep roots of its own, and honesty requires them in the record. The unified Telugu state was not an accident of 1956 but a project with a pedigree: the Visalandhra idea, a single state for all Telugu speakers, had been propagated by the Andhra communists of the 1940s, set out in P. Sundarayya's writings and carried into the reorganisation debates as the democratic alternative to the princely and provincial map; the Andhra State of 1953 was its first instalment, and the merger of 1956 its completion. Rayalaseema's consent had its own prior bargain, the Sribagh Pact of November 1937 between coastal and Rayalaseema leaders, which promised the drier region safeguards on irrigation and a share of institutions if the two ever formed a state, a document Rayalaseema's leaders produced in 2013 and 2014 to argue that they were being severed from one partner by a decision in which their region's interests counted least of the three. The Samaikyandhra mobilisation of 2009 to 2014 thus spoke in three regional voices that did not fully harmonise: the coastal voice of capital invested in Hyderabad, the Rayalaseema voice of a region that feared subordination in a residuary state dominated by the coast, and the Hyderabad settler voice of ordinary employees and traders whose lives, whatever the lawyers decided, were located in the city. The movement against bifurcation held them together for four years on the single proposition that the state should not be divided. Its failure was not a failure of argument. It was a failure of arithmetic in Delhi, where the Congress leadership had concluded by July 2013 that the costs of refusal in Telangana exceeded the costs of concession in Seemandhra, and where the principal opposition party, the Bharatiya Janata Party, supported bifurcation on its own long-stated preference for smaller states. Renegotiation by citizens is decided, in the last instance, by whether the forum of decision can still afford to say no; by 2013 it could not, and the pepper spray of February 2014 was the sound of a case that had lost its forum.

### What did the Samaikyandhra movement argue?

The united-state movement argued that Hyderabad had been built with the savings and enterprise of all three regions, that separation would strand Seemandhra without a capital, unsettle water sharing on the Krishna and Godavari, and leave Andhra settlers in the city exposed in their homes, jobs and businesses.

That case was not a fiction of vested interests, and this article records it as its holders made it: in the employees' strikes of 2013, in the Assembly's rejection resolution of 30 January 2014, in the resignations of December 2009, and in the pepper spray of 13 February 2014, which its user defended as the act of a member watching his state dismembered without a counted vote. The adjudication the citizen-renegotiation reading offers is narrow. The Samaikyandhra mobilisation proves the framework rather than refuting it: both regions conducted citizen-renegotiation of the same settlement, and the settlement's renegotiation was always going to be decided by which side's instruments the Union's arithmetic could not refuse. The Telangana side prevailed because its instruments, a single-point party, a joint action committee commanding forty-two days of general strike, and a leader's body staked in a hospital bed, raised the cost of refusal above the cost of concession, while the united side's instruments, resignations that injured only their own government and a chief minister campaigning against his own party, raised the cost of concession without raising the cost of refusal in Delhi. The comparison with [the comparative study of Indian state political settlements](/2019/12/21/comparative-indian-state-political-settlements/) is instructive: settlements endure where renegotiation channels absorb pressure early, and Andhra Pradesh's channels, advisory councils and consensus committees, were designed to absorb and defer.

## The Documentary Habit: Audits as the Movement's Method

No Indian movement has left a thicker paper trail of its own grievance, and the trail explains the movement's durability better than any speech. The method was born in the safeguard decade: if the bargain was written, its breach could be tabulated. The Bhargava Committee of 1969 tabulated the surplus from the state's accounts. A later official examination under Kumar Lalit, working over the years 1956 to 1968, computed the diversion of Telangana's revenues at Rs. 63.9 crore and itemised the mechanisms by which the region's money had been spent in the capital and credited to the region's development. The employees' associations tabulated the appointment registers year by year. When the Telugu Desam government of N. T. Rama Rao issued Government Order 610 in December 1985, directing the repatriation of employees appointed in the region in violation of the Presidential Order, the order itself became the movement's favourite exhibit: the state had confessed, in its own gazette, that the violations were real and enumerable. The order's deadline of March 1986 passed with the repatriations unmade, and the gap between the gazette and the register furnished the second movement with its founding syllabus.

The habit completed itself in the second phase. In 2001 the state government appointed a one-man commission under J. M. Girglani to examine violations of the Presidential Order and Government Order 610; the commission's report, submitted in 2004, recorded violations on a scale that neither government of the period chose to publish into consequence. The Telangana Rashtra Samithi's submissions to the Srikrishna Committee ran to volumes of tabulated grievance on water, funds and appointments, and the committee's own report conceded the region's relative backwardness on several indices while disputing the remedies. This is why the citizen-renegotiation reading claims the case against its rivals on method as much as on outcome. A linguistic movement petitions; a regional party manoeuvres; a citizenry in renegotiation audits, and keeps the audit current for forty-five years, through changes of regime, of party and of generation, until the audit's conclusion, that no safeguard administered by the counterparty can be trusted, becomes the premise of the final demand. The statehood demand of 2009 to 2014 was not the escalation of the audit. It was the audit's verdict, delivered in the only court the Constitution provides for such verdicts, the arithmetic of Parliament persuaded at last to divide what it had joined.

## The Interior of the Demand: Caste, Class and the Second Phase

The regional-political reading's most serious evidence is internal, and K. Srinivasulu's work of 2002 states it best: the demand for Telangana was articulated, in each phase, through the region's caste and class structure, led by its educated and propertied strata and liable to stall whenever those strata were bought off, as the Samithi's leadership was bought off in 1971. The first phase fits the charge in part. Its cadres were students and government employees, the two groups with the most direct stake in the Mulki registers; its leader was a former minister of the old Hyderabad political class; and its ten parliamentary seats were harvested by a leadership that settled for office within a year. The second phase was built, self-consciously, to answer the charge. The Joint Action Committee's constituent map ran from the employees' federations through the lawyers and teachers to caste associations and cultural bodies claiming the region's backward classes, Scheduled Castes and minorities; the demand's idiom shifted from the safeguards of a region to the social composition of its power, and Professor Kodandaram and the movement's intellectuals argued in print that a Telangana administered by the same coastal-dominated networks would merely relocate the grievance, a position the movement's left and bahujan critics pressed further, demanding to know whose Telangana the new state would be.

That internal argument, unresolved at the moment of victory, is preserved here because the stance charter requires the contested points to remain contested on the page. The Samithi's own leadership answered the critics with the movement's record: the general strike's breadth, the martyrology's social spread, the district-level committees that no single caste commanded. Its critics answered in turn that the first government's composition and the movement party's ticket distribution reproduced familiar hierarchies, a dispute the electoral record of the new state would go on adjudicating. For the citizen-renegotiation reading, the interior dispute is not an embarrassment to the framework but a confirmation of its mechanism: a renegotiation conducted by citizens rather than decreed by a vanguard generates its own second-order renegotiations, over who speaks for the citizenry and who inherits the settlement. Andhra Pradesh's tragedy across 1956 to 2014 was not that its citizens renegotiated; it was that its institutions offered renegotiation no channel cheaper than the street, the fast and the general strike, so that every generation paid the full price of the lesson again.

## Two Generations: The Human Continuity of the Demand

Movements that last forty-five years must solve the problem of inheritance, and Telangana's solution was biographical. Konda Laxman Bapuji, a freedom fighter from Asifabad who served as a minister in Kasu Brahmananda Reddy's cabinet, resigned his office in 1969 in support of the separate state, refusing the settlement his own government offered; three decades later, on 27 April 2001, the Telangana Rashtra Samithi was founded at Jala Drushyam, Bapuji's Hyderabad home, the veteran's household serving as the new party's first address. The choice of venue was a deed of succession executed in architecture: the demand of 1969, which had ended in a leader's merger and a generation's bitterness, was relaunched from the house of the one minister who had resigned rather than settle. The student generation of 1969 supplied its own continuity. Mallikarjun, the Osmania student leader who had faced the police firing of that year and gone to prison for the demand, carried the memory into the Congress cabinets of a later decade and died in 2013, months before the appointed day his studenthood had demanded. Professor K. Jayashankar, born in Akkampet in Warangal district and a former vice-chancellor of Kakatiya University, served the second phase as its ideologue, lecturing the districts on the triad of water, funds and jobs and standing at the fasting leader's bedside in December 2009 to offer the lime juice that ended the fast; he died on 21 June 2011, at the height of the committee years, and the movement buried him as it buried its martyrs, with the demand unfinished and the funeral converted into a mobilisation.

The inheritance ran downward as well as across. The students of Osmania and Kakatiya who staffed the second phase's committees were, in district after district, the children and grandchildren of the cohorts of 1969, raised on the martyrs' lists and the surplus audits as family knowledge; the movement's organisations formalised the transmission, marking the deaths of 1969 at the Gun Park memorial and the anniversaries of the fast as a civic calendar. It is this transmission that the regional-political reading, for all its strengths, persistently undervalues: parties die, merge and are bought, but a grievance installed in a region's family memory and institutional calendar acquires a half-life longer than any organisation's. The Samithi of 2001 did not have to persuade Telangana that the safeguards had failed. It had to persuade Telangana that this time the instrument would not be sold, and it made that argument biographically, from Bapuji's house, with Jayashankar's lectures, in the names of 1969's dead. The state of 2014 was the first Indian state created by a movement whose founders could point, in the same room, to a ministerial resignation of 1969 and a fast of 2009 and call them one act.

## Residue: What the Movement Left Standing

A movement's residue is the test the hub framework applies to every case in this pillar, and Telangana's residue is unusually legible because so much of it is statutory. The residue includes one episode that belongs to the movement's own grammar and is recorded here in fairness to it. K. Chandrashekar Rao had declared repeatedly during the committee years that if the Congress granted Telangana, the Telangana Rashtra Samithi would merge itself into the Congress, the instrument dissolving into the settlement it had extracted. When the statute passed in February 2014, the merger did not follow: by March 2014 Rao announced that the Samithi would contest the elections on its own, citing the terms on which the state had been granted and the need to guard the settlement in its infancy, and the Congress, which had calculated on absorbing the movement as it had absorbed the Samithi of 1971, fought the Telangana election as the movement's rival and was routed in the region it had just created. The episode closes the article's argument with a symmetry neither side's literature enjoys: the citizen-renegotiation grammar of bargain, audit and refusal was, in the end, practised by every actor in the story, including the movement upon its own promise, and the electorate of the new state, given the choice in May 2014, ratified the refusal by sixty-three seats to the Congress's twenty-one in the region. A framework that explains only the movement's victories would be a pamphlet. The framework earns its keep by explaining the whole sequence, the promises made by governments and the promises made to governments, as one continuous negotiation conducted by citizens who had learned, across forty-five years, to read every text for the clause that would be breached. The Reorganisation Act itself stands first, its boards, formulas and shared-capital decade still generating the disputes, over river waters above all, that the residuary arrangements were built to contain; the governance of the bifurcation's aftermath, its cadre divisions and capital questions, is traced in [the Andhra Pradesh and Telangana bifurcation settlement](/2020/02/03/andhra-pradesh-telangana-bifurcation-2014/), and this article leaves that ground to it. The movement also left a governing party formed from its own command structure, an outcome rarer in the pillar's record than the party's founders admit and one that converted the Joint Action Committee's alumni into a state apparatus within a single electoral cycle. And it left a completed demonstration, available to every later movement in the republic, of the full citizen-renegotiation sequence run to its end: agreement, breach, audit, mobilisation, concession, renewed breach, and finally the replacement of the settlement itself. That the same sequence, in the same decades, produced the women's reservation movement's partial statutory victories, traced in [the Indian women's movement and the reservation question](/2021/02/05/indian-womens-movement-reservation/), and produced stalemate in other valleys and other capitals, is the comparative fact with which the pillar's argument closes. Telangana's distinctness was never the justice of its grievance, which it shared with half the Union. It was the discipline with which two generations of its citizens kept returning to the text of a promise, demanding that the promise be read aloud, and refusing, at the cost of 369 lives in its first phase and by its own count more than a thousand in its second, to accept that the reading was finished. The new state institutionalised the movement's calendar as its own: 2 June is kept as Telangana Formation Day, the festivals the movement had politicised, Bathukamma and Bonalu, were adopted as state festivals by the first government, and the martyrs of both phases entered the official commemorations of the state the movement built. That adoption is the residue's final layer, and its most double-edged. A movement absorbed into a state's ceremonial loses the adversarial edge that made it formidable, and the Joint Action Committee's veterans said so in print within years of the victory, some of them resuming public criticism of the government their strike had installed. The citizen-renegotiation framework predicts exactly this afterlife: a settlement performed is a settlement that can be audited, and the citizens who audited the bargains of 1956 and 1973 did not surrender the habit in 2014. They merely acquired a nearer text. The state of 2 June 2014 is what that refusal built.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the 1969 first Telangana movement?

The 1969 movement was the first mass agitation for a separate Telangana state, triggered by a hunger strike at Khammam on 6 January 1969 over the violation of the Mulki employment safeguards promised in the Gentlemen's Agreement of 1956. Students of Osmania University and government employees carried the demand across all ten districts, the state answered with police firing in town after town, and the movement's record counts 369 dead, most of them students. The Telangana Praja Samithi converted the agitation into a party and won ten of the region's fourteen Lok Sabha seats in 1971, before its leadership merged the party into the Congress in September 1971 without winning the state.

### Q: Who was K. Chandrashekar Rao?

Kalvakuntla Chandrashekar Rao, universally shortened to KCR, is the central figure of the movement's second phase. Born on 17 February 1954 at Chintamadaka in Medak district, he entered politics through the Telugu Desam Party, was elected from Siddipet in 1985, served as a minister in N. Chandrababu Naidu's cabinet and as Deputy Speaker of the Assembly from 2000. On 27 April 2001 he resigned both the office and the party and founded the Telangana Rashtra Samithi on a single-point programme. He served as Union Minister for Labour and Employment, resigned that office in 2006 over the delayed statehood decision, staked his life on an eleven-day fast in 2009, and took oath on 2 June 2014 as the first Chief Minister of Telangana.

### Q: When was the TRS founded?

The Telangana Rashtra Samithi was founded on 27 April 2001 at Jala Drushyam in Hyderabad. K. Chandrashekar Rao had resigned that day as Deputy Speaker of the Andhra Pradesh Assembly and as a member of the Telugu Desam Party, judging that the statehood demand required a party that could pursue no other object. The party's constitution carried a single-point agenda, a separate state of Telangana with Hyderabad as its capital, and the design was deliberate: after the Telangana Praja Samithi had been absorbed into the Congress in 1971, the new party was built so that absorption would be visible as betrayal. Within weeks it contested local body elections across the region and won enough parishad seats to prove the demand had an electorate.

### Q: What was the 9 December 2009 Telangana announcement?

Late on the night of 9 December 2009, with K. Chandrashekar Rao on the eleventh day of a fast unto death and the region in uprising after the death of the student Srikanth Chary, Union Home Minister P. Chidambaram announced in Delhi that the process of forming the state of Telangana would be initiated and that an appropriate resolution would be moved in the state Assembly. The announcement followed an all-party meeting of 7 December at which the state's major parties had recorded their assent. Rao broke his fast that night. Fourteen days later, on 23 December 2009, after the coastal and Rayalaseema districts rose against the division and their legislators resigned in large numbers, the government paused the process for further consultations, and the announcement became the movement's central exhibit of a bargain withdrawn.

### Q: When was Telangana formed as separate state?

Telangana was formed on 2 June 2014, the appointed day fixed by the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act 2014. The Act had been passed by the Lok Sabha on 18 February 2014 and by the Rajya Sabha on 20 February, and received the President's assent on 1 March 2014, creating Telangana as the twenty-ninth state of the Indian Union with ten districts, one hundred and nineteen Assembly seats and seventeen Lok Sabha seats. Hyderabad became the common capital of Telangana and the residuary Andhra Pradesh for a period not exceeding ten years. K. Chandrashekar Rao, whose party had won sixty-three Assembly seats in the elections held weeks earlier, took oath as the state's first Chief Minister on the day of formation.

### Q: What is the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act 2014?

The Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act 2014 is the statute by which Parliament divided Andhra Pradesh into the states of Telangana and Andhra Pradesh. Passed in February 2014 and assented to on 1 March 2014, it apportioned territory, assets, liabilities and All India Services cadres between the successor states across its main text and ten schedules, fixed Hyderabad as the common capital for a maximum of ten years with a common Governor holding special responsibility for security in the capital area, constituted management boards for the Krishna and Godavari rivers, declared the Polavaram project a national project to be executed by the Union government, and set 2 June 2014 as the appointed day on which the new state would come into being.

### Q: What is the Hyderabad shared-capital architecture?

The shared-capital architecture was the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act's arrangement under which Hyderabad served as the capital of both Telangana and the residuary Andhra Pradesh for a period not exceeding ten years from 2 June 2014. Both governments functioned from the city, a common Governor discharged the responsibilities of both states, and Section 8 of the Act laid on that Governor a special responsibility for the security of life, liberty and property of all residents of the common capital area, assisted by two advisors appointed by the Union government. The design aimed to give the residuary state time to build a capital of its own while protecting residents who had settled in Hyderabad from every district. The statutory term closed on 1 June 2024, from which date Hyderabad stood as the capital of Telangana alone.

### Q: What was the All-Party Accord of January 1969?

The All-Party Accord of 19 January 1969 was the first concession extracted by the 1969 movement, reached at a meeting of the state's political parties convened by Chief Minister Kasu Brahmananda Reddy a fortnight after the Khammam fast began. Its terms promised the immediate transfer of non-Telangana employees out of posts reserved under the Mulki rules, the application of Telangana's revenue surplus to Telangana's advancement, and the enforcement of the safeguards of 1956. Students and employees accepted the accord's text and rejected its sufficiency within days, since it repeated promises whose breach had started the agitation, and the movement resumed with the demand escalated from enforcement of safeguards to a separate state. The accord matters in the movement's own history as the first entry in a ledger pattern: a written concession, an audit, a finding of non-performance, and a larger demand.

### Q: What was the Eight-Point Plan announced in April 1969?

The Eight-Point Plan was Prime Minister Indira Gandhi's formula of 12 April 1969 for settling the Telangana agitation within a united Andhra Pradesh. Announced after talks in Delhi with leaders of both regions, it promised accelerated advancement of the Telangana region, a high-powered committee to determine the region's revenue surplus and apply it to the region, continued safeguards in public employment, and machinery to watch over the settlement's performance. The Telangana Praja Samithi and the student organisations rejected the plan as another instalment of the concession sequence that had begun with the All-Party Accord, and the agitation continued through 1969. A Five-Point Formula followed in November 1969, adjusting the Mulki arrangements for the twin cities. The plans are remembered in the movement's literature as evidence that Delhi conceded the grievance's facts while refusing its conclusion for forty-five years.

### Q: What was the Jai Andhra movement of 1972 and 1973?

The Jai Andhra movement was the counter-mobilisation of the coastal Andhra and Rayalaseema districts in the winter of 1972 and 1973, demanding a separate Andhra state. It was triggered by the Supreme Court's judgment of 3 October 1972 upholding the Mulki rules, which its holders read as locking Andhra's educated young men out of government employment in Hyderabad. Organised through the Andhra Praja Front and student committees in Vijayawada, Guntur and Visakhapatnam, it drew ministerial resignations through December 1972, lost its most respected leader Kakani Venkataratnam during the agitation, and faced police firing in the coastal towns. The movement subsided after the Six-Point Formula of September 1973 replaced the Mulki rules with state-wide local cadres, a bargain both regions accepted and whose later history each region audited against the other.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide on the Mulki Rules in October 1972?

On 3 October 1972, a five-judge bench of the Supreme Court presided over by Chief Justice S. M. Sikri decided The Director of Industries and Commerce, Government of Andhra Pradesh v. V. Venkata Reddy, reported at AIR 1973 SC 827. Reversing the Andhra Pradesh High Court, the Court held that the Mulki rules, the Hyderabad employment code descending from the Nizam's firman of 1919, were law in force preserved by Article 35(b) of the Constitution and carried through the States Reorganisation Act of 1956, so that the requirement of fifteen years' residence for posts in Telangana remained valid. The judgment vindicated Telangana's employees and provoked the Jai Andhra counter-movement within a day. Parliament superseded the position a year later when the Six-Point Formula abolished the rules and the Thirty-Second Amendment inserted Article 371D.

### Q: Why did the Telangana demand recede between 1973 and 2001?

The demand receded because the settlement of 1973 removed its instruments one by one. The Telangana Praja Samithi had already merged into the Congress in 1971, the Mulki rules were abolished, the Regional Committee was dissolved, and the Presidential Order of 1975 converted a regional safeguard into a state-wide administrative scheme whose violations had to be proved file by file. Politics in the state reorganised around other axes: the Congress system, then N. T. Rama Rao's Telugu Desam after 1983, whose platform of Telugu self-respect cast the unified state itself as the regional cause. Yet the demand survived in documentary keeping, through the Telangana Information Trust founded in 1986, employees' ledgers of violations, cultural associations and forums such as the Telangana Jana Sabha. When three new states were created in 2000 without any comparable mobilisation, the disparity revived the argument that the demand had been shelved rather than answered.

### Q: What did the Common Minimum Programme of 2004 promise on Telangana?

The Common Minimum Programme of the United Progressive Alliance, adopted on 27 May 2004, promised that the government would consider the demand for a separate state of Telangana at an appropriate time, after due consultations and consensus. The sentence was the Telangana Rashtra Samithi's price for joining the alliance with its five MPs, and its qualifying clauses became the contested ground for the next five years. In January 2005 the alliance constituted a sub-committee under Pranab Mukherjee to examine the demand; the sub-committee consulted parties for eighteen months without producing a recommendation, since consensus within a divided state was unattainable by construction. K. Chandrashekar Rao cited the programme's non-performance when he resigned his Union ministry in August 2006 and his Karimnagar seat the following month, and the movement thereafter treated written promises as instruments to be enforced by mobilisation rather than awaited.

### Q: How did the Telangana Joint Action Committee organise the movement after December 2009?

The Telangana Joint Action Committee was formed on 24 December 2009, the day after the Union government paused the statehood process, under the convenership of Professor M. Kodandaram of Osmania University. It bound the Telangana Rashtra Samithi to the employees' federations, students' committees, lawyers' and teachers' associations and scores of caste, cultural and professional bodies in a single command structure with district and mandal committees across the region. Its method was the synchronised instrument: relay hunger strikes, boycotts of courts and classrooms, the Million March of March 2011, the forty-two-day general strike of September and October 2011 that shut offices, schools, transport and the Singareni collieries, and the Telangana March of September 2012. Because the committee could call a stoppage and be obeyed across ten districts, it functioned as the movement's parliament between elections, and its alumni staffed much of the new state's first administration.

### Q: What was the Million March of March 2011?

The Million March was the Joint Action Committee's mass action of 10 March 2011, called after the Srikrishna Committee's report failed to move the Union government toward a decision. Despite prohibitory orders and the sealing of Hyderabad's entry points, marchers converged on Tank Bund, the boulevard along Hussain Sagar lake lined with statues of Telugu cultural and political figures erected by the unified state, and by the evening several statues had been pulled down and thrown into the lake. The iconoclasm was the event's meaning in both camps' readings: the movement's supporters read it as a repudiation of the unified state's pantheon, its critics as vandalism that damaged the movement's moral standing. The march demonstrated, whatever else it did, that the committee could fill the capital on a weekday in defiance of the police, a datum Delhi's calculators recorded.

### Q: What was Chapter 8 of the Srikrishna Committee report?

Chapter 8 was the confidential chapter of the Srikrishna Committee's report, submitted to the Union Home Ministry in a sealed cover on 30 December 2010 alongside the nine public chapters. It addressed law and order and the management of the situation that any announcement would create, including assessments of the movement's organisation and advice on handling the political and media environment of a decision. Its contents became public through court proceedings in 2011, when their disclosure was ordered and contested, and the chapter's existence transformed the report's reception in Telangana. A committee appointed to consult had also, in the movement's reading, advised the government on containing the consulted, and the Samithi thereafter treated the report's preferred option, a united state with an empowered regional council, as an artefact of the same management. The government never acted on the chapter, and the report as a whole was shelved without a decision.

### Q: What did the Congress Working Committee resolve on 30 July 2013?

On 30 July 2013 the Congress Working Committee, the party's highest decision-making body, resolved unanimously to request the Union government to take steps in accordance with the Constitution to form a separate state of Telangana within a definite time frame. The resolution covered the ten districts of the region, proposed Hyderabad as the common capital of both successor states for ten years, and was announced by the party's spokesperson Ajay Maken, with Digvijay Singh, the general secretary responsible for the state, presenting it as the fulfilment of a long-standing demand. A committee under A. K. Antony was appointed the same evening to address the anxieties of the party's legislators from the coastal districts and Rayalaseema. Coming from the ruling party at the Centre, the resolution converted the statehood demand from a question of whether into a question of drafting, and set the sequence that ended in the statute of February 2014.

### Q: What happened in the Lok Sabha on 13 February 2014?

On 13 February 2014, as Union Home Minister Sushilkumar Shinde rose in the Lok Sabha to introduce the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Bill, Lagadapati Rajagopal, a Congress member from Vijayawada who had been expelled from his party for defying the bifurcation decision, discharged pepper spray inside the chamber. Members fled coughing from the House, proceedings collapsed, and sixteen members were suspended for the disorder of that sitting. Rajagopal defended his act as a protest against the dismemberment of his state without a counted vote, and the image of the chamber emptying became the counter-movement's defining emblem. The bill was introduced once order returned, passed by the Lok Sabha by voice vote on 18 February 2014 in proceedings whose live telecast was interrupted, and passed by the Rajya Sabha on 20 February after the Prime Minister's assurances to the residuary state.

### Q: What special responsibility did the Reorganisation Act give the Governor for Hyderabad?

Section 8 of the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act 2014 laid a special responsibility on the common Governor of the two successor states for the security of life, liberty and property of all residents of the Hyderabad common capital area. In discharging that responsibility the Governor was to act on the advice of the Telangana Council of Ministers in the ordinary course, while retaining a discretion guided by the Act's purpose, and was assisted by two advisors appointed by the Union government; the arrangement also kept the city's police leadership within the Governor's special charge for the common-capital period. The provision was among the Act's most contested clauses. Its defenders in the residuary state read it as the settlers' guarantee; its critics in Telangana read it as a Union foothold inside their capital. The responsibility ran with the common-capital arrangement and closed with it on 1 June 2024.

### Q: Why do accounts differ on the number of suicides claimed for Telangana between 2009 and 2014?

The counts differ because the holders count for different purposes from different records. The Telangana Rashtra Samithi and the Joint Action Committee compiled a martyrology of named individuals, drawn from district committees, press reports and families' testimony, which their publications carry above twelve hundred for the second phase as a whole; each name in those lists is entered as a death for the cause, many supported by suicide notes naming statehood. The state government's statements to legislatures and courts acknowledged a smaller number of deaths officially recorded as connected with the agitation, compiled from police records that classified many cases without reference to motive. Historians of the movement, including Gautam Pingle in The Fall and Rise of Telangana (2014), record the movement's figures while noting the absence of a single authoritative register. The discrepancy is itself evidence in the argument between the readings, since the movement deployed its count as an audit of the Union's delay and the state deployed its own as a statistic of order.
