## Romeo and Juliet Arrived in China as a Story, Not a Play

Romeo and Juliet entered Chinese theatre by a route no other Shakespeare play took in quite the same order. The lovers arrived first as a story, retold in classical prose for readers who had never seen a play; then as a scenario, improvised on stage by actors who had no complete text; then as a translated script, argued over by rival translators; and only much later as an operatic property, sung in the forms that had carried Chinese drama for centuries. The verdict of this article is that China did not receive the play once. China refounded it three times, and each refounding remade it: the tale made it Chinese property, the spoken drama made it a political instrument, and the opera stage made it a local feud. The Butterfly Lovers parallel, the legend routinely named China's Romeo and Juliet, is the proof of the metabolization rather than the explanation of it. The pattern of young love blocked by an older order was already Chinese property a thousand years before the play arrived. What the play added was a feud between two houses at war, a machinery of stars, and a tomb that offers no consolation.

The record this article follows is uneven, and its unevenness is part of the evidence. Some of its entries rest on production documentation, named companies, theatres and dates. Some rest on the surveys of scholars who have spent careers in the archives, Li Ruru and Guo Jinghua and Alexander Huang among them. A few famous entries rest on anecdote, and the article marks them as anecdote rather than promoting them to fact. A history of a play in a foreign theatre is always partly a history of what that theatre needed the play to be, and the Chinese record is unusually frank about its needs. Reformers needed a weapon. Translators needed a bookshelf. A state needed a certificate of culture. A festival needed a collision. The lovers were conscripted into every one of those projects, and the article's task is to keep the projects distinct while following the lovers through all of them.

The evidence for that verdict runs in a single chronology, and the article follows it. The word play itself needs discipline in this history. Three different objects travel under the title in the Chinese record. The first is a plot, a sequence of events that can be retold in classical prose, carried in a scenario, or mapped onto a local legend. The second is a text, a sequence of words in English and then in Chinese, whose fortunes are the fortunes of translation. The third is a production, an event in a theatre, with a company, a director, a date and a quarrel attached. The three objects have separate chronologies, and much confusion in writing about Shakespeare in China comes from running them together. The plot arrived in 1904. The text arrived in the early 1920s. The production, in the full sense of a staging of a translated text, arrived in 1937. The article keeps the three apart, because the play's Chinese life is the story of their gradual, contested convergence. Where the record's own counts disagree, in the festival arithmetic, in the translation dates and in the anecdote rows of the map, the disagreement is reported rather than smoothed.

One question hangs over the chronology, and it is worth answering before the evidence. Why did this play, of all the plays in the canon, become the Chinese test case, among the first two translated entire, argued over in wartime, and mapped onto the national legend? The answer lies in the plot's portability. The play's engine is a situation rather than a style: two houses, two children, a secret marriage, a failed message, a tomb. A situation survives every mediation intact. It can be retold by a stylist who never read it, improvised by actors who never saw it, sung by performers who never spoke it, and recognized by audiences who know it under another pair of names. Hamlet's engine is a consciousness, and Macbeth's is an ambition, and both resist summary in ways the lovers' situation does not. The play that travelled farthest in China is the play that needed its words least, which is the founding irony of its Chinese life: the least verbal of receptions happened to the most verbal of love stories.

The name of Shakespeare reached Chinese readers in 1856, when the British missionary William Muirhead, working with a Chinese assistant in Shanghai, published a modified translation of Thomas Milner's history of England that carried a brief reference to the playwright in its chapter on Elizabethan England. The reference was a name without a text. In 1894 the reformer Yan Fu wrote in The Evolution of Nature that Shakespeare was an English poet and playwright whose works would spread far and wide among readers across the world. The prediction was still a name without a text. The text arrived in the form of a story, and the story arrived through Charles and Mary Lamb.

## Lin Shu, Wei Yi and the Tale Retitled Forged Love

In 1903 the Shanghai Dawen News Agency published ten stories from the Lambs' Tales from Shakespeare in classical Chinese, under the title Xiewai Qitan. In 1904 Lin Shu and Wei Yi published all twenty tales, under the title Yingguo Shiren Yinbian Yanyu, through the Commercial Press. The second book is the one that mattered. Lin Shu, born in 1852 in Fujian, knew no foreign language at all. Wei Yi read the English aloud, and Lin composed the Chinese. The collaboration produced more than 180 Western works in literary Chinese across Lin's career, and the Shakespeare tales were among the earliest and the most consequential. The method had made Lin famous before Shakespeare entered it. His first translation, of Dumas's La Dame aux Camélias, published in 1899, was an immediate success, and the collaboration model, an interpreter's voice and Lin's pen, went on to produce versions of more than 170 Western titles. Critics of his prose place its manner in the Tongcheng tradition, simple and graceful classical writing prized for the expression of genuine feeling, which is why the tales read as Chinese stories rather than as foreign documents in Chinese dress. The style was the smuggling route. A reader who would never open a missionary's textbook opened Lin's tales because they sounded like literature the reader already owned.

The mediation deserves a moment's attention, because it set the pattern for everything that followed. The lovers reached China through a triple filter. Shakespeare's verse became the Lambs' prose, a retelling made for English children, in which the play's bawdy, its wordplay and its civic machinery were already thinned to a story of two families and a friar. The Lambs' prose then became Lin's classical Chinese, heard aloud and recomposed by a stylist who shelved the result among stories of gods and spirits. What survived the double journey was the plot's skeleton and its emotional weather: the feud, the secret marriage, the potion, the tomb. What did not survive was everything in the play that lives in language rather than in events. The Chinese reception therefore began with a story whose centre of gravity was narrative, and the habit persisted. Chinese Romeo and Juliet has always been, first, a story a culture tells, and only later a text a scholar edits or a script a company rehearses.

Lin renamed each play with two stylish characters. Hamlet became Guizhao, the ghost's command. Romeo and Juliet became Zhuqing, rendered in characters as 铸情, cast love or forged love: a title that already interprets the story as love worked under pressure, hammered into shape by forces outside the lovers.

Lin's version of the lovers' story was not a translation of the play. It was a retelling of the Lambs' retelling, in semi-classical prose, and it behaved like a Chinese tale. Lin classified the collection on its cover as stories of gods and spirits, shenguai xiaoshuo, a category familiar to every Chinese reader. The classification was a piece of cultural engineering. It told readers that Shakespeare belonged beside the strange tales they already knew, not beside the foreign textbooks they distrusted. Lu Xun, the leading figure of modern Chinese literature, enlisted Shakespeare in the opposite direction, placing him with Dante, Milton, Goethe, Byron, Shelley and Pushkin as fighters in the spiritual realm, needed for the revitalization of China. The two enlistments, Lin's domestication and Lu Xun's weaponization, ran side by side from the start, and they never reconciled. Chinese Shakespeare has lived between them ever since.

### Q: Did Lin Shu know any English?

Lin Shu knew no foreign language and said so himself, crediting the gentlemen who interpreted the texts for him. Wei Yi read the English of the Lambs' tales aloud, and Lin composed the Chinese prose from the hearing. The method explains both the vigor of the result and its distance from the play.

Lin made no secret of the method or its limits. In his translator's preface to Dickens's The Old Curiosity Shop he wrote that he had no foreign languages and could not pass for a translator without the aid of several gentlemen who interpreted the texts for him, adding that he was fortunate to have his error-plagued, rough translations kindly accepted by the learned. The confession was disarming and, for his New Culture critics, damning: the man who had introduced Western literature to China had read none of it in the original. Arthur Waley, the leading English translator from Chinese and Japanese of the next generation, returned the opposite verdict from the other side of the language barrier, holding a high opinion of Lin's translations and suggesting they were not inferior to Dickens's originals. The two verdicts, the critics' contempt and Waley's admiration, frame the question Qian Zhongshu would settle.

The contempt had a specific grievance. During the New Culture Movement, Lin was smeared and ridiculed as a defender of literary Chinese against the vernacular, the reactionary stylist whose versions had to be cleared away before modern Chinese literature could begin. The charge was convenient and partly false. Lin did not oppose the vernacular's use and wrote poems in it himself; what he opposed was the total abolition of the classical language, and the distinction was lost in a polemic that needed a villain. The Tales were collateral damage in a language war, condemned for the company their prose kept.

The love scene in Lin's retelling was rendered, in the words of the scholar Chan-Fai Cheung, in a very subtle way. The modern vocabulary of love had not yet settled: the words lian'ai and aiqing, and ai used as a verb in the phrase wo ai ni, entered the language only after 1900, in Cheung's dating, and before 1918, when Lu Xun published his poem The Goddess of Love. Lin's lovers therefore love in a register closer to devotion and fate than to the declared passion of the English text. The subtlety was not a failure of nerve. It was the state of the language, and it shaped a generation's sense of what the story was about. For two decades, as the scholar Li Ruru records, Lin's version was the only Shakespeare known to the Chinese-speaking readership, and every early stage version was adapted from it.

The defense of Lin Shu came late, and it came from the highest authority in modern Chinese letters. Qian Zhongshu's essay Lin Shu's Translation, published in 1963, revived interest in work the New Culture Movement had smeared. Qian quoted Goethe's simile of translators as busy matchmakers and judged that Lin had served well as a matchmaker between Western literature and Chinese readers, noting that Qian himself had been motivated by Lin's translations to learn foreign languages. Qian also refused to whitewash the record: Lin often improved the original as well as abridging it, and Qian divided the career at 1913, the early phase vigorous despite all the mistranslations, the later phase dull, a means of earning a living. The adjudication stands: Qian wins the first phase and loses nothing by conceding the second. The matchmaker verdict is the right one because Lin's achievement was not fidelity to a text he could not read. It was the creation of a Chinese audience for a story that would otherwise have had none, and the audience he created outlasted his errors.

Lin's tales outlived their own obsolescence, and the market delivered its own verdict on the matchmaker theory. Li Ruru records that the 1904 collection was reprinted a number of times even after complete translations of the original plays had become available. Readers who could buy Tian Han's text or Zhu Shenghao's chose, in substantial numbers, to keep reading the story as Lin had cast it. The tale had become the canonical form of first acquaintance, and first acquaintance is the acquaintance that shapes a culture's sense of what a story is. The defence prevailed institutionally as well. In 1981 the Commercial Press, the original publisher of many of Lin's renditions, reprinted ten of them with modern punctuation, returning the tales to the shelves alongside the complete works they had preceded by four decades.

## The Scenario Stage: Shakespeare Before Translation

The stage came before the script. The earliest Shakespeare production with Chinese actors was a Merchant of Venice performed in English in 1902 by students of the Foreign Language Department at Shanghai's St John's College, staged to promote English study rather than to present a play. The first production in Chinese came in July 1913, when the New People's Society, Xinmin She, a Shanghai dramatic organization, staged The Merchant of Venice under the title Rou quan, Contract of the Flesh. More than twenty Shakespearean adaptations followed over the ensuing decade, and the Internet Shakespeare Editions record of the period carries the essential qualification: these productions were based on Lin Shu's summaries of the storylines and were staged before any translation of a complete Shakespeare play was available. They were scripted as mubiao, scenarios, in which actors were given only an outline of the plot and expected to expand it on stage through improvisation, somewhat in the manner of commedia dell'arte.

The programme behind the repertoire is stated plainly in the Internet Shakespeare Editions account of the period. As the Manchu empire went into terminal decline, Chinese intellectuals became concerned to transform the people into new citizens, and one route was the promotion of Western literature. The scenario stage was that programme at work in a theatre. A plot that could be carried by an outline and a company of improvisers could travel to towns that had never seen a printed play, and the repertoire's founding choice fits the programme's logic: The Merchant of Venice is a machine for arguing about law, money and mercy in public, which is what a theatre founded to manufacture citizens most needed to practise. A second route ran beside the first. Missionaries taught Shakespeare in their English-language schools, as the British Library's account records, and the St John's performance of 1902 belongs to that route, the play as an instrument of language instruction for the sons of the treaty ports. The scenario practice was not confined to the new spoken drama. The British Library's account records that traditional theatre troupes also used the Lamb translations, taking the plot outline and improvising the rest, so that the sung stage handled the stories, in scenario form, decades before it handled any complete play. Between the schoolroom and the scenario stage, the plots circulated for a decade before the text arrived, and the circulation fixed the order of the Chinese reception: Shakespeare was usable before he was readable.

The scenario Shakespeare was political from its first seasons, and the record's sharpest example is a Hamlet. In 1916 an adaptation of Hamlet was presented in Shanghai whose personae took their names from Lin's Ghost's Command, and whose burden, under its successive titles, was an attack on President Yuan Shikai, who was attempting to restore the monarchy. The Danish prince's hesitation became a commentary on a usurper in Beijing. The example matters beyond its ingenuity. It shows that the allegorical use of Shakespeare, the use Alexander Huang's scholarship treats as the signature of the play's Chinese career, did not wait for the translated text or for the professional spoken drama. It began in the scenario years, when all the companies possessed was a plot and a grievance, and the two were sufficient.

The scenario stage matters because it shows what Chinese theatre wanted from Shakespeare before it had his words. The reformers who built the new theatre regarded the traditional music theatre, xiqu, with its strictly conventionalized acting, singing, recitation, dance and martial arts, as merely ornamental, scarcely a suitable vehicle for radical ideas. They borrowed what they could learn of Western practice to found a new theatre with a clear ideological commitment: civilized drama, wenmingxi, from which the modern spoken drama movement, huaju, later emerged. Shakespearean adaptations formed a vital part of the civilized drama repertoire, and the repertoire's logic was political before it was literary. The plays were instruments for making new citizens out of a population the reformers regarded as needing transformation. A plot was enough. The verse could wait.

## Tian Han's Relay: The First Complete Romeo and Juliet in Chinese

The verse did not wait long. Tian Han, born in 1898, studied in Japan from 1916 to 1922, intending a career in the military or politics, and abandoned both for the theatre. The British Library's account of his motive has the shape of a founding anecdote and the documentation of a library record: while studying in Japan, Tian Han heard the Japanese disparage Chinese culture because the nation did not even have a complete translation of Shakespeare, and he answered by translating. His Hamlet was finished in 1921 to 1922, published in the magazine Young China in 1921 and in book form in 1922. His Romeo and Juliet was finished in 1923 to 1924 and published in a journal he edited in the mid-1920s. The two plays were the country's first fully translated Shakespeare plays, and the British Library's verdict on them is unsparing: beautiful but unplayable.

Tian Han's own preface to the Hamlet gives the measure of his ambition. He compared Shakespeare's works to oil painting and Ibsen's to marble sculpture, and he translated Romeo and Juliet three years after the Hamlet because he thought there were too many Ibsen-style works and wanted to present readers and audiences with something different. The choice is revealing. The first complete Chinese Romeo and Juliet was not a scholarly exercise. It was a deliberate alternative to the dominant Western model of the day, offered by a dramatist who would go on to write the words of the national anthem and to found the modern Chinese theatre.

Tian Han's Shakespeare work sits inside a larger translation practice that his later fame has obscured. His three translations, of Wilde's Salome, of Hamlet and of Romeo and Juliet, are rarely considered by translation researchers, as a study of the local colour in his versions observes, yet they show a dramatist thinking in two traditions at once. The versions carry features of traditional Chinese culture and of the Xiangchu culture of his native Hunan. The empathy that drove the Hamlet was personal as much as scholarly: the MIT Global Shakespeares essay records that his strong personal empathy with the protagonist enabled him to use the work of translation to vent his own emotions, and that he used the Hamlet as a reaction against the Ibsen-dominated spoken drama of the day. The Romeo and Juliet belongs to the same campaign. It was translated by a poet for readers, and its unplayability is the measure of its priorities. The beauty was the argument.

### Q: From what language did Tian Han translate Romeo and Juliet?

The scholarly likelihood is Japanese, carried at that strength and no stronger. The Internet Shakespeare Editions note records that there is no direct proof, because no attribution was given in his work, and that his knowledge of English did not seem adequate to translate the plays from the original, while he translated Hamlet as a student in Japan.

The Japanese channel deserves emphasis because it was the era's common route. The generation that built modern Chinese literature had disproportionately passed through Tokyo, and Japanese versions, Japanese criticism and Japanese theatrical practice mediated a large share of the Western material that entered Chinese in the 1910s and 1920s. Tian Han's relay translation was not an eccentricity. It was the standard supply line of his literary generation, and it means that the first complete Chinese Romeo and Juliet carries, inside its Chinese, the impress of the Japanese Shakespeare that the series examines in the Tsubouchi article linked in this section.

The relay matters for the whole Chinese history of the play. The first complete Romeo and Juliet in Chinese reached its readers through a Japanese intermediary, and the Japanese route ran through the translation enterprise examined in [Tsubouchi and the Japanese Translation](/2030/05/10/tsubouchi-japanese-translation/), the series' account of the founding of a national Shakespeare in Japan. The chain of transmission, English into Japanese into Chinese, explains features of the early Chinese text that a direct translation would not have: the archaisms, the plot-first fidelity, the verse that reads beautifully and will not act. The relay is also the reason the Birmingham anecdote of 1942 stung. At an exhibition of foreign translations of Shakespeare's works held in Birmingham in 1942, the Japanese booth displayed a complete works in dozens of hardbound gilt volumes, and beside it sat a thin Chinese volume of Romeo and Juliet translated by Tian Han. The contrast became the emblem of a national lack that Chinese translators of the period felt keenly: the thin volume standing for a culture that had the tales but not yet the works.

The anecdote's afterlife is part of its evidence. Chinese accounts of the translation century retell the Birmingham contrast as a parable, the gilt Japanese shelves against the single thin Chinese volume, and they attach the parable to Zhu Shenghao, whose whole career reads as its answer: a complete Shakespeare in Chinese, made by one man in wartime, that could fill a shelf. The parable circulates at the strength of national memory rather than of an exhibition catalogue, and the article records it at that strength. What is documentary is the imbalance it dramatizes. In 1942 the Japanese display held a complete works in dozens of gilt volumes, while the Chinese holding for this play was a beautiful, unplayable version not yet twenty years in print.

The relay's consequence for the stage was delay. A text that cannot be acted leaves the theatre where the scenario companies left it, with plots and without a performable script, and fifteen years separate the first complete Chinese Romeo and Juliet from the first production built on a complete translated text. When the production came, in 1937, it came from a director rather than a translator. The translators' answer to the unplayability charge was meanwhile being written by a young man in Zhejiang who worked through a war in poverty and died at thirty-two.

## The Translators' Relay: Zhu Shenghao, Liang Shiqiu and the Prose-Verse Argument

Zhu Shenghao, born in 1912 in Jiaxing, Zhejiang, began translating Shakespeare in 1935 and worked until his death in December 1944, at the age of thirty-two, in poverty and failing health, through a war that repeatedly destroyed his work. His manuscripts were lost amid the fighting, and he retranslated them: in June 1942, after his marriage to his university classmate Song Qingru in Shanghai on 1 May 1942, the couple moved to Changshu, where he finished retranslating the lost manuscripts, and in January 1943 they moved to Jiaxing, where Romeo and Juliet, King Lear and Hamlet were translated. Thirty-one plays were translated in all, twenty-seven of them published before 1949, and the complete works built on his translations appeared from the Shijie Bookstore in 1947. His arrangement was itself a translator's decision for Chinese readers: Zhu set aside the chronological order of the Oxford edition and divided the plays into four classes, comedy, tragedy, history and miscellaneous pieces, so that the canon arrived shelved by kind, in the manner of a Chinese library. Until 2000, Zhu's work made up the core of the mainland Chinese version of the Complete Works, and his translations were the texts normally used for stage performances.

### Q: Did Zhu Shenghao translate Romeo and Juliet in verse or prose?

Zhu translated in prose, by a stated principle. He judged that rendering Shakespeare's verse as verse would limit the language and block the popularity and fluency he wanted, so he chose prose with occasional rhymed couplets, and the stage accepted the bargain.

The bargain had a price, and the Internet Shakespeare Editions note states it with a single example. In Hamlet, the word unnatural in the line "Let me be cruel, not unnatural" becomes unfilial in Zhu's rendering. The same note gives the companion cost: the line that follows in Hamlet, about speaking daggers to her, is spelt out at ponderous length in the rendering, the metaphor unpacked into explanation. Domestication and dilation are the prose method's twin signatures. The substitution is not an error. It is the Confucian pull on the text, the pressure of a culture in which a son's cruelty to his mother is first of all a failure of filial duty. The example belongs to Hamlet, but the pressure it demonstrates runs through Zhu's Romeo and Juliet as well, where family duty is the force the lovers break and the force that breaks them. The prose translations domesticated the plays into a Chinese moral vocabulary, and that domestication is why they held the stage for half a century.

Liang Shiqiu was the first Chinese speaker to translate all of Shakespeare's works, beginning in 1930 and finishing thirty-seven years later, in 1967, in Taiwan, after politics drove him to flee with the Nationalists. His efforts were not seen on the mainland until the mid-1990s. Liang also translated in prose, and his rendering of Juliet's most famous line is a small masterpiece of fidelity to meaning over sound. Where English readers hear "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" at 2.2.33, Liang wrote 哦，罗密欧，罗密欧！你为何是罗密欧？, which glosses as "Oh, Romeo, Romeo, why are you Romeo?" The rendering keeps the why that the series' earlier article on the line's meaning established as the correct gloss, against the where reading that generations of readers have assumed. A translator working alone across three decades got the crux right.

The two prose settlements divided the canon between them along the era's political fracture. Zhu's texts served the mainland stage. Liang's complete works, finished in Taiwan, were not seen on the mainland until the mid-1990s. For three decades, therefore, Chinese Shakespeare existed in two authorized bodies, a mainland body that actors spoke and a Taiwan body that scholars annotated, and the division had nothing to do with translation theory. It was the map of China drawn through a bookshelf. The division also postponed the verse argument. While the two prose canons faced each other across the strait, the question of whether the plays should exist in Chinese as poetry at all remained a minority report, filed by poets.

The prose settlement was challenged from inside the translation profession. Sun Dayu, the first translator to use poetry for Shakespeare in Chinese, insisted that it was a pity to translate blank verse with prose because readers would lose the beauty of the plays as poetic drama, and in his King Lear of 1948 he tried a syllabic unit of his own invention to produce the effect of beat in the verse. The verse complete works edited by Fang Ping, published on the mainland in 2000, aimed to convey the poetry in the Chinese rendering. The adjudication between the two schools is a split verdict, and the split is the point. Prose won the stage: Zhu's texts were the acting standard for two generations. Verse won the poetry argument: Sun Dayu and Fang Ping were right that prose cannot carry blank verse, and the mainland's later turn to verse complete works concedes it. A translation history that ends in a single winner would falsify the record. The Chinese record has two winners, in two different theatres, the stage and the page.

The minority report had a signatory the prose school could not dismiss. Bian Zhilin, one of the major Chinese poets of the century, published his Hamlet in 1956, a poet's verse-conscious rendering from inside the literary establishment of the People's Republic. The verse argument was never refuted. It was outvoted by the stage, which needed texts actors could speak at length without defeating an audience, and by readers, who needed texts that could be finished. Nancy Pellegrini's account of the translation problem, written for the British Library, states the stage's difficulty from the modern side: the full translations that emerged from the heroic decades were better suited to the page than to the stage. The Chinese-language stage was thus caught between a literature it revered and a repertory it could not easily perform, and that catch is the condition the 2016 translation project, treated in the map, set out to relieve. The translation scholar Saihong Li, surveying the whole record, describes its translators as moulding the plays to resonate with Chinese audiences, and the moulding metaphor is the fair summary of the century: every major Chinese version of the play is a moulding, Lin's tale, Tian Han's poem, Zhu's stage prose, and the differences among them are differences of mould, not of obedience.

## Shanghai 1937: Zhang Min's Romeo and Juliet at the Carlton

The first great Chinese production of the play is also the most argued about. In 1937 the Shanghai Practical Drama Company staged Romeo and Juliet at the Carlton Theatre in Shanghai, under the direction of Zhang Min, and the production was, in the summary of Jinghua Guo's survey of Chinese adaptations, a very successful one. Zhang Min, a left-wing director, read the play as an attack on the feudal order. His interpretation survives in the history of Chinese spoken drama compiled by Ge Yihong, and it deserves quotation entire: an ethical code destroys the tender shoot that desires for freedom; the flame of youth burns the feudal fetters. The reading is a mapping of the play's machinery onto the May Fourth programme. The feud becomes feudal ethics. The parents become the old order. The lovers become youth itself, and their deaths become an indictment of the system that required them.

The mapping is worth testing against the text, because Zhang Min's reading makes a claim about where the play's causation lives. The street scenes state the ethical code in its pettiest form. The play opens on servants trading the thumb-biting insult, "Do you bite your thumb at us, sir?" at 1.1.42, an offence manufactured out of nothing because the code requires offences. Romeo enters the catastrophe of the duel scene refusing the code's terms, telling Tybalt that he loves him for a reason Tybalt cannot know, and the code reclaims him within a hundred lines, through Mercutio's death, into the killing he had refused. The Prince's closing audit assigns the costs to the houses and their hate. A director who reads the play as the destruction of youth by an ethical code is not importing a politics into a romance. He is reading the play's own causal chain and naming its links in the vocabulary of his movement.

On Zhang Min's premises, the production's centre of gravity shifts from Romeo to Juliet. The anti-feudal frame makes her the tragedy's agent rather than its ornament. She chooses the marriage against both houses, she refuses Paris to her mother's face in the confrontation of 3.5, she takes the potion on her own judgement, and she dies by her own hand when the machinery fails. Capulet's rage in that same scene, the father whose fingers itch to strike the daughter who will not obey, is the ethical code photographed at the moment of enforcement. A May Fourth audience did not need the code explained. Daughters were still being married by arrangement in the Shanghai of 1937, and a heroine who answered arrangement with a secret marriage and a knife was legible in a way no programme note could improve. The reading's feminism is not an anachronism imposed on the production. It is the production's own logic, drawn from the play's distribution of courage, in which the youngest person on stage is the only one who never obeys.

The reading was contested at the time, on grounds that had nothing to do with the play. Some critics thought a love tragedy an inappropriate choice when the Japanese had occupied north-eastern China. Their objection survives in the same record: a play should be relevant to our country and our time. It should be of some use. The objection is the utilitarian demand that built the Chinese theatre, the demand that every production justify itself as an instrument of national purpose, turned against a play whose purpose was being argued as exactly that.

### Q: Why did critics object to the 1937 production?

The objection was about 1937, not about the text. With Japanese forces occupying the north-east, critics held that a tragedy of young love was the wrong instrument for a nation at war, and that a play should be relevant to the country and the time, and of some use.

The adjudication splits, and the split is instructive. Zhang Min was right about the play. The text's causation is social before it is cosmic: the Prince closes the action by laying the deaths at the door of the houses' hate, in the sentence "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate" at 5.3.291, and the lovers die because a social order made their marriage a crime and their messenger a casualty. A reading that blames the ethical code is a reading the play licenses. The critics were right about the moment. A production is an event in a city, and in Shanghai in 1937 the city's emergency was real. What the dispute establishes is that by 1937 the play in China was no longer a foreign ornament. It was Chinese cultural property, valuable enough to fight over on Chinese grounds.

The production's success, which Guo's survey records in plain terms, settled the practical half of the dispute. The argument about appropriateness is itself the strongest evidence of naturalization: a love tragedy at the Carlton was debated in the language of national emergency, the language a culture reserves for property it regards as its own. Zhang Min's production is therefore the moment two of this article's histories converge. The tale Lin Shu had domesticated became, thirty-three years after its publication, the instrument of a modern theatre arguing over the oldest question in the Chinese moral tradition, what the old owe the young and what the young owe the old.

The wartime record continues with a production in 1944 that the Internet Shakespeare Editions history lists among the significant productions built on complete translated texts, and with a second distinguished translation: Cao Yu, the leading figure in modern Chinese theatrical circles, translated Romeo and Juliet in 1944. The translation and the production are separate entries in the record, and no document consulted for this article ties the 1944 staging to Cao Yu's text. The distinction is kept here because the record keeps it. What the two entries together show is a play being worked on, in text and on stage, in a country at war, by the central figures of its new theatre.

The pattern is structural, and it is worth naming. The men who translated the play in its first Chinese decades, Tian Han and Cao Yu, were the men building the modern Chinese theatre itself, and Zhu Shenghao, the exception, was a scholar and poet rather than a practitioner. Translation was not a service performed for the theatre from outside. It was one of the theatre's founding acts, performed by its founders, which is why the translated texts carry the period's polemics inside them rather than beneath them.

## Old Man Sha: Canonization, Silence and the 1986 Festival

By the mid-1920s the transliteration Shashibiya, which had served as the standard Chinese rendering of the playwright's name, was judged no longer suitable, and Chinese readers began to call him Sha Weng, Old Man Sha, a name of respect and affection. The canonization was reinforced, after 1949, by doctrine: Engels, in the introduction to the Dialectics of Nature, had called the Renaissance the greatest progressive revolution mankind had experienced, a time that produced giants in power of thought, passion and character, and Shakespeare, as a Renaissance giant, gained an honoured status in the People's Republic that no Chinese playwright of the period could match. The status created its own problem. The Internet Shakespeare Editions history names it precisely: the image of Shakespeare as the symbol of high culture tended to deter Chinese practitioners from staging his plays, because the modern Chinese theatre had been created to address the needs of people and society in the prevailing historical context, and practitioners felt that Old Man Sha's works could not be done any old way one liked.

The decades of the canon's consolidation were decades of translation rather than of production for this play. Guo's survey lists the harvest of the 1940s and 1950s: a Timon of Athens in 1944, a Julius Caesar the same year, Sun Dayu's King Lear in 1948, Fang Ping's Much Ado About Nothing in 1953 and his Henry the Fifth in 1955, Bian Zhilin's Hamlet in 1956. The pattern is the deterrence paradox at work. The bookshelf filled while the stage hesitated, because the same reverence that commissioned the translations made producers afraid of the plays.

The name's career between the tales and the canon explains the paradox. The Internet Shakespeare Editions history notes that for decades Chinese writers who may never have read the works themselves invoked Shakespeare's name to legitimize their arguments. A name that functions as a warrant of seriousness will be fought over, borrowed and eventually resented, and the sequence from Lin's shelving to Lu Xun's enlistment to the post-1949 doctrine is the biography of such a warrant in China. Stan Lai's brand charge, treated in this article's modern section, is the same observation made from inside the theatre a century later.

The Cultural Revolution, from 1966 to 1976, silenced the stage almost entirely. Western films were banned in the period, Olivier's Hamlet among them, along with Russian Shakespeare films and the Prokofiev ballet of Romeo and Juliet.

The silence had a prehistory in the canon's own paradox. The high status that doctrine conferred made the plays precious and therefore risky to stage, and the Internet Shakespeare Editions history observes that the highpoint of Chinese Shakespeare production came after the Cultural Revolution, when people had an opportunity to pursue artistic ideals and to escape from the theatre of propaganda. The observation explains the energy of what followed. The 1986 gathering was not only a celebration of a canon. It was a profession reclaiming the right to make art that was not an instrument, in the name of the author who had been most often conscripted as one. The lifting of the bans in the late 1970s brought the textual settlement with it: a mainland complete works of Shakespeare, thirty-seven plays and the poems, appeared in 1978, built substantially on Zhu Shenghao's translations, and has been regarded as the most authoritative Chinese version, even though, as Guo's survey concedes, the unique style of Shakespeare's language was neglected in favour of content in that edition's heavily prose-based texts.

The highpoint of the post-revolutionary boom was the first China Shakespeare Festival, held from 10 to 23 April 1986 in Beijing and Shanghai, organized by the Shakespeare Research Society of China, the Chinese Dramatic Art Research Society and the Beijing and Shanghai drama academies. The review of Xiao Yang Zhang's study counts 102 performances of eighteen plays by twenty-three theatre companies. The counts in the literature differ, Guo's survey recording more than two dozen versions of sixteen plays, and the scale is not in dispute even where the arithmetic is. Li Ruru's account of the festival preserves its real significance, which was not the count but the gathering: for the first time in China, Shakespearean scholars, traditional operatic performers, orchestras, experts on foreign literature and audiences of local opera were brought together in one theatre. Four operatic productions of Shakespeare were staged in Shanghai during the festival, and Li Ruru's classification of them is the sharpest instrument the festival produced. They fell, roughly, into three types: those that emphasized the foreignness of the plays, those that fully sinicized the plays, and those that synthesized Chinese and Western elements both textually and theatrically. The Shanghai Kunju Theatre Company adapted Macbeth into kunju for the festival. A jingju Othello, premiered in 1983, was replayed as part of the festival. At a lively forum on their productions, the directors of the four operas unanimously described their creative process as one of violent and painful collision. A second festival followed in Shanghai in 1994, presenting ten plays alongside radio plays and lectures, and, in the detail that marks a canon fully domesticated, festival t-shirts.

The festival also built the discipline's infrastructure. Shakespeare societies were founded across the mid-1980s, the first of them in Jilin Province in 1985. Professor Lin Tongji established a Shakespeare research library at Shanghai's Fudan University, and in 1984 the Central Academy of Drama in Beijing created a special collection of Shakespeare materials for universities and drama schools. In 1993 Wuhan University sponsored the first International Conference on Shakespeare in China. The sequence matters. The play's Chinese life had been carried for eighty years by enthusiasts, translators and companies; after 1986 it was carried by institutions, with libraries, conferences and shelves, and institutional memory is what turns a vogue into a tradition.

## The Play Inside the Opera House: Peking Opera, Gezaixi and the Collision

The brief for this article names Yue opera and Peking opera as the traditional forms that received the play, and the record requires grading rather than simple assent. Guo's survey states that many of Shakespeare's classics, among them Hamlet, King Lear, Macbeth and Romeo and Juliet, have been adapted for the Peking Opera. That is a survey's general claim, offered without a named production for this play. The sung forms had handled the plots as scenarios in the early decades, as the scenario record shows; what they have not left, for this play, is a named operatic production in the documentary record. The named, dated operatic evidence for Shakespeare in the sung forms clusters on other plays: Macbeth in kunju at the 1986 festival, Othello in jingju from 1983. For Romeo and Juliet itself, the best-documented operatic version is not on the mainland at all. It is a Taiwanese opera version, in the gezaixi form, and it is documented at production strength.

The conversion problem explains the clustering. Guo's survey, in its analysis of cross-cultural reception, identifies the core difficulty as the conversion of blank verse, which in Chinese practice must become prose dialogue or sung verse inside a conventionalized form, and notes that directors have taken approaches from the modern metaphorical to the realistic. A sung form is not a receptacle into which a spoken play can be poured. Xiqu assigns its performers role types, vocal schools and movement vocabularies built over centuries, and a foreign plot must be re-authored inside those assignments before it can be sung at all. The plays that crossed first were the plays whose plots survive re-authoring as spectacle and situation, a Scottish thane's ambition, a Moor's jealousy. The pattern repeats wherever the record is specific. Huang's essay records a Sichuan opera Hamlet under its plot-title, Murdering His Elder Brother and Marrying His Sister-in-Law, a re-authoring so complete that the title itself discards the prince's name in favour of his crimes. The lovers' play, whose substance is verbal, two young people talking their way toward a shared soul in sonnets and wordplay, is the hardest of the tragedies to re-author in a form that subordinates talk to song and gesture. Its operatic scarcity is a fact about the forms, not about the play's popularity, which the spoken stage demonstrates abundantly.

The term xiqu covers more than three hundred regional styles of traditional drama, of which jingju, Peking opera, emerged as by far the most representative and produced the system's most famous actors, in the MCLC review's summary of Huang's book. A claim that a play has been adapted for the Peking Opera is therefore a claim about the summit of the system, which is why the survey's general sentence about this play carries weight, and why, equally, a named production would carry more.

The Holo Taiwanese Opera Troupe's A Story of Love and Feud was first performed in 2001 and revived in 2011. The adaptation set the lovers in Mengjia, the old port settlement of Taipei, and rebuilt the feud from the intra-ethnic battles between settlers from Zhangzhou and settlers from Quanzhou during the Qing dynasty. Guo Chunmei, a celebrated xiaosheng specialist, one of the male-impersonation performers of Taiwanese opera, played the leading role, the figure equivalent to Romeo. The substitution is the purest act of localization in the play's Chinese record. Where Verona's houses are a given, unexplained by the play beyond their ancient grudge, the gezaixi version grounds its feud in a documented historical violence between two communities of settlers, the kind of feud examined in [The Feud: Why the Capulets and Montagues Hate](/2022/04/01/capulet-montague-feud-explained/), the series' account of the two houses as the text presents them. The feud stops being a premise and becomes a memory.

Huang's phrase, the ethnic vocabularies of history, names what the substitution accomplishes. Zhangzhou and Quanzhou are not costumes for Montague and Capulet. They are origin identities that structured Taiwanese society for centuries, dividing speech, settlement and marriage, and the gezaixi stage had carried stories of their feuding long before Shakespeare arrived. The imported plot gave that inherited material a new prestige form, and the inherited material gave the imported plot a local truth that no translation could supply: an audience in Taiwan did not have to imagine what it meant for two communities to raise their children inside a quarrel neither child began. The casting completes the localization. In the Taiwanese opera tradition, the romantic male lead is a xiaosheng, a male-impersonation specialism performed by women, and Guo Chunmei's Romeo figure belongs to that convention, as every lover in Yue opera belongs to an all-female stage. In the Chinese opera forms, the lover is a woman's star part, and the play's erotic charge is carried by craft rather than by casting realism.

Taiwan contributes a second documented experiment, in the spoken theatre, that sharpens the point about bodies. In 2004 Lu Po-shen directed an adaptation of the play for the Tainaner Ensemble, his second attempt at the text, set in a modern world with popular elements aimed at young audiences. The cast was all male, five actors most of them under twenty, freshmen of the Department of Theatre Arts at National Sun Yat-sen University, and the all-male choice was made deliberately as a tribute to the Elizabethan convention of boy actors in female roles. The scholarship on the production reads it against Taiwan's own cross-dressing stage traditions, the all-female casts of Taiwanese opera and the male-impersonation heritage of Beijing opera. A play about a girl who must move through the world on terms set by men acquires, in a theatre where gender is a performed assignment rather than a casting fact, a second subject: the performance of sex itself.

Alexander Huang's essay on Romeo and Juliet and the ethnic vocabularies of history takes the Taiwan productions as its evidence and draws the general conclusion: Chinese interpretations of the play have reflected the ideological investments of their moments and places, and the feud in particular has served as an allegorical instrument for conflicts that are not about love at all. A second Taipei production makes the same point in a different medium. Yumei and Tianlai, a bilingual Taiwanese-Mandarin Romeo and Juliet staged at the Shakespeare in Taipei festival in 2003, assigned the Montagues and the Capulets different languages, so that the feud was audible before it was visible, and the play's capacity as national allegory was tested in the ear. The Taipei festival, a month-long event in May 2003, took the opposite institutional form from the mainland's state festivals of 1986 and 1994: a platform for artistically innovative and commercially viable experimental work, in Joubin's account, rather than a ratification of a canon.

The scholarly disagreement here is genuine and worth adjudicating. Xiao Yang Zhang, in his 1996 comparative study, argued that Shakespeare's impact on Chinese theatre had been so total that traditional Chinese drama faced a choice between dying out as a feeble and decaying art and surviving through reform at the risk of losing its distinctiveness. Zhang's claim was maximal: Shakespeare had become, in his phrase, the most important and authoritative dramatic form in Chinese cultural circles. Huang's Chinese Shakespeares, published in 2009, refuses that frame. Its subtitle names the alternative: two centuries of cultural trade, a two-way transaction in which Chinese theatres took what they needed and returned transformed goods. The MCLC review of the book states the methodological refusal precisely: Huang does not wish simply to trace how elements of Shakespearean stories and dramas found their way into Chinese culture through a filtering process. The operatic evidence adjudicates the dispute in Huang's favour, with Li Ruru's correction attached. The traditional forms were not displaced by the play, and they did not preserve themselves by refusing it. They conscripted it. The gezaixi Romeo, the kunju Macbeth and the jingju Othello are not Western plays in Chinese costume; they are Chinese operas that found foreign plots serviceable. But the trade was not the frictionless commerce Huang's metaphor can suggest. The 1986 directors' own words, violent and painful collision, are the accurate verb for what adaptation cost, and the verdict of this article adopts them.

## The Butterfly Lovers: A Legend Older Than the Play

Every account of the play in China meets the Butterfly Lovers, and the meeting needs to be set out at documentary precision, because the parallel is both a fact of reception and a temptation to lazy analysis. The legend of Liang Shanbo and Zhu Yingtai, abbreviated as Liang Zhu, is set in the Eastern Jin dynasty. Zhu Yingtai, a young woman of a wealthy family, disguises herself as a man in order to study, and spends three years as a fellow student of Liang Shanbo, who never learns her sex. At their parting she hints at the truth by offering to arrange his marriage to her younger sister, a sister who is herself. Liang comes late to understand, and later still to propose. By then her family has betrothed her to Ma Wencai. Liang dies of grief. At her wedding procession, Yingtai halts at his grave; the grave opens; she leaps in; and the pair rise as butterflies, together and inseparable.

The legend's antiquity is the first thing the parallel gets right. The earliest written record traced by scholarship is a line from the late Tang dynasty, in Liang Zaiyan's Shidao Sifan Zhi: the righteous woman Zhu Yingtai was buried together with Liang Shanbo. Zhang Du's Xuan Shizhi, from the years around 850 to 880, carries an expanded account. Wilt Idema's edition of four premodern versions traces the legend's records through the Song and Tang and prints a prosimetric ballad of the thirteenth century. The butterfly metamorphosis itself, the legend's most famous feature, entered the story only in the Ming dynasty. The legend was selected as one of China's Four Great Folktales by the Folklore Movement in the 1920s, alongside the Legend of the White Snake, Lady Meng Jiang and the Cowherd and the Weaving Maid. Idema's edition follows the story through the centuries between the records and the modern stage, printing ballads from the period 1100 to 1450 and tracing the adaptations for the stage that multiplied after the fifteenth century, so that by the time Yue opera took the legend up, the tale had already lived for half a millennium as sung and staged entertainment. Six Chinese cities collaborated from 2004 on a formal application for the legend's proclamation as a masterpiece of oral and intangible heritage at UNESCO.

The detail of the Ming butterflies disciplines the parallel's most repeated image. The butterflies, the feature every comparison quotes first, are a Ming-dynasty addition, younger than the joint-burial record by some six centuries and roughly contemporary with the play itself. The consolation is younger than the grief. The legend also has its own Verona problem, a material cult with rival custodians. Official gazetteers from Ningbo and from Yixing both record the lovers, which is the documentary form of a custody dispute: two regions inscribed the story in their official memory. Ningbo maintains the stronger material claim, with its Liang Shanbo temple and cultural park and, in 1997, an excavated tomb at Gaoqiao that local archaeology identified as the lovers' joint burial, complete with a tablet bearing their names. A story old enough and loved enough generates addresses, and the addresses generate custodians, and the custodians generate the certainty that the story happened here. The Chinese legend reached that stage of fame centuries before the play arrived to be compared with it.

### Q: Is the Butterfly Lovers legend older than Shakespeare's play?

Yes, by several centuries at the level of written record. A late Tang source records the joint burial of Zhu Yingtai and Liang Shanbo, and a ninth-century account carries the bulk of the tale. The butterfly ending is a later addition, arriving only in the Ming dynasty.

The form that carried the legend is itself part of the explanation. Yue opera grew up in Shengzhou in Zhejiang in the early twentieth century and became famous for its all-female troupes in the 1920s, women playing every role, the male lovers by xiaosheng specialists. A form whose entire company is female, singing in a soft and lyrical register devoted to emotion and romance, was the natural custodian of a legend whose heroine spends three years passing as a man: the disguise that drives the plot is, in Yue opera, the condition of the stage itself. The form was recognized in 2006 as part of China's national intangible cultural heritage, and the legend is the centre of its repertory.

The convergence of the two stories also has a personal form. Judith Zeitlin's study of opera and film in the period records that the Shanghai Yue Opera Troupe performed The Butterfly Lovers, the signature piece of its repertory, at a private performance at Zhongnanhai for Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai, the first Yue opera Mao had seen. The troupe's committed patron in those years was Tian Han, the first translator of Romeo and Juliet into Chinese, who helped adapt and revise scripts for the Yue opera stage. The man who had carried the Verona lovers into Chinese prose spent his later career in the service of the Hangzhou lovers' operatic home. The parallel was, among other things, a biography. Out of that stage tradition came the Shanghai Yue opera film of the legend, released in 1954 and generally described as China's first color feature film. The film's international moment is the parallel's most famous episode, and it is carried in this article at the strength its sources support, which is the strength of a celebrated anecdote. The Geneva Conference of 1954, convened on Korea and Indochina, was the People's Republic's first appearance as a major power at a great international meeting, and the screening belonged to the new state's cultural diplomacy. The story is told that at the Geneva Conference of 1954, Premier Zhou Enlai directed that foreign journalists be invited to a screening of the film, and that the invitation describe it in a single line as China's Romeo and Juliet. The anecdote, retold from memoir, adds that the hall filled and that journalists wept at the grave scene and the butterflies. Whatever its archival status, the anecdote is itself part of the parallel's history: it records the moment the equation of the two stories became official Chinese usage, offered to the world as a translation key. In 1958 and 1959 the legend passed into concert music, when He Zhanhao and Chen Gang, students at the Shanghai Conservatory of Music, composed the Butterfly Lovers Violin Concerto, first performed in 1959 by the violinist Yu Lina as part of the celebrations for the tenth anniversary of the People's Republic. The Shaw Brothers film The Love Eterne, directed by Li Han-hsiang in 1963 in the Huangmei opera form, carried the legend to Hong Kong and beyond, and Michael Berry's description of that film's story as the Romeo and Juliet of the Far East shows the equation still doing its work a decade after Geneva. The Hong Kong film is the legend's other great screen monument, and its existence complicates the equation it advertises: by 1963 the legend had two canonical opera films, in two Chinese operatic forms, from two Chinese cinemas, a depth of native production the imported play has never matched in China.

The scholarly literature on the parallel is older than the equation's fame suggests. Hong Xin published a comparison of Liang Zhu and Romeo and Juliet in the journal Drama Studies in 1985. Chan-Fai Cheung's essay in the Journal of Chinese Philosophy in 1999 tested the claim, associated with Denis de Rougemont, that romantic love is absent from Chinese culture, and used the two stories as its philosophical evidence, concluding against the absence claim. The parallel, that is, is not merely a slogan of cultural publicity. It has been an instrument in a serious argument about whether Chinese tradition possesses a concept of passionate love at all.

The comparison's longevity in scholarship is itself a datum. Hong Xin's comparison of 1985 and Cheung's essay of 1999 stand fourteen years apart, in a drama journal and a philosophy journal, and they ask different questions of the same pair of stories: the drama scholar asks how the two tragedies are built, the philosopher asks what each culture means by love. Cheung's frame is qing, the Chinese category of feeling, set against the Western category of romantic love, and his test of the absence thesis turns on the legend's existence. A tradition that produced Liang Zhu cannot, on the evidence Cheung assembles, be said to lack the idea of love that destroys and transfigures. It can only be said to have housed the idea in a different architecture, one of duty and disguise rather than of feud and vow. Handled at this level, the parallel stops being a publicity equation and becomes a research programme, and the programme's finding is double: the pattern is shared, the architecture is not.

### Q: What does the Butterfly Lovers parallel flatten?

It flattens almost everything except the outline. The legend has no feud between two houses; its obstacle is one family's betrothal of a daughter and a young man's class position. Its heroine drives the story through disguise and schooling, machinery the play never uses. And its ending consoles, where the tomb scene refuses consolation.

What the parallel reveals is real, and it is large. It reveals that the deep pattern of the play, young love blocked by the authority of family and social order and destroyed by that order's machinery, was native Chinese property long before 1904, which is the strongest available explanation for the speed of the play's domestication in China. A culture that had told Liang Zhu for a thousand years did not need to be taught what kind of story Romeo and Juliet was. What the parallel flattens is everything that makes the play this play and not another doomed-love tale. There is no feud in the legend: no second house, no street war, no Mercutio cursing both houses with "A plague o' both your houses" at 3.1.99, no Prince auditing a city at the close. The legend's catastrophe is arranged by one betrothal, and its agents are parents, not a civic order at war with itself. The legend's ending is a transformation, an ascent into nature, where the play's ending is an audit: two statues of gold and a sentence of blame. The love-death pattern the two stories share belongs to the long tradition examined in [The Liebestod: Love and Death Entwined](/2024/01/05/love-and-death-liebestod-analysis/), the series' account of love and death as one subject; but the play's distinctive contribution to that tradition is precisely what the parallel hides, the social machinery that grinds between the lovers and their grave. The verdict on the parallel is therefore a grading, not a dismissal. As reception history, the parallel is the central fact of the play in China. As analysis, it explains the welcome and misdescribes the guest.

The asymmetry of the two stories' centres sharpens the grading. The legend develops mainly around Zhu Yingtai, as the medievalists' summary of the tale puts it: she conceives the plan, sustains the disguise, delivers the hints, refuses the betrothal and chooses the grave, while Liang Shanbo is acted upon at every turn, learning the truth late and dying of the knowledge. The play distributes agency between its lovers and its city. Romeo kills twice and dies by his own misreading; Juliet plans, feigns and dies by her own hand; and around them the feud's adults, the friar's machinery and the Prince's law all take their shares of the causation. The parallel, which treats the two stories as the same shape at different longitudes, hides the difference between a heroine's story and a city's tragedy.

## Two Lovers, Two Names: Romeo and Zhu Yingtai on One Stage

The parallel stepped out of the publicity line and onto the stage in 2008, when the Shanghai Dramatic Arts Center produced Ning Caishen's Romeo and Zhu Yingtai, directed by He Nian. Ning called it a tragedy told in comic manners. Key scenes from the play were staged as plays within the play, in French, Japanese, English and Mandarin Chinese, and the star-crossed lovers traversed 1937 Shanghai and twenty-first-century New York in search of new personal and cultural identities. The production is the parallel's theatrical form: not a comparison but a double exposure, the Verona lovers and the Hangzhou students occupying one stage, each story exposing the other's negatives.

The choice of 1937 for the production's Shanghai is a citation of the play's own Chinese biography. Nineteen thirty-seven is the year of Zhang Min's Carlton production and of the argument about whether a nation in crisis could afford a love tragedy, and Ning's lovers pass through that Shanghai as through a family photograph, the play's Chinese past treated as part of its plot. A mashup of this kind is possible only when both stories are common property, when an audience can be trusted to hold the Verona feud, the Hangzhou schoolroom, the Carlton argument and the Manhattan present in a single field of vision. The production is therefore evidence as well as entertainment: by 2008 the play's Chinese history was itself old enough, and known enough, to be quoted on stage.

Six years later the play's Chinese history produced its most elegant act of localization, and it came out of the names themselves. The National Theatre of China created a new Romeo and Juliet, adapted and directed by Tian Qinxin, which opened to the public for the first time on 1 March 2014 at the Hong Kong Academy for Performing Arts, as part of the forty-second Hong Kong Arts Festival and in conjunction with the 450th anniversary of the playwright's birth, before touring cities in China. The cast included Yin Tao and Li Guangjie.

The production had been announced in Beijing in January 2014 as an act of naturalization. The company's stated ambition, in the phrasing of its announcement, was to tell the love story of Chinese young people, and to let the classic that had influenced the Chinese theatre for nearly a century take up residence in China. The phrase is worth pausing on. A century after Lin Shu, the national theatre still framed the play as a resident whose naturalization needed completing, and framed its own production as the completion. Benny Lim's study of the production reads it through ritual, and the emphasis fits the ambition: the adaptation's most prominent Christian rite, the lovers' marriage in church, is retained and centred, so that the production's Chinese situations are built around a ceremony the imported text supplies and the host culture must make its own. Benny Lim's study of the production, reading it through its rituals, records the decisive change: the two opposing families are not the Montagues and Capulets but the Luo clan and the Zhu clan. The transliteration had always carried the feud inside it. Romeo in Chinese is Luo Mi'ou, and Juliet is Zhu Liye: the lovers' Chinese given names begin with the characters Luo and Zhu, and the production simply promoted those syllables into surnames, so that the feud in Chinese writes itself out of the names Chinese audiences had used for a century. Lim's judgment, that the version is more modernized and that its approach and characters are more Chinese, understates the wit of the device. The production found the one localization that was not an imposition but a discovery, latent in the language since Tian Han.

Tian Qinxin's own account of her method resists the vocabulary of subversion.

### Q: What did Tian Qinxin say about subverting Shakespeare?

She rejected the premise. Because Shakespeare belongs to the world, she argued, there is no need to subvert him; the task is to understand and express him with Chinese situations. Her own description of the work was a translator's, a machine for conveying the emotions the plays carry.

As she put it, because Shakespeare belongs to the world, there is no need to subvert him; the task is to understand and express him with Chinese situations. She described her work as becoming a translating machine for the emotions the plays convey, and she noted that her Buddhism, which treats love as possessing and controlling and fear of loss, obliged her to approach the story from the point of view of a nonbeliever. Against that position stands the director Stan Lai's account of the Chinese Shakespeare cult: that Chinese reverence for the playwright has essentially turned him into a brand. The adjudication between the two directors is the adjudication of the whole modern record. The brand charge is true of the name, which functions in Chinese public life as a certificate of cultural value, deployable on programmes and invitations. It is not true of the best work done under the name, and the 2014 production is the counterexample: a staging that found, inside the Chinese language itself, a feud the English text does not contain. Reverence produced the brand. Practice, at its best, produced the Luo and Zhu clans. The 2014 production holds both truths in one frame: it was mounted in conjunction with a 450th anniversary, which is the brand's calendar, and it solved its central problem with a device drawn from the Chinese language itself, which is the artist's answer. Anniversary commissions are how the brand reproduces. The Luo and Zhu clans are how the art does.

The imported versions complete the modern picture. The French musical Roméo et Juliette, composed by Gérard Presgurvic and premiered in Paris in 2001, toured to China in 2016, playing Guangzhou and Beijing. Its officially licensed Chinese-language version then premiered at the Beijing Tianqiao Performing Arts Center on 24 December 2021, starring Ayunga, with direction and choreography by Carl Portal. The sequence restates the article's whole argument in miniature: the play arrives as a foreign triumph, and then, promptly, it is refounded in Chinese.

The French production had toured eighteen countries and played to more than six million spectators before its China dates, and its contemporary style was aimed squarely at the young. The Chinese version's translation team included the lyricist Liang Mang, and its four casts of lovers alternated across the Beijing run. A visiting British dance production tells the same story from the other side. Matthew Bourne's Romeo and Juliet, premiered in 2019 with the feud rebuilt as the conflict between the guards and the young inmates of the Verona Institute, toured Shanghai, Beijing, Guangzhou and Macao in 2024, imported entire. The imports arrive as finished goods. The refoundings are made at home.

Hong Kong recurs in the record at the joints. The legend's second great opera film was a Hong Kong production of 1963. The National Theatre of China's naturalization production opened not in Beijing but at the Hong Kong Academy for Performing Arts, inside the city's arts festival, before touring the mainland. The pattern fits the city's long function in the play's transmission: a place where Chinese and Western performance economies meet, close enough to the mainland to premiere its national company's work and separate enough to give the premiere an international frame. The play's Chinese life has repeatedly been notarized in Hong Kong before being filed on the mainland.

## The InsightCrunch China-Theatre Map

The chronology the article has followed is set out here as the InsightCrunch China-theatre map, the findable artifact of the piece. A map of this density is assembled from production files, reviews, memoirs and promptbooks, the kind of evidence researchers keep in order in a local research vault such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/), where each row keeps its source beside its claim. The map's rows are graded by the strength of their documentation, and the grading is part of the map: production rows rest on named companies, theatres and dates; the Geneva row rests on a celebrated anecdote retold from memoir and is marked as such; the Birmingham row rests on a reported exhibition contrast. A map that hid those differences would misdescribe the record it maps. The Geneva row is the map's honesty test. It is among the most repeated stories in the play's Chinese history and among the least archivally secured, and the map keeps it, marked, because a chronology of reception that deleted its most repeated story would misdescribe what the reception consisted of. The map also omits what the record omits: no row claims a first night for the play in Chinese that the documents do not supply, and the 1944 row keeps its translation and its production as separate entries because the record keeps them separate.

The map's last translation row deserves its sentence. In 2016 the Royal Shakespeare Company launched its Shakespeare Folio Translation Project, embedding translators in rehearsal rooms alongside actors and directors to create stage-focused translations for China. The project is the institutional form of a lesson the Chinese record taught a century ago, when Tian Han's versions were judged beautiful but unplayable: a translation of this play succeeds in the theatre or not at all.

The project was launched in 2016, the year of the four-hundredth anniversary of the playwright's death, and its method answers the anniversary's central Chinese question, which was never whether the plays mattered but whether they could be played. The project's premise, as the British Library presents it, is that the century-old Chinese problem is a workflow problem. The translators of the heroic decades worked alone, at desks, through wars, and delivered literature. The Folio project embeds the translator in the rehearsal room, where lines are tested on actors' breath before they are fixed in print. The method is a return, by a foreign institution, to the insight the Chinese theatre began with: the mubiao companies had the play as performance before anyone had it as text, and the Folio translators work as if performance were still the primary fact.

## The Verdict: China Refounded the Play Three Times

The record supports one verdict, and the article has argued it throughout. Romeo and Juliet in Chinese theatre is not the history of an import settling in. It is the history of three foundings. The story founding, in 1903 and 1904, made the lovers Chinese narrative property before any Chinese actor spoke a line of the play, and it did so in the hands of a translator who could not read a word of English and who shelved the tale among stories of gods and spirits. The spoken-drama founding made the play an instrument of national argument: Tian Han translated it against Ibsen, Zhang Min aimed it at the feudal order, its critics attacked it for failing the war, and the argument itself is the proof that the play had become Chinese property by 1937. The operatic founding made it local: the gezaixi version rebuilt its feud out of settler history, and the festival directors of 1986 named the process honestly, a violent and painful collision in which Chinese forms conscripted a foreign plot and were changed by the conscription.

The three foundings had three different agents, and the agents explain the results. The story was founded by a translator working from dictation, and it came out as Chinese prose. The spoken drama was founded by directors and polemicists, and it came out as an argument. The operatic founding was made by performers inside inherited forms, and it came out as local history with foreign names. Each agent got the Romeo and Juliet its tools could build, and none of the three superseded the others. All three coexist in the play's Chinese life, in the schoolroom where the tale is read, in the theatre where the argument is staged, and in the opera house where the feud wears a local face.

The Butterfly Lovers parallel is the ground of the whole history, and its correct use is the discipline this article has tried to practise. The parallel is true as reception: the equation was made by Chinese officials at Geneva, by Hong Kong publicity for The Love Eterne, by scholars in Drama Studies in 1985 and in the Journal of Chinese Philosophy in 1999, and by a Shanghai playwright who put Zhu Yingtai into the play's title in 2008. The parallel is false as identity: the legend has no feud, no street war, no divided city, and an ending that consoles where the play refuses consolation. China could receive the play so fast, and remake it so freely, because the legend had already built the room the play was received into. The play repaid the hospitality by adding what the legend lacks: a feud between two houses, a machinery of stars announced in its prologue, and a tomb in which nobody rises as anything. The classroom afterlife of that exchange, in China and across the world, is taken up in [The Play in Global Classrooms Today](/2030/06/28/play-in-global-classrooms/), the series' account of the play's school life across countries. What the Chinese record finally shows is that a play survives translation into another civilization not by being preserved but by being refounded, and that refounding, repeated three times in a single century in a single country, is the strongest evidence the play's Chinese theatre provides for the play's own account of itself: a story that every teller must build again.

The three foundings have a common heir: the institution. The play's Chinese life is carried, in the festival century the map records, by societies and their journals, by anniversaries and their commissions, by a national theatre's repertoire and a foreign company's translation project, and custody of that kind changes the questions that get asked. The tale asked whether the story could be Chinese. The argument asked what the story was for. The institutions ask how the story is to be kept, taught and handed on, and the keeping is the least dramatic and most durable of the play's Chinese states.

The general lesson is about translation itself. The Chinese record refuses the metaphor of the bridge, across which a finished work is carried intact. Nothing in the record crossed intact: not the tale, which arrived before the language of love it needed had settled; not the text, which arrived through another language and was rebuilt twice, in prose and then in verse; not the production, which arrived as an argument and was rebuilt as local history. What crossed was a pattern, a feud, a pair of names and a tomb, and what the pattern met on the far side was a civilization with a matching pattern of its own and a thousand years of practice in telling it. The play's Chinese theatre is the record of that meeting, and its verdict is the article's: the play was not preserved in China. It was, three times, begun again. The record ends where it began, with tellers. Lin Shu told the story to readers who had never seen a stage. The institutions keep it for audiences who have never known a China without it. Between the telling and the keeping lies the whole Chinese century of the play, and the lovers, who in Verona could not survive their world, have survived three of China's.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: When did Shakespeare's name first reach Chinese readers?

The name arrived in 1856, in Shanghai, inside a history book rather than a play. William Muirhead, a British missionary working with a Chinese assistant, published a modified translation of Thomas Milner's history of England, and Milner's brief reference to Shakespeare in the chapter on Elizabethan England was the first time the playwright's name came to the notice of the Chinese reading public. The reference was a name without a text. In 1894 the reformer Yan Fu predicted in print that Shakespeare was an English poet and playwright whose works would spread far and wide among readers across the world, and the prediction too was a name without a text. Nearly half a century passed before the Lambs' tales gave Chinese readers the stories in 1903 and 1904, and almost seventy years before a complete play existed in Chinese.

### Q: What title did Lin Shu give the story of Romeo and Juliet?

Lin Shu retitled the tale Zhuqing, in characters 铸情, cast love or forged love. The retitling was his system for the whole 1904 collection, Yingguo Shiren Yinbian Yanyu, in which each of the Lambs' twenty tales received two stylish characters of his own: Hamlet became Guizhao, the ghost's command, and the other tales were renamed in the same manner. The Romeo and Juliet title already interprets the story. It presents love as a substance worked under pressure, cast in a mould by forces outside the lovers, which is a fair compression of the plot Lin had heard read aloud by Wei Yi and recomposed in semi-classical prose.

### Q: Why could Chinese actors perform Shakespeare stories before any play had been translated?

Because the early Chinese stage worked from scenarios, not scripts. Lin Shu's tales supplied the plots, and companies of the civilized drama movement scripted productions as mubiao, outlines from which actors were expected to expand the action on stage through improvisation, in a manner the Internet Shakespeare Editions history compares to commedia dell'arte. The sequence began with the New People's Society's Merchant of Venice in Chinese in July 1913 and ran to more than twenty Shakespearean adaptations over the following decade. The reforming theatre needed plots with ideological charge for its project of making new citizens. The verse, which no complete translation yet supplied, could wait. The practice reached even the traditional stage: the British Library's account records that xiqu troupes likewise took the plot outlines from the Lamb translations and improvised the rest.

### Q: Who produced the first complete Chinese translation of Romeo and Juliet?

Tian Han produced it, finishing the work in 1923 to 1924, after his Hamlet of 1921 to 1922, and publishing the play in a journal he edited in the mid-1920s. The British Library's account gives his motive as a student's answer to disparagement: studying in Japan, Tian Han heard Japanese interlocutors disparage Chinese culture for lacking a complete Shakespeare, and he responded by translating. The Internet Shakespeare Editions record adds the scholarly caution that his versions likely relied on a Japanese intermediary text, since no attribution was given in his work and his English was judged inadequate for the original. The versions are described in the British Library's phrase as beautiful but unplayable. His Hamlet preface had already declared the campaign: Shakespeare's works were oil painting, Ibsen's marble sculpture, and the Romeo and Juliet, published some three years after the Hamlet, was the alternative he offered a spoken drama he thought overrun with Ibsen-style works.

### Q: What happened to Zhu Shenghao's first translations of the plays?

They were lost amid the war with Japan, and Zhu Shenghao retranslated them. He had begun the Shakespeare project in 1935. In May 1942 he married his university classmate Song Qingru in Shanghai, and in June the couple moved to Changshu, where he finished retranslating the manuscripts the war had destroyed. In January 1943 they settled in Jiaxing, where Romeo and Juliet, King Lear and Hamlet were translated. He worked in poverty and declining health until his death in December 1944, at thirty-two, having translated thirty-one plays. The translations were published posthumously, twenty-seven of them before 1949, and the collection appeared in 1947. His arrangement of the canon was itself a decision for Chinese readers: setting aside the Oxford edition's chronological order, he divided the plays into comedy, tragedy, history and miscellaneous pieces.

### Q: Why do Chinese readers call Shakespeare "Old Man Sha"?

The affectionate name Sha Weng, Old Man Sha, replaced the standard transliteration Shashibiya by the mid-1920s, when scholars judged the plain transliteration no longer suitable for a figure of his standing. The name marks the speed of the canonization: within two decades of the first tales, the foreign playwright had been adopted as a venerable elder of Chinese letters. After 1949 the standing was reinforced by doctrine, since Engels had praised the Renaissance as an age of giants in thought, passion and character. The reverence carried a cost recorded by the Internet Shakespeare Editions history: practitioners felt that Old Man Sha's works could not be done any old way one liked. The name is also the affectionate counterpart of the transliteration's failure: Shashibiya named a foreigner, while Sha Weng adopted a grandfather.

### Q: What did Zhang Min say the 1937 Shanghai production was about?

Zhang Min, the left-wing director of the Shanghai Practical Drama Company's production at the Carlton Theatre, read the play as an attack on the feudal order. His interpretation survives in Ge Yihong's history of Chinese spoken drama: an ethical code destroys the tender shoot that desires for freedom; the flame of youth burns the feudal fetters. The mapping is exact. The feud stands for feudal ethics, the parents for the old order, the lovers for youth itself, and their deaths for the indictment of the system that required them. Other critics objected that a love tragedy was the wrong choice with Japan occupying the north-east, a dispute that shows the play had already become Chinese cultural property. Guo's survey records the production, by the Shanghai Practical Drama Company at the Carlton Theatre, as a very successful one, which settled the practical half of the argument.

### Q: Which languages carried the two houses in the 2003 Taipei production?

Mandarin Chinese and Taiwanese Hokkien carried them. Yumei and Tianlai, the bilingual Romeo and Juliet staged at the Shakespeare in Taipei festival in 2003, assigned the Montagues and the Capulets a different language each, as Alexa Alice Joubin's account of the production records. The device made the feud audible before it was visible: an audience heard the division of the city in the division of its tongues, and the lovers' scenes crossed a linguistic border as well as a familial one. The production belongs to the Taiwan pattern in which the play serves as national allegory, its two houses made to speak the island's own divided speech.

### Q: Which historical feud replaced the Capulets and Montagues in the Taiwanese opera version?

The battles between settlers from Zhangzhou and settlers from Quanzhou, the intra-ethnic wars of Qing-dynasty Taiwan, replaced the Veronese feud. The Holo Taiwanese Opera Troupe's A Story of Love and Feud, first performed in 2001 and revived in 2011, set the lovers in Mengjia, the old port settlement of Taipei, and grounded its conflict in that documented communal violence. Guo Chunmei, a celebrated specialist in xiaosheng, the male-impersonation roles of Taiwanese opera, played the figure equivalent to Romeo. The substitution matters because the play never explains its ancient grudge, while the gezaixi version's feud is a remembered history its audience could name. The casting carried the localization further: in Taiwanese opera the romantic male lead is a xiaosheng, a male-impersonation specialism performed by women, so the Romeo figure arrived inside the form's own star convention rather than against it.

### Q: What is Romeo and Zhu Yingtai?

Romeo and Zhu Yingtai is Ning Caishen's stage work produced by the Shanghai Dramatic Arts Center in May 2008 and directed by He Nian. Ning called it a tragedy told in comic manners. Key scenes from Shakespeare's play were staged as plays within the play, in French, Japanese, English and Mandarin Chinese, and the star-crossed lovers traversed 1937 Shanghai and twenty-first-century New York in search of new personal and cultural identities. The piece matters to the play's Chinese history because it literalizes the Butterfly Lovers parallel: instead of comparing the Verona lovers with Zhu Yingtai in an essay, it puts the two stories on one stage and lets each expose the other. Its choice of 1937 Shanghai for one pole of the lovers' journey cites the play's own Chinese biography, the year of Zhang Min's Carlton production, treating the play's national history as part of its plot.

### Q: How did the transliterated names create the two clans in Tian Qinxin's 2014 production?

Through the first characters of the lovers' Chinese names. Romeo in Chinese is Luo Mi'ou and Juliet is Zhu Liye, so the transliteration had carried the syllables Luo and Zhu at the head of the two names for a century. Tian Qinxin's production for the National Theatre of China promoted those syllables into family names, and the opposing houses became the Luo clan and the Zhu clan. Benny Lim's study of the production records the change. The device is a localization of a special kind: not an imposition on the text but a discovery inside the Chinese language, where the feud had been latent in the names since the translations settled.

### Q: Who directed the National Theatre of China's 2014 Romeo and Juliet, and where did it open?

Tian Qinxin adapted and directed it. The production opened to the public for the first time on 1 March 2014 at the Hong Kong Academy for Performing Arts, as part of the forty-second Hong Kong Arts Festival and in conjunction with the 450th anniversary of the playwright's birth, and it then toured cities in China. The cast included Yin Tao and Li Guangjie. Benny Lim's study reads the production through its rituals and records its decisive changes, among them the renaming of the houses as the Luo and Zhu clans. Lim judges the version more modernized, and its approach and characters more Chinese, than a straight staging would have been. The production had been announced in Beijing that January as an act of naturalization, an attempt to let a classic that had influenced the Chinese theatre for nearly a century take up residence in China by telling the love story of Chinese young people.

### Q: What did Zhou Enlai reportedly tell his staff to write on the Geneva invitations?

The celebrated anecdote reports that at the Geneva Conference of 1954, Zhou Enlai directed that journalists be invited to a screening of the Yue opera film of the Butterfly Lovers with a single descriptive line: China's Romeo and Juliet. The story, retold from memoir, adds that officials doubted the film would draw a foreign press corps, that Zhou backed the wording, that the hall filled, and that journalists wept at the grave scene and the emergence of the butterflies. The anecdote is carried here at memoir strength rather than archival strength. Its wide circulation is itself evidence: it records the moment the equation of the legend with the play became official Chinese usage. The conference, convened on Korea and Indochina, was the People's Republic's first appearance as a major power at a great international meeting, which is why a film screening carried diplomatic weight at all.

### Q: When was the Butterfly Lovers violin concerto first performed, and who played it?

The concerto was first performed in 1959 by the violinist Yu Lina, as part of the celebrations for the tenth anniversary of the People's Republic. It had been composed in 1958 and 1959 by He Zhanhao and Chen Gang, two students at the Shanghai Conservatory of Music, and it sets the legend's episodes in the form of a Western violin concerto, drawing its melodic material from Yue opera. The work's history matters to the play's Chinese record because it carried the legend into the world's concert halls under its own name, at the same period when the legend was being introduced abroad principally as China's Romeo and Juliet. Its fame travelled further than any single staging: the concerto entered the international repertory while the play's own Chinese productions remained domestic events.

### Q: Which Chinese opera form made the Butterfly Lovers its signature piece?

Yue opera made it so. The form, which grew up in Shengzhou in Zhejiang and is performed by all-female troupes, carries Liang Shanbo and Zhu Yingtai as the signature piece of its repertory, with the lovers played by women in both the female role and the xiaosheng male-impersonation role. The Shanghai Yue opera film of the legend, released in 1954 and generally described as China's first color feature film, came directly out of that stage tradition, and it was that film which the Geneva anecdote attached to the play's name. The documented operatic Romeo and Juliet, by contrast, belongs to Taiwan's gezaixi stage rather than to the Yue opera stage. Judith Zeitlin's study records the legend's standing at the summit of the state: the Shanghai Yue Opera Troupe performed the piece at a private performance at Zhongnanhai for Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai, with Tian Han, the play's first Chinese translator, as the troupe's committed patron.

### Q: What did Qian Zhongshu argue in defence of Lin Shu's free translations?

Writing in 1963, Qian Zhongshu argued that Lin Shu should be judged as a matchmaker rather than as a copyist. His essay Lin Shu's Translation quoted Goethe's simile of translators as busy matchmakers and held that Lin had served well in that office between Western literature and Chinese readers, observing that Lin's versions had moved Qian himself to learn foreign languages. The defence was not a whitewash. Qian noted that Lin often improved the original as well as abridging it, and divided the career at 1913, the early work vigorous despite all the mistranslations, the later work dull and done for a living. The verdict has held: the first phase's vigor, not its fidelity, was the achievement. The defence also prevailed institutionally: in 1981 the Commercial Press, Lin's original publisher, reprinted ten of the renditions with modern punctuation, an act of canonization performed by the market and the academy together.

### Q: Why did the verse translators object to the prose versions of the plays?

Because prose cannot carry blank verse, and the plays are poetic drama. Sun Dayu, the first translator to use poetry for Shakespeare in Chinese, insisted that it was a pity to translate blank verse with prose, since readers would lose the beauty of the plays as poetic drama, and his King Lear of 1948 used a syllabic unit of his own invention to reproduce the beat of the verse. The verse complete works edited by Fang Ping, published on the mainland in 2000, extended the argument to the whole canon. The record adjudicates the dispute as a split: prose won the stage, where Zhu Shenghao's texts were the acting standard, and verse won the argument about poetry. The poet Bian Zhilin's verse-conscious Hamlet of 1956 shows the minority position held from inside the literary establishment itself, a generation before the verse complete works made it policy.

### Q: What three kinds of opera Shakespeare did Li Ruru distinguish at the 1986 festival?

Li Ruru distinguished productions that emphasized the foreignness of the plays, productions that fully sinicized the plays, and productions that synthesized Chinese and Western elements both textually and theatrically. The classification arose from the four operatic productions of Shakespeare staged in Shanghai during the first China Shakespeare Festival of April 1986, of which the Shanghai Kunju Theatre Company's Macbeth is the documented example most often cited. At the festival forum the directors of the four operas unanimously described their creative process as one of violent and painful collision, a phrase that captures the cost of carrying a spoken verse drama into sung conventionalized forms better than any metaphor of exchange. The festival's gathering itself was the first occasion in China on which scholars, traditional operatic performers, orchestras, foreign-literature specialists and local opera audiences met in one theatre.

### Q: When did a licensed Chinese-language version of the French Romeo and Juliet musical open in Beijing?

The licensed Chinese-language version of Gérard Presgurvic's Roméo et Juliette opened at the Beijing Tianqiao Performing Arts Center on 24 December 2021, starring Ayunga, with direction and choreography by Carl Portal. The French original, premiered in Paris in 2001, had already toured to China in 2016, playing Guangzhou and Beijing in French. The 2021 production was the work's refounding in Mandarin: an imported musical property, licensed and cast locally, running across the New Year with four casts of lovers. Its sequence, foreign triumph first and Chinese version promptly after, restates the pattern the play itself followed in China a century earlier. The Mandarin version's translation team included the lyricist Liang Mang, and four casts of lovers alternated across the Beijing run.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch China-theatre map to argue that China received the play three times over, and remade it at each receiving?

An essay should use the map's rows as dated evidence and argue from their sequence. The story rows of 1903 and 1904 show a receiving that produced a tale, shorn of verse and shelved among stories of gods and spirits. The translation and production rows from 1921 to 1947 show a second receiving that produced an instrument, fought over by translators and directors as property of a national argument. The opera and adaptation rows, from the gezaixi version of 2001 to the Luo and Zhu clans of 2014, show a third receiving that produced local works with foreign plots. The parallel rows of 1954 and 1959 serve as the control case: a native legend performing the same cultural work without any import at all. An essay should also respect the map's grading, in which production rows rest on named companies and dates while the Geneva row rests on anecdote, since the difference between those strengths is itself part of the argument about how the reception was made.
