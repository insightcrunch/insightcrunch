A reader who buys a modern edition of Romeo and Juliet buys an argument. Five books dominate the field: the Arden Shakespeare in its second series form, edited by Brian Gibbons, and in its third series form, edited by René Weis; the Oxford Shakespeare, edited by Jill L. Levenson; the New Cambridge Shakespeare, edited by G. Blakemore Evans; and the Folger Shakespeare Library volume, edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine. All five print a text descended from the second quarto of 1599, yet no two of them print the same play. They differ in the readings they choose where the early witnesses disagree, in the stage directions they admit, in the weight and placement of their notes, in the story their introductions tell about what kind of work the tragedy is, and in the reader each book silently assumes. The verdict defended here is plain. The differences are not decoration around a stable core; they are decisions about evidence, and they decide what most readers take the play to say. A reader choosing among the modern editions is choosing among five defensible plays, and the choice should be made by use: the Folger for a first encounter, the Arden third series for study in depth, the Oxford for the play as a thing performed, the New Cambridge for the play's life on stage across four centuries, with Gibbons's Arden second series still unmatched on the sources from which the tragedy grew.

![Modern editions of Romeo and Juliet compared, Arden, Oxford, New Cambridge and Folger - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The stakes are easiest to see at the level of the single line. Every edition prints the Prologue's opening, "Two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue, line 1), and every edition prints Juliet's "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" (2.2.33). Beneath that agreement, the books diverge in hundreds of smaller places: a stage direction present in one and absent from another, a punctuation mark that redistributes the emphasis of a speech, a prose passage set as verse, a word glossed at length in one volume and passed over in silence by the next. None of the five editors invented these divergences. Each inherited two early texts that disagree with each other, the short first quarto of 1597 and the full second quarto of 1599, together with the First Folio text of 1623 that descends from the second quarto's line, and each editor built a reading text out of that inheritance by applying a policy. Comparing the modern editions as finished books, their policies, their apparatus and their audiences, is the only honest way to answer the question readers actually ask, which is not which text Shakespeare wrote but which book deserves a place on the desk.

## Five Modern Editions of Romeo and Juliet, Five Printed Texts

The common description of the play's text, a single work that editions merely reproduce with varying accuracy, fails at the first collation. The second quarto supplies the words for nearly every line in all five volumes, because it is the fullest early witness and the only one whose title page claims correction and augmentation. Yet an edited text is not a reprint. Each editor emends the copy-text where its readings seem corrupt, imports stage directions the copy-text lacks, repunctuates throughout, modernizes spelling, assigns speeches where the early books are ambiguous, and decides the layout of passages the early compositors set as prose or verse by the space on the page. The result is that the five volumes agree in the large and differ in the texture, and texture is where reading happens. A student who quotes Romeo's dying words, "Thus with a kiss I die" (5.3.120), quotes a line every witness supports. A student who describes how Juliet enters for the orchard scene, what the stage picture is when the Friar begins "The grey-eyed morn smiles on the frowning night" (2.3.1), or how the dawn parting in 3.5 is punctuated, describes an editorial construction that varies from book to book.

The construction begins before the first line, in the introduction each volume carries. Gibbons's introduction is a study of the tragedy's making, dense with the Italian novella tradition and Arthur Brooke's poem. Levenson's introduction gives the play a double life, as a text with two early forms and as a performance that has been continually remade. Evans's introduction is weighted toward the stage history of the play in England and America. Weis's introduction returns to the two quartos with a textual scholar's patience and a performer's ear. The Folger volume carries shorter front matter, an account of the playwright's life and language, and reserves its bulk for the text with facing explanation. A reader meets the editor's play before meeting the playwright's, and the meeting colours everything after. That is not a complaint about editing; it is the condition of reading a play that survives in no authorial manuscript. The comparison that follows takes the five books one by one, then sets their policies side by side on the questions that divide them: the authority granted to the first quarto, the rationale for the copy-text, the philosophy of annotation, and the reader each book is built to serve.

## Why Every Modern Edition Starts From the Second Quarto

The shared foundation deserves its own account, because the foundation is a choice and not a fact of nature. The second quarto of 1599 is roughly a fifth longer than the first quarto of 1597, and its title page announces that the text has been "newly corrected, augmented, and amended," a publisher's claim that the fuller book's own contents broadly honour. Its verse is more regular, its speeches fuller, its stage directions thinner and more literary, and its errors are the errors of a printing house working from difficult copy rather than the errors of memory. The first quarto, by contrast, is swift, theatrical and loose in its wording, with directions that name the performers' world, including the actor Will Kemp in a direction for the servants' scene. The systematic collation of the two books, passage by passage, is set out in [the comparison of Q1 against Q2 and what their differences reveal](/2026/04/10/q1-vs-q2-romeo-and-juliet-differences/), and its findings govern everything the modern editors do: the second quarto records the play nearer to the writing desk, the first nearer to the stage.

From that finding the editorial tradition drew a rule. W. W. Greg, the bibliographer whose 1950 essay "The Rationale of Copy-Text" codified the method of the New Bibliography, argued that an editor should take one early witness as copy-text to govern the accidentals of the edited text, its spelling and punctuation, while emending its substantive readings, the words themselves, wherever the evidence of the other witnesses or the sense demanded. For this play the second quarto was the inevitable copy-text under Greg's rule, because it stood closest to the author's papers in the prevailing account of its origin, printed from foul papers, the author's own draft. Every one of the five volumes compared here takes the second quarto as its base. The agreement ends there, because the five editors differ in how much of Greg's rationale they still believe, in how freely they emend, and in how much authority they allow the first quarto to exercise over the text they print. Those differences, not the shared base, are what a comparison of the modern editions actually compares.

## Brian Gibbons and the Arden Second Series Volume

Brian Gibbons edited the play for the Arden Shakespeare's second series in 1980, and his volume held the field as the standard scholarly edition for a generation. Its copy-text is the second quarto, handled in the orthodox Greg manner: the quarto's substantives stand unless Gibbons judges them erroneous, its accidentals are modernized, and readings from the first quarto and the Folio are admitted sparingly, each adoption argued in the textual notes. The edition's character, though, is set less by its text than by its introduction and commentary. Gibbons wrote the fullest account in any single volume of the tragedy's sources, tracing the story from the Italian novelle through Boaistuau and Painter to Brooke's long poem, and showing at each stage what the playwright kept, compressed and transformed. A reader who works through Gibbons learns how the play was made, and the commentary keeps that making in view, glossing the lovers' language against the Petrarchan conventions it inherits and revises.

The commentary is the volume's second strength. Arden second series notes sit at the foot of the page, and Gibbons fills them with the dense, allusive annotation the series then prized: verbal parallels, proverb lore, staging possibilities, and the history of earlier editorial opinion compressed into a running argument with the text. The effect is a book for the study rather than the rehearsal room. Its text of the orchard scene prints the second quarto's substance with minimal theatrical supplementation; its directions are chaste; its interest is in what the words carry. Where the first quarto offers a livelier direction or a variant reading, Gibbons records the variant and usually declines it, on the stated ground that the first quarto's authority is derivative, a performed reduction of the fuller text. That judgement was the consensus of its decade, and the volume embodies it with a consistency that makes the book a historical document as well as an edition: it shows exactly how the Greg tradition read this play at the tradition's confident height.

### Is the Gibbons edition superseded by the Arden third series?

No, not wholly. Weis's third series volume replaces Gibbons as the Arden text of record, with a fresher text, fuller performance commentary and a more generous hearing for the first quarto, but Gibbons remains the deepest single volume on the sources, and libraries keep both because their strengths barely overlap.

The limitation of the Gibbons volume is the obverse of its virtue. A reader who wants the play as an event in a theatre, the thing the first quarto imperfectly preserves, finds Gibbons's apparatus angled elsewhere. His stage history is brief beside his source study. His text gives the actor little help beyond the words, and his notes, for all their learning, address the reader of poetry more than the practitioner of scenes. None of this diminishes the book's achievement; it defines its use. Among the five volumes compared here, Gibbons is the edition of the study and the source hunter, the one that explains why the tragedy has the shape it has by displaying the materials from which the shape was cut. Its textual conservatism, the reluctance to let the first quarto speak, is the position against which its own successor in the same series defined itself, and that internal Arden argument is the clearest single thread in the modern editorial history of the play.

## René Weis and the Arden Third Series: The First Quarto Let Back In

René Weis edited the play for the Arden third series in 2012, and the volume is best read as a courteous, thorough refutation of its predecessor's textual temper. The copy-text remains the second quarto; Weis is no revolutionary, and his text differs from Gibbons's in a minority of readings. The change is in the standing granted to the first quarto. Weis came to the play from his celebrated parallel text edition of King Lear, a work built on the premise that two early texts can embody two authorial versions rather than an original and a corruption. In the Romeo and Juliet volume that premise is tempered but active. Weis treats the first quarto throughout as a witness with its own authority, theatrical in origin and possibly preserving, in places, an earlier state of the play than the second quarto's copy. He adopts a series of its readings and, still more visibly, its stage directions, so that the Arden third series text is fuller in its theatrical furniture than any earlier Arden text of the play.

The consequences reach the reader's eye. Where Gibbons prints the second quarto's spare directions and lets the dialogue imply the action, Weis supplies the movement the first quarto records: entrances clarified, positions specified, the physical business of the feast, the orchard and the tomb set down where the early stage book had it. At the dawn parting in 3.5, where the second quarto prints overlapping drafts of the lovers' exchange, the famous doublet in which Juliet's "It is the lark that sings so out of tune" (3.5.27) belongs to a passage the author seems to have written twice, Weis prints a chosen text with the rejected lines fully recorded and the problem explained as revision rather than error. His commentary, correspondingly, keeps performance in the foreground: notes address how a speech plays, what an actor must decide, where the verse asks for speed or stillness. The introduction retells the textual story with the first quarto promoted from delinquent copy to evidence, and the volume as a whole is the fullest demonstration available that a Greg copy-text and a generous eclecticism can coexist in one book.

### Is the Arden third series volume a revision of Gibbons or a new edition?

It is a new edition. Weis re-collated the early witnesses, rethought the copy-text's emendation passage by passage, rewrote the introduction around the two quartos and performance, and rebuilt the commentary; only the series format, the second quarto base and the play are inherited from Gibbons's volume of 1980.

The Weis volume is not beyond criticism. A text that admits first quarto directions and readings on a case by case basis invites the charge of eclecticism without a rule, and conservative textual scholars have asked by what principle a direction is theatrical gold in one place and theatrical dross in another. Weis's answer, given in his textual introduction, is that the principle is judgement informed by the whole pattern of the first quarto's evidence, and that an editor who refuses such judgement merely hides it. Whether that answer satisfies depends on the reader's theory of editing, and the disagreement is taken up in the adjudication below. As a reading book, the volume's claim is strong: it is the most complete single edition of the play in print, the one whose notes most often answer the question a serious reader is actually asking, whether that question is textual, theatrical or verbal.

## Jill Levenson's Oxford Edition and the Play in Performance

Jill L. Levenson edited the play for the Oxford Shakespeare in 2000, in the series shaped by Stanley Wells's general editorship, and her volume is the most balanced of the five: a second quarto text, a thorough textual introduction, and an account of the play that grants the study and the stage equal citizenship. Levenson had spent a career on the play's texts and its afterlife, and the introduction shows it. The two quartos receive one of the fullest treatments in any single-volume edition, setting out the first quarto's theatrical character and the second quarto's foul papers descent without forcing the evidence into a slogan. The stage history that follows is equally substantial, following the tragedy from the Restoration adaptations through Garrick's long reign to the modern period, and treating performance not as illustration of the text but as a second transmission line through which the play has reached its readers.

The Oxford text itself is a model of declared method. Levenson takes the second quarto as copy-text, emends it where she judges error, admits first quarto and Folio readings with recorded reasons, and prints a collation that lets a reader reconstruct the state of the early witnesses at every contested point. Her stage directions follow the Oxford house tendency of the Wells years, supplying the movement the dialogue implies in bracketed or recorded form, so that the printed page sits closer to a performed event than Gibbons's page does, without going as far as Weis in naturalizing the first quarto's theatrical directions. The commentary notes, at the foot of the page, are explanatory in the Oxford manner: generous with the history of words, staging practice and earlier criticism, and written in a prose that a strong undergraduate can follow without a tutor at the elbow. A full study of the editor and her volume is given in [the account of Jill Levenson and the Oxford edition](/2027/06/18/jill-levenson-oxford-edition/), and the present comparison confines itself to the book's place among its rivals.

### Does the Oxford edition print the first quarto in full?

No. Levenson's text is built on the second quarto, with first quarto readings and directions admitted selectively and the variants recorded in her collation and discussed in the introduction. A complete modern text of the first quarto stands in Lukas Erne's separate Cambridge volume in the Early Quartos series.

Set against its rivals, the Oxford volume's distinction is synthesis. Gibbons is deeper on sources, Weis fuller on text and performance combined, Evans richer in continuous stage history, the Folger more accessible to a beginner. Levenson alone gives every dimension of the play a substantial hearing inside one set of covers, and her introduction's double argument, that the tragedy is at once a poem of extraordinary verbal density and a script that has lived by being remade in theatres, is the most persuasive short account of the work's nature among the five. For a reader who will own one scholarly edition and read it whole, introduction included, the Oxford is the hardest of the five to fault, and its textual introduction remains the recommended first map of the two-quarto problem for a student meeting that problem as scholarship rather than as story.

## G. Blakemore Evans and the New Cambridge Edition

G. Blakemore Evans edited the play for the New Cambridge Shakespeare in 1984, and the volume was reissued in the series' updated form in 2003. Evans brought to the task the heaviest textual credentials of any editor in this comparison: he had edited the Riverside Shakespeare, the one volume complete works through which two generations of students met the canon, and his name carried the authority of the Greg tradition at its most learned. His Romeo and Juliet is accordingly the most conservative text among the scholarly four. The second quarto is copy-text; emendation is restrained; the first quarto is treated with respect as evidence and with reserve as authority, its readings admitted where the second quarto fails in sense and rarely elsewhere. The collation is full, the textual introduction exact, and the whole volume breathes the discipline of an editor who believed that an editor's first duty is not to improve the copy.

The New Cambridge format gave Evans a different emphasis from the Arden's. The series aimed its introductions at the play in the theatre and the study together, and Evans's introduction contains a long, careful account of the tragedy's stage history, from the first performances through the great nineteenth century revivals to the modern stage, alongside a reading of the play's structure and imagery. His commentary notes are briefer than the Arden's and pitched a degree lower, at the advanced student rather than the specialist: difficult phrases are glossed, sources indicated, staging questions raised where they matter, and the documentation of opinion left lighter. The updated edition of 2003 refreshed the account of performance and criticism while leaving Evans's text and its rationale intact, a sign of how completely the volume represents a settled position. Among the five books, the New Cambridge is the edition of the record: the one that tells a reader what has been done with this play, on stages and in studies, with the least editorial theatre of its own. Its conservatism is a genuine limitation for a reader who wants the first quarto's evidence weighed afresh; its steadiness is a genuine virtue for a reader who wants to know what the tradition, at its most careful, has made of the evidence.

## The Folger Edition of Mowat and Werstine: A Different Editorial Method

The Folger Shakespeare Library volume, edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine and first published in 1992, is the outlier among the five in design, price and method, and the differences are connected. The book is a mass market paperback built for the classroom and the general reader: the text stands on the right hand page, explanation on the left, with scene summaries, illustrations drawn from the Folger's collections, and essays on the playwright's life and language carried as front matter. Its notes gloss as a reader meets difficulty, word by word and line by line, without collation, without documentation of editorial history, and without presuming any Latin, any bibliography or any prior Shakespeare. For the reader the other four volumes silently exclude, the beginner, the Folger is not a simplified version of scholarship but a different instrument, and in classrooms across the English speaking world it is the edition through which the play is first read.

The method behind the friendliness is the most heterodox of the five. Werstine is the leading sceptic of his generation about the narratives on which Greg's rationale rests, and his published work argues that the category of foul papers, authorial draft copy supposedly standing behind the second quarto, is an editorial construction rather than a documented kind of manuscript, and that the stories editors tell about copy, of drafts and prompt books and memorial reconstructions, outrun anything the surviving evidence can prove. The Folger text of the play is accordingly built without the romance: the second quarto is taken as base because it is the fullest and earliest authoritative text, collated against the first quarto and the Folio, emended where the editors judge error, and presented with a textual note that states the facts of the witnesses without pretending to know the manuscript history behind them. The result is a text that differs from the Arden and Cambridge texts in a modest number of readings and in a larger number of directions and punctuation choices, each made by judgement and none justified by appeal to Shakespeare's own papers.

### Is the Folger text reliable enough to quote in an essay?

Yes, with a condition. Its text is edited by two leading textual scholars from the second quarto, collated against the first quarto and the Folio, and is accurate for quotation at school and undergraduate level; the condition is to name the edition cited, since its readings and line numbering differ in places from the scholarly series.

The Folger's limitation is the price of its clarity. A reader who wants to know why a reading stands, what the rejected witness reads, or how the editorial tradition has argued the point, finds no apparatus to consult: the decisions are present, the reasoning is in the editors' other writings. The introduction's refusal of the foul papers story can also mislead by omission, since a beginner given no story may assume there is no problem, where the scholarly volumes perhaps mislead by commission, giving a story neater than the evidence. Between those faults the Folger's is the more honest for its audience. The book completes the field of five by proving that an edition is an answer to a question about readers before it is an answer to a question about copy: its editors asked what a first reader needs, and built the book, price included, to that specification.

## Copy-Text on Trial: Greg's Rationale and Werstine's Objection

The deepest disagreement among the five volumes is not over any single reading but over the warrant for the shared foundation, and it deserves adjudication in the open. On one side stands the tradition codified by W. W. Greg, the bibliographer whose rationale of copy-text taught editors to distinguish the accidentals of a witness, which the copy-text governs, from its substantives, which the editor emends eclectically. Greg's method came with a history attached: for this play, the second quarto deserved its privilege because it was printed from the author's foul papers, and the first quarto deserved its subordination because it was a memorial reconstruction, a text rebuilt from actors' memories. Gibbons and Evans both work inside that history; their introductions retell it as the ground of their textual choices, and their editions' confidence in the second quarto is the confidence of editors who believe they know what kind of manuscript the printers used.

On the other side stands Werstine, co-editor of the Folger volume and author of a series of studies examining the evidentiary basis of the New Bibliography's categories. His charge is that no surviving manuscript answers to the description of foul papers as the tradition defined them, that the plays said to be printed from such copy do not behave as the theory predicts, and that the narrative of the good quarto from the desk and the bad quarto from the memory is a story editors invented and then cited as fact. The full trial of that narrative, with its Pollard origins and its modern audit, is conducted in [the study of memorial reconstruction and the bad quarto myth](/2026/04/24/memorial-reconstruction-bad-quarto-myth/), and the verdict there, that the category survives as possibility rather than proof, is the verdict this comparison adopts. The Folger volume is Werstine's dissent made practical: an edition that uses the second quarto while declining to certify its pedigree.

### Why do editors keep the second quarto as copy-text if the foul papers story is doubted?

Because the choice does not need the story. The second quarto is the fullest early text, its title page claims correction and augmentation, its errors are printing house errors, and collation shows the first quarto's pattern of agreement and drift fits descent from a fuller written text; the base survives the biography written for it.

The adjudication, then, runs in Werstine's favour on the history and against any editor who still prints the foul papers account as settled fact. It does not follow that the Greg tradition edited badly. Gibbons's and Evans's texts stand, reading by reading, almost everywhere a modern collator would put them, because the second quarto's substantive superiority is demonstrable from collation without any manuscript romance: where the first quarto is fuller it is usually fuller by theatrical expansion, where it differs in wording it differs as memory and performance differ from writing, and where it corrects the second quarto it does so at points any eclectic editor can find case by case. Greg's method, detached from Greg's history, survives as a discipline for keeping an edition's accidentals coherent, and Weis's volume shows the method at its most flexible, holding the second quarto as copy-text while hearing the first quarto as a witness rather than a suspect. The disagreement that matters for readers is therefore narrower than the scholarly quarrel suggests: all five editors print substantially the same words, and they divide over how much of the first quarto's theatrical evidence to admit into the text and how honestly to label the uncertainty that remains. On the second point the Folger and Weis, at opposite ends of the market, are the most candid of the five.

## How the Editions Treat the First Quarto

If the copy-text question is the field's shared foundation, the first quarto is its live fault line, and the five volumes distribute themselves along it in an order that predicts most of their other differences. Gibbons stands at the conservative end: the first quarto is a reported text, valuable for a handful of corrections and for its testimony about early staging, kept firmly outside the edited text except where the second quarto is unintelligible. Evans stands near him, a degree more courteous and no more generous. Levenson moves the boundary: her introduction argues the first quarto's case as a theatrical version with its own coherence, her text admits its evidence more often, and her collation gives its variants a prominence that lets a reader judge the case independently. Weis goes furthest among the editors of the second quarto text, treating the first quarto's directions as the best surviving evidence of the play in its first performances and importing them wholesale at many points, while adopting its readings where they compete on equal terms with the copy-text's. The Folger editors, characteristically, print a text that borrows the first quarto's clarity in directions and occasional readings while refusing the theoretical frame, neither reported text nor authorial draft, that the scholarly volumes argue about.

Alongside these five treatments stands a sixth fact that reframes them all: the first quarto is available as a book in its own right. Lukas Erne, whose study of the playwright as a literary dramatist argued that the early quartos were produced for readers as well as players and that the short texts have been maligned by a theory built to demote them, edited the first quarto of this play for the New Cambridge Shakespeare's Early Quartos series in 2007, printing it complete in modern spelling with its own introduction and notes. Erne's volume is the practical consequence of the revisionist case, and its existence changes the comparison among the other five. An editor of the second quarto no longer decides whether the first quarto shall be read; that decision has been taken elsewhere. The question for Gibbons's successors is only how much of the first quarto their own text will acknowledge, and on that question the history of the five volumes is a history of steadily increasing acknowledgement, from Gibbons's guarded citations to Weis's open adoption, that mirrors the wider discipline's reassessment of the short text across the same decades. The reassessment has a name and a brief. Erne's study of the playwright as a literary dramatist, published in 2003, compared the editorial handling of the short quartos across the canon and charged the Greg tradition with building its hierarchy on a circular proof: the short texts were demoted because they were short and derivative, and their shortness and drift were then cited as the evidence of their demotion. Against that circle Erne set the publication record itself, arguing that the quartos were produced for a market of readers as well as for the playhouse, and that a text printed to be read cannot be waved aside as a stenographic accident. Applied to this play, his case recasts the first quarto as a version, shorter by design or by occasion, whose differences from the second quarto are data about the work's early life rather than debris from its transmission. The five volumes register his argument at different depths: Weis absorbs it into practice, Levenson argues alongside it, the Folger's editors reach a neighbouring scepticism by Werstine's different road, and Gibbons and Evans stand as the position it was written against. A comparison of the editions that omitted the comparer's own book, Erne's edition of the first quarto, would miss the field's centre of motion, since every volume after 2007 was produced in a world where the short text could be bought, taught and quoted as a book in its own right.

The reader's interest in this fault line is practical. Stage directions shape the imagined play: an orchard scene whose directions come from the second quarto alone is a conversation in an undefined space, while the same scene in Weis's text, furnished from the first quarto, has a window, a wall and a geography. Readings shape it too, at the level of nuance rather than plot, since the first quarto's variants seldom change who does what and often change the colour of how it is said. An edition is therefore also a decision about which century's evidence, the writing desk's or the playhouse's, shall furnish the reader's inner stage, and the five volumes answer that decision across the full range of defensible answers. The comparison's verdict on the fault line follows the discipline's own movement: the first quarto's theatrical evidence is real evidence, an edition that admits it under record, as Weis and Levenson do, serves the reader better than one that quarantines it, and the Greg tradition's subordination of the short text is the one element of the older editions that has dated beyond defence.

## Annotation Compared: What the Notes Do to the Reading

Annotation is where an edition's imagined reader becomes visible, because a note exists only where an editor predicts a reader's need. The five volumes annotate the same lines at wildly different densities, and the pattern of their choices is a map of their audiences. The Arden volumes annotate most: Gibbons and Weis both write commentary that assumes a reader who wants the word's history, the parallel passages, the proverb, the staging problem and the state of scholarly opinion, and both are willing to spend a paragraph where a phrase will bear it. The Oxford annotates generously but with a schoolmaster's economy, explaining first and documenting second. The New Cambridge annotates least among the scholarly four, glossing hard words and clarifying references while declining to reproduce the history of opinion. The Folger annotates most frequently and most briefly, a gloss wherever a beginner might stumble, set on the facing page so that help arrives without a journey to the foot of the page or the back of the book.

Mercutio's bawdy shows the philosophies at work. His exchanges with Romeo in 2.4 run on puns that a modern reader can miss entire, and the editions part company over how much rescue to perform. The Arden commentary unpacks the wordplay at length, printing the double senses and letting the scene's wit recover its edge, at the cost of slowing the reader to a scholar's pace. The Oxford explains the principal jokes and leaves the lesser ones to inference. The New Cambridge glosses the words and moves on. The Folger, writing for classrooms that include young teenagers, explains plainly what can be explained and lets the rest pass in a decent brevity, a policy its editors defend as respect for the reader rather than prudery. None of these policies is an error. Each prices the same good, comprehension, against a different cost, interruption, and the right choice among them is a function of the reading being attempted: the student writing on Mercutio's language needs the Arden's fullness, the student meeting the scene for the first time needs the Folger's tact and speed, and the same person at different hours needs both.

### Do the editions modernize spelling and punctuation in the same way?

No. All five modernize spelling by house rule, but punctuation is editorial judgement exercised line by line: one volume's comma against another's colon redistributes a speech's pauses and emphasis, so the same speech can read as breathless in one edition and measured in another, with no early witness to appeal to.

The lesson of the annotation comparison is that notes are not a transparent window but a second text, and a reader follows an editor's attention as surely as the playwright's words. The volumes that annotate the sources teach the play as a made thing; the volumes that annotate the stage teach it as an enacted thing; the volume that annotates the vocabulary teaches it as a readable thing. A comparison that ranked annotation by bulk alone would crown the Arden and miss the point. The right question is what a given reading is for, and the annotation sections of the five books answer it more honestly than their title pages do.

## Introductions as Rival Readings of the Tragedy

An introduction is an edition's concealed essay on the play, and the five introductions compared here advance five distinguishable readings. Gibbons reads the tragedy backward into its materials: the introduction's centre of gravity is the novella tradition and Brooke's poem, and the play that emerges is a lyric transformation of inherited story, its famous speed and compression explained as the pressure of old narrative under new dramatic form. Levenson reads the play outward into its performances: her introduction's centre is the double transmission, two early texts and four centuries of staging, and the play that emerges is a work that has always existed in versions, completed afresh by each generation of players. Evans reads the play through its stage biography with a historian's patience, and the work that emerges is a classic in the literal sense, a text whose meaning includes its accumulated performances. Weis reads the play forward from its two quartos, treating the textual evidence as the record of a work that existed in more than one state from the beginning, and the tragedy that emerges is restless, theatrical, aware of its own performance in every line.

The Folger volume, true to its design, declines the concealed essay: its front matter teaches the reader how to read the language rather than what to think of the play, and its refusal is itself a reading, the position that the tragedy needs mediation at the level of words, not of interpretation. Set side by side, the five positions map the main roads of modern criticism of the play, source study, performance history, textual revision and plain reading, and a student who reads two introductions against each other has begun the discipline's real work, which is not the accumulation of opinions but the comparison of methods. The early editors whose choices all five inherit, the scene locations, the act divisions tidied, the text conflated from quarto and Folio across two centuries, are traced in [the history of editing the play from Rowe to Malone](/2026/06/05/editing-rowe-to-malone-history/), and the modern volumes are best understood as that history's continuation rather than its end.

## The Text on the Page: Layout, Lineation and Scene Headings

Beyond readings and notes, the five books differ as physical arrangements of type, and the differences steer the eye. All five print scene locations at the head of scenes, "Verona. A public place," "Capulet's orchard," that no early witness supplies in that form; the locations descend from the eighteenth century editors, and their presence in every modern volume is the quietest proof that the play readers know is a composite built across four hundred years. All five print the act and scene divisions the Folio regularized, and all five modernize the speech prefixes into uniform names. Beneath that shared furniture the layouts part. The Arden and Oxford and Cambridge volumes print their commentary at the foot of the text page, so that eye and note share a single field and the reading rhythm is vertical, line to gloss and back. The Folger divides the opening instead: text on the right, explanation on the left, in short entries keyed to the lines they face, with a summary of the coming scene at its head. The Arden page is the densest object among the five, the Folger the airiest, and the physical difference encodes the editorial one, density being the form scholarly completeness takes and air the form welcome takes.

Lineation divides the books more subtly. Where the early texts set verse as prose, as the second quarto does in stretches where its compositors saved space, every editor must decide afresh whether the passage is verse mislined or genuine prose, and the decisions are recorded in the scholarly collations but invisible in the Folger, whose readers receive the settled layout without the archaeology. Shared lines, the half lines that two speakers divide between them in quick exchange, are arranged by all five with the indentation that marks the join, yet the assignment of fragments to verse or prose, and the decision of what counts as a fragment, shifts in small places from volume to volume, altering the counted shape of scenes. Punctuation is the most pervasive invisible divergence: no early witness punctuates as any modern reader expects, every editor repunctuates every speech, and two editions can give the same words different tempi by comma and colon alone. A comparison of the books at this level teaches the necessary humility of quotation: a line cited from the play is a line cited from an edition, and the edition's name belongs in the citation's conscience even where scholarly convention leaves it out of the reference.

## One Passage in Five Editions: The Dawn Parting

The opening of 3.5, the lovers' dawn parting after the wedding night, concentrates every editorial problem in the comparison into forty lines, and walking the passage through the five books shows what each policy costs and buys. The second quarto, the copy-text for all, prints at this point one of its famous duplications: within the exchange that begins "Wilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day" (3.5.1), the quarto preserves overlapping versions of the lovers' debate over the lark and the nightingale, as though the author's first attempt and his revision had both reached the printing house and been set in sequence. Every editor must choose a single strand for the reading text and account for the rejected lines somewhere. The first quarto, meanwhile, gives the scene a firmer theatrical frame than the second, with Juliet placed at her window and the parting's physical descent made explicit, where the fuller text leaves the staging to inference.

Gibbons prints a chosen strand, records the rejected lines in his collation, and discusses the duplication briefly as a printing problem, an authorial revision imperfectly executed in the copy. The reader of Gibbons receives a clean scene and a footnote's worth of archaeology. Evans proceeds alike, with the New Cambridge economy: the strand chosen, the alternative noted, the stage history of the scene given more room than its textual history. Levenson prints the strand and uses her commentary to stage the scene, remarking the window, the rope ladder's implied geography, and the long performance tradition of the parting, so that the textual problem becomes one element in a scene understood as theatre. Weis gives the doublet the fullest hearing of the five: his textual discussion treats the duplicated lines as the author's revision caught in the act, prints his chosen text with the rejected version's claims argued rather than dismissed, and furnishes the scene with the first quarto's directions, so that his reader sees both what the author rewrote and how the company played it. The Folger prints the strand, clean, without visible trace of the duplication, its facing notes busy with the lark and nightingale imagery and the words a beginner might miss. Each treatment is rational. They are rational answers to different questions, and a reader who has seen the passage in all five books understands the modern editions better than a reader who has studied any one of them for a year: the scholarly four differ in how much of the evidence they display, the Folger differs in declining to display it, and display, not accuracy, is the axis of the whole comparison.

## Emendation in Practice: Where the Five Texts Actually Part

Abstract talk of policy becomes concrete in the classes of place where the five texts visibly diverge, and a reader who learns the classes can predict each book's behaviour at a new crux. The first class is the supplied stage direction. The second quarto is sparing of directions; the first quarto and the Folio are fuller in different places; every editor prints more directions than any single witness contains, and the volumes differ by source and by candour. Weis draws directions from the first quarto most freely and records the borrowing. Levenson supplies the movement the dialogue implies, marking her additions in the Oxford manner. Gibbons and Evans keep closest to the copy-text's spareness, adding only what staging strictly requires. The Folger prints a full theatrical set of directions with no record of their mixed parentage. The second class is speech assignment. At a handful of points the early texts give a line to different speakers or divide an exchange differently, and each editor's choice silently reassigns wit, blame or pathos; the scholarly volumes record the alternatives in collation, the Folger settles the matter invisibly.

The third class is the layout of prose and verse, settled at the musicians' scene, the servants' scenes and stretches of clowning where the second quarto's compositors set verse as prose to save their pages. Editors relineate by metre and by parallel, and their lineations agree in the large and differ in detail, which is why the same scene can occupy different line numbers in different books. The fourth class is punctuation and the rhetoric it creates: the lovers' soliloquies reach modern readers wearing exclamation marks and question marks that no early witness distributes as the editions do, and one volume's Juliet, punctuated urgently, sounds younger and more frantic than another's, punctuated in balanced periods. The fifth class is the small substantive variant, the word level disagreement between quarto and quarto, where the second quarto's reading is nonsense or flat and the first quarto's, or a later editor's conjecture, restores sense. Such places are few in this play beside the great cruxes of Hamlet or Lear, and all five editors resolve most of them alike; where they differ, the pattern follows the fault line already mapped, the Greg disciplined volumes staying with the copy-text longest, Weis and Levenson reweighing the first quarto's claim, the Folger choosing the reading that reads best and saying nothing. Emendation in practice is thus a small set of recurring decisions wearing different degrees of disclosure, and the comparison of editions is finally a comparison of disclosure: what each book lets its reader see of the road not taken.

## The Ending in Five Editions

The last scene tests every policy at once. The second quarto's close gives the Friar's long explanation, the Prince's judgement and the final couplet over the bodies, and the five editions print that architecture intact. Around it the witnesses diverge in sequence and detail: the first quarto orders the discoveries and entrances of the closing movement differently, brings its witnesses before the Prince in its own order, and compresses the explanations, so that its ending plays faster and rawer than the fuller text's. No modern editor adopts the first quarto's sequence for the reading text; the Greg discipline and the evidence alike forbid it. What divides the volumes is the record. Weis's textual notes give the first quarto's ending its fullest hearing, setting out the alternative sequence and its theatrical logic, the speed of a company that had played the catastrophe into a shape of its own. Levenson stages the close in her commentary, attending to how the discoveries accumulate in performance and how directors have handled the crowded tomb. Gibbons records the variants with his usual economy and passes to the sources of the final speeches. Evans keeps the record brief and the stage history long. The Folger prints the close clean, its facing notes given to the Prince's language of judgement and the families' reconciliation, and its reader never learns that the scene was ever arranged otherwise.

The state of the ending across the five books is the comparison in epitome. The words are the same words, in the same order, saying the same reconciliation over the same bodies, and the books are five different books: one that shows the reader an alternative ending's evidence and argues it down, one that stages the received ending, one that sources it, one that historicizes it, one that simply hands it over with help at the difficult words. Nothing in the scene is corrupt, nothing needs rescue, and still the editorial temperaments diverge, because an edition's work is not exhausted by fixing error. It extends to everything the witnesses leave open, and the witnesses leave open more than any single volume can say. A reader who grasps that has grasped why the comparison of editions is a branch of criticism and not a branch of shopping.

## Reading the Apparatus: Collations, Sigla and Textual Notes

The scholarly four carry, at the foot of the page or in a separate gathering, the apparatus that distinguishes an edition from a reading text, and a comparison of the books should teach the apparatus's use, since it is the part of the volumes most often bought and least often read. The collation is a compressed record of disagreement among the witnesses, keyed by line number, in which the lemma, the reading printed, is followed by the rejected readings with their sigla, the shorthand names of the early books: Q1 and Q2 for the quartos, F1 for the Folio, and a tail of editors' names for conjectures adopted or worth recording. A single line of collation can restore an entire scene's textual history, showing where the Folio followed the third quarto, where the first quarto stands alone, and where some eighteenth century editor first printed the word the modern page inherits. The Arden volumes print the fullest collations among the five for this play's purposes, Weis's being the most current; the Oxford's is complete and clearly set; the New Cambridge's is exact and more compact; the Folger prints none, its textual note confining itself to the principles of the text and a handful of famous cruxes.

The textual notes, or commentary on the text, are the apparatus's discursive half, and the Arden third series makes the most of them: at the dawn doublet, at disputed assignments, at the places where the first quarto's directions enter the text, Weis argues his decisions in prose a reader can contest, which is the highest service an apparatus performs. Gibbons's textual commentary is terser and more magisterial, the verdicts of a settled tradition. Levenson writes her textual introduction as a narrative of the witnesses and distributes her argument between it and the collation, a design that rewards the reader who starts at the front of the book. Evans's textual notes carry the Riverside tradition of comprehensive record in miniature. The practical counsel of the comparison is simple: a reader who never opens the apparatus owns half an edition. The notes answer the questions the text raises; the apparatus answers the questions the notes presuppose; and the difference between the scholarly volumes and the Folger is, in the end, precisely the presence of that second answering layer, priced into the scholarly books and waived in the classroom one.

## The Complete Works Behind the Single Volumes

Each of the five books also stands in the shadow of a complete works, and the relationship matters because many readers meet the play inside a collected volume rather than a single edition. Evans's authority descends from the Riverside Shakespeare, the complete works he edited, whose text of the play rests on the same second quarto discipline as his New Cambridge volume and whose annotations served the undergraduate market the Folger later contested. The Oxford single volume descends from the Oxford Complete Works of 1986, edited by Stanley Wells and Gary Taylor, the project famous for printing two texts of King Lear on the theory of authorial revision; Levenson's Romeo and Juliet carries that project's performance centred spirit into a single play volume, without a second text to print, since the case for revision in this play's transmission has never persuaded as the Lear case did. The Arden third series volume belongs to a series whose complete works gathering appeared under the Arden name, and Weis's edition was built to the series standard that project set. The Folger text exists beyond its paperback as part of the Folger Shakespeare Library's digital texts, freely published for readers and teachers, which makes the Folger the only one of the five whose edited text circulates as the institution's public gift rather than solely as a commodity.

The complete works connection explains a feature of the single volumes that puzzles readers who compare them: their line numbering does not quite agree. Each project numbers the play's lines by its own lineation decisions, counting prose by lines of type and verse by metrical lines according to its own divisions, so that a reference exact in the Arden may be a line or two adrift in the Oxford. The disagreement is trivial in size and large in meaning: the line number, the most basic unit of scholarly address, is itself an editorial artifact. A reader citing the play does well to cite act, scene and line and to know that the numbers travel with the edition, a discipline the comparison of five books teaches better than any single book can. It also explains why the five texts, for all their shared copy-text, cannot be interchanged in a seminar room without friction: each is a self consistent system, text, numbering, notes and introduction fitted to each other, and mixing them mixes the systems.

## The Institutions Behind the Books

Editions are also institutional acts, and the five volumes carry the characters of the houses that made them. The Arden Shakespeare, the oldest of the series represented, built its reputation across the twentieth century on the single play volume with a long introduction and dense commentary, and its third series, under general editors including Richard Proudfoot, Ann Thompson and David Scott Kastan, renewed that model with performance aware introductions and freshly collated texts, of which Weis's volume is a characteristic product. The Oxford Shakespeare made its name as a project with the complete works of 1986, edited by Wells and Taylor, whose willingness to print two Lear texts announced a house philosophy hospitable to versioning and to the theatre; Levenson's single volume is that philosophy applied to a play whose two texts resist the Lear solution. The New Cambridge Shakespeare, launched in the 1980s under Philip Brockbank's general editorship, defined itself against Arden density with a cleaner page, a stage centred introduction and a student friendly commentary, a format Evans's volume exemplifies and the series' updating programme later refreshed. The Folger Shakespeare Library, the great Washington collection, publishes its editions as an act of public scholarship, priced and designed for the widest readership and extended, in the Folger's digital texts, to readers who will never buy the book.

These institutional characters explain the volumes' disagreements better than any tale of individual temperament. An Arden editor inherits the obligation of completeness and writes commentary to discharge it. An Oxford editor inherits a project that treats performance as evidence and writes an introduction to match. A Cambridge editor inherits a format promise, clarity and stage history, and keeps it. The Folger editors inherit a library's mission to put sound texts in common hands and refuse, on principle, to let the mission wait on the resolution of scholarly quarrels. Even the famous disagreement over the first quarto maps onto the houses: the series that sells completeness absorbs the new evidence fastest, the series built on a mission of access absorbs it silently, and the conservative tradition holds its line longest in the house whose editor embodied it. A reader choosing among the modern editions is therefore choosing among institutions as much as among editors, which is why the books keep their characters across changes of editor, and why the Arden argument between Gibbons and Weis, the sharpest in this comparison, happened inside one house across one generation.

## Which Edition for Which Reader: A Verdict by Use

The practical question the brief for any comparison must answer is which book a given reader should buy, borrow or assign, and the answer follows from the policies, not from a league table. For the first time reader, the school student and the playgoer preparing for a performance, the Folger wins without contest. Its facing page help removes the friction that kills first readings, its price respects a student's means, its summaries keep the plot's machinery visible, and its text, whatever the apparatus lacks, is the work of two of the field's leading textual scholars. The beginner who finishes the Folger has read the tragedy; the beginner who stalls in an Arden commentary has read about it. The Folger's proper sequel, for a reader whose appetite grows, is the Oxford, whose introduction will then teach the two text problem the Folger declined to narrate.

For the undergraduate writing essays and the teacher preparing classes, the Oxford and the New Cambridge divide the honours by temperament. Levenson's volume is the better single purchase for most: its introduction is the most complete short account of the play's texts, performances and meanings, its commentary supports quotation hunting and close reading alike, and its textual discussion equips a student to write about the quartos without embarrassment. Evans's volume serves the teacher of the play's stage life, its introduction a compact history of performance that turns productions into evidence, and its lighter annotation respects a reader who wants the text to keep moving. For the actor and the director, Weis's Arden third series is the working book: its text carries the fullest stage directions, its commentary speaks to playable choices, and its respect for the first quarto means its stage picture descends from the earliest performances rather than from editorial caution. An acting company that can afford a second book should add Erne's first quarto, the script the earliest players may have used, whose shorter, swifter shape has repeatedly tempted modern companies.

For the scholar, the answer is a shelf rather than a book. Weis for the fullest modern commentary and the most engaged textual intelligence; Levenson for the synthesis and the stage record; Gibbons, still, for the sources, since no successor has matched his account of Brooke and the novelle; Evans for the discipline of the conservative text and the Riverside lineage behind it; the Folger for teaching and for the methodological provocation its editors' scepticism represents. The scholar's shelf is completed by Erne's first quarto, the volume that converted a century of subordination into a standing invitation to read the play twice. For the reader who returns to the play across a lifetime, the comparison recommends a sequence rather than a choice: the Folger for the first reading's momentum, the Oxford for the second reading's understanding, Weis for the third reading's depth, Gibbons whenever the making of the tragedy is the question, and Evans whenever its stage life is. Editions, in that view, are not rivals to be eliminated but instruments to be taken up in the order in which a reader's questions ripen, and the shelf that holds two or three of them is not indecision but equipment; the five books were built for different hours of a life spent with the play, and every one of those hours is real. What no reader should do is treat any one of the five as the play itself. The editions are five instruments for reading a work that survives in two early forms and no manuscript; the reader who knows which instrument is in hand reads better than the reader with the finest instrument used blindly, and that knowledge, more than any single recommendation, is what a comparison of the modern editions exists to give.

## What the Disagreement Among Editions Reveals

The comparison's final lesson concerns the play rather than its editors. A work whose text were simple would have simple editions; the fivefold diversity of these volumes is the documentary trace of a genuinely double inheritance, a tragedy that reached print once from the stage's side and once from the study's, and has carried both births ever since. The cliché that treats the text as one, a fixed poem that editions copy with greater or lesser fidelity, dissolves under the comparison: what the centuries actually transmit is a family of texts, and the modern editor's work is one more act in the family's long negotiation between the written and the performed. Each of the five books is that negotiation conducted under a different flag, Greg's discipline, Werstine's scepticism, Erne's revisionism, the classroom's need, and the reader who understands the negotiation owns the play in a way no single edition can confer.

That is also the answer to the suspicion that textual scholarship is pedantry interposed between a reader and a masterpiece. The pedantry is the masterpiece's biography. Mercutio's dying curse, "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1), reaches every modern reader through quarto, Folio, eighteenth century conflation and five modern policies, and arrives intact, which is the real marvel the editions document: not that the text is unstable, but that so much survives the instability, carried by editors who disagreed about nearly everything except the duty to carry it. The five books on the desk are five arguments about how the carrying should be done. Choosing among them is not a preliminary to reading the play. It is the first act of reading it.

## What a Reader Pays: Price, Format and the Life of These Books

The five volumes occupy four distinct price worlds, and the pricing is editorial policy by other means. The Folger is a mass market paperback priced for a student buying a set text with term money, and its price is a deliberate act of the Folger Shakespeare Library's mission: the institution subsidizes access by design, accepting a thin apparatus as the cost of a book that can be assigned to a class of thirty without a bookshop negotiation. The scholarly four are trade paperbacks priced for the individual buyer and the library, the Arden and Oxford and Cambridge volumes costing several times the Folger's price, with hardback library bindings above them for institutions that shelve for permanence. The price gap buys identifiable goods: collation, textual argument, the history of opinion, paper and binding that survive annotation, and introductions written as contributions to scholarship rather than as gateways. Whether the goods are worth the multiple depends, once again, on the reading in view; the comparison's point is that price in this field is never mere pricing, since each house has decided what an edition is for and set the price where that purpose can be sustained.

Format extends the same logic across the books' physical lives. The Arden volume is built to be written in, its margins and its commentary inviting the pencil; generations of Gibbons copies survive annotated into palimpsests of successive courses. The Oxford travels between the seminar and the general reader's shelf, issued both in the Oxford Shakespeare's scholarly dress and in the World's Classics paperback line that carries Levenson's text to readers who will never open a collation. The New Cambridge's cleaner page suits the lecture hall and the teacher's desk copy, marked for passages rather than for argument. The Folger, cheapest and most handled, is the volume most often replaced, its classroom life measured in school years; the Folger's publishers answer the attrition with the institution's digital texts, which carry the same edited words without a spine to break. A complete comparison of the editions must count these material facts among the evidence, because books are read as objects before they are read as arguments, and the five objects preach their purposes at first touch: density, balance, clarity, welcome. The books' second lives obey the same economics. Superseded scholarly volumes persist in libraries and the secondhand trade, where Gibbons now circulates at prices the Folger would recognize, so that the source hunter's classic has become, by the market's irony, a bargain; the classroom Folger, by contrast, is bought new in cohorts and discarded in cohorts, its cheapness renewed each term. A comparison that ignored these circulations would miss how the field actually reproduces itself: through syllabi, library standing orders and the secondhand shelf, the five editions keep finding the readers their makers designed them for, and keep missing the others.

## The Screen and the Single Text: Digital Forms of the Editions

The editions also exist beyond their bindings, and their digital afterlives extend the comparison onto ground where the differences sharpen. The Folger's texts, published digitally by the Folger Shakespeare Library without charge, give any reader with a screen the Mowat and Werstine text, searchable and portable, though stripped of the paperback's facing page theatre of help; the words travel, the pedagogy stays in the book. The scholarly editions circulate digitally through library platforms and collected databases, where their introductions and notes serve readers who may never learn which physical volume they are reading from, a detachment that quietly dissolves the books' identities into the series brands. Against these authorized digital forms stands the play's unedited digital multitude: the countless websites and documents that print a text of the tragedy with no editor named, most of them descending, through layers of copying, from nineteenth century texts or from the Folio tradition, modernized irregularly, punctuated by unknown hands, and innocent of the second quarto's authority except by inheritance. A quotation lifted from such a source is a quotation from an anonymous edition, and its readings may be nobody's considered choice at all.

The digital field therefore repeats the print field's lesson at higher speed. Where an edited text names its editor, its copy-text and its principles, a reader can weigh its choices; where no name stands behind the words, the words carry no warranty. The five volumes compared in this article are, among other things, five warranties, and their digital forms preserve the warranty exactly to the degree that they preserve the edition's identity. The Folger digital text keeps its editors' names on its title; the library platforms keep the series and editor attached to the scholarly texts; the anonymous multitude keeps nothing. A comparison that began with five books on a desk ends with a principle for every form the play can take: the text of Romeo and Juliet is always somebody's edition, and reading well begins with knowing whose.

## The Prologues in Five Editions

The two Chorus speeches offer a compact second test of the five policies, small enough to hold entire and rich enough to divide the field. The Prologue sonnet, "Two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue, line 1), stands in both quartos and the Folio; the second Chorus, introducing Act 2, stands in the quartos and is absent from the Folio, a witness disagreement that every editor resolves the same way in the text, printing the speech from the second quarto, and differently in the commentary, where the Folio's silence becomes evidence. For Levenson the missing speech belongs to the play's theatrical history, one of the places where the Folio's copy shows the play as a going concern in a playhouse that no longer needed a Chorus to bridge its acts. For Gibbons the speeches belong to the poem's formal architecture, sonnets framing a tragedy saturated with sonnet thinking, and his notes bind the Prologue to the lovers' shared sonnet at their meeting and to the Petrarchan economy of the whole. Weis, attentive to both frames, treats the Chorus as the author's own stage manager and prints the second speech with its quarto authority argued. Evans records the facts with his usual brevity and lets the stage history carry the interest. The Folger glosses the sonnet's terms for a first reader, households, dignity, civil blood, and asks nothing about witnesses at all.

The divergence instructs because the text, for once, barely does. Where the witnesses agree, the editions still differ, in the weight of attention they pay and the story they hang on the agreed words, which proves that an edition's character lies as much in its commentary's choices as in its text's. A reader comparing only the printed lines of the five volumes would pronounce them near identical across the Prologues and miss the five different accounts of what a Chorus is: a formal device in a lyric tragedy, a piece of playhouse machinery, an authorial signature, a datum of transmission, a comprehension hurdle. The comparison of editions is therefore not finished when the collation is done. It extends to everything the editor elects to say, and the Prologues, fourteen lines at a time, display the election in miniature.

## Replaced, Updated, Reissued: The Volumes' Second Lives

The five books also differ in how their houses maintain them, and the maintenance models are a final axis of comparison. The Arden model is replacement: a series volume is commissioned afresh from a new editor when the field has moved, and the predecessor is retired to the library shelf, which is how Gibbons's 1980 volume, standard for thirty years, gave way to Weis's in 2012 without a word of Gibbons being revised. The model honours scholarship's progress and wastes nothing but continuity; a citation of Arden across the divide names two different editions of the play. The Cambridge model, by contrast, is the update: the New Cambridge updating programme returned Evans's volume to print in 2003 with its text and rationale intact and its accounts of criticism and performance refreshed, wagering that a sound edition ages in its surroundings faster than in its text. The wager is itself an editorial philosophy, conservative about words and liberal about context, and it produced the oddity of a twenty first century volume whose textual heart beats with 1984.

The Oxford volume represents a third model, endurance without event: Levenson's edition has neither been replaced nor formally updated, circulating in the Oxford Shakespeare's dress and in the World's Classics paperback, its authority maintained by the standing of its argument rather than by revision. The Folger represents a fourth: continuous stewardship, in which the institution keeps the text in print, refreshes the surrounding materials across printings and carries the edition into digital form under its own imprint, treating the volume less as a scholar's monument than as a public utility under permanent maintenance. Each model prices a different risk. Replacement risks the loss of a great predecessor's virtues, and the Arden's readers did lose Gibbons's source study from the current volume when Weis replaced him. Updating risks embalming a superseded text inside fresh surroundings. Endurance risks quiet obsolescence. Stewardship risks the flattening of scholarship into service. The reader who understands the models reads the volumes' dates correctly: not as rankings of merit, but as the signatures of four theories about how knowledge of a play should be kept alive.

## Bibliographies, Further Reading and the Wider Shelf

Every edition closes by sending its reader elsewhere, and the sending is a final signature. The scholarly volumes carry select bibliographies that function as dated maps of the field: Gibbons's bibliography is a portrait of the play's scholarship as it stood in 1980, rich in source studies and the older textual learning, innocent of the performance turn and the first quarto's rehabilitation that would follow; Weis's, a generation later, charts a field reorganized around performance, versioning and the book trade. Levenson's reading lists lean toward the stage record and the play's afterlife; Evans's toward the textual and theatrical documentation his introduction practices. To read the four bibliographies in sequence is to watch the discipline change its mind about what matters, and a student who cites from them without noting their dates inherits their blind spots along with their guidance. The Folger closes differently, with a short further reading list chosen for accessibility and a guide to the Folger's own resources, an honest signal that its reader's next book is likelier to be another play than a monograph.

Beyond the five stands a wider shelf the comparison should acknowledge, since many readers meet the play in books outside the field entirely. Critical editions bind the edited text to a dossier of sources and selected criticism, serving the essay writer who needs Brooke's poem and the landmark essays between one pair of covers. Educational publishers issue classroom texts with activities and glossaries, built to examination specifications and innocent of apparatus by design. Collected works of every scale, from the Riverside lineage to single volume modern spelling texts for the general reader, carry the play in texts of varying pedigree, some freshly edited, some inherited from earlier projects. None of these displaces the five compared here, but each competes with them for a real reader's hour, and each confirms the field's central fact from a new angle: the play reaches its readers only through mediation, and the choice of mediator, scholarly, pedagogical or anonymous, is the first interpretation every reading obeys. The five volumes earn their comparison not by exhausting the shelf but by representing its principled best, the books whose mediations are argued, signed and open to audit.

## The Witnesses' Faces: Facsimiles, Photographs and Illustrations

The volumes also differ in what they show, and the visual matter is evidence of a kind. The scholarly editions reproduce the early books' faces: title pages of the quartos appear in the textual discussions, so that a reader can see the first quarto's boast of performance and the second quarto's promise of correction in the original lettering, and the Folger volume, drawn from a library that holds early copies of both quartos among its treasures, illustrates its pages with images from the collection, early title pages, pages of the early texts, and the material world of the book trade the play passed through. Production photographs perform the same office for the stage tradition: the volumes weighted toward performance, Levenson's and Evans's above all, print images of famous productions, so that the reader sees the balconies, feasts and tombs that successive centuries built, and the stage history in the introduction acquires faces. Weis's commentary, though its page is typographically austere in the Arden manner, keeps the visual imagination fed by its attention to what scenes look like when played. The Folger scatters its illustrations through the text itself, placing images beside the scenes they illuminate, portraits, places and objects that give a first reader's imagination its furniture. Gibbons's volume, truest to its decade, is the least illustrated and the most verbal, its world built from parallel passages rather than pictures. Even the pictures, then, argue: an edition that shows the quartos teaches transmission, an edition that shows productions teaches performance, an edition that shows objects teaches a beginner's Verona, and an edition that shows nothing teaches that the words are enough. The comparison's five books divide along this axis as faithfully as along any other.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch editions table, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch editions table sets the five major modern editions of the play side by side by editor, series, copy-text, treatment of the first quarto, annotation philosophy and intended reader. Its pattern is unanimity at the foundation and diversity everywhere else: every row rests on the second quarto of 1599, yet no two rows produce the same book, because each editor emends, annotates, introduces and prices the shared base for a different reader and under a different theory of evidence. The table exists to make that pattern visible at a glance, so that a choice among editions can be made from the policies themselves rather than from habit, price or the prestige of a series name. The table's discipline is comparison without coronation: it ranks nothing, because the comparison's finding is that rank depends on use, and a table that crowned one edition for every purpose would falsify the evidence its own columns display. Its rows can nevertheless be read as verdicts by any reader who knows the purpose in view, which is why the article's recommendations are stated by reader type immediately after it.

### Q: Who edited the Arden Shakespeare's second series and third series volumes of the play?

Brian Gibbons edited the second series volume, published in 1980, and René Weis edited the third series volume, published in 2012. Gibbons was a scholar of the play's sources and of Renaissance narrative, and his volume carries the fullest account of Brooke's poem and the Italian novella tradition in any single edition. Weis came to the play from his parallel text edition of King Lear and from a career spent on the relations among early texts, and his volume re-collated the witnesses, rebuilt the commentary around performance and the two quartos, and replaced Gibbons's book entire rather than revising it, in the Arden pattern of generational replacement. The pairing also frames the volumes' internal argument: the Arden name promises continuity while the Arden practice delivers replacement, and the two editors, who never collaborated, conduct across thirty years the sharpest single debate in the modern editing of the play, over the standing of the first quarto and the duties an editor owes to performance.

### Q: How does the page layout of the Folger edition differ from the Arden, Oxford and Cambridge volumes?

The Folger prints the text on the right hand page and its explanation on the facing left page, in short entries keyed to the lines beside them, with a summary before each scene and illustrations throughout. The Arden, Oxford and New Cambridge volumes print text and commentary on the same page, the notes gathered at the foot of the page beneath the lines they explain, with collation entries interleaved or appended. The practical difference is the reading rhythm each layout imposes: the Folger reader glances sideways for help without leaving the scene, while the scholarly reader moves vertically between line and documented note, slower, with the evidence of the early witnesses kept in view. The layouts also differ in what they do with uncertainty: the scholarly page surrounds a doubtful line with the record of its doubt, while the Folger page presents the settled line and reserves its space for the next difficulty, a distribution of attention that beginners experience as kindness and scholars as silence.

### Q: Which of the compared editions prints the fullest textual commentary, and what does a textual commentary record?

The Arden third series volume, edited by René Weis, prints the fullest textual commentary among the five. A textual commentary records the editor's reasoning at the places where the edited text departs from its copy-text or where the witnesses disagree: which reading was chosen, which were rejected, what the first quarto, the second quarto and the Folio each print, and what argument from sense, metre, staging or transmission settled the choice. Weis argues such places in discursive prose, notably at the dawn parting's duplicated lines in 3.5, where the second quarto preserves overlapping drafts; Gibbons's commentary is terser, Levenson's divides its argument between introduction and collation, and the Folger prints no textual commentary at all. The commentary's presence or absence is the surest single test of an edition's ambitions: a volume that argues its text can be cross examined, and a volume that prints no argument asks to be trusted, which is why the scholarly four all carry the apparatus in some form and why its bulk is a feature, not an accident of pricing.

### Q: How does René Weis's Arden edition differ from Brian Gibbons's in its treatment of the first quarto?

Weis grants the first quarto a standing that Gibbons withholds. Gibbons treats the 1597 text as a derivative, reported version of the fuller play, cites it for occasional corrections and stage evidence, and admits its readings rarely and under argument. Weis treats it throughout as a witness with its own authority, theatrical in origin and possibly preserving an earlier state of the play in places, adopts a series of its stage directions wholesale, admits its readings where they compete evenly with the copy-text's, and argues its case at length in his textual introduction. The copy-text is the same in both volumes; the difference is the hearing the shorter witness receives, and it changes the printed play's stage picture most of all. The difference shows in small furnishings as well: Weis's text gives the orchard scene a window and a geography drawn from the first quarto's directions, where Gibbons lets the dialogue imply the space, so that two readers of the same series picture different stages at the play's most famous moment.

### Q: What does Jill Levenson's Oxford introduction argue about the play in performance?

Levenson's introduction argues that performance is a second transmission line for the play, coequal with print in carrying the work to its audiences. Her account follows the tragedy from the early quartos through the Restoration adaptations and Garrick's long dominance to modern productions, treating each era's staging as an interpretation with the same claim on attention as an editor's reading, and she reads the two early texts themselves as deposits of the play's double life, one nearer the study and one nearer the stage. The consequence for her edition is a text and commentary that keep playing conditions in view, so that a reader is continually reminded that the words were built to be spoken by particular bodies in a particular wooden theatre. The argument has a textual edge as well: an editor who believes performance transmits the work will treat the first quarto, the text nearest the early stage, as evidence of the play rather than as damage to it, and Levenson's introduction makes exactly that connection, one reason her volume sits nearer Weis than Gibbons on the field's fault line.

### Q: How does G. Blakemore Evans's New Cambridge edition use the play's stage history?

Evans builds his introduction around a long, continuous account of the play in performance, from the first staging through the celebrated revivals of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries to the modern stage, and uses that history as the frame for his reading of the text. Productions become evidence in his volume: cuts, restorations and famous interpretations mark the points where the text has proved ambiguous or irresistible, and the commentary refers staging questions to the record of what companies have done. The design is the New Cambridge series' signature, a stage centred introduction joined to a conservative text, and it makes Evans's book the fullest short history of the tragedy in performance among the five editions compared. Evans's own standing reinforced the design: as editor of the Riverside Shakespeare he had spent a career on the whole canon, and his Cambridge volume reads as the work of a scholar for whom the play's long stage life, rather than its brief printing history, was the larger and more instructive story.

### Q: What makes the Folger edition's editorial method different from the scholarly series' method?

The Folger method differs in refusing the manuscript narratives on which the scholarly series rest their copy-text choices. Its co-editor Paul Werstine has argued in print that foul papers, the authorial draft copy supposedly behind the second quarto, is an editorial construction without documentary foundation, and the Folger text is built accordingly: the second quarto is used as base because it is the fullest authoritative early text, collated against the first quarto and the Folio, emended on judgement, and presented without a certified pedigree. The scholarly volumes, in varying degrees, tell the older story of desk and stage as the warrant for their choices; the Folger states the witnesses' facts and lets the warrant go. The method's cost is a certain flatness in the book's own voice: an edition that declines every story about its copy can tell its reader less about why this word stands here, and the Folger accepts the cost openly, wagering that a first reader needs the play more than the play's bibliography.

### Q: Which edition serves an actor preparing a role best, and why?

The Arden third series volume serves an actor best. Weis prints the fullest set of stage directions, drawing on the first quarto's theatrical record, so the physical shape of scenes, entrances, positions and business at the feast, the orchard and the tomb, stands on the page rather than in the actor's guesswork. His commentary addresses playable questions, tempo, emphasis, the decisions a speech forces, and his introduction treats the early texts as scripts for performance. An acting company with room for a second book should add Lukas Erne's Cambridge edition of the first quarto entire, the shorter, swifter text nearest to what the earliest players used, which several modern companies have found actable in its own right. The choice also suits the director's table: a company building a production from Weis inherits a text whose directions have already been tested against the earliest staging evidence, and whose commentary anticipates the questions rehearsals ask, from the feast's choreography to the tomb's crowded discoveries.

### Q: Which edition should a teacher put into students' hands first?

The Folger edition should go into students' hands first. Its facing page explanations remove the vocabulary friction that ends first readings, its scene summaries keep the plot's machinery visible, its essays on the playwright's life and language supply the common background a class needs, and its price allows a whole class to own the book. Its text is edited from the second quarto by two leading textual scholars, so nothing a student quotes from it is unsound. The teacher's own desk copy is better chosen from the Oxford or the Arden third series, whose introductions and apparatus answer the questions the Folger deliberately leaves unasked, and a course can move strong readers from the Folger to those volumes within a single term. The pairing of a class text and a desk text is the comparison's most practical yield for schools: the Folger puts a sound play in every hand at a price a department can carry, and the scholarly volume behind the teacher converts the class's questions into a guided tour of the evidence.

### Q: If every compared edition starts from the second quarto, why do the printed texts still differ?

The texts differ because a copy-text is a starting point, not a result. Each editor emends the second quarto at places judged corrupt, and judgement differs; each imports stage directions the quarto lacks, from the first quarto, the Folio or inference, in different measure; each repunctuates every speech, modernizes spelling under house rules, assigns disputed lines to speakers, and settles the layout of passages the early compositors set as prose. Multiplied across three thousand lines, those thousands of small decisions yield five distinguishable plays on one foundation, and the scholarly volumes record the decisions in their collations while the Folger absorbs them silently. The shared base guarantees broad agreement in wording; everything that makes a text this text rather than another is editorial. Line numbering carries the same lesson into the smallest unit of reference: because each book lineates by its own decisions, the same speech can sit a line or two apart in different volumes, so that even the address of a quotation is an editorial fact, a humility the comparison teaches better than any preface.

### Q: How much annotation does each edition supply, and who is each annotation style written for?

The Arden volumes annotate most, with dense foot of page commentary on words, parallels, sources, staging and scholarly opinion, written for specialists and advanced students. The Oxford annotates generously with a teacher's economy, explaining first and documenting second, for undergraduates and general scholarly readers. The New Cambridge annotates least among the scholarly four, glossing hard words and references while declining the history of opinion, for advanced students who want the text to keep moving. The Folger annotates most frequently and most briefly, a facing page gloss wherever a beginner might stumble, for first readers and school classes. The governing rule is that annotation is priced in interruption, and each house has set the price for its reader. Density also interacts with format: the Folger's frequent short glosses sit on the facing page and cost almost no interruption, while the Arden's long notes repay a slower reading, so that the true comparison is not how much each book says but what kind of reading each quantity of saying creates.

### Q: Which edition is the least expensive, and what does its price exclude?

The Folger edition is the least expensive by a wide margin, a mass market paperback priced for classroom purchase. Its price excludes the scholarly apparatus: no collation recording the witnesses' variants, no textual commentary arguing the editor's choices, no documented history of editorial opinion, and only a summary account of the two quarto problem in its textual note. What the price includes is a sound text edited from the second quarto by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine, facing page explanation, scene summaries, illustrations and essays on the playwright's life and language. The exclusion is a deliberate design, the Folger Shakespeare Library's mission being to put reliable texts in common hands rather than to publish the evidence behind them. The exclusion should not be read as a defect in the text itself: the Folger's words are as carefully edited as any in the field, and a reader who needs only the words, well chosen and clearly helped, is not short changed by the absence of an apparatus that reader would never open.

### Q: What changed in the Arden edition's view of the first quarto between Gibbons and Weis?

The first quarto rose from suspect to witness. Gibbons, writing in 1980 within the confident Greg tradition, treats the 1597 text as a reported reduction of the fuller play, valuable chiefly for a few corrections and for testimony about early staging, its readings admitted rarely. Weis, writing in 2012 after the discipline's reassessment of the short texts and after his own work on the two text King Lear, treats the first quarto as a theatrically authoritative version possibly preserving an earlier state of the play, imports its stage directions wholesale at many points, and hears its readings on equal terms. The change inside one series across one generation is the clearest single index of how the editorial ground moved under all five volumes. The shift was not Weis's eccentricity but the discipline's: between the two volumes the short quartos were reassessed across the canon, Erne's literary dramatist argument and the Cambridge Early Quartos series gave the first quarto a book of its own, and the Arden's generational replacement simply registered, in one play, a change that had already happened everywhere.

### Q: How do the five introductions disagree about what kind of play this is?

The introductions advance five distinguishable plays. Gibbons presents a lyric transformation of inherited story, its centre the novella tradition and Brooke's poem. Levenson presents a work that has always existed in versions, completed afresh by each generation of players. Evans presents a classic whose meaning includes four centuries of accumulated performance. Weis presents a restless theatrical text that existed in more than one state from the beginning. The Folger volume declines a thesis and presents a reading problem, a great text in a difficult language that mediation at the level of words can open. The disagreements are methodological before they are interpretive: sources, performance history, versioning and plain reading are four rival accounts of where the play's meaning is made. A student can use the five positions as a syllabus: Gibbons for the making, Levenson for the playing, Evans for the afterlife, Weis for the texts, and the Folger for the experience of the words unmediated by thesis, and the set of five introductions becomes a short course in the methods of modern criticism.

### Q: What extra material does each edition carry besides the edited text?

Each volume surrounds the text with a different estate. The Arden volumes carry long introductions, full commentary, textual notes and lists of roles and abbreviations, with Weis adding the most extensive discussion of the early texts. The Oxford adds its substantial introduction on text and performance, a stage history and a chronology of the playwright's works in the house manner. The New Cambridge adds its stage centred introduction and a reading list refreshed in the updated edition. The Folger adds essays on the playwright's life and language, scene summaries, illustrations from the Folger collections, a guide to further reading and, in its digital form, the text itself published freely by the Folger Shakespeare Library. The estates reveal each house's wager about what a reader lacks: the scholarly volumes assume a reader who lacks evidence and argument, the Folger a reader who lacks only the words' meanings and the story's thread, and the extras are priced, sized and written to those two different lacks.

### Q: Can a reader rely on a quotation from the play taken from a website that names no edition?

A reader cannot rely on it for scholarly purposes. A text with no named editor descends, in most cases, through layers of copying from nineteenth century editions or from the Folio line, modernized irregularly and punctuated by unknown hands, with readings that may be nobody's considered choice. For casual reading the words will serve; for quotation in an essay or an argument, the line should be checked against a named edition, the Arden, Oxford, New Cambridge or Folger, and the edition named in the citation, since readings and line numbering differ among editions and an unnamed text cannot be verified, challenged or found again by another reader. The same caution applies in print: an anthology extract or a quotation in a textbook carries an unnamed edition's choices too, and the discipline the comparison recommends is uniform, namely to ask of any text of the play whose it is, and to prefer, wherever the answer matters, a text that can give the answer.

### Q: Which edition best serves a reader who wants the play's sources and analogues?

The Arden second series volume, edited by Brian Gibbons, serves that reader best. Its introduction contains the fullest account in any single edition of the story's transmission, from the Italian novelle through Boaistuau's French version and Painter's prose to Arthur Brooke's poem of 1562, the playwright's immediate source, and it shows at each stage what the tragedy kept, compressed and transformed. Gibbons's commentary keeps the sources in view at the level of the line, setting the lovers' language against the Petrarchan conventions the tradition supplied. Later editions discuss the sources competently, and Weis is fuller on the texts of the play itself, but none has replaced Gibbons as the source hunter's volume. The volume's endurance is part of the recommendation: although the third series Arden has replaced it as the Arden text of record, libraries keep Gibbons in circulation precisely for the source study, and teachers of the play's making still send students to it first, an afterlife few superseded editions enjoy.

### Q: Which edition should a graduate student working on the play's text choose, and what should accompany it?

A graduate student working on the text should choose the Arden third series volume as the base edition and accompany it with Lukas Erne's Cambridge edition of the first quarto. Weis offers the fullest modern collation and textual commentary, argues his departures from the second quarto in prose that can be contested, and gives the first quarto's evidence its most generous hearing inside a second quarto edition; Erne's volume supplies the shorter witness entire, in modern spelling with its own apparatus, so that every claim about the relation of the texts can be tested against both books open side by side. Levenson's Oxford introduction is the recommended first map of the problem, and the pair of volumes plus that map equip a researcher for serious textual work. The pairing also disciplines ambition: with both quartos open beside the chosen edition, a researcher can see the edited text as one settlement among possibilities and can write about the play's transmission without mistaking a modern page for a seventeenth century fact, which is the founding error the whole comparison exists to prevent.

### Q: What does the disagreement among the modern editions reveal about the play itself?

The disagreement reveals that the play survives as a family of texts rather than a single fixed work. It reached print in two early forms, one nearer the writing desk and one nearer the stage, in no authorial manuscript, and every modern edition is a negotiated settlement between those inheritances, conducted under a theory of evidence. Five settlements, all defensible and none final, show that the instability is not damage done to the play but part of its documentary nature, the record of a work born twice. The reader who grasps the editions' quarrel owns the tragedy more completely than any single volume can confer, because the quarrel itself is the fullest surviving account of how the play came to be. Seen from that vantage, the five editions stop looking like rivals for a crown and start looking like successive surveys of one territory, each accurate where its instruments reach, and the play itself appears as the territory: larger than any survey, and knowable only through them.
