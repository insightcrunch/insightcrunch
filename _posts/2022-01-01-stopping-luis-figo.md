Most wide defending is taught around the wrong objective, and the clearest way to see it is to study a wide player whose take-on was never the point. How defenders stopped Luis Figo is a question that produces a useless answer if it is asked in the usual way, because the usual way assumes the duel is about possession of the ball. Against a delivery specialist it is not. The full-back who prevents the take-on and concedes the cross has lost the exchange. The full-back who is beaten and forces the delivery from a position his own box has already prepared for has won it.

That inversion is the whole article, and it is worth stating plainly at the start because it contradicts the instruction almost every young defender receives. He is told not to get beaten. Against this profile, not getting beaten is neither necessary nor sufficient, and a coach who measures his full-back by take-ons conceded is measuring the wrong thing and will therefore coach the wrong behaviour.

## What the Luis Figo duel actually was

Figo was a Portuguese right winger, right-footed, whose value came from beating the full-back on the outside in order to deliver. He played across Sporting CP, Barcelona, Real Madrid, and Internazionale, and had a long international career with Portugal. The tactically important part of that description is the phrase in order to. The take-on was instrumental. It existed to produce a delivery from a position he had chosen, and every part of his game upstream of it was organised around arriving there.

That makes him a different category of problem from the wide players who dominate discussion of this topic. Against a wide player whose threat is his own entry to the penalty area, the defensive objective is to prevent the entry, and preventing the entry means winning or delaying the duel. Against a delivery specialist, the entry is not the danger and the duel is not the objective. The danger is a ball struck from a specific area into a box that has not organised itself for it, and the duel is only relevant to the extent that it determines where the ball is struck from.

His technical profile followed from that purpose. He was not primarily a pace threat, and describing him as one misreads the mechanism. What he had instead was a range of deliveries produced from body shapes that looked alike until very late, and the patience to work the full-back into a position where any of them was available. The separation he needed was small, because he did not need to run past anyone; he needed a yard of clearance and a fixed foot, and a yard is a much easier thing to manufacture than a five-metre gap.

This is why his duels look undramatic on film and why he is under-discussed relative to his contemporaries in the wide-player conversation. There is no signature move to compile. There is a repeated, unglamorous process of manoeuvring a defender into an unfavourable position and then delivering, and the process is the skill.

### What made Figo's delivery so dangerous?

Variety produced from a body shape that stayed ambiguous until the last moment. Three different deliveries from the same approach means the box cannot commit early, and a box that cannot commit early is defending a ball it has not yet identified, which is the hardest defensive task in football.

## The geometry of the crossing angle

Everything that follows depends on getting the geometry right, so it is worth building it carefully rather than asserting it.

A cross is defined by two things: where it is struck from, and the body position of the player striking it. Both are variables the defending side can influence, and neither is the same as whether the take-on succeeded.

Take the location first. As a crosser moves from the halfway line toward the byline along the touchline, the ball's flight path relative to the goal rotates continuously. From deep, a cross travels forward and across, arriving on a path that is broadly convergent with the runs of attackers moving toward goal, which is comfortable for a goalkeeper because the ball is coming toward him and comfortable for defenders because they are facing it. As the crosser advances toward the byline, the flight path rotates until, from close to the byline, the ball travels across the face of the goal on a path that is broadly perpendicular to the goalkeeper's line of movement and broadly parallel to the goal itself.

That rotation is the entire reason byline deliveries are more dangerous than deep ones, and it is a geometric fact rather than a matter of quality. A ball crossing the face of goal cannot be attacked on a converging path by the goalkeeper, has to be intercepted rather than met, and arrives to attackers who are running onto it rather than turning back for it. Defenders facing a byline delivery are also facing away from their own goal and toward the ball, which means every runner behind them is in their blind area.

The second variable, the body position of the crosser, determines what kind of ball is available. A right-footed crosser who is square to the touchline with the ball on his right foot and his hips open to the pitch can deliver anything: the early ball to the back post, the flat driven cross, the cutback. The same player forced to strike with his hips closed toward the touchline, or off balance, or on his weaker side, has a much narrower menu, and a narrow menu is what allows a box to commit early.

Put those two together and the defensive objective becomes obvious once it is stated. The full-back is not trying to keep the ball. He is trying to determine two coordinates: how far from the byline the delivery happens, and what the crosser's hips are doing when it does. Everything else in the wide duel is instrumental to those two.
## The angle-not-the-man rule

That gives the namable claim of this article. Call it the angle-not-the-man rule: against a delivery specialist the defensive objective is the position of the cross rather than the possession of the ball, which means a full-back can be beaten and still have defended correctly, and can win the ball and still have defended badly.

The rule sounds like a technicality and is not, because it changes four things at once.

It changes the success criterion. A full-back is evaluated on where deliveries came from rather than on how many take-ons he conceded. That is a measurable standard, it can be tracked from the touchline without equipment, and it is a fairer measure of the job he was actually given.

It changes the instruction. A defender told to win the ball will engage, and engaging is what produces the cheap loss and the byline delivery. A defender told to control the angle will hold his distance and shepherd, which looks less committed and produces better outcomes.

It changes what the rest of the team does. If the delivery position is the objective, then the box shape is not a reaction to a cross that has already happened; it is a pre-arranged response to an angle that the full-back is actively determining. That makes the wide duel and the box defending one system rather than two, which is how good sides defend crosses and how ordinary sides do not.

It changes what counts as a mistake. Conceding a cross from thirty metres out with the crosser's hips closed is a successful defensive sequence. Conceding a cutback from the byline while never being beaten one on one is a failure. Reversing those two judgments, which is what most touchline reaction does, teaches defenders to protect the wrong thing.

### Should a defender block the cross or stay on his feet?

Stay on his feet in almost every case. A sliding block that misses removes the defender entirely and converts a controlled delivery into an unopposed one. The exception is a genuinely last-ditch situation inside the penalty area where no cover exists and the delivery is the final action.

## The crossing-angle denial table

The artifact that makes the rule usable is a mapping from wide position to defensive requirement. Each row is a position from which a delivery can be struck, crossed with the delivery it enables, the box shape required to defend it, the forcing objective for the wide defender, and the concession that objective accepts. A coach who wants one page from this article should take this one.

| Wide position of the crosser | Delivery it enables | Box shape required | Full-back's forcing objective | Concession accepted |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deep, level with own half or just inside | Early diagonal to the back post, switch | Line pushed up, far centre-back highest, goalkeeper on his line | Allow it, close the inside route only | The early ball, which is the least dangerous delivery available |
| Wide, level with the edge of the box, hips closed | Hopeful high cross, recycled possession | Standard box shape, near-post defender attacks the flight | Force the closed hips, do not engage | Possession, and a delivery the box is facing |
| Wide, level with the edge of the box, hips open | Flat driven ball across the six-yard box | Near-post contact fixed to a body, second defender at the spot | Prevent the open hips by staying goal-side of the crossing foot | The cross itself, but not the shape of it |
| Half-space, level with the penalty spot | Cutback and the pull-back to the edge | Screen at the edge of the area, near-post defender holds depth | Deny the inside entry, force back outside | Territory outside the area |
| Wide, level with the six-yard box | Cutback to the penalty spot, low cross across the face | Two defenders retreating, one occupying the shooting line | Delay only, retreat at his speed, never engage | The delivery, which is now unavoidable |
| On the byline, goalkeeper committed | Pull-back to the arriving runner | Far-post defender watching the runner, screen holding the edge | Force him past the byline or force the touch to the goalkeeper | Everything except the pull-back lane |
| Cut inside onto the weaker foot, central | Shot, through ball to the far runner | Compact block, centre-backs holding the line | Force this deliberately if the delivery is the greater threat | The shot from a narrowed angle |
| Set-piece delivery from a dead ball | Full menu, no defensive pressure available | Rehearsed marking scheme, first-contact responsibilities | Not applicable, the duel does not exist | The whole delivery, which is why dead balls are a separate problem |

Two rows carry most of the value. The row for the wide position level with the edge of the box, split into hips open and hips closed, is the single most useful distinction in wide defending, because it is the same location producing two entirely different threats and because the full-back can influence which one occurs. The byline row is the one that establishes the priority order: by that point the delivery is conceded and the only remaining defensive action belongs to the far-post defender and the screening midfielder.

The final row is included to mark a boundary rather than to be used. A dead-ball delivery removes the wide duel entirely, which makes it a different problem with a different solution, and that problem belongs to [the treatment of defending elite set-piece takers](/2030/06/01/defending-elite-set-piece-takers/) rather than to this article.
## The forcing technique: what the full-back actually does

The rule is worthless without an execution, and the execution is a specific set of body and distance decisions that can be taught in a session and checked from the touchline.

The first decision is the starting distance, and against a delivery specialist it is longer than against a dribbler. The reason is that the threat is not the take-on, so the defender does not need to be close enough to contest the first touch; he needs to be close enough that the crosser cannot strike the ball unopposed. Two metres is roughly right at walking pace and shortens as the crosser advances. A defender who closes to one metre has given away his ability to retreat and will be beaten by a simple touch past him, which converts a controlled duel into a byline delivery.

The second decision is the angle of approach, and this is where most of the coaching value sits. The defender approaches on the goal side of the crossing foot rather than on the touchline side. Against a right-footed player on the right flank, that means the defender's body is between the crosser's right foot and the goal, which sounds obvious and is the opposite of what most defenders do, because most defenders position to prevent the outside route. Positioning to prevent the outside route puts the defender on the touchline side, which leaves the crossing foot free and open, and a free open crossing foot is the whole of the threat.

This is the central practical inversion, and it should be stated as plainly as possible. Against a delivery specialist, the defender concedes the outside route in order to occupy the crossing foot. He accepts being beaten toward the byline because a beaten defender who is still goal-side of the crossing foot forces the ball to be struck across his own body, which produces the closed hips and the narrow menu.

The third decision is the retreat speed. Once the crosser is moving toward the byline, the defender retreats at exactly the crosser's speed, staying goal-side, and never engages. The purpose is to make the crosser travel as far as possible before he can strike, because every metre closer to the byline costs him crossing options while the defender remains in the way. This looks passive and is the most aggressive thing the defender can do to the delivery menu.

The fourth decision is the moment of the block, and there is only one legitimate version. The defender's front foot presents itself to the ball at the moment the crosser plants, without going to ground and without lunging. A planted front foot with the body sideways on occupies the low driven lane, which is the most dangerous delivery from that position, and leaves the defender on his feet for whatever happens next. The sliding block is a last-ditch action and nothing else, because a slide that misses removes the defender from the sequence entirely.

The fifth decision is the recovery line after the delivery. A full-back who has just conceded a cross has one remaining job, which is to occupy the space at the near edge of the six-yard box where cutbacks and second balls arrive. Full-backs almost universally do the opposite and follow the crosser, which is the least useful thing available to them.

### How did sides deny Figo the outside route?

Mostly by not trying to, which is the counterintuitive answer. Denying the outside route requires standing on the touchline side of the crossing foot, which leaves the delivery open. The sides that handled him best conceded the outside step deliberately and stayed goal-side of his right foot, forcing him to strike across his own body.

## Delivery variety: the complication

Everything above assumes the defender can influence the type of delivery by controlling the body shape. Against a crosser with genuine variety, that assumption weakens, and the weakening is the most interesting thing about this profile.

The mechanism is disguise through similarity. If three different deliveries are produced from the same approach and the same hip position, then controlling the hips no longer controls the menu, and the box is back to defending a ball it cannot identify until it is in the air. That is a version of the undecidable problem that appears throughout wide defending: the defender is asked to make a choice, every choice loses to one of the available options, and the attacker is under no obligation to reveal which option is coming.

Three deliveries recur in a varied crosser's repertoire and each attacks a different defensive commitment. The early ball to the back post attacks a defensive line that has stepped up to compress the box, arriving behind the far centre-back into the area he vacated by stepping. The flat driven ball across the six-yard box attacks a box that has settled into a marking arrangement, because it is too fast to be marked and has to be attacked at the first contact. The cutback attacks a box that has retreated toward its own goal, arriving behind the retreating defenders into the space they have created by dropping.

The reason variety is so effective is that the three defensive responses are mutually exclusive. Compressing the box beats the cutback and loses to the early ball. Dropping deep beats the early ball and loses to the cutback. Holding a fixed marking arrangement beats neither and loses to the driven ball. A box facing a crosser who can produce all three is choosing which goal to concede rather than whether to concede one.

There is a defensible answer and it is not a shape, it is a rule. The box does not try to defend all three. It defends the two that are available from the position the full-back has actually forced, which requires the full-back's forcing objective and the box's shape to be linked by a spoken rule rather than by hope. That is why the table above pairs each position with a shape: the pairing is the plan, and the full-back's job is to deliver the input the box has prepared for.

### How did Figo vary his delivery?

By producing three deliveries from approaches that stayed similar until late: the early ball behind a stepping line, the flat driven ball across the six-yard box that has to be attacked at first contact, and the cutback behind a retreating box. The three beat three mutually exclusive defensive responses.
## Matching box shape to the angle conceded

The centre-back's job against a delivery specialist is not the job he thinks it is. He believes he is marking a striker. He is in fact solving a geometry problem in which the striker is one variable, and the controlling variable is the position the delivery will come from.

The organising instruction is that the box shape is set by the angle rather than by the man, which is the same rule applied one line further back. Three shapes cover almost everything, and the trigger for each is a position on the pitch rather than a movement by an opponent.

The first is the high compact shape, used when the delivery is coming from deep. The line steps to the edge of the penalty area, the far centre-back holds the highest position because the early ball to the back post is the danger, and the goalkeeper stays on his line because a deep cross gives him time and no path to attack. The near-post area is largely irrelevant from this angle, which frees a body.

The second is the fixed-contact shape, used when the delivery is coming from the wide area level with the edge of the box with hips open. The driven ball is the danger and it cannot be marked, only attacked, so the near-post contact is assigned to a specific player who attacks the flight regardless of who is near him. A second defender occupies the penalty spot. The remaining defenders take runners rather than zones, because a driven ball's second contact is the goal source.

The third is the retreat-and-screen shape, used when the delivery is coming from the byline area. Defenders retreat with their runners and, critically, watch the runners rather than the ball, because a byline delivery travels across goal and the arriving attacker is in space rather than in contest. A midfielder screens the edge of the area for the pull-back, which is the highest-value delivery from that position and the one most often left undefended.

The failure that produces most crossing goals is not a marking error. It is a shape error, in which the box has adopted one of the three arrangements while the delivery is coming from a position that calls for another. That happens because the box sets its shape when the ball goes wide rather than when the delivery position becomes clear, which is roughly two seconds too early.

The correction is a single habit: the box does not settle until the full-back has been beaten or has forced the crosser to a stop. The two seconds of unsettled shape feel uncomfortable and are the price of arriving in the right arrangement. Sides that settle early look organised and defend the wrong ball.

### How did box marking change when Figo had the ball wide?

It should have changed with his position rather than with his possession, which is the error most sides made. The box needs a different shape for a deep delivery, a driven ball from the edge of the area, and a byline cutback, and settling into one arrangement the moment the ball goes wide guarantees defending the wrong one.

## The goalkeeper's starting position by delivery angle

The goalkeeper is the player whose positioning is most sensitive to the crossing angle and the one who is most often left to work it out alone.

Against a deep delivery the goalkeeper stays on his line or a step off it. The ball is travelling toward him on a converging path, he has time to read the flight, and advancing gains him nothing while creating an empty net behind him if the ball is flighted over.

Against a driven ball from the wide area level with the edge of the box, the goalkeeper's start moves toward the near post and stays deep. The near-post delivery is the fastest ball he will face and the one that allows least reaction, and depth buys the fraction of a second he needs. He is not attacking this ball; he is covering the near-post lane and reacting to a deflection, which is the most common goal source from that angle.

Against a byline delivery the goalkeeper's start moves closer to the near post again and he adopts a decision rule rather than a judgment: he attacks only balls travelling toward him, and never balls travelling away across the six-yard box. The reason is geometric rather than technical. A ball crossing the face of goal has to be intercepted at a point rather than met on a converging path, and a misjudged interception leaves the goalkeeper beyond the ball with the goal open.

Against a cutback the goalkeeper's job is the one nobody trains, which is to be set and low with his weight forward, because the cutback produces a first-time strike from close range that is defended by reaction rather than by position. A goalkeeper who is still moving when the cutback is struck has no chance regardless of where he is, which means the requirement is stillness at the right moment rather than any particular location.

The communication that matters happens before the ball is struck rather than during its flight. Telling the near-post defender to step and the far-post defender to hold his runner two seconds before the delivery is worth more than any call once the ball is in the air, and it is the single easiest improvement available to most sides.

### How did goalkeepers position for Figo's crosses?

By varying the start with the delivery angle rather than holding one position. Deep crosses call for staying on the line, driven balls from the edge of the area call for a deep near-post start, and byline deliveries call for a rule that he attacks only balls travelling toward him.
## Doubling up against a delivery specialist

Committing a second defender to the flank is the standard response to a wide player who keeps beating his marker, and against this profile it is frequently the wrong response, for reasons that follow directly from the angle-not-the-man rule.

A double team is an instrument for taking the ball away or for closing a route. Against a delivery specialist neither objective is the point. He does not need to go anywhere; he needs a yard of clearance and a fixed foot. A second defender arriving from the inside actually helps him in one specific way, because a defender approaching from inside is approaching on the touchline side of the crossing foot, which is exactly the side the first defender should not be on. Two defenders can therefore arrive in a shape that leaves the crossing lane more open than one defender correctly positioned would have left it.

There is a second cost. The player who doubles is almost always the near-side central midfielder, and he is the player responsible for screening the edge of the penalty area against the cutback. Pulling him wide to double the crosser removes the defender of the highest-value delivery from the byline area in order to contest a delivery from further out. That is a straight downgrade, and it happens constantly because doubling feels like doing something and screening feels like standing still.

Doubling does have a correct use against this profile, and it is narrow. It works when the second defender arrives goal-side, meaning between the crosser and the goal rather than between the crosser and the pitch, and when he arrives during the ball's travel toward the winger rather than after the reception. Arriving that early converts the duel into a two-on-one before the crosser has set his feet, which is the only moment at which numbers genuinely help. Arriving after the reception adds a body to a problem that was never about bodies.

The other correct use is temporal rather than positional: doubling in the last fifteen minutes of a match a side is protecting, when the cost of conceding possession outside the area is low and the cost of conceding any delivery is high. That is a game-state decision rather than a tactical one, and it should be made explicitly rather than drifted into.

### Did doubling up on Figo work?

Rarely, and often it made things worse. The second defender usually arrived on the touchline side of his crossing foot, leaving the delivery lane open, and he was usually the midfielder screening the edge of the area, so doubling removed the defender of the cutback in order to contest a cross from further out.

## Drifting inside, and the goal threat question

Two features of the profile complicate the plan above, and both need honest treatment because a defensive plan that ignores them will be beaten by them.

The first is the inside drift. A wide player who only ever holds the touchline is a fixed problem, and a fixed problem can be defended by a full-back with a covering centre-back and nothing else. A wide player who drifts into the half-space to receive changes the identity of the defender responsible for him, which is the hardest transition in defensive organisation. The full-back cannot follow him inside without opening the flank behind, the centre-back cannot step out without opening the space he occupies, and the midfielder in front frequently has not noticed.

Figo did drift inside to receive, though the drift served a different purpose from the one it serves for an inverted winger. An inverted winger comes inside to shoot or to enter the box. A delivery specialist comes inside to change the geometry of his own next action, because a ball received in the half-space and moved back out to the touchline arrives at a crosser who is now facing the pitch with a defender who has been dragged out of position. The drift is a manufacturing process for a better crossing position rather than a threat in itself.

The defensive answer is a pass-on rule that is spoken before the match. When he crosses the line into the half-space, he becomes the near-side midfielder's problem and the full-back holds the flank. That rule is simple, it is frequently ignored, and the ignoring is what produces the sequence in which three defenders converge on him and the space he vacated is filled by an overlapping full-back.

The second feature is the goal threat, and here the honest answer shapes the priorities. He scored, at every club and internationally, and he took set pieces and penalties, so treating him as a player who could be allowed to shoot would be a mistake. But his shooting threat was not the organising danger, and a plan that spends its resource narrowing his shooting angle at the cost of opening his crossing angle is trading the smaller threat for the larger one.

This produces a rule that feels wrong to say aloud and is correct: against this profile, allowing the cut inside is often the better concession. The cut inside onto the weaker side produces a narrowed shooting angle against a set goalkeeper and a compact block, which is a low-yield outcome. The outside route produces the byline delivery, which is a high-yield outcome. Where the defensive resource is scarce, it goes on the outside route, and that is close to the opposite of the standard instruction.

### Did Figo drift inside to receive?

Yes, but for a different purpose than an inverted winger's. He came into the half-space to manufacture a better crossing position rather than to shoot or enter the box, because a ball received inside and moved back out arrives at a crosser facing the pitch with the defender dragged out of position.

## Was he a goal threat himself?

The answer belongs in the plan rather than in the biography, so it is worth separating the three components.

He scored regularly enough that leaving him unattended in the penalty area was never acceptable, and any plan that treats a delivery specialist as harmless once he is inside the box is a plan that will concede to him. That is the first component and it constrains everything else.

He was a dead-ball threat, which matters because it removes the wide duel from the equation entirely and therefore removes every tool this article has described. A side that has solved him in open play has solved nothing at set pieces, and the defensive requirement there is a rehearsed marking scheme rather than a forcing objective.

And his open-play shooting threat was real but secondary, concentrated in situations where the cut inside was available and the block was not compact. That is the component that determines the concession: it is the threat a side can afford to accept in order to protect against the delivery.

Put together, the priority order for a defensive plan is delivery first, set piece second, open-play shot third. Almost every side that faced this profile and got it wrong got it wrong by inverting the first and third.

### Was Figo a goal threat himself?

Yes, sufficiently that leaving him unattended in the box was never acceptable, and he was a dead-ball threat, which removes the wide duel from the problem entirely. His open-play shooting threat was real but secondary, which is why the cut inside is the concession a plan can afford.
## The crossing-is-inefficient objection

The strongest argument against everything in this article comes from the analytical mainstream, and it deserves a full hearing rather than a dismissal, because it is broadly correct.

The objection runs as follows. Crossing converts at a low rate. Measured across large samples, the expected value of a cross into the penalty area is poor relative to almost any other way of using possession in the final third, and sides that cross more do not score more. A defensive plan that spends its resource preventing crosses is therefore spending resource to prevent a low-value action, and the resource would be better spent preventing entries to the penalty area, which convert far better. On this reading the correct defensive instruction against a delivery specialist is to concede the cross cheerfully and defend the box with numbers.

Every part of that is defensible in aggregate, and a coach who took only that lesson would defend better than one who took no lesson at all. It is also incomplete in three ways that matter, and the incompleteness is exactly where a delivery specialist lives.

The first gap is that the aggregate averages over delivery quality, and delivery quality is not normally distributed. The population of crosses includes a very large number of hopeful balls launched under pressure from poor positions, and those deliveries drag the average down for everyone. The relevant question for a coach is not what a cross is worth on average but what a cross from this position, struck by this player, into this box, is worth. Those conditional values differ enormously, and the difference is the entire subject of this article.

The second gap is that the aggregate averages over box occupation, which is a property of the attacking side rather than of the cross. A delivery into a box with one static striker is a different event from the same delivery into a box with three runners arriving from different depths on staggered timing. Cross conversion is a joint outcome of the delivery and the occupation, and studies of cross value that do not condition on occupation are measuring a mixture.

The third gap is the one most often missed, which is that the aggregate ignores the second ball and the territorial effect. A cross that is cleared to the edge of the area produces a possession in a dangerous position, a corner, or a shot from the recycled ball. A side that crosses repeatedly also pins the defending side deep, which suppresses its ability to counter and produces the sustained pressure that eventually converts. None of that appears in a conversion figure attached to the cross itself.

The synthesis is not a rejection of the analytical position but a conditioning of it. Crossing as a default strategy is inefficient. Crossing from specific positions, with specific delivery quality, into a box with rehearsed occupation, is efficient enough that defending it properly is worth the resource. The practical rule for a coach is therefore to concede deep crosses freely, defend the byline area seriously, and treat delivery variety as the signal that the aggregate no longer applies.

### Is crossing an efficient way to attack in modern football?

Not as a default. Aggregate cross conversion is poor, largely because the population includes many hopeful balls from poor positions. It becomes efficient when conditioned on delivery position, delivery quality, and rehearsed box occupation, which is precisely the combination a delivery specialist supplies.

## Measuring the duel: cross location rather than cross volume

The angle-not-the-man rule implies a measurement, and the measurement is available to an analyst with no data budget at all.

The wrong measure is take-ons conceded, which is the standard number attached to full-backs and which rewards the behaviour that loses matches. A full-back who engages early and wins the ball often will post a good number and will concede byline deliveries every time he misses. A full-back who shepherds and never engages will post a poor number and will produce a match in which every delivery came from thirty metres with closed hips.

The right measure has two components and both can be recorded by hand. The first is the delivery location, logged as one of four bands: deep, wide level with the edge of the area, wide level with the six-yard box, and byline. The second is the crosser's hips at the moment of striking, logged as open or closed. Two marks per delivery, and a full-back's performance becomes legible.

A third component is worth adding where the resource exists, which is the outcome band of each delivery: cleared outside the area, cleared inside, contested, or met cleanly. This turns the record into an argument, because it lets a coach show a full-back that the deliveries he conceded from deep were all cleared and the two he conceded from the byline both produced shots.

The most useful thing about this measurement is that it is a coaching instrument rather than an evaluation instrument. A full-back who is shown that ten of his twelve conceded deliveries came from the byline band knows exactly what to change, and the change is a distance and an angle rather than an effort level. A full-back who is shown that he lost seven duels knows only that he should have tried harder, which is not information.

For a coach keeping this record across a season and building a picture of which opponents produce which delivery bands against his side, the accumulated file is the asset rather than any individual match, and keeping it somewhere organised by opponent rather than scattered across notebooks is what [an opposition planner such as VaultBook, free to use](https://vaultbook.org/tools/football-match-planner.html) is built to do.
## Which defender profile suited the duel

The profile that handles a delivery specialist is not the profile that handles a dribbler, and selecting the wrong one is a common and expensive error.

The dominant requirement is positional discipline over a long duel, because the duel is long. A dribbler resolves his duel in two seconds; a delivery specialist works a defender for eight or ten, manoeuvring for the position he wants, and the defender who loses concentration for one of those seconds concedes the striking position. That favours a defender with an unusually flat attention curve rather than one with explosive reactions.

The second requirement is the willingness to be beaten without responding to it. A full-back facing this profile will be beaten toward the byline repeatedly by design, since conceding the outside step is part of the plan, and a defender whose competitive instinct treats each of those as a personal defeat will start engaging. Engaging is the failure mode. This is a temperament requirement as much as a technical one and it should be part of the selection.

The third requirement is the front-foot block on his feet, which is a specific and trainable technique: body sideways on, front foot presented at the moment of the plant, no lunge, no slide. Defenders who have this technique concede the same number of crosses and change the quality of a large fraction of them.

The fourth requirement sits outside the individual and is the same one that governs every wide duel: a covering centre-back who will travel far enough to make the concession survivable, and a near-side midfielder who will screen rather than double. Without those two the full-back's correct behaviour produces worse outcomes than his incorrect behaviour, which is the fastest way to lose a defender's belief in a plan.

What is not required is pace, and this is the selection insight that saves sides money and matches. A slower full-back with excellent positional discipline handles a delivery specialist comfortably, because he is never being asked to run. The same defender is exposed by a pace threat, which is why the selection has to be made against the specific opponent rather than as a permanent preference.

### Which defender profile suited facing Figo?

A positionally disciplined full-back with a flat attention curve, the temperament to be beaten toward the byline without responding to it, and a clean front-foot block on his feet. Pace was close to irrelevant, because the duel never became a race and the defender was never asked to run.

## Which defenders coped well

The honest form of this question is about method rather than about names, because the record of who handled whom in individual fixtures is thinner and more contested than the confident accounts suggest, and asserting specific duels would be inventing evidence.

What can be said is that three approaches recur among sides that limited the damage, and each corresponds to a defender profile.

The first is the disciplined shepherd, which is the individual solution described above: a full-back who conceded the outside step, stayed goal-side of the crossing foot, and forced the delivery from further out. Sides using this approach conceded a high volume of crosses and a low volume of dangerous ones, which looks poor in a match report and good on the scoreboard.

The second is the structural denial, which solves the problem before the duel by pressing the supply into the wide area so that the reception happened facing his own goal or did not happen at all. This is the highest-return approach available against any wide threat and it costs the near-side midfielder his attacking position, which is why sides use it less than they should.

The third is the box-first approach, which conceded the duel and the delivery entirely and invested everything in the penalty area: an extra body, a fixed near-post contact, a screen at the edge, and a goalkeeper with a stated rule. This is the approach most consistent with the analytical objection above and it works, at the cost of surrendering territory and inviting sustained pressure.

The approach that consistently failed was the aggressive individual duel, in which a full-back was told to win the ball and did so often enough to look successful while conceding the small number of byline deliveries that decided matches. The number of duels won is not the outcome; the location of the deliveries conceded is.

### Which defenders coped well with Figo?

Method rather than aggression separated them. Three approaches limited the damage: the shepherd who conceded the outside step but stayed goal-side of the crossing foot, the side that pressed his supply so the reception never happened cleanly, and the side that conceded the flank and invested in the box.
## Youth coaching: stop judging full-backs on take-ons conceded

The most damaging thing done to young full-backs is the scoreboard kept in their heads, and it is kept there by adults who mean well.

A young defender learns very quickly what the touchline rewards. Being beaten one on one produces an audible reaction; conceding a cross from thirty metres produces nothing. He therefore optimises for not being beaten, which means engaging early, which means diving in, which means being beaten more often and more expensively. The behaviour that the reaction was meant to discourage is produced by the reaction itself.

The correction is a change in what is praised rather than a change in what is taught. Praise the retreat that forced the crosser wider. Praise the front-foot block that stayed on its feet. Praise the recovery to the near edge of the six-yard box after a cross was conceded. Say nothing at all when a duel is lost cleanly by a defender who was in the right position, because a clean loss by a well-positioned defender is a normal event in football and treating it as a failure is how a fourteen-year-old learns to lunge.

The second correction is a change in the instruction itself. Young full-backs are taught to show a winger down the line, and the instruction is correct in most amateur contexts for reasons that have nothing to do with this profile and everything to do with the covering structure available. Against a delivery specialist the instruction is exactly wrong, and a coach should be willing to say so rather than delivering a universal rule. The general form of that decision, and the four contexts in which it resolves differently, belongs to [the article that settles whether to show a wide opponent inside or outside](/2022/01/15/show-inside-or-outside-defending/).

The third correction is to teach the front-foot block early and the sliding block almost never. The slide is a spectacular action with a poor expected value and an enormous appeal to a young player, and every repetition of it in training is a vote for a habit that will cost him penalties later. Teach it as a last-ditch action inside the six-yard box and nowhere else.

The fourth correction is the one that requires the most patience: teach the retreat to the near edge of the six-yard box after the cross is conceded. Young full-backs universally follow the crosser or stop and watch. The retreat is the highest-value action available to a beaten full-back and it is invisible, so it has to be taught deliberately and praised specifically or it will never appear.

## What a young winger takes from this profile

The attacking content transfers unusually well, because none of it depends on athletic gifts that cannot be acquired.

The first item is that separation requirements are smaller than young players believe. A yard and a fixed foot is enough to deliver, and a yard can be manufactured with a change of pace and a body feint by a player of ordinary speed. Young wingers spend years trying to acquire the five-metre gap that a pace threat needs, and never learn to manufacture the one-metre gap that a delivery threat needs, which is available to everyone.

The second item is the discipline of the striking foot. A crosser who can only deliver from a comfortable position will be denied it, and the practice that matters is delivering from awkward ones: off balance, with the body closing, on the move, and from the second touch rather than the third. That is unglamorous repetition work and it is the whole of the craft.

The third item is variety produced from similarity. A young player who can strike three different deliveries is more valuable than one who can strike one perfectly, and he is far more valuable if the three come from approaches a defender cannot distinguish. The coaching point is the approach rather than the strike: practise arriving in the same body position and then choosing.

The fourth item is the manufacturing process, which is the part almost nobody teaches. The inside drift, the ball moved back out to the touchline, the second reception facing the pitch with the defender displaced: that sequence produces a better crossing position than any take-on, and it costs nothing athletically. The modern version of the same manufacturing idea, using combination play and the cutback rather than the traditional cross, is developed in [the counter blueprint built around a modern combination-and-cutback winger](/2021/06/15/how-to-stop-bukayo-saka/), and the comparison is instructive because the geometry is identical and the mechanism is different.

### What can a young winger learn from Figo's crossing?

That a yard and a fixed foot is enough, which is achievable without pace. That the striking practice worth doing is from awkward positions rather than comfortable ones. That variety matters most when deliveries come from approaches a defender cannot tell apart.
## The other half of the sequence: the target in the box

A delivery is half of an event, and a defensive plan built entirely on the crosser is defending half of it. The other half belongs to the players attacking the ball, and the interaction between the two is what determines whether any of this matters.

The interaction has a specific shape. A delivery specialist's value is contingent on box occupation in a way that a dribbler's value is not, which means the same crosser produces sharply different output depending on who is in front of him. That contingency runs in both directions: a side with a dominant aerial target converts deliveries that would otherwise be cleared, and a crosser with genuine variety converts a target who would otherwise be marked out of the fixture.

For the defending side this creates a resource question. Against a crosser with a strong target ahead of him, the defensive priority shifts from the delivery position toward the first contact in the box, because the target will convert deliveries from a wide range of positions and narrowing the range buys less. Against a crosser without a strong target, the delivery position is worth more, because the box can be defended by occupation and the only deliveries that hurt are the ones that arrive behind the defence.

The practical consequence is a selection decision that has nothing to do with the full-back. Against a crossing side with a genuine aerial focal point, the third centre-back or the extra body in the box is the highest-value change available, and it should be made before kick-off rather than at seventy minutes. Against a crossing side without one, the resource goes to the near-side midfielder screening the edge of the area, because the cutback and the second ball are where the damage will come from.

The mechanics of defending the aerial target himself, the running jump, the contact before the ball arrives, and the marking decisions that follow, are a separate craft, and they belong to [the treatment of the box target that owns that side of the sequence](/2020/12/15/stopping-didier-drogba/) rather than to this article. The point here is only that the two problems are linked and that solving one without reference to the other is how sides concede from crosses they thought they had defended.

## Would he be as effective in a modern possession side?

The question is worth taking seriously rather than answering with a slogan, because it exposes a real tension between the profile and contemporary structures.

The case against is that positional possession systems have reduced the number of situations in which a wide player receives isolated with a defender in front of him and space to work. The systems generate width through the full-back or through a wide forward pinning the touchline, and the intended entry to the box is a combination through the half-space rather than a delivery from the flank. In that structure a delivery specialist receives less often in the position his game requires, and the crosses he does produce arrive into a box that is occupied by fewer bodies, because possession structures keep players behind the ball for rest defence.

The case for is that possession structures have created a specific and growing need for exactly this skill. A side that dominates the ball against a compact low block faces the defensive problem that has no positional solution: everything is congested, the half-spaces are occupied, and the combination routes are closed. The two remaining answers are the shot from distance and the delivery from wide, and the delivery from wide is the higher-value of the two if the quality is there. A crosser with genuine variety, who can produce three deliveries from the same shape into a rehearsed box occupation, is the most direct answer available to a low block that has been drilled to defend the middle.

The synthesis is that the profile would be used differently rather than less. He would receive less often in transition and more often in sustained possession, would be asked to deliver against a set defence rather than a scrambling one, and would need the box occupation to be rehearsed rather than improvised. All three of those are changes to the surrounding structure rather than to the skill, and the skill itself is scarcer now than it was, which raises its value rather than lowering it.

The genuine cost is defensive. Modern wide players carry an out-of-possession workload that the role did not previously demand, and a specialist whose value is concentrated in delivery has to earn the selection against a competitor who offers less delivery and more pressing. That is the real constraint, and it is a squad-building question rather than a tactical one.

### Would Figo be as effective in a modern possession side?

Used differently rather than less. He would receive fewer isolated transitions and more sustained possession against a set low block, which is exactly the problem a varied delivery answers best. The genuine constraint is the out-of-possession workload modern wide roles carry.

## The debate register

Five positions recur in serious discussion of this profile, and each deserves a verdict.

The first is that crossing is an inefficient way to attack. The verdict is that this is correct as a default and incorrect as a conditional, and the conditioning variables are delivery position, delivery quality, and box occupation. A defensive plan that concedes deep crosses freely and defends the byline area seriously is acting on the correct version of the finding. A plan that concedes everything because a spreadsheet said crossing is inefficient is acting on the average and will be beaten by the tail.

The second is that he lacked the pace of his contemporaries. The verdict is that the observation is accurate and the implication drawn from it is wrong. Pace is a mechanism for creating separation, and separation requirements scale with what the player intends to do. A player who intends to enter the penalty area needs a large gap; a player who intends to deliver needs a yard and a fixed foot. Measuring a delivery specialist against a pace threat's separation requirement is measuring him against a job he was not doing.

The third is that delivery specialists are obsolete in modern systems. The verdict is that the specialist role has narrowed and the skill has become scarcer and therefore more valuable within it. The number of players employed primarily to cross has fallen; the number of situations in which a high-quality varied delivery is the best available answer has not fallen, and against a compact low block it has risen. Scarcity of the skill inside a persistent need is a market inefficiency rather than obsolescence.

The fourth is that his output depended on the strikers ahead of him. The verdict is that this is true, is true of every crosser who has ever played, and is not a criticism. Contingency on teammates is a property of the role rather than a limitation of the player, and the same argument applied consistently would reduce every provider in the sport's history to an accessory. What can be said fairly is that a delivery-first profile has a higher variance of output across squads than a self-sufficient one, which is a genuine consideration for a recruiter and not an assessment of quality.

The fifth is that a modern full-back would deny him comfortably. The verdict is that a modern full-back would deny him the outside route more often and would concede more deliveries doing it, which is the trade this article has argued against. Modern full-backs are better athletes, better coached in the one-on-one, and worse at the specific job this duel requires, because their training emphasises preventing the take-on rather than controlling the crossing angle. The defensive gain against this profile over the intervening period is collective rather than individual: better box organisation, rehearsed screening, and goalkeepers with stated rules.
## Defending the cutback

The cutback deserves separate treatment because it is the highest-value delivery in the sequence, the least defended, and the one that most clearly exposes the difference between a box that is organised and a box that is merely occupied.

The geometry is what makes it dangerous. A cutback is struck from close to the byline back toward the penalty spot and the edge of the area, which means it travels away from the goal rather than toward it. Every defender in the box is facing the wrong way: they have retreated toward their own goal to defend the cross, and the ball is now going in the opposite direction to the space behind them. The arriving attacker meets it running forward at a stationary or retreating defensive line, which is the most favourable striking condition available in open play.

Four defensive requirements answer it and all four are frequently missing.

The first is the screen. A midfielder holds the edge of the penalty area and does not retreat with the defence, which feels wrong to him because everyone around him is dropping and the instinct in a defensive moment is to get goal-side. The screen's job is to occupy the arrival lane and to be facing the ball, and he is the only player in the box who is. Sides that pull this player wide to double the crosser have removed the defence of the cutback in order to contest a cross, which is the trade discussed earlier and it is always a bad one.

The second is the near-post defender's depth. Against a byline delivery the near-post defender must hold his depth rather than stepping to meet the ball, because stepping opens the lane the cutback uses. This is counterintuitive and it is the correction most worth making, because the near-post step is a trained reflex against crosses and is precisely wrong against cutbacks from the same area.

The third is the far-side defender watching the runner. Cutbacks are frequently struck to a player arriving from the far side, and a defender following the flight of the ball has lost him before it is struck.

The fourth is the goalkeeper's stillness. A cutback produces a first-time strike from a short distance, and it is defended by reaction rather than by position. A goalkeeper still adjusting his feet when it is struck has no chance regardless of where he was going, which means the requirement is to be set early and to accept a slightly imperfect position rather than to be perfectly placed and moving.

The reason the cutback is under-defended is that it does not look like a crisis while it is developing. The ball is going away from goal, the defence is intact, and the danger only becomes visible at the moment of the strike. Sides therefore rehearse the aerial cross, which looks dangerous, and never rehearse the cutback, which is more dangerous.

### How do you defend a wide player who beats you in order to cross?

Take the delivery position rather than the ball. Approach on the goal side of his crossing foot, concede the outside step, retreat at his speed without engaging, and force the delivery from further out with his hips closing. Then match the box shape to the band he was forced into.

## The full-back's attacking cost

An instruction has a price elsewhere, and the price of the angle-not-the-man instruction falls on the defending side's own attack.

A full-back playing two metres off his opponent, retreating at the crosser's speed, and recovering to the near edge of the six-yard box after every delivery is a full-back who is deep, facing his own goal, and not available for the transition. Sides that install this instruction and then wonder why they cannot counter are paying a cost they did not price.

Three mitigations exist and they are all structural rather than individual.

The first is to use the opposite full-back as the transition outlet, accepting that the attacked flank is a defensive flank for the fixture. This is the cheapest option and it requires only that the far-side full-back is told, because otherwise both full-backs will sit and the side will have no width at all when it wins the ball.

The second is to give the near-side winger the counter responsibility instead, holding him higher and accepting that the defensive workload on that flank falls on the full-back and the midfielder. This works against a crosser who does not track back and fails against one who does.

The third is to change the instruction by game state rather than holding it for ninety minutes. A side chasing a match cannot afford a full-back permanently deep, and the correct adjustment is to accept more byline deliveries in exchange for the attacking outlet, made deliberately and communicated rather than allowed to happen through drift. Naming the trade converts an unforced degradation into a decision.

The general point is worth stating because it applies to every defensive instruction in this series. A plan that only names what it prevents is half a plan. The other half is what it costs, and a coach who cannot state the cost has not finished thinking.
## Coaching the forcing objective

Installing this instruction is a behaviour change rather than a knowledge transfer, and behaviour changes fail for predictable reasons. Four coaching points carry almost all the difficulty.

The first is the approach angle, and it has to be corrected in slow motion before it is ever attempted at speed. Ask a full-back to walk to a stationary crosser and stop where he thinks he should be, and he will almost always stop on the touchline side, because that is where twelve years of instruction have put him. Physically moving him to the goal side of the crossing foot, showing him what the crosser can and cannot do from each position, and letting him feel the difference is worth more than any amount of explanation. This is a two-minute intervention with a permanent effect and it is almost never done.

The second is the distance, which degrades under pressure faster than any other component. A defender who is beaten once will close the distance on the next repetition, because closing feels like a correction. The coaching response is to name it in advance: you will want to get closer after you are beaten, that is the instinct to resist, the distance is the plan. A player who has been warned about an urge is much more able to notice it.

The third is the retreat speed, which is a matching problem rather than a running problem. The defender's job is to travel at exactly the crosser's speed, and the common failures are retreating faster, which opens the striking window early, and retreating slower, which turns the sequence into a race he loses. This is trainable with nothing more than a pair of players and a touchline, walking then jogging then running, and it transfers quickly because it is a rhythm rather than a technique.

The fourth is the front-foot block, and the coaching point that matters is the body angle rather than the foot. A defender square to the crosser presents his shins and blocks nothing; a defender sideways on presents the length of his leg across the low lane. Ten repetitions of the sideways presentation, without a ball, changes the shape permanently.

What ruins the installation is running it as a live one-on-one exercise from the start. A live duel returns the defender to his instincts under competitive pressure, which is exactly the environment in which the old behaviour reasserts itself, and the coach then concludes that the instruction does not work. Constraint first, competition later, and the constraint that works best is a simple one: the defender may not attempt to win the ball at all, and the exercise is scored on where the delivery came from.

The full session architecture for installing a wide defensive instruction, including the progressions and the small-squad version, belongs to the training articles in this series rather than here, and this section is the coaching points rather than a substitute for that design.

## What an analyst logs across a season

The measurement described earlier becomes far more valuable when it accumulates, and the accumulation is what separates a coach who knows an opponent from one who remembers him.

Four records are worth keeping per fixture and none takes more than a few minutes.

The first is the delivery band distribution, meaning how many crosses each opponent produced from each of the four bands against your side. Over a season this reveals which opponents are actually a crossing threat and which merely cross a lot, and those are very different things. A side producing twenty deliveries from deep is not a crossing threat. A side producing six from the byline band is.

The second is the hips-open rate, which measures how well your own full-backs are executing. A rising open-hips rate against a stable opponent profile is the clearest early signal that an instruction is decaying, and it decays gradually rather than suddenly, which is why it is invisible without the record.

The third is the concession map, meaning where the deliveries that produced shots came from. This is the record that settles arguments, because it converts a debate about whether the full-back is doing his job into a picture of which two positions on the pitch are producing all the damage.

The fourth is the response record: what the opponent changed when the flank was denied. Most sides have exactly one adjustment, and knowing it in advance is worth more than any amount of general information about their attacking play, because it lets a coach prepare the second problem before it arrives rather than after.

None of this requires software, a camera, or an assistant. It requires a decision before kick-off about what will be watched, and a habit of writing it down in a place where it can be found again ten months later against the same opponent.
## The far side, and the switch

A plan built around one flank has a mirror image, and the mirror image is where well-organised sides get punished by delivery specialists rather than by anything the specialist does himself.

The mechanism is straightforward. A side that has committed its near-side midfielder to screening and its full-back to a deep shepherding role has, by construction, concentrated attention on one flank. The opposing side's cheapest response is not to keep attacking that flank; it is to switch, arriving on the far side against a full-back who has spent twenty minutes watching a duel he is not involved in and a covering structure that has shifted across.

Three requirements answer it and they are all preparation rather than reaction.

The first is that the far-side full-back holds a narrower position than his instinct suggests while the ball is on the opposite flank, so that the switch arrives to a defender who is already inside rather than one who has to travel. This costs him the ability to press the receiver immediately, which is the correct trade because an unpressed receiver forty metres from goal is not a threat and a beaten full-back in the wide area is.

The second is that the far centre-back rather than the far full-back is the player who must not drift. Defensive lines slide toward the ball, which is correct, and the failure mode is that they slide too far, leaving the far centre-back level with the penalty spot when a switch arrives. The measurement that works is the far centre-back staying within the width of the six-yard box extended, and it is checkable from the touchline.

The third is that the screening midfielder does not follow the ball across. His job is the edge of the penalty area and it does not change when the ball switches, which feels like laziness to everyone watching and is the difference between defending a cutback and not defending one.

The reason this matters specifically against a delivery specialist is that the switch is his side's natural adjustment when the flank is denied. A crossing side facing a well-organised near-side response does not usually invent something new; it moves the problem to the other flank and runs the same sequence with a different player. A coach who has prepared one flank has prepared half a fixture.

## Why this duel is the best teaching case in wide defending

A final note on why this profile is worth studying at all, given that it is less spectacular than every other subject in its cluster.

The reason is that it isolates a variable. Against a pace threat, a dribbler, or an inverted finisher, the wide duel is contaminated by the individual contest: a defender can be beaten by superior athleticism or superior technique, and the structural questions get buried under the question of whether he was good enough. Against a delivery specialist, the individual contest is close to irrelevant, because the crosser does not need to beat anyone decisively. What remains, once the athletic question is removed, is pure geometry and pure organisation, which is exactly the content a coach can actually change.

That is why the angle-not-the-man rule emerges most clearly here and applies everywhere else. Every wide duel is really a question about where the opponent's final action will happen and whether your side has prepared for that location. Against most opponents the question is obscured by the drama of the take-on. Against this one there is no drama, so the question is visible.

The practical consequence is that a coach who installs the instruction against a delivery specialist will find it improves his side against every other wide profile as well, because the underlying behaviours, the goal-side approach, the maintained distance, the matched retreat, the front-foot block, the recovery to the near edge of the six-yard box, are correct against all of them and are merely less obviously correct when the opponent is doing something more spectacular. The unspectacular opponent is the best teacher available.
## The set-piece boundary

One boundary has to be marked clearly, because a plan that works in open play can create a false sense of having solved a crossing threat.

Every tool described in this article depends on the existence of a duel. The goal-side approach, the maintained distance, the matched retreat, the front-foot block, and the forcing of a closed hip position all require a defender to be within influencing range of the crosser at the moment he strikes. A dead ball removes all of that. The delivery happens unopposed, from a position chosen by the taker rather than forced by anyone, with the full menu of deliveries available and the box static.

That means a delivery specialist who also takes set pieces presents two separate problems with two separate solutions, and solving one does nothing for the other. The open-play problem is answered by forcing the angle. The dead-ball problem is answered only by the marking scheme, the first-contact responsibilities, and the rehearsal that assigns bodies to zones and runners before the whistle. A side that has shepherded him competently for eighty minutes and then defends a corner with an unrehearsed arrangement has not defended him at all.

There is a second-order effect worth naming, because it changes in-match decisions. If the defensive plan is to concede the outside route and force deliveries from further out, the plan will produce more blocked crosses, and blocked crosses produce corners. A side installing this instruction should expect its corner concession rate to rise and should be prepared for that rather than surprised by it. The trade is still correct, since a corner is a more defendable event than a byline cutback, but it is a trade rather than a free gain and it should be counted.

The full treatment of dead-ball delivery, including the marking schemes and the rehearsal that answers a specialist taker, belongs to [the article that owns defending elite set-piece takers](/2030/06/01/defending-elite-set-piece-takers/), and this section marks the boundary rather than crossing it.

## Reading the flank in the first fifteen minutes

A plan is a hypothesis, and the first fifteen minutes are when it gets tested. Four observations, all available without equipment, tell a coach whether the instruction is holding.

The first is the delivery band. If three or more of the first handful of deliveries come from the byline band, the forcing objective has failed and the correction is distance rather than effort: the full-back is engaging and getting beaten cheaply, and telling him to try harder will make it worse. Telling him to start a metre deeper will fix it.

The second is the hip position. If the deliveries are coming from an acceptable band but the crosser's hips are open, the defender is on the touchline side of the crossing foot. This is an approach-angle correction and it can be made in twenty seconds at the first stoppage, which makes it the highest-value in-match intervention available in wide defending.

The third is the screen. Watch the near-side midfielder rather than the ball for one sustained attack. If he is drifting wide to help the full-back, the cutback lane is open and the next dangerous moment will come from there. The instruction is to leave him alone and hold the edge, and it has to be repeated because the drift is instinctive rather than deliberate.

The fourth is the opponent's adjustment. A crossing side denied the byline band will do exactly one of two things: switch to the opposite flank, or drop the crosser deeper to receive facing the pitch and deliver earlier. Knowing which one is coming is worth more than any pre-match information, because the answers differ. The switch is answered by preparing the far-side full-back identically before kick-off. The deeper reception is answered by pushing the near-side midfielder up rather than across, which is the opposite of the screening instruction and therefore has to be an explicit change rather than a drift.

A coach who takes only one of these four should take the second, because the approach angle is the cheapest thing to fix and the most expensive thing to leave alone.
## The amateur version of the whole plan

Everything above assumes a coach can rehearse a box shape and brief a screening midfielder. Most cannot, so the plan needs a compressed version that survives one training session a week and a squad that changes every Saturday.

Three instructions carry nearly all the value and they can be delivered in ninety seconds before kick-off.

The first is to the full-back and it is one sentence: stay two metres off him, keep your body between his kicking foot and our goal, and let him go outside. That single instruction produces most of the benefit, because it changes the approach angle, which is the variable with the largest effect and the one that never corrects itself. The reason it works without any of the supporting structure is that it does not depend on anyone else doing anything.

The second is to the nearest midfielder and it is also one sentence: when the ball goes wide, you stand on the corner of the penalty area and you do not go and help. This is the hardest instruction to get obeyed at amateur level, because helping is what a well-intentioned player does, and it is the instruction that prevents the goal type that decides most amateur matches, which is the ball pulled back to an unmarked runner arriving at the edge of the box.

The third is to the goalkeeper and it is a rule rather than an instruction: you come for balls travelling toward you, you stay for balls travelling across you. Amateur goalkeepers are routinely encouraged to be brave, and bravery applied to a ball crossing the face of goal produces the goalkeeper stranded beyond the ball with an open net, which is the single most demoralising goal a side can concede.

What an amateur coach should not attempt is the full box-shape system. Three different arrangements triggered by delivery band require rehearsal time that does not exist, and a half-installed system is worse than a simple one because players hesitate between two answers. The workable compression is one shape used always: near-post contact assigned to a named player, screen on the corner of the area, everyone else takes a runner and watches the runner rather than the ball. It is not optimal against any single delivery and it is never badly wrong against any of them, which is the correct trade when rehearsal time is the binding constraint.

The fourth thing worth doing costs nothing and compounds: after the match, write down which two positions on the pitch the dangerous deliveries came from. Do that for a season and a coach knows more about how his own side concedes than any amount of general defensive coaching would have told him.

## What this article deliberately does not answer

Two questions sit close to this material and belong elsewhere, and marking them prevents the plan from being over-extended.

The first is the general decision about whether to show a wide opponent inside or outside. This article gives the answer for one specific opponent type, the delivery specialist, and the answer for that type is to concede the outside step in order to occupy the crossing foot. That answer does not generalise. Against a pace threat, an inverted finisher, or a two-footed carrier the calculation is entirely different, and it turns on variables this article has not examined: the height of the block, the recovery speed behind the full-back, and above all whether the covering centre-back can travel. The general decision belongs to its own treatment and reading this article as a universal instruction would be a serious misapplication.

The second is the individual duel technique itself, meaning the footwork, the jockeying, and the body mechanics of the one-on-one. Those are a craft with their own progressions and their own failure modes, and they are owned elsewhere in the series. This article has treated the duel as a geometry problem because that is what it becomes against a delivery specialist, and the geometry framing is incomplete against opponents whose threat is the take-on itself.

Holding both boundaries matters because the temptation with a clean rule is to apply it everywhere. The angle-not-the-man rule is a rule about objectives rather than about technique, and its scope is opponents whose final action is a delivery. Inside that scope it is close to complete. Outside it, it is one input among several.
## The limitations, stated as a coach would state them

Every profile has conditions under which it produces less, and naming them here is planning rather than diminishment.

The first is dependence on the reception. The sequence requires the ball to arrive in a wide position with the pitch in front of him, and a side that pressed the supply removed the whole apparatus without ever defending the duel. Any wide threat whose value begins with a specific reception is more disruptable upstream than in the duel itself, and this one was no exception.

The second is dependence on box occupation, treated at length above. Output was contingent on who was attacking the ball, which produces variance across squads and across matches, and a plan that emptied the box of quality reduced the flank's value without touching the flank.

The third is the vulnerability of a delivery-first profile to compression. A compact block that denies entry to the wide areas in the attacking third denies the crossing position, and compression is the defensive development that has advanced most since the period in question. A modern low block would concede fewer of the byline positions this game depended on, not through better duelling but through the pitch being shorter.

The fourth is the out-of-possession cost, which is the honest constraint on the profile in contemporary terms. The role as he played it did not carry the pressing workload now expected of a wide player, and a squad choosing between a specialist deliverer and a competent deliverer who also presses is making a real trade rather than an obvious one.

### What was Luis Figo's biggest weakness?

The threat was contingent rather than self-sufficient. It required a specific reception in a wide position, which made it disruptable upstream by pressing the supply, and it required quality occupation of the penalty area, which meant the flank could be won repeatedly with little scoreboard effect against a well-defended box.

## The plan, assembled

The analysis assembles into a sequence, and stating it in order is more useful than leaving it distributed.

Begin by naming the priority order out loud in the team meeting: delivery first, set piece second, open-play shot third. Most sides invert the first and third without ever deciding to, and saying it prevents the drift.

Press the supply as the primary intervention. The near-side midfielder's job is to make the pass into his wide starting position late, difficult, or impossible. This removes the sequence rather than defending it, and it costs the midfielder his attacking position, which should be acknowledged so he does not quietly stop doing it.

Instruct the full-back on the angle rather than the man. Starting distance of roughly two metres, approach on the goal side of the crossing foot rather than the touchline side, concede the outside step deliberately, retreat at the crosser's speed, never engage, front-foot block on the plant, and recover to the near edge of the six-yard box once the cross is gone.

Forbid the double team except in two cases: a second defender arriving goal-side during the ball's travel toward the winger, and the last fifteen minutes of a match being protected. Otherwise the near-side midfielder screens the edge of the area and stays there.

Link the box shape to the delivery band by a spoken rule. High and compact for deep deliveries, fixed near-post contact for driven balls from the edge of the area, retreat and screen with far-side defenders watching runners for byline deliveries. The box does not settle until the delivery position is clear, and the two seconds of unsettled shape are the price of arriving in the correct one.

Give the goalkeeper a rule rather than a judgment: on the line for deep deliveries, deep and near-post for driven balls, and attack only balls travelling toward him from the byline area. Have him make his positional calls to defenders two seconds before the delivery rather than during the flight.

Decide the box-resource question before kick-off based on the opponent's target rather than on the crosser. Strong aerial focal point means an extra body in the area; no focal point means the resource goes to the screen at the edge.

And log two marks per delivery during the match: the band it came from and whether the hips were open. That record is the whole of the in-match diagnostic, it takes no equipment, and it tells a coach by the half hour whether the full-back's instruction is working or whether the flank needs reinforcing.

## Verdict

Defenders stopped Luis Figo by changing what stopping him meant. The sides that struggled were the ones that treated the wide duel as a contest for the ball, because that framing rewards engagement, engagement produces the cheap loss, and the cheap loss produces the byline delivery that was the actual danger. The sides that coped conceded the duel in a controlled way and took the delivery position instead, which meant conceding a high volume of crosses and a low volume of dangerous ones.

The angle-not-the-man rule is the portable version of that finding. Against a delivery specialist the defensive objective is the position of the cross rather than the possession of the ball, which means a full-back can be beaten and still have defended correctly, and can win the ball and still have defended badly. Everything else in this article is an implementation of that single inversion: the goal-side approach on the crossing foot, the retreat at the crosser's speed, the box shape triggered by the delivery band rather than by the ball going wide, the goalkeeper's rule, the screen that must not be pulled wide to double, and the measurement that logs delivery location rather than duels won.

The reason the inversion is hard to install is not that it is complicated. It is that it requires a coach to praise a defender who has just been beaten, and to say nothing when a defender wins a duel in a way that produced a dangerous cross. Every incentive on a touchline runs the other way, and until the incentive changes the instruction will not survive contact with a Saturday.

The wider class this profile belongs to, and the other mechanisms by which wide players separate from defenders, is set out in [the cluster guide to containing the greatest dribblers](/2021/09/01/greatest-dribblers-how-to-contain/), which owns the general containment question and which this article routes to rather than restating.
## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did defenders stop Luis Figo?

By changing the objective rather than by winning the duel. The sides that coped conceded the outside step deliberately, kept a defender goal-side of his crossing foot so the ball had to be struck across his own body, retreated at his speed without engaging, and forced the delivery from further out with his hips closing. That produces a high volume of conceded crosses and a low volume of dangerous ones. The sides that struggled treated the duel as a contest for the ball, because that framing rewards engagement, engagement produces the cheap loss, and the cheap loss produces the byline delivery that was the actual danger. The other method that worked well was upstream: pressing the pass into his wide starting position so the reception never happened cleanly, which removes the sequence rather than defending it.

### Q: What made Figo's delivery so dangerous?

Variety produced from approaches that stayed ambiguous until very late. Three deliveries recur in a varied crosser's repertoire and each attacks a different defensive commitment: the early ball to the back post beats a line that has stepped up to compress the box, the flat driven ball across the six-yard box beats a box that has settled into a marking arrangement because it is too fast to mark and must be attacked at first contact, and the cutback beats a box that has retreated toward its own goal. The three defensive responses are mutually exclusive, so a box facing a crosser who can produce all of them is choosing which goal to concede rather than whether to concede one. The disguise was in the similarity of the approach rather than in any individual strike.

### Q: What was Luis Figo's biggest weakness?

The threat was contingent rather than self-sufficient, and both contingencies are where a plan against it lives. It required a specific reception in a wide position with the pitch in front of him, which made it disruptable upstream by pressing the supply rather than by defending the duel. And its output depended on quality occupation of the penalty area, which meant the flank could be won repeatedly with very little scoreboard effect against a box that was well defended. Beyond those two, a delivery-first profile is vulnerable to compression, since a compact block that denies entry to the wide areas in the attacking third denies the crossing position without contesting anything, and compression is the defensive development that has advanced most since that period.

### Q: How do you defend a wide player who beats you in order to cross?

Take the delivery position rather than the ball, which means accepting that being beaten is not the failure. Start about two metres off rather than tight, approach on the goal side of his crossing foot rather than the touchline side, concede the outside step deliberately, and retreat at exactly his speed without ever engaging. The purpose is to make him travel as far as possible before he can strike, because every metre closer to the byline costs him options while you remain in the way. Present the front foot at the moment he plants, with your body sideways on and no lunge. Then recover to the near edge of the six-yard box rather than following him. Finally, match the box shape behind you to the band he was forced into.

### Q: Which defenders coped well with Figo?

Method rather than aggression separated them, and three approaches recur. The disciplined shepherd conceded the outside step, stayed goal-side of the crossing foot, and forced deliveries from further out, which looks poor in a match report and good on the scoreboard. The structural denial pressed his supply so that the reception happened facing his own goal or did not happen at all, which is the highest-return approach available against any wide threat and which costs the near-side midfielder his attacking position. The box-first approach conceded the flank entirely and invested everything in the penalty area, with a fixed near-post contact, a screen at the edge, and a goalkeeper with a stated rule. What consistently failed was the aggressive individual duel that won plenty of balls and conceded the few byline deliveries that decided matches.

### Q: Should a defender block the cross or stay on his feet?

Stay on his feet in almost every case. A sliding block that misses removes the defender from the sequence entirely and converts a contested delivery into an unopposed one, and the probability of missing is high against a player who is looking at your feet and choosing his moment. The correct action is the front-foot block: body sideways on, the front foot presented at the moment the crosser plants, no lunge and no commitment of the weight. Sideways on matters more than the foot itself, because a defender square to the crosser presents his shins and blocks nothing while a defender sideways on presents the length of his leg across the low driven lane. The slide belongs to genuinely last-ditch situations inside the six-yard box where no cover exists.

### Q: How did sides deny Figo the outside route?

Mostly by not trying to, which is the answer that surprises people. Denying the outside route requires standing on the touchline side of the crossing foot, and standing there leaves the delivery lane open, which trades the smaller threat for the larger one. The sides that handled him best conceded the outside step on purpose and stayed goal-side of his right foot, so that every delivery had to be struck across his own body with his hips closing. Where the outside route was genuinely denied, it was denied structurally rather than individually, by compressing the pitch so that he never received in the attacking third wide areas, or by pressing his supply so that the reception happened facing his own goal.

### Q: Did doubling up on Figo work?

Rarely, and it frequently made things worse. A double team is an instrument for taking the ball or closing a route, and against a delivery specialist neither is the point, since he needs a yard and a fixed foot rather than somewhere to run. The second defender usually arrived from the inside, which is the touchline side of the crossing foot, leaving the delivery lane more open than one correctly positioned defender would have left it. Worse, the doubling player was almost always the near-side midfielder screening the edge of the penalty area, so doubling removed the defender of the cutback in order to contest a cross from further out. The two exceptions are a second defender arriving goal-side during the ball's travel, and the closing stages of a lead being protected.

### Q: How did Figo vary his delivery?

Through three distinct balls produced from approaches a defender could not tell apart until late. The early ball to the back post arrives behind a defensive line that has stepped up, into the area the far centre-back vacated by stepping. The flat driven ball across the six-yard box is too fast to be marked and has to be attacked at first contact, which beats a box that has settled into a marking arrangement. The cutback, struck back toward the penalty spot from close to the byline, arrives behind a box that has retreated toward its own goal, meeting a runner going forward at a stationary defence. Because compressing beats the cutback and loses to the early ball, and dropping deep does the reverse, the variety forced an unwinnable choice on the defending box.

### Q: Was Figo a goal threat himself?

Yes, sufficiently that leaving him unattended in the penalty area was never acceptable, and any plan treating a delivery specialist as harmless once he is inside the box will concede to him. He was also a dead-ball threat, which removes the wide duel entirely and makes set pieces a separate problem requiring a rehearsed marking scheme rather than a forcing objective. His open-play shooting threat was real but secondary, concentrated in situations where the cut inside was available against a block that was not compact. That ordering is what determines the concession: the priority is delivery first, set piece second, open-play shot third, and most sides that got this wrong got it wrong by inverting the first and third.

### Q: How did box marking change when Figo had the ball wide?

It should have changed with his position rather than with his possession, and failing to make that distinction is what produces most crossing goals. Three shapes cover almost everything. Deep deliveries call for a high compact shape with the far centre-back holding the highest position and the goalkeeper on his line. Driven balls from the wide area level with the edge of the box call for a fixed near-post contact assigned to a specific player who attacks the flight regardless of who is near him. Byline deliveries call for retreat and screen, with far-side defenders watching runners rather than the ball. The habit that makes it work is not settling the shape until the delivery position is clear, which costs two uncomfortable seconds.

### Q: Did Figo drift inside to receive?

Yes, though for a different purpose than an inverted winger's. An inverted winger comes inside to shoot or to enter the penalty area. A delivery specialist comes inside to change the geometry of his own next action, because a ball received in the half-space and moved back out to the touchline arrives at a crosser who is facing the pitch with the defender dragged out of position. The drift is a manufacturing process for a better crossing position rather than a threat in itself. The defensive answer is a pass-on rule agreed before the match: when he crosses into the half-space he becomes the near-side midfielder's problem and the full-back holds the flank, which prevents the sequence where three defenders converge and the vacated space is filled by an overlap.

### Q: How did goalkeepers position for Figo's crosses?

By varying the start with the delivery angle rather than holding one position for every cross. Against deep deliveries the goalkeeper stays on his line or a step off it, because the ball travels toward him on a converging path and advancing gains nothing while creating an empty net behind. Against driven balls from the wide area level with the edge of the box, the start moves toward the near post and stays deep, because that is the fastest ball he will face and depth buys reaction time. Against byline deliveries he needs a rule rather than a judgment: attack only balls travelling toward him and never balls travelling away across the six-yard box, because a ball crossing the face of goal must be intercepted at a point rather than met.

### Q: What can a young winger learn from Figo's crossing?

That the separation requirement is far smaller than young players assume. A yard and a fixed foot is enough to deliver, and a yard can be manufactured with a change of pace and a body feint by a player of ordinary speed, whereas the five-metre gap a pace threat needs is not available to everyone. That the striking practice worth doing is from awkward positions rather than comfortable ones: off balance, with the body closing, on the move, and from the second touch rather than the third. That variety matters most when the deliveries come from approaches a defender cannot distinguish, so the approach is the thing to rehearse rather than the strike. And that the inside drift is a manufacturing process rather than a flourish.

### Q: Would Figo be as effective in a modern possession side?

He would be used differently rather than less. Positional possession structures generate fewer isolated wide receptions in transition, which reduces one supply route. They also create a specific and growing need for exactly this skill, because a side dominating the ball against a compact low block faces a problem with no positional solution, and the two remaining answers are the shot from distance and the delivery from wide. A varied crosser producing three deliveries from the same shape into rehearsed box occupation is the most direct answer available to a block drilled to defend the middle. The genuine constraint is not tactical but physical: modern wide roles carry an out-of-possession workload the role did not previously demand, which is a squad-building trade rather than a tactical one.

### Q: Which defender profile suited facing Figo?

A positionally disciplined full-back with an unusually flat attention curve, because the duel lasts eight or ten seconds rather than two and a single lapse concedes the striking position. He needs the temperament to be beaten toward the byline repeatedly without responding to it, since conceding the outside step is part of the plan and a defender who treats each concession as a personal defeat will start engaging. He needs a clean front-foot block on his feet. Pace is close to irrelevant, because the duel never becomes a race, which makes a slower positionally excellent defender a good selection here and a poor one against a pace threat. The supporting requirements matter equally: a covering centre-back who travels, and a midfielder who screens rather than doubles.
