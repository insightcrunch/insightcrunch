Marxist economics is routinely described, by opponents who have not looked and by sympathisers who have not looked either, as a doctrine that no evidence could ever touch. The description survives because it is convenient to both camps. A critic who believes the theory sealed against refutation need never examine its numbers, and a defender who believes the same thing need never produce any. Against that shared convenience stands an inconvenient fact: a body of empirical work inside this tradition exists, some of it built with standard econometric tools, some of it published in journals read well beyond the tradition's own circle, and some of it strong enough that economists with no Marxian commitments have taken its models up and estimated them on their own data. The honest answer to the testability question is therefore divided. Parts of the programme have been tested and have survived testing in qualified form. Parts can be measured only after a translation of official statistics whose rules are contested, so that the measurement inherits the contest. And one part, the existence of value magnitudes apart from the prices that supposedly express them, cannot be tested at all with any data that exists.

This article maps that divided answer rather than arguing for one side of it. Its centrepiece is the single strongest case the tradition can offer: a formally specified model of the wage and employment cycle, published in 1967, which turned a Marxian mechanism into mathematics precise enough to fail, and which has since been fitted to the records of several economies by researchers working both inside and outside the tradition. Around that centrepiece the article arranges the rest of the empirical programme, component by component, stating what each part claims, whether anyone has modelled it formally, whether anyone has estimated it, and what the estimation showed. It then turns to the obstacles, and states them as plainly as the tradition's critics could wish. The deepest obstacle is not a shortage of data. It is the absence of an agreed rule for translating official statistics into Marxian categories, a difficulty this article names the classification convention problem, and which the series has by now encountered in four separate empirical literatures. Readers who want the findings of the component literatures in full should follow the routes given along the way; the task here is the map, the verdict on testability, and an honest account of what good practice would require.

## The short answer is a divided one

### Can Marxist economics be tested empirically?

Partly. A formal model of the wage and employment cycle has been estimated on real data for several economies. Rates of profit, exploitation, and price-value correlations have all been measured. What cannot be tested is the existence of value magnitudes independently of prices.

That division, between the testable, the convention-bound, and the untestable, is the finding around which everything else in this article is organised. It will disappoint readers who arrived hoping for a verdict of guilty or innocent, because falsifiability is not a single property that a research programme either possesses or lacks. A programme is a bundle of claims held together by a vocabulary and a set of habits. Some claims in the bundle are stated sharply enough to be wrong, and have been sharpened further by formal modelling until data could judge them. Others are stated in terms that require translation before any dataset can speak to them, and the translation rules are themselves part of what the rival schools dispute. Still others are framework commitments, propositions about what exists and what explains what, which guide the construction of testable claims without being testable in their own right. Nearly every mature research programme in economics has all three layers. What distinguishes programmes is how much of their weight rests on each layer, and how honestly the layers are labelled. The wider methodological question, whether the tradition as a whole deserves the name of science, is treated in the series article on [whether Marxism qualifies as a science](/2019/10/01/is-marxism-a-science/); the concern here is narrower and more practical, namely which specific claims have met data and with what outcome.

The charge of unfalsifiability also has a history that the honest answer should acknowledge rather than dodge. In its classic form, pressed by Karl Popper in the middle of the twentieth century, the charge was not that Marxian claims were all vague. It was that a theory which began by making risky predictions had been protected, after the predictions failed, by reinterpretations that absorbed every disappointment as further confirmation. Whatever one thinks of that verdict on the tradition's political doctrines, the habit it describes is a real temptation in empirical work too. A researcher who responds to every adverse result by adjusting the classification rule until the result turns friendly is not testing anything. Parts of the empirical literature discussed below have been accused of exactly that manoeuvre, and the accusation cannot be waved away, because the classification step gives it room to operate. The defence of the programme therefore cannot rest on pointing at the existence of empirical papers. It has to rest on the cases where the result could have come out against the claim and the researchers reported what they found anyway, and on standards that make the protective manoeuvre visible when it happens.

There is a second preliminary point, about what the evidence in this field can and cannot establish even at its best. Suppose every component test went the tradition's way: the cycle model fitted beautifully, profit rates moved as the tendency arguments suggest, price and value magnitudes tracked one another across whole economies. The theory as a whole would still not stand vindicated, because a research programme is more than the sum of its tested components, and because rival programmes can often accommodate the same findings inside different explanations. Conversely, a run of adverse results on particular components would damage specific claims without dissolving the vocabulary overnight. Empirical work in this field, as in every other field that studies whole economies, disciplines claims one at a time. Anyone who cites a single estimation as settling the standing of Marxist economics, in either direction, is misusing the evidence, and one purpose of the map offered here is to make that misuse harder by showing exactly which component each result belongs to.

## What testing Marxist economics involves

The empirical programme, stated as a definition, comprises attempts to measure value-price relations, rates of exploitation, rates of profit, distributional shares, and the dynamics that link wages, employment, and profitability, using national accounts data reclassified according to Marxian categories. Each element of that definition carries weight. The objects to be measured are defined by the theory, not by the statistical office. The data were collected for other purposes, by agencies answering to finance ministries and central banks, inside categories designed for Keynesian demand management and tax administration. And the bridge between the two, the reclassification, is where nearly every difficulty in this literature is born. The concepts themselves, the labour theory of value, the rate of exploitation, and the rate of profit, are explained in their own articles in this series and are linked rather than re-derived here, because the subject of this article is not what the categories mean but what happens when someone tries to count with them.

A test, in the sense used throughout this article, has three parts. First, a claim stated sharply enough that some imaginable dataset would count against it. Second, a stated rule for translating the available statistics into the claim's terms, fixed before the answer is known. Third, a comparison between what the claim leads one to expect and what the translated data show, reported whether the comparison flatters the claim or not. Much of what passes for empirical argument about Marxist economics, on both sides of the question, skips at least one of these parts. Assertions that the theory is obviously confirmed by the existence of crises skip the first and third. Assertions that it is obviously untestable skip the second, by ignoring the translation work that researchers have in fact published and defended in print. Holding all three parts in view at once is the discipline this article tries to maintain, and it is the standard against which the component literatures are graded in the map below.

### What does a test of a Marxian claim actually require?

A test requires a claim sharp enough to fail, a translation rule for the statistics that is fixed before results are known, and a comparison between expectation and outcome that is reported either way. Where any of the three is missing, argument replaces evidence and the claim remains untested.

The translation rule deserves emphasis because it is the element outsiders most often miss. National accounts sort economic life into industries, institutional sectors, and income types. Marxian categories sort it differently: into production that creates value and activity that redistributes it, into labour that is exchanged against capital and labour exchanged against revenue, into the portions of the social product that replace used-up inputs, sustain the workforce, and remain as surplus. No statistical office collects its figures in those sorts. Every empirical Marxist therefore begins by building a concordance, a set of decisions about which official categories count as which Marxian ones, and the decisions are numerous and genuinely contestable. Whether the labour of bank employees counts as productive, whether government output enters the surplus or stands outside it, whether the incomes of supervisors belong with wages or with the surplus they help to extract: each answer moves the resulting rates, sometimes enough to reverse a trend. A test in this literature is thus always a joint test of the claim and the concordance, and honest reporting has to say so.

### Why are directions safer evidence than levels?

A level, such as the height of a profit rate in a given year, is manufactured jointly by the data and by the classification rule, so a rival rule manufactures a different level from identical statistics. A direction of movement, sustained across rival rules, is far harder to manufacture.

That asymmetry governs the evidential hierarchy of the whole field. Evidence counts for most exactly where it reports directions, sequences, and turning points, and for least where it reports heights, because heights are the quantities a rulebook can move without touching a single observation.

That asymmetry, between levels that shift with conventions and directions that often survive them, recurs throughout the map. It explains why the most robust findings reported below are about the shape of movements: the phase pattern of a cycle, the broad drift of a share over decades, the co-movement of prices and values across thousands of commodities. It also explains why the most sterile disputes in the field are arguments about levels conducted as if the levels were facts of nature rather than artefacts of a stated rule. A researcher who announces that the rate of exploitation stood at some precise height in some year is making a claim about a concordance as much as about an economy, and a reader who repeats the figure without the concordance is repeating an artefact. None of this is peculiar to Marxist economics, a point developed later in this article, since every tradition that uses national accounts inherits conventions of its own. What is peculiar here is the size of the gap between the categories the theory needs and the categories the statisticians supply, which makes the translation step larger, more visible, and more consequential than in most rival programmes.

## The growth cycle at the centre of the evidence

The strongest exhibit in the whole empirical programme began as a mechanism described in prose. Marx argued, in passages scattered across the first volume of Capital and the notebooks published as Theories of Surplus Value, that accumulation disciplines itself through the labour market. When capital accumulates quickly, the demand for workers rises, the reserve of unemployed labour shrinks, and wages climb. Climbing wages eat into the surplus that accumulation feeds on. Accumulation then slackens, demand for workers falls, the reserve army is replenished, and wage growth stalls until profitability recovers and the wheel turns again. As a piece of prose reasoning the mechanism is plausible, quotable, and almost useless for testing, because prose mechanisms can be narrated over any episode whatever. A boom followed by a slump can always be retold as the wheel turning, whatever actually drove it. For a century the wage and employment cycle lived in that condition: widely believed inside the tradition, widely ignored outside it, and incapable of being wrong.

In 1967 the American economist Richard Goodwin, then teaching at Cambridge, changed the mechanism's condition. In a chapter titled A Growth Cycle, contributed to a volume of essays honouring Maurice Dobb, Goodwin wrote the mechanism down as a formal system with two moving parts. The first moving part is the rate of employment, the share of the workforce holding jobs. The second is the share of output going to wages. Between them he placed a pair of opposed effects. A high rate of employment strengthens the bargaining position of workers, so the wage share rises. A high wage share leaves a thinner margin of profit, and since accumulation in the model is financed from profits, accumulation slows, and employment falls back. Falling employment weakens bargaining power, the wage share subsides, the profit margin fattens, accumulation recovers, and employment climbs again. Neither variable settles. Each one's movement plants the seed of its own reversal, and the pair trace a closed circuit, rising and falling in a fixed sequence, like the populations of predators and prey in the ecological models whose mathematical shape Goodwin borrowed. The cycle needs no external shock to sustain it. The economy's own distributional conflict is the engine.

### How does the growth cycle model work?

Two variables drive one another in a loop. When employment runs high, bargaining power lifts the wage share; a lifted wage share squeezes profits and slows accumulation, so employment falls; weakened bargaining then lets the wage share subside, profitability recovers, and accumulation carries employment upward again.

Every element of that description is a commitment that observation can check. The model does not merely predict that economies oscillate; many mechanisms predict that. It predicts an order of events around the loop. Employment crests first, while the wage share is still climbing. The wage share crests next, after employment has begun to give ground. Employment bottoms out while the wage share is still subsiding, and the wage share bottoms out last, once recovery is under way. Plotted against one another across a full swing, the two series should trace a circuit rather than a line, travelling around the loop in one consistent direction. A dataset in which the wage share led employment instead, or in which the two moved together and apart without sequence, would count against the model in a way no reinterpretation could comfortably absorb. This is what formal specification buys. It converts a story that fits everything into a pattern that fits one thing and excludes others.

Goodwin built the model in deliberate austerity. In its bare form it assumes that workers consume what they earn and owners invest what they receive, that techniques change in a steady background rhythm or not at all, and that the state, the banks, and foreign trade stand outside the frame. Nobody, including its author, mistook those assumptions for descriptions. Their purpose was the purpose of all such scaffolds: to isolate one mechanism and exhibit its pure behaviour, so that the mechanism's signature could be recognised when it appears mixed with others in real data. Later builders in the same line added the missing floors to the scaffold, introducing technical change that responds to wage pressure, capacity utilisation that moves with demand, public spending, and financial conditions, and each addition changed some property of the cycle while leaving the central loop recognisable. The model thereby became a family rather than a single artefact, and the empirical question became which member of the family, if any, matches the record of a given economy.

### Why does formal specification matter for testability?

A mechanism described only in prose can be stretched to fit almost any episode, since its terms bend. Written as a formal system, the same mechanism acquires commitments about sequence and phase that observation can contradict. Specification converts an interpretation of cycles into a hypothesis about them.

The point generalises beyond this one model, and it explains the shape of the whole testability map. The components of the programme that have been formalised are, without exception, the components that have been genuinely tested, because formalisation is what forces a claim to declare its variables, the direction of each effect, and the conditions under which the effect holds. The components that resist formalisation are the ones still waiting for a test. Marx's cycle intuition waited ninety years for its Goodwin. The theory of value magnitudes has waited longer, and the reason is not lack of ingenuity among its defenders. A magnitude that is defined as existing prior to, and independently of, the prices it supposedly governs cannot be written into a system alongside those prices without either collapsing into them or floating free of every observable. Estimation needs observables. Where a programme's central magnitude has no observable counterpart, the programme can be tested at its edges, in its implications for things that can be counted, but the centre itself stays beyond the reach of data. That boundary, between a testable edge and an untestable centre, is the single most important line on the map.

## The estimation record of the cycle

A model earns its place at the centre of an empirical programme only when people bother to fit it to records, and the growth cycle has been fitted many times. Beginning in the decades after its publication and continuing in waves since, researchers have estimated versions of the model on the postwar records of the large industrial economies, the United States and Britain prominent among them, and on the records of smaller European economies as data permitted. The estimators came from more than one camp. Marxian economists estimated it, as one would expect. So did mainstream macrodynamicists who had no stake in the tradition's standing and some of whom arrived at the model through the mathematics of nonlinear cycles rather than through any interest in Marx. That second group matters evidentially. A finding reproduced by researchers who would gain nothing from its success carries a different weight from the same finding reported only by believers, and the cycle is one of the few places in this literature where the reproduction has genuinely crossed the camp boundary.

### What did the estimations of the cycle find?

Fitted to the records of several economies, the model reproduced the broad circuit the theory predicts: employment and the wage share chasing one another around a loop with the expected sequence of peaks. Support was strongest for the pattern's existence, weakest for its exact timing and strength.

The broad circuit deserves a word of unpacking, because it is the kind of result this article counts as strong. When the wage share and the employment rate of an economy are plotted against each other across several decades, the points do not scatter formlessly, nor do they line up along a single relation. In the economies where the model has been most carefully estimated, they travel around a loop, and the direction of travel around the loop matches the model's sequence: employment cresting while the wage share climbs, the wage share cresting after employment turns, and so on through the four stations of the circuit. The loop in the data is ragged, its size varying from swing to swing, its period stretching and compressing, and no estimator claims otherwise. But a ragged loop traversed in the predicted direction is a different thing from no loop at all, and it is not a pattern the model could have produced by accident of construction. Economies run on mechanisms that could have left these two series unrelated, or related in the opposite sequence, and the fact that several national records show the predicted choreography is the closest thing this tradition possesses to a confirmed structural prediction.

### Where does the cycle evidence remain disputed?

Three disputes persist. The model's cycles keep a constant amplitude while real ones swell and shrink. Its timing of turning points fits some economies better than others. And its specified mechanism, bargaining strength, competes with explanations in which demand, capacity use, or pricing behaviour drives the loop.

Each dispute is instructive. The amplitude problem is a known artefact of the bare model's mathematics: its closed orbits conserve their size, so a cycle once established repeats at the same scale forever, whereas observed distributional cycles plainly grow and decay with wars, policy regimes, and financial conditions. Estimators treat this as a reason to enrich the model rather than as a refutation of the loop, and critics reply, reasonably, that a model which needs enrichment at exactly the point where it meets data has had its claim trimmed. The timing problem is empirical and varies by country, which is what one would expect if the loop is real but its speed is set by national institutions, labour law, union density, and wage-setting customs that the bare model ignores. The mechanism dispute is the deepest of the three, because the loop pattern alone cannot certify its own cause. A circuit in the data is consistent with Goodwin's bargaining story. It is also consistent with stories in which firms raise margins when demand is strong and concede wages when order books thin, with the causality running through pricing rather than through the bargaining table. Discriminating among those stories requires evidence about the links inside the loop, not merely the loop's outline, and the literature's frontier sits exactly there. None of this is fatal to the centrepiece. It is what a live empirical literature looks like from inside: a confirmed pattern, a family of rival explanations for it, and a shared understanding of which further observations would separate them.

One more feature of the cycle literature should be registered before leaving it, because it contrasts so sharply with the literatures mapped below. The wage share and the employment rate are, by the standards of this field, easy magnitudes. Statistical offices publish both, the concepts sit close to their Marxian counterparts, and the translation between the published figures and the model's variables is short and comparatively uncontentious, involving adjustments for the self-employed and for the boundary between wages and salaries that move the series at the margin rather than reversing them. The cycle could be tested as cleanly as it was partly because its variables survive translation with their identity intact. Where translation is long and contested, as it is for exploitation and profit, the testing story changes character entirely, and it is to those harder territories that the map now turns.

## The testability map

The whole programme can now be laid out on a single instrument. The testability map below takes one row per component and asks the same five questions of each. What exactly is claimed? Has the claim been given a formal model, so that its implications are pinned down? Has anyone then estimated that model, or measured that magnitude, on real records? What did the encounter with data show? And is the component open to testing at all, given the data that exist? Read down the rows and a gradient appears, and the gradient is the article's central exhibit. At the top sit claims about relations among observables, prices against values, employment against wage share, where modelling and estimation are both feasible and have both been done. In the middle sit the great rates of the system, exploitation and profit, which can be measured only after a contested translation and therefore inherit the contest. At the bottom sits the framework's innermost commitment, the existence of value magnitudes prior to prices, for which no measurement route has ever been produced. Testability in this programme is not a verdict pronounced on the whole. It is a slope, descending from the periphery of the theory toward its core.

Readers who keep structured research notes may find the map worth transferring into them, row by row, since each row is also a reading assignment pointing at one component literature. The [VaultBook study notes prepared for this series](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) include a ready-made card for the map, with the five questions as fields, so that a reader working through the component articles can record, for each component, what the literature actually delivered against what it promised.

| Component of the programme | What is claimed | Formally modelled | Estimated on data | What the results show | Testable at all |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Price and value relations | Market prices gravitate toward labour values | Yes, in input-output form | Yes, for many economies | Prices and computed values move closely together; the meaning of the closeness is disputed | Yes |
| Wage and employment cycle | Bargaining strength and profitability generate a distributional cycle | Yes, completely | Yes, for several economies | A circuit in the predicted sequence appears broadly; timing, size, and mechanism are argued over | Yes |
| Rate of exploitation | The surplus portion of new value follows the dynamics the theory assigns it | In part | Yes, under competing classifications | Long movements often agree in direction across rules; heights diverge sharply by rule | For directions |
| Rate of profit | Profitability carries a falling tendency under stated conditions | Yes, in rival variants | Yes, in many competing series | Whole series disagree about the long-run direction, and the disagreement tracks the classification used | Contested |
| Labour share dynamics | The wage share responds to bargaining power and the pace of accumulation | In part | Yes, chiefly in a neighbouring literature | Movements are tracked closely; their causes remain contested among several explanations | In part |
| Crisis tendencies | Accumulation breeds breakdown or stagnation pressures from within | Only in fragments | Seldom, and never directly | Support is gathered from episodes and episodes are open to rival narration | Weakly |
| Value magnitudes apart from prices | Values exist as magnitudes prior to the prices that express them | No operational form | Never | No route from any dataset to the magnitude itself has been produced | No |

Two cautions govern the use of the map. The first is that a row's position records the state of a literature, not the truth of a claim. The crisis row sits low because its claims have been left in narrative form, not because crises fail to happen; an unformalised claim may be true and merely untested, which is a different failing from being false, and a more repairable one. The second caution is that rows interact. The profit rate row cannot rise until the classification row beneath the whole table is settled, because every profit series is built on a concordance; the exploitation row labours under the same dependency. The map is therefore best read as a dependency chart as well as a scorecard. Several of its rows are waiting on one shared piece of unfinished business, and that business, the classification convention problem, gets its own section after the component routes have been walked.

## Prices and values: a measured relation with a disputed meaning

The oldest empirical literature in the programme concerns the relation between prices and values, and it is the natural first stop on the map's upper slope. The claim under examination is the one at the foundation of the labour theory of value: that the exchange ratios of commodities are governed, beneath the noise of supply and demand, by the labour time their production requires. Testing that claim became feasible when statistical offices began publishing input-output tables, the great grids that record what each industry buys from every other. With such a grid, a researcher can work out, for each sector of an economy, the total labour embodied directly and indirectly in a unit of its output, and set that computed value beside the sector's actual market price. Studies of this kind have been carried out for a succession of economies, among them the United States, Britain, Greece, and others as tables became available, by researchers in several countries and across several decades. The full account, with its methods and its critics, belongs to the series article on [the evidence bearing on the labour theory of value](/2010/10/01/labour-theory-of-value-evidence/); what the map needs from the literature is its shape.

### What have price and value studies established?

Computed labour values and market prices move together closely across the sectors of whole economies, in study after study and country after country. That association is the established finding. What the association means, and whether it is closer than rival explanations would lead one to expect, remains argued.

The association itself is worth pausing on, because it was not a foregone conclusion. Computed values are built from the physical and labour structure of production alone, with no reference to prices at any stage of the arithmetic. Prices emerge from markets, bargaining, taxation, and monetary conditions. Two independently constructed magnitudes could, in principle, have wandered far apart, and a generation of critics expected exactly that. Instead the two series track one another across hundreds of sectors with a closeness that surprised commentators on both sides. Whatever the ultimate explanation, the crude version of the untestability charge, the version in which value magnitudes float free of anything observable, does not survive this literature. Values as computed from input-output data are observable in the only sense that matters for this row of the map: they can be calculated from published statistics by a stated procedure, and their relation to prices can be inspected by anyone who repeats the calculation.

### Why is the closeness of prices and values argued about?

Because closeness alone cannot identify its cause. Large industries carry large values and large prices for reasons no theory disputes, so some association would appear even if values explained nothing. The argument concerns whether the observed association exceeds that background expectation by enough to credit the theory.

That argument has run for decades and has produced better method on both sides. Defenders of the literature have answered the size objection by comparing the price-value association against associations between prices and other candidate magnitudes, by examining deviations rather than totals, and by testing whether values predict the pattern of price changes when techniques shift. Critics have answered in turn that the benchmarks remain too forgiving and that the theory's actual claim, gravitation through time around centres set by values, is a claim about a moving process which cross-sectional snapshots cannot capture. Both the criticism and the replies are the ordinary currency of empirical science, and their existence matters for the map's verdict. This row records a literature in which a Marxian magnitude was defined operationally, computed for whole economies, compared with its observable counterpart, and argued about at the level of method for a generation. If the entire programme looked like this row, the testability question would have been closed long ago in the programme's favour. The rows below it show why the question stays open.

## Measuring the surplus: the rate of exploitation

Descend one row on the map and the ground changes. The rate of exploitation, the proportion of the new value produced in an economy that is appropriated as surplus rather than paid out as wages, is the tradition's central distributional magnitude. It is also a magnitude no statistical office reports, and reconstructing it from published accounts requires the longest act of translation in the whole programme. The full treatment, with its rival reconstructions set side by side, is the series article on [measuring the rate of exploitation](/2011/03/01/measuring-rate-of-exploitation/). The map needs the reason for the row's middling position, and the reason has a name: before any counting can start, the researcher must decide which workers count as producing the value to be divided.

### What must be settled before a rate of exploitation can be computed?

The boundary of productive labour. Official statistics pay every wage alike, while the category counts only labour exchanged against capital in production as value-creating. Where clerks, sales staff, guards, and officials are placed decides the size of both the surplus and the workforce that produces it.

Marx's own texts supply the principle and leave the border posts movable. Labour is productive, in the strict sense his categories require, when it is exchanged against capital and set to work producing commodities whose sale returns more value than the labour cost. Labour is unproductive, without any imputation of uselessness, when it is exchanged against revenue and consumed as a service, or when it belongs to the sphere of circulation, the buying, selling, bookkeeping, and guarding through which already-produced value changes hands. A teacher in a commercial school chain, a singer employed by a recording company, and a machinist all stand on one side of the line by this test; a teacher in a state school, a domestic servant, and a bank clerk stand on the other. The distinction is analytical, not moral, and generations of readers have stumbled over the vocabulary. For measurement, what matters is that the boundary runs through the middle of the modern service economy, so that placing it differently moves enormous volumes of wages from one side of the accounts to the other.

The most ambitious reconstruction programme, associated with Anwar Shaikh and Ahmet Tonak and published in book form as Measuring the Wealth of Nations in 1994, rebuilt the accounts of the United States across the postwar decades on a strict reading of the boundary. Production workers generate the value product; trade, finance, and government absorb portions of it as the costs of circulation and rule; the wage bill is split accordingly, and the resulting series for exploitation, profit, and the weight of unproductive activity are computed on that single consistent basis. Other researchers, Fred Moseley prominent among them, have rebuilt the same national accounts on a narrower reading of unproductive labour and arrived at series that differ in level and in some shorter movements while resembling the stricter reconstruction in broad outline. Between the rival rulebooks, certain findings recur: the measured rate rises across much of the postwar period in both, and the expansion of employment outside production registers in both as a growing claim on the surplus. The levels, by contrast, belong to the rulebooks as much as to the economy, and no honest citation of this literature quotes a level without naming the rule that produced it. The row therefore stands where the map places it. Directions, cautiously, can be tested. Levels cannot travel.

## The rate of profit: many series, no settled direction

The most contested row of the map belongs to the rate of profit, and its condition is the classification convention problem in its sharpest documented form. The claim at stake is the tradition's most famous tendency: that accumulation raises the mass of means of production set in motion by each worker faster than it raises the surplus extracted from that worker, so that profitability carries a downward pressure which counteracting influences may delay, disguise, or periodically reverse, but which reasserts itself across long periods. As a piece of theory the claim has been formalised repeatedly, and it has also been disciplined from within economics itself, most sharply by the result associated with Nobuo Okishio showing that cost-reducing techniques of the kind capitalists actually adopt raise the equilibrium rate of profit unless real wages rise to absorb the gain. That result did not end the argument; it relocated it, pushing defenders of the tendency toward versions resting on rising wages, on competition forcing over-accumulation, or on the valuation of capital through time. An empirical literature testing a claim whose defensible form is itself under negotiation starts with a handicap no dataset can remove.

The measurement literature is nevertheless large. Profit rate series for the United States, Britain, and other advanced economies have been constructed by Marxian research teams, by heterodox institutes, and by mainstream scholars computing conventional counterparts. And here the map must record the field's most uncomfortable datum. Rival Marxian series for the same economy over the same decades disagree not merely about the height of the rate but, in the most contested cases, about the direction of its long-run movement. One reconstruction shows a falling tendency interrupted by recoveries. Another shows a rate that fell into the early nineteen eighties and then recovered much of the lost ground. A third, built on different valuation choices, shows something closer to a long plateau with cyclical swings. Each builder can defend the rulebook. The capital stock can be valued at what was paid for it or at what replacing it would cost, and over decades of changing prices the two valuations diverge enough to tilt a trend. Finance can be counted inside the productive economy or placed among the claimants on its surplus. The state can be treated as an outside consumer of the surplus or as a department of the economy itself. Taxes can be taken before the rate is struck or after. None of these decisions is foolish, and together they are sufficient to manufacture contradictory verdicts from one statistical record.

### Why do profit rate series for one economy disagree?

Because each series is two objects at once: an economy and a rulebook. Valuation of the capital stock, the placement of finance and government, and the handling of taxes are decided differently by different builders, and over long spans those decisions are large enough to reverse the trend they feed into.

The full dossier on this literature, series by series and decision by decision, is assembled in the series article on [the empirical evidence for the rate of profit](/2013/12/01/rate-of-profit-empirical-evidence/), and the present article deliberately confines itself to what the dossier means for testability. Three salvages are worth noting. First, turning points travel better than trends: rival series tend to agree about when profitability peaked and when it troughed within business cycles, even when they disagree about the long slope, so the cyclical anatomy of profitability is firmer knowledge than its secular direction. Second, the disagreement is itself informative about magnitudes: it measures, roughly, how much of any published profit rate is bookkeeping and how much is economy, a calibration no single series could supply. Third, the field knows its own condition. The builders of rival series criticise one another's rulebooks in print, in detail, which is the behaviour of a literature that understands its problem even though it has not solved it. What the row cannot honestly claim is a verdict on the tendency itself. On the map's terms, the tendency stands contested: formalised, estimated many times over, and undecided because the measuring instrument is part of the dispute.

## The labour share: the neighbouring literature

One component of the programme is best approached through a literature that mostly does not speak the programme's language. National accountants have long computed the labour share, the portion of national income paid as compensation to employees, and a large mainstream literature tracks its movements. Across many advanced and emerging economies, that conventionally measured share drifted downward over the closing decades of the twentieth century and into the twenty-first, a movement documented widely enough to count among the more secure directional findings in distribution research, and debated as to cause among explanations invoking technology that substitutes for labour, the integration of low-wage producers into world markets, weakening bargaining institutions, and rising markups in concentrated industries. The series treats this evidence in its own right in the article on [the evidence concerning the labour share](/2021/03/01/labour-share-evidence/). Its relevance here is comparative and cautionary in equal measure.

Comparative, because the labour share literature demonstrates that cumulative measurement debate about distribution is possible at scale, with shared datasets, rival explanations tested against common evidence, and gradual sorting of what is robust from what is not. Cautionary, because the conventional labour share is not the Marxian wage share, and conflating them corrupts both literatures. The conventional figure counts every salary, including the pay of executives and of workers in circulation and government. It must impute a labour income to the self-employed, who receive a blend of wage and profit in a single payment, and the imputation is a convention famous enough to have its own methodological literature. It takes no position on the productive boundary that dominates the exploitation row. A Marxian reading that borrows the conventional share's decline as confirmation of its own distributional story is therefore borrowing a magnitude built to a different specification, and the borrowing should be flagged whenever it occurs. Honesty also requires a second flag, in the opposite direction. A broad decline in labour's share sits comfortably with the tradition's expectations about bargaining power, but comfort is not prediction: the tendency was documented by researchers outside the tradition, for reasons their own debates supplied, and a programme earns no confirmatory credit for accommodating, after the fact, a finding it did not stake itself on in advance. That distinction, between predicting a movement and absorbing one, will return when this article turns to the standards the field should be held to.

## The classification convention problem

Every difficulty encountered so far converges on a single obstacle, and the obstacle deserves the status of this article's namable claim. The classification convention problem is the proposition that the principal obstacle to empirical progress in Marxist economics is not a shortage of data, nor a shortage of ingenuity, but the absence of an agreed rule for translating official statistics into Marxian categories. The world's statistical offices publish abundant, careful, long-running data. What the field lacks is a shared concordance for reading them. In its absence, results are frequently incomparable across research teams, and disagreements that present themselves as arguments about the world are, on inspection, arguments about conventions. The claim is not an excuse invented to protect the programme from its results. It is a diagnosis with evidence behind it, namely the documented behaviour of the literatures already walked through: an exploitation literature whose levels belong to rulebooks, a profit rate literature whose rival series reverse one another's trends, and, standing against both, a cycle literature whose variables needed little translation and which therefore produced the programme's firmest result. The correlation between translation distance and evidential firmness, across the whole map, is the strongest argument that the diagnosis is correct.

### What decisions hide inside a reclassification?

Decisions about which labour creates value, how the capital stock is valued, whether finance and the state stand inside or outside the productive economy, how depreciation and taxes are struck, and how imputations are handled. Each is defensible. Together they fix a range inside which results must fall.

Walk through the anatomy of a single reclassification and the claim stops being abstract. The researcher begins with the published accounts of one economy for one run of years and must first draw the productive boundary, assigning every industry and occupation to the creation of value or to its circulation and administration. That is the largest single decision, and the theory guides it without dictating it, since the texts that define the boundary were written against a nineteenth-century economy whose service sector was a fraction of the modern one. Next the capital stock must be valued, at historic cost, which preserves what firms actually paid, or at replacement cost, which tracks what the means of production would fetch now, and the two series drift apart across any period of changing prices. Depreciation must be taken as the accountants report it or recalculated on an assumed lifespan of equipment. The output of banks must be handled somehow, although much of it is never sold at a stated price and the national accountants themselves impute it by formula. Owner-occupied housing generates, in the official accounts, an imputed rent that no one pays, and the researcher must decide whether a magnitude that exists only by statistical courtesy enters the value product. Government output is valued in the official books at its cost of production, a convention that guarantees the state can show no surplus, and the Marxian researcher must decide whether to accept that convention, relocate the state's wage bill among the claimants on surplus produced elsewhere, or rebuild the sector from its tax base. Taxes on production, subsidies, inventory profits during inflation, the earnings of the self-employed, the boundary between a wage and a salary drawn at some rung of the managerial ladder: the decisions accumulate into the dozens, and at every fork both paths have been taken by competent builders whose theoretical commitments are identical.

The deeper reason translation is treacherous here is a mismatch of sorting principles. Statistical categories are institutional. They sort the economy by industry, by legal form, and by the kind of unit that reports the figures, because those are the sorts a tax authority or a census can actually observe. Marxian categories are functional. They sort by the role an activity plays inside the circuit of capital: whether it produces value, realises value, guards value, or consumes the surplus that value production yields. A single institution routinely performs several functions at once. A supermarket chain produces nothing, yet its logistics operation moves goods in ways that shade into transport, its own-brand contracting shades into manufacture, and its wage bill mixes the labour of circulation with labour that any functional account must treat carefully. Translation between an institutional grid and a functional one is therefore not a clerical act. It is an act of interpretation repeated hundreds of times per dataset, and interpretations, unlike clerical rules, do not converge on their own. Two researchers can share a theory, share a dataset, share a decade of the same economy, compute with equal care, and publish opposed findings, with the entire opposition located in the concordance neither thought to argue about because each regarded it as mere technique.

## No shared rulebook: the missing accounting convention

It clarifies the problem to notice how the rest of economics avoids its mildest version. National accounting itself is a convention, a vast negotiated settlement about what counts as production, how to value what is never sold, and where the boundary of the economy runs. The settlement is codified in the System of National Accounts, maintained by international statistical bodies, and revised at long intervals, in 1968, in 1993, and in 2008, whereupon countries restate their historical series so that the past is measured by the rules of the present. Nobody supposes the settlement fell from the sky. Its categories embody decisions, some of them fiercely argued at the time, about the treatment of banking, of research, of military equipment. What the settlement provides is not truth but coordination: a single rulebook, publicly maintained, so that a figure computed in one country or one decade can be set beside a figure computed in another with a known margin of comparability. Mainstream empirical economics complains about the settlement constantly and depends on it absolutely.

Marxian economics has no equivalent instrument and no body capable of issuing one. Its schools, mapped in [the complete guide to Marxist economics](/2016/04/01/marxist-economics-complete-guide/), maintain their measurement practices the way its journals maintain their editorial lines, by reputation and inheritance rather than by charter. A research group settles its concordance, publishes series under it, trains students in it, and defends it against rivals whose concordances differ. Nothing in the field's institutional structure could convene the parties and bind them to a common rulebook, and the parties, it should be said plainly, do not all want one: a concordance is also a theoretical commitment, and some disputes about classification are genuine disputes about the theory wearing statistical dress. The consequence for testability is structural rather than scandalous. Comparability fails not because individuals are careless but because the coordinating instrument does not exist, and each new series, however scrupulous, adds one more dialect to a literature that needs a common language.

### What would an agreed accounting convention require?

A published concordance between official categories and Marxian ones, maintained by a body whose revisions the field accepts, with back-series recomputed when the rules change. National accounting itself works exactly this way. Marxian economics has journals and schools where such a body would need to be.

Two fairness points belong in this section, because the diagnosis can be abused in both directions. The first is that classification trouble is not unique to this tradition, and the article's neutrality on the point is a requirement, not a courtesy. Every magnitude in empirical macroeconomics rests on conventions: the capital stock is an accumulated sum of past investments deflated by contested price indices, productivity residuals are computed against production functions no one observes, and the treatment of finance in the official accounts has been revised repeatedly because the statisticians themselves could not settle what banks produce. A reader who concluded that Marxist economics is disqualified by having conventions, while its rivals proceed without any, would be drawing exactly the wrong moral. The second fairness point cuts the other way. The Marxian translation gap is genuinely larger than the gaps its rivals face, because the distance between functional categories and institutional data is longer than the distance between the national accountants' categories and the uses mainstream theory makes of them. Larger is a difference of degree with consequences of kind: the rivals' conventions move their results at the margin, while the concordance choices reviewed here can reverse the sign of the field's most famous tendency. Both points can be true at once, and the map's verdict depends on holding them together.

## A small literature and the scarcity of replication

Even a perfect concordance would leave the programme with a second weakness, simpler to state and harder to fix: there are not many people doing this work, and the work they do is seldom repeated by anyone else. Empirical Marxist economics is produced by a few dozen active research groups scattered across universities in several countries, publishing in a short list of journals, some belonging to the tradition and some hospitable to it. Concentration on that scale has consequences that operate quietly. Refereeing is done by the same small circle whose members built the rival series, so scrutiny, while often fierce, is scrutiny from inside the family of assumptions. Datasets assembled by one team remain that team's property in practice, described in articles closely enough to admire and seldom closely enough to rebuild. And replication, the unglamorous labour of reconstructing another researcher's figures from the raw accounts to see whether they stand, is close to absent.

### What makes replication rare in this literature?

Replication is unrewarded across economics, and here it carries an extra cost: before checking a result, the checker must rebuild the author's classification rule from descriptions that are often incomplete. Small teams, few journals, and privately held data files do the rest.

Part of that explanation is generic, and fairness requires saying so. Replication is unrewarded across the whole discipline of economics, where journals prize novelty and careers are built on new results rather than on the verification of old ones; whole subfields of the mainstream have discovered, when systematic replication projects finally examined them, that a share of their canonical findings could not be reproduced from the authors' own materials. The Marxian literature shares that general condition and then adds its own multiplier. Replicating a mainstream result usually means rerunning code on a published dataset. Replicating a Marxian result means first redoing the interpretive labour of the concordance, industry by industry and category by category, from prose descriptions that compress dozens of decisions into a methodological paragraph. Where the description is incomplete, the replicator must guess, and a failed replication then shows only that two concordances differ, which everyone knew. The literature's size completes the trap. Smallness alone does not make a field unreliable; economic history sustains solid cumulative work with a modest headcount, because its sources are public and its claims can be rechecked against archives anyone may visit. The infirmity here is not headcount but checkability. A result that only its author's team can recompute has, for the rest of the world, the epistemic status of a report rather than a finding, and too much of this programme's evidence still has that status.

## Testing where the theory is strong rather than where it is weak

A third weakness is a matter of aim. The field shows a documented tendency to direct its tests at the places where support is expected rather than at the places where the theory is most exposed. The friendly territories are chosen again and again: the price-value association, which was always likely to be strong; the circuit of the cycle in economies whose institutions suit it; the broad direction of distributional shares in periods when every observer agrees on the drift. The exposed territories are visited less often. The profit tendency is estimated, repeatedly, but almost always under the estimator's own concordance, the one setting in which its verdict is least independent. The bargaining mechanism inside the cycle is asserted against its demand-driven rivals more often than it is pitted against them in a design that could separate the two. The result is a literature whose confirmations are genuine but whose selection of battlegrounds flatters it.

The charge must be entered with its mitigation attached, because the tendency described is not a Marxian peculiarity. Every empirical discipline tests where testing is feasible, and feasibility is kinder to some claims than to others; the file drawers of psychology and medicine are filled with the null results their fields found unpublishable, and the testing of macroeconomic theories of every school clusters in the decades and countries where the data are richest rather than where the theories are weakest. Singling out this programme for a universal sin would be rhetoric, not criticism. What can fairly be said is narrower. A small literature can afford the universal sin less than a large one, because it generates fewer tests in total and because its audience has fewer independent results against which to discount the selection. And the remedy is known, portable, and in the programme's own interest.

### What would testing at the point of greatest vulnerability look like?

It would mean estimating the profit tendency under a rival school's classification rather than one's own, designing observations that separate the bargaining mechanism of the cycle from its demand-driven rivals, and publishing the outcome when the favoured claim loses. Programmes grow by surviving tests they might have failed.

Adversarial estimation is the sharpest available instrument and the literature already contains its prototype in miniature, since builders of rival profit series sometimes report, in footnotes and appendices, what their series would look like under a competitor's valuation rule. Making that practice central rather than footnoted would transform the row's meaning: a tendency that survived under hostile rulebooks would command assent in a way no friendly series can, and a tendency that collapsed under them would be known to rest on bookkeeping, which is also knowledge. The same spirit applied to the cycle would commission the discriminating observations the mechanism dispute needs, wage settlements, pricing decisions, and capacity data read against the loop's timing, rather than further demonstrations that the loop exists. None of this requires new theory. It requires the field to spend its scarce labour where the information is, at the joints where claims can break.

## The strongest objection: the finding is built into the setup

The objections so far have been about practice. There is a stronger one about principle, and this article's brief requires it at full strength, because a map that hides the best argument against the territory it charts is propaganda with better production values. The objection runs as follows. Evidence counts, in the ordinary sense, when the world gets a vote: when a procedure is arranged so that the answer could come back against the claim, and the answer is then accepted. In this programme's central measurements, the world never gets a clean vote. The analyst chooses the classification. The classification, as this article has documented at length, can determine the result, reversing the direction of the field's most famous tendency. Therefore the finding is contained in the setup, the procedure is a machine for manufacturing confirmations whose settings are chosen by the people being confirmed, and the empirical wing of Marxist economics does not generate evidence in the ordinary sense at all. Its internal disagreements, on this view, are not a sign of scientific life. They are quarrels among machinists about settings, conducted in a vocabulary that disguises bookkeeping as discovery. That is the objection with its gloves off, and any honest treatment has to concede how much of the territory it covers before answering it.

The answer begins by limiting the objection's range, because pressed to its absolute limit it proves too much. All measurement in economics is theory-laden and convention-bound, from the capital stock to the unemployment rate, whose definition excludes discouraged workers by a decision every bit as consequential as any concordance choice reviewed here. If the presence of a consequential classification voided evidence, economics would have no evidence anywhere, and the objection would abolish the mainstream's empirical standing in the same breath as the Marxian one. The operative question is therefore one of degree: how far does the classification merely constrain a result, and how far does it dictate one? Constraint is compatible with evidence. A rule fixed in advance binds its author's hands, and the proof that it binds is that builders are sometimes surprised and disappointed by their own series, reporting movements against the tradition's expectation under rulebooks they had no reason to rig. A machine for manufacturing confirmations does not embarrass its manufacturers. This literature's machines have done so in print, which is evidence that the world retains a vote, even if the ballot is printed in a dialect the voter did not choose.

### Which results survive a change of classification?

The ones stated as patterns rather than heights. The circuit of employment and wage share, the broad drift of distributional shares across decades, and the co-movement of prices with computed values appear under rival rulebooks as well as friendly ones. Findings that persist while the setup changes earn their standing.

Cross-convention survival is the empirical heart of the answer. When the same pattern emerges from Shaikh and Tonak's strict boundary and from Moseley's narrower one, the pattern is not an artefact of either rulebook, because the rulebooks disagree and the pattern declined to follow them. When the cycle's circuit appears in national records processed by mainstream statisticians for mainstream purposes, bargaining categories and all, it is not a product of Marxian bookkeeping at all. The objection, notice, cannot touch results of this kind, and the programme possesses several. What the objection does destroy, and should be allowed to destroy, is the evidentiary standing of levels and of every argument built on them. A cited height for the rate of exploitation, a cited height for the rate of profit, a secular trend read off a single friendly series: these are artefacts wearing the dress of facts, and the objection is a correct and useful solvent for them. It also leaves the profit tendency itself wounded, since that claim, as matters stand, is precisely a long-run direction read off contested series, and until it survives adversarial classification it remains a hypothesis with a literature rather than a finding. The map marks it contested for exactly that reason. An objection that is answered by being granted its proper province, and refused the rest of the map, has been answered in the only way strong objections can be.

## What good practice would look like

Diagnosis earns its keep only if it issues in standards, and the standards this programme needs follow directly from the weaknesses named. They are four in number, and none of them requires new theory, new data, or new money on any scale worth mentioning. What they require is a change in what the field counts as a finished piece of work.

The first standard is pre-specification. The classification rule for a study should be fixed, written down in full, and published before the results are computed, or, failing that, defended on grounds stated independently of the results it produces. The point is not bureaucratic. A concordance chosen after the answer is visible can be tuned, consciously or not, until the answer pleases, and neither the author nor the reader can afterwards tell how much tuning occurred. A concordance fixed in advance converts the researcher's discretion from a hidden variable into a public commitment, and it costs nothing but the willingness to be bound. The second standard is sensitivity reporting as a matter of course. Every published result should travel with an account of its behaviour under the main rival rules: what the series looks like when finance changes sides, when the capital stock changes valuation, when the productive boundary moves to the main alternative position. Sensitivity reporting does not settle which rule is right. It does something more valuable at this stage of the field's development: it tells the reader which findings are load-bearing and which are decorative, and it converts private quarrels about rulebooks into public information about robustness.

### Which standards would change the evidential situation most?

Pre-specification and sensitivity reporting, because they attack the programme's central weakness directly. A classification fixed before computation cannot be tuned to the answer, and a result published with its behaviour under rival rules lets every reader price the convention into the claim. The other standards build on those two.

The third standard concerns what is tested. Given that levels are convention-dependent, the field's tests should be aimed at directions, turning points, and patterns of co-movement, the quantities that survive translation, and claims should be stated in those terms from the outset rather than translated into them after an embarrassing result. A literature that habitually predicts directions and reports levels as illustrations would find its confirmations and its refutations alike becoming sharper, because both would concern quantities the data can actually adjudicate. The fourth standard is the falsification condition stated in advance. Each empirical paper should declare, before its results, what outcome would count against the hypothesis under test. The declaration is a small paragraph and a large discipline: it forecloses the reinterpretive drift that Popper's classic charge described, the drift in which every outcome becomes a confirmation and the theory ages without ever having been at risk. A field that wrote those paragraphs routinely would also write better hypotheses, since a claim that cannot specify its own defeat condition usually turns out, on inspection, to be two claims sharing a sentence.

Neighbouring practice shows these standards are attainable rather than utopian. Mainstream empirical economics, whose comparison with the Marxian programme is drawn in the series article on [Marxist and mainstream economics side by side](/2016/07/15/marxist-vs-mainstream-economics/), has spent two decades arguing itself, imperfectly and against resistance, toward pre-analysis plans, archived data and code, and robustness appendices as default furniture. The Marxian field's version of that furniture must include one extra piece, the published concordance itself, deposited with the transformed dataset so that replication stops meaning re-interpretation from prose. Journals in the field could require the deposit tomorrow, at no cost but editorial will. Until they do, the standards above remain a description of what the best work in the literature already approximates, and a reproach to the rest.

## The pattern across the programme: measurement decides

Step back from the individual rows and a pattern emerges that is larger than any of them, and naming it is a debt this article owes to the series it belongs to. Four times now, in four separate empirical literatures, the investigation of a Marxian magnitude has arrived at the same destination by different roads. The price and value literature found its measurement feasible and its interpretation contested. The exploitation literature found its levels owned by rulebooks. The profit rate literature found its very direction reversible by classification. And the general survey undertaken here finds the whole map sloping according to translation distance. This recurrence, which the series calls the measurement dominance pattern, is not a coincidence of exposition. It is a finding about the field, and it deserves to be stated as one: in empirical Marxist economics, the binding constraint on knowledge is metrological. It is not the absence of theory, of which the field has a surplus. It is not the absence of data, which the statistical offices supply in embarrassing abundance. It is the absence of settled instruments for turning the one into terms the other can answer.

That finding reorders what progress would mean. A field constrained by theory progresses by producing better theory. A field constrained by measurement progresses by producing better instruments and duller infrastructure: concordances that strangers can apply, archives that strangers can audit, replications that strangers can afford to run. The history of other sciences suggests the reordering is not a demotion. Astronomy advanced when its catalogues and instruments improved, chemistry when its measures of purity and weight were standardised, and national accounting itself was the instrument-building project that made modern macroeconomics testable at all. For this programme, the unglamorous conclusion follows that an hour spent building a public concordance is worth a shelf of new models, because new models enter a literature that cannot yet agree on what its quantities are. The field's map of itself, given in [the complete guide to Marxist economics](/2016/04/01/marxist-economics-complete-guide/), describes a rich architecture of schools and claims. The measurement base beneath that architecture is thinner than the architecture deserves, and thickening it is the programme's most promising direction of work.

## Falsifiability belongs to programmes, not to slogans

It remains to place the whole inquiry in its philosophical frame, briefly, because the vocabulary of the debate misleads even careful readers. Falsifiability, in the strict sense its originator intended, is a property of statements: a statement is falsifiable when some describable observation would contradict it. Research programmes are not statements. They are federations of statements, habits, exemplars, and commitments, and asking whether a federation is falsifiable, yes or no, is a category error that both camps in this debate commit for convenience. The better question, posed by the philosophy of science that followed Popper, Imre Lakatos most usefully, is whether a programme's protective adjustments generate new content or merely absorb shocks: whether the belt of auxiliary hypotheses around the core predicts facts nobody had looked for, or only explains, after the fact, the facts that embarrassed it. Judged by that standard, the empirical record surveyed here is genuinely mixed, and the mixture is more informative than either camp's slogan. The growth cycle was a content-generating move: a prose intuition converted into a system that predicted a pattern in data nobody had arranged for the purpose. Parts of the profit rate literature, by contrast, have behaved absorbatively, meeting each disappointment with a fresh concordance rather than a sharper claim. A programme containing both behaviours is not unfalsifiable and not vindicated. It is a programme with healthy and unhealthy organs, which is the condition of every living research tradition that has ever been examined closely.

Five misreadings cluster around this subject and are worth correcting outright, since each one poisons discussion in a characteristic way. The first holds that no empirical work in this tradition exists at all; the map above is its refutation, and its persistence says more about the reading habits of the programme's critics than about the programme. The second holds the mirror image, that the existence of empirical work vindicates the theory; it does not, because results attach to components, and several components remain untested or contested. The third treats falsifiability as a single yes-or-no property of the whole tradition, which the previous paragraph has already refused. The fourth maintains that classification problems are a Marxian affliction; they are not, since every tradition that reads national accounts inherits the accountants' conventions, and the honest statement is that the gap between this theory's categories and the statisticians' is unusually wide, not that it is uniquely present. The fifth infers unreliability from smallness, as if a literature's truth were a function of its headcount; reliability tracks whether claims can be checked, and small fields with public sources outperform large ones with private habits. Readers who clear away all five will find the remaining question smaller than the slogans suggested and more interesting: not whether Marxist economics can be tested, but which of its claims have been, under what rules, and with what discipline. The wider standing of the tradition as a claimant to scientific status is argued on its own ground in the companion treatment of [Marxism and the scientific label](/2019/10/01/is-marxism-a-science/), to which readers whose question is the larger one should now repair.

## An empirical habit older than the charge

Sceptics sometimes write as though measurement entered this tradition late and reluctantly, smuggled in by a few econometricians against the resistance of a purely textual orthodoxy. The historical record runs the other way. The founding text of the programme, the first volume of Capital, is saturated with the statistical material of its century: factory inspectors' reports quoted at length and cross-examined, parliamentary papers, census returns, public health investigations, and the proceedings of official inquiries into banking and the poor laws. Marx treated the British state's own statistical apparatus as a weapon, partly because its findings carried an authority his polemics could borrow, and partly because he held, as a methodological conviction, that the laws of motion of an economy would show themselves in its aggregates or nowhere. His collaborator Friedrich Engels built an early reputation on a study of working-class conditions assembled from observation and official returns. Whatever the tradition later became in some of its defensive moods, it did not begin as an enemy of counting.

### Was the tradition always hostile to measurement?

No. Its founding texts are dense with official statistics, factory inspectors' reports, and census material, and its early twentieth-century institutions ran statistical bureaus of their own. The hostility belongs to a later defensive period, not to the tradition's origins or to its enduring character.

The institutional record of the following century reinforces the point. The mass socialist parties of the early twentieth century maintained research bureaus and statistical yearbooks. The young Soviet state, whatever its later deformations, founded a Conjuncture Institute in Moscow whose business cycle research, associated with Nikolai Kondratiev, produced statistical work on long waves in prices and production that economists outside any Marxian commitment continued to cite for generations. German and Austrian social democracy sustained research institutes whose staff moved between party work, trade union statistics, and university economics. The Frankfurt Institute for Social Research, remembered for philosophy, ran empirical studies of workers' attitudes with questionnaires and field methods in its early years. The thread connecting these enterprises is not a shared politics of the spreadsheet. It is a standing assumption, inherited from the founders, that claims about capitalism should be able to meet the capitalism that statistics describe. The era in which the unfalsifiability charge gained its plausibility was a specific and later one, when the tradition's political organisations had hardened their doctrines into catechisms and treated internal statistical heresy as disloyalty. Charges formed against that era were then generalised, by critics with an interest in the generalisation, into a verdict on the whole programme across its whole history. The map in this article is, among other things, an attempt to re-particularise the verdict: to ask which parts of the programme, in which periods, by which standards, have actually stood in front of data.

## An episode tested in public: the profit squeeze

The abstract grading of literatures can make testing sound like a ceremony performed in journals, so it is worth following one case in which a Marxian claim was tested in the open, under political pressure, and visibly changed by the encounter. In the early nineteen seventies, the economists Andrew Glyn and Bob Sutcliffe argued that British capitalism's difficulties had a distributional cause that could be checked: the share of profits in national income had been squeezed by a rising wage share, as strong workplace organisation converted full employment into wage gains that productivity growth did not match, and the compressed profit share had in turn weakened the incentive and the means to invest. The claim was Marxian in lineage, descending from the same bargaining mechanism that drives the growth cycle, and it was issued with numbers attached, in a country whose statistics were public and whose economists were numerous, hostile, and numerate.

### What happened when the profit squeeze thesis met the data?

The thesis held that rising wages had compressed British profitability and caused the investment weakness of its period. It was tested against wage, productivity, and tax evidence by critics and defenders alike, revised in scope, and left standing in a narrower form than its authors first proposed.

The ensuing argument is a small model of how component testing is supposed to work. Critics disputed the measurement of the squeeze itself, arguing that part of the apparent wage gain was absorbed by rising taxes on employment and by the changing composition of the workforce. Others granted the squeeze and disputed its causal office, pointing to international competition and to the rising relative price of imported materials as alternative compressors of the margin available to domestic producers. Defenders recalculated, conceded the tax point in part, separated the squeeze on profits from the squeeze attributable to foreign rivals, and restated the claim in a narrower and more defensible form: distributional conflict had been one major compressor among several, operating through identifiable channels, in a specific institutional setting. The episode ended without a victor's parade, and that is precisely its value as evidence about evidence. A claim from inside the tradition had been stated in measurable terms, attacked on measurement and on mechanism by researchers outside it, defended with recalculation rather than excommunication, and trimmed to what the data would carry. Nobody involved supposed the fate of Marxist economics hung on the outcome. The claim's fate did, and it was settled by the ordinary machinery of empirical dispute. Literatures in which such episodes are routine do not need articles like this one. The point of recounting the profit squeeze at length is that its machinery, public data, hostile scrutiny, revision under fire, is available to the whole programme whenever the programme chooses claims of the right shape and size.

## Crisis tendencies: the least formalised row

The map's weakest row deserves its own examination, because its weakness is diagnostic. The claims gathered there, that accumulation generates from within itself pressures toward breakdown, stagnation, or chronic under-use of capacity, descend from the tradition's core and carry much of its public reputation. They are also, in the form usually stated, nearly untestable, and the reasons are structural rather than accidental. A crisis tendency is a claim about a whole system at its extremes. Extremes are rare, so the sample is a handful of episodes across a century and a half. Each episode arrives crowded with candidate causes, monetary, political, military, and accidental, among which the tendency must pick its way. And the claim's temporal shape is elastic: a tendency that can be counteracted indefinitely, and whose confirming instance may always be deferred to a later breakdown, has arranged in advance to survive every quiet decade. Critics who call the programme unfalsifiable usually have this row in mind, and on this row their description fits the literature's habits uncomfortably well.

### Why have crisis claims resisted formalisation?

Because they are claims about the behaviour of whole systems at their breaking points, where the relevant variables multiply and the episodes are few. A tendency stated for exceptional moments offers history a handful of cases, each crowded with rival causes, and statistics starves on such a diet.

The honourable exception inside this row is the stagnation variant associated with Paul Baran and Paul Sweezy, whose work on monopoly capital attempted something the breakdown tradition had mostly avoided: an accounting of the economic surplus and its absorption that could, in principle, be estimated. Their argument held that mature monopoly capitalism generates a rising surplus while its normal outlets for absorbing that surplus, investment driven by competition, consumption, and civilian government spending, fail to keep pace, leaving the system dependent on military expenditure, the sales effort, and financial expansion to avoid stagnation. Followers and critics alike attempted the estimation the argument invited, reconstructing surplus shares from national accounts and tracing the growth of the absorbing categories. The results were instructive in a way neither camp fully intended. The broad rise of the categories named as absorbers was visible under several rulebooks. The size of the surplus itself, and therefore the tightness of the stagnation bind, swung with the classification of whole sectors, reproducing at the level of a whole research programme the convention problem that haunts the exploitation and profit rows. A literature that set out to test the system's largest claim ended by demonstrating, once again, that the measuring instrument was the message. Even here, though, the exercise was not empty: it disciplined a generation of argument about military spending and advertising into quantitative form, and it gave critics definite magnitudes to dispute rather than a mood to deplore. The row stays low on the map because its central tendency claims remain under-formalised and its episodes under-determine their explanations. It does not sit at the bottom, because parts of it have at least been dragged within reach of arithmetic.

## The model beyond its first form

The centrepiece has a biography after 1967 that itself testifies to its standing, because models that fail to interest anyone outside their home tradition do not get naturalised. Goodwin's cycle passed into the general literature of economic dynamics as a standard exhibit of an endogenous cycle, a cycle generated by the internal relations of an economy rather than by external shocks, and it appears in that capacity in textbooks whose authors have no Marxian allegiance to declare. Its predator-prey skeleton proved portable. The same mathematical frame that carries wolves and hares through their oscillations carries employment and the wage share through theirs, and the portability taught a generation of dynamicists to see distributional conflict as one member of a wide family of feedback systems. Within the tradition, the model was extended in the directions its austerity invited. Builders added technical change that accelerates when wages squeeze, so that the system can spiral or settle rather than orbit forever. Others added a government sector taxing and spending against the cycle, a financial sector whose lending standards tighten and loosen with profitability, and capacity utilisation responding to demand, until the spare two-variable loop had grown into a small architecture of interacting mechanisms, each addition trading the original's transparency for a closer fit to the institutional record of some particular economy.

### How far has the cycle model travelled beyond its origins?

Into mainstream macrodynamics textbooks as a standard exhibit of endogenous cycles, into estimations for economies on several continents, and into extensions adding technical change, government, and finance. Few Marxian constructions have been so thoroughly naturalised by researchers who owe the tradition nothing.

Estimations travelled as well. Researchers fitted members of the model family to the records of economies in Latin America and Asia as distributional data allowed, asking whether the circuit appears where labour institutions differ most sharply from the postwar European setting in which the model was conceived. The answers were mixed in an informative way: the circuit's signature appeared in some records and not in others, and the variation lined up, plausibly, with the strength and centralisation of wage bargaining, which is what the mechanism would lead one to expect if the mechanism is real. A pattern that shows up where its enabling conditions hold and fades where they do not is behaving the way genuine mechanisms behave in every science that studies open systems. That conditional character is sometimes held against the model, as though a mechanism that requires its conditions were a defect. The objection mistakes the kind of claim being made. The cycle model never promised that every economy must exhibit the circuit at all times. It specified a machine and the conditions under which the machine runs, and the cross-country record, with its appearances and its absences, is evidence about both the machine and the conditions. This is what mature component testing looks like, and its existence inside the programme is the standing refutation of the slogan that nothing in the tradition can be tested. It is equally a standing reproach to the rows of the map where nothing comparable has been attempted.

## One magnitude under two rulebooks: a worked miniature

The classification convention problem can sound, in the abstract, like an ailment that afflicts only careless researchers. A miniature in prose shows otherwise. Take the rate of profit for a single advanced economy across the inflationary decade of the nineteen seventies and the disinflation that followed, and compute it twice, each time by a rulebook a serious scholar has defended. Under the first rulebook, the capital stock is carried at historic cost, the sum of what firms paid for their equipment and structures in the prices of the years when they bought them. Inflation then plays a trick on the series. As prices rise, the denominator of the rate, the value of the capital stock, is anchored in the cheaper money of earlier years, while the numerator, the flow of profits, is reckoned in the swollen money of the present. The measured rate rides upward on the discrepancy, and the economy appears to enjoy a profitability boom that no manager of the period would recognise. Under the second rulebook, the stock is carried at replacement cost, revalued each year at the prices then ruling. The denominator swells with inflation alongside the numerator, the phantom boom disappears, and the same decade shows profitability under genuine pressure, squeezed between wage growth and the rising price of imported energy. Two honest computations, one statistical record, and opposite stories about whether profitability rose or fell in the decade when the era's politics turned on exactly that question.

Neither rulebook is a fraud. Historic cost has the accountant's virtues of verifiability and continuity with firms' own books. Replacement cost has the economist's virtue of measuring the sacrifice that replacing the capital stock would actually require. The choice between them is a judgement about what the rate of profit is for, and it cannot be settled by the data, because the data are what the choice constitutes. Multiply this fork by the others catalogued earlier, the boundary of the productive sector, the placement of finance, the treatment of the state, the handling of taxes, and the space of defensible series for a single magnitude in a single economy becomes a small crowd. The crowd is the finding. It does not mean the magnitude is meaningless; replacement cost and historic cost answer different questions, and an analyst who knows which question is being asked can use each series intelligently. It means that citing the rate of profit, without its rulebook, as a premise in an argument about the world is a category error, and that a literature which tolerates the citation is tolerating the error. The standards proposed in this article are, at bottom, a campaign against that single tolerated error.

## Datasets the programme has barely touched

The testable territory is not fixed, because the data frontier moves. Three bodies of material, all underexploited by this literature, could widen it materially. Firm-level accounts, gathered by statistical offices and commercial registries into panels that follow enterprises across years, would let researchers examine distributional magnitudes where they are actually generated, inside firms and industries, rather than inferred from aggregates whose composition shifts beneath the analysis. Wage and employment records held by tax and social insurance authorities, increasingly available to researchers under controlled access in a number of countries, would allow the bargaining mechanism inside the growth cycle to be observed at the level of settlements and workplaces instead of being inferred from the loop it supposedly drives. And multi-country input-output tables, assembled by international projects to trace supply chains across borders, extend the price-value computations of the oldest row on the map from single economies to the world market in which values, on the theory's own account, are finally formed. Each source carries the field's signature difficulty in a new key: firm accounts sort by legal entity rather than function, tax records know nothing of productive and unproductive labour, and the international tables impose their own harmonising conventions on the national statistics fed into them. The territory widens; the classification convention problem moves house rather than retiring.

### Which untapped data could widen the testable territory?

Firm-level accounts, wage records held by tax authorities, and multi-country input-output tables that follow value chains across borders. Each speaks to magnitudes the national aggregates blur, and each arrives with classification problems of its own, so the territory widens without the central difficulty dissolving.

There is a strategic moral in the widening. A programme whose binding constraint is measurement should invest where measurement is improving, and the three sources named are improving fastest precisely at the points where the programme's classic magnitudes are blurriest: within-firm distribution, the wage bargain itself, and the international formation of value. Whether the field's institutions, its journals, training programmes, and hiring patterns, will route scarce young researchers toward those sources is a sociological question this article cannot answer. The map can only record that the road exists. Programmes are not obliged to take their own best roads, and the history of this one includes long stretches in which the best road was visible and the traffic went elsewhere, into textual commentary whose marginal returns diminished decade by decade. An honest survey should end its component tour with that observation on the table, since it converts the article's findings into a choice the field continues to make, in one direction or the other, with every cohort it trains.

## Joint tests and what follows from them

One refinement of method deserves a section of its own, because it governs how every result in this literature should be read. Economists borrow from the philosophy of science the observation that hypotheses are never tested alone. A prediction is derived from a claim together with a scaffolding of auxiliary assumptions, about measurement, about background conditions, about the stability of institutions, and when the prediction fails, logic alone does not say which member of the scaffold to blame. In this programme the scaffolding has a permanent member that other programmes possess only transiently: the concordance. Every Marxian test is a joint test of a claim and a classification rule, and the jointness is not a defect to be apologised for but a fact to be managed. Managed well, it has a productive side. When a result disappoints, the dispute over whether the claim or the concordance failed forces both into the open, and the field's most instructive controversies, the profit series wars among them, have been exactly such forced disclosures. Managed badly, jointness becomes the universal solvent of the Popperian charge, the standing excuse by which any failure is reassigned to the scaffolding and the claim emerges untouched. The difference between the two managements is not temperament. It is the standards again: pre-specification limits the solvent's use, sensitivity reporting exposes it, and the advance falsification condition names, before the fact, the outcomes for which no reassignment will be attempted. A reader equipped with that paragraph can grade any paper in this literature in minutes, by asking three questions. Was the rule fixed before the answer? Is the answer's behaviour under rival rules reported? Did the author say, in advance, what losing would look like? The proportion of the literature that survives the three questions is the honest measure of the programme's empirical maturity, and it is a proportion the field could raise within a decade by editorial decision alone.

## Formal results that discipline without data

Between the estimated models of the upper rows and the untestable centre lies a middle territory this survey has not yet visited: propositions that formal work has settled without any dataset at all. Mathematics cannot tell anyone what an economy did, but it can tell everyone what a theory commits them to, and several of the programme's oldest confusions were cleared by proof rather than by measurement. The transformation debate supplied the first famous instance. When Ladislaus von Bortkiewicz reconstructed Marx's procedure for converting values into prices of production inside a simultaneous equation system at the start of the twentieth century, he showed that the two aggregate identities Marx had asserted, total price equalling total value and total profit equalling total surplus value, could not both be guaranteed in general. The demonstration did not refute the theory of value, whatever its author's hopes and its later citers have claimed. It refuted one proposed property of one proposed transformation, and it forced every subsequent defender to specify which identity their construction preserves and which it surrenders. A century of argument was thereby made more precise, and precision, as the growth cycle already showed, is the precondition of every test that follows.

### What did the Fundamental Marxian Theorem establish?

A formal proof that positive profit in a capitalist economy is possible exactly when workers perform more labour than the labour embodied in their consumption, and conversely. It tied the existence of profit to the existence of surplus labour with a rigour no side disputes.

The theorem, proved by Michio Morishima in the nineteen seventies and since generalised by other hands, occupies a distinctive place in the map's logic. It is not an empirical result; no dataset appears in its proof. Yet it disciplines empirical work throughout the exploitation row, because it certifies that the connection between surplus labour and profit is not a definitional trick of Marxian bookkeeping but a structural property of a wide class of economies, holding wherever its stated conditions hold. A critic who grants the theorem and still wishes to deny the exploitation account of profit must attack the conditions or the interpretation, not the arithmetic, and the debate is better for knowing exactly where the attack must land. John Roemer's subsequent programme carried the same strategy further, rebuilding the concepts of exploitation and class inside formal general equilibrium models stated in the mainstream's own mathematical dialect, and extracting from them results that journals of the first rank argued over on equal terms. Whatever verdict history reaches on Roemer's conclusions, the episode demonstrated something about testability's neighbourhood: the programme's concepts can survive translation into the most demanding formal idiom available, and when they do, they generate propositions sharp enough for anyone to attack. Formalisation is not testing, and this article does not count theorems as evidence. But a programme whose core claims can be formalised, proved about, and thereby exposed to exact refutation is in a different epistemic position from one whose claims dissolve whenever a mathematician approaches, and the map's middle rows should be read with that difference in mind.

## Gravitation: testing the pull toward prices of production

The price and value row of the map was drawn from cross-sectional studies, snapshots comparing computed values with market prices at a moment. Marx's own claim, however, was about a process in time: prices of production act as centres around which market prices oscillate, because capital migrates toward sectors earning above the average rate of profit and away from sectors earning below it, expanding supply where returns are fat and contracting it where they are thin until the rates tend toward equality. That dynamic claim is testable in a different way from the snapshot association, and a distinct literature has grown up around testing it. Its data are long runs of industry-level records. Its question is whether sectoral profit rates, which differ widely at every moment, show a statistical pull toward their average across decades, wandering without drifting away, in the manner the theory's metaphor of gravitation describes.

### What would gravitation of prices look like in data?

Sectoral profit rates would differ persistently yet pull toward equality over long horizons, and market prices would wander around prices of production rather than drifting away from them. Researchers look for that wandering-with-return in long industry records, and report finding its traces amid heavy noise.

The researchers who pursue this programme, the Greek economist Lefteris Tsoulfidis and his collaborators prominent among them, report results of the now-familiar shape: the equalising pull is detectable, slow, and easily swamped over short horizons by the noise of particular markets, so that the gravitation claim survives as a statement about long-run centres rather than about any year's configuration. Two features of this literature deserve the map's emphasis. First, it tests the theory in the tense the theory was written in, the long run of capital migration, rather than in the frozen tense of the snapshot. Second, it does so with magnitudes, sectoral profit rates computed from published industry data under comparatively light translation, that sit near the easy end of the classification spectrum. The gravitation row therefore behaves like the cycle row: a formally stateable dynamic, a light concordance, and a result that is real but modest, a tendency visible through noise rather than a law displayed in clean figures. Critics reply that a tendency so slow and so noisy approaches the unfalsifiable in practice, and defenders answer that the time scale is a property of the object, not an excuse of the theory, since capital stock turns over in decades and no honest test can demand a faster verdict than the mechanism can deliver. The dispute is genuine and it is the right dispute, about the match between a claim's clock and a test's patience, and it recurs wherever long-run tendencies are asserted in economics, inside this tradition and far beyond it.

## Value transfers and unpaid labour: the widening edge

Two further measurement frontiers show the programme's categories being carried into territory their founders could not have surveyed. The first concerns trade between economies at very different wage levels. The theory of unequal exchange, stated most influentially by Arghiri Emmanuel, holds that when commodities trade at prices of production formed across a world market with a roughly equalised profit rate but persistently unequal wages, value flows systematically from low-wage to high-wage economies inside apparently equal exchanges. For decades the argument ran on algebra and indignation. The arrival of multi-country input-output tables changed its epistemic condition, because labour embodied in traded goods can now be computed along supply chains that cross borders, and several research teams have estimated the direction and rough scale of the transfers implied by the world's trade structure. The results, reported in the directional terms this article's protocol requires, consistently indicate net flows of embodied labour from poorer to richer economies, with the size of the flow sensitive, as the reader will by now predict, to the treatment of skill differences and to the conversion between currencies. A claim born as a polemic has become, in part, a computation.

The second frontier concerns labour that no market pays for at all. The reproduction of the workforce, cooking, cleaning, caring for children and the elderly, is performed overwhelmingly outside employment, and a tradition of feminist economics, partly Marxian in descent and partly independent, has built measurement programmes around it, using time-use surveys that record how populations actually spend their hours. Statistical offices have cooperated to a degree that would have astonished earlier generations, publishing satellite accounts that value unpaid household production alongside the market economy it sustains. The estimates are convention-laden to a degree that makes the profit rate wars look restrained, since valuing an hour of care at a housekeeper's wage, at the carer's own forgone wage, or at the price of the nearest market substitute yields three different economies. Yet the directional finding survives every convention applied to it: unpaid labour is enormous relative to the paid economy, it is performed disproportionately by women, and any account of exploitation that stops at the factory and office gate is measuring a fraction of the labour that capital's workforce requires in order to exist. Both frontiers teach the same lesson as the classic rows. The categories travel. The computations follow. And the conventions multiply exactly in step with the ambition of the question, which is why this article's central problem is not a local embarrassment but the permanent condition of empirical work in the tradition.

## The mirror held up by neighbouring fields

Standards are easier to demand in the abstract than to recognise in a mirror, so it is worth asking how the neighbouring fields have fared when their own testability was audited. Mainstream macroeconomics, the programme against which this one is most often measured, passed through a public reckoning after the financial crisis of 2008, when its representative models were charged, by some of its own senior figures, with having excluded by construction the phenomena that had just wrecked the world economy. The charge had the same logical shape as the one this article has been considering: a framework whose core commitments filtered what could be observed, defended by a community whose journals rewarded internal refinement over external confrontation. The mainstream's recovery programme, still incomplete, ran toward exactly the furniture proposed above: micro-level data admitted against the representative agent's convenience, pre-specification spreading from experimental subfields outward, replication archives acquiring editorial teeth. Empirical microeconomics, for its part, rebuilt its credibility in a generation by narrowing its questions to ones that research design could answer decisively, accepting smaller claims in exchange for firmer verdicts. The lesson the mirror returns is not that the Marxian programme is uniquely sick. It is that programmes recover their empirical standing by the same unglamorous route, claim by claim and standard by standard, and that the route is open to any field willing to trade the sweep of its questions for the firmness of its answers on the questions that matter most. Whether the tradition surveyed here will accept that trade at scale is a question about its institutions, not about its logic. Its logic, as the upper rows of the map show, permits the trade wherever its practitioners choose to make it.

## What a decisive study would require

Suppose the field's institutions resolved, once, to fund and execute the single most informative project available to them. The design writes itself from this article's diagnosis. An adversarial collaboration would be commissioned, staffed jointly by the builders of the leading rival profit series and by statisticians from outside the tradition with no position to defend. Its concordance would be negotiated before any modern data were examined and published in full, together with the rival concordances it displaced, so that sensitivity to the negotiation's outcome could be computed by anyone. Its data would span several economies and the longest runs the archives allow. Its hypotheses would be registered in advance, in the falsification-condition form the standards require: the tendency's expected direction under each rulebook, the turning points it must reproduce, the outcomes under which its proponents would concede the claim in its strong form. And its transformed datasets would be deposited where strangers could rebuild every figure. Nothing in the design exceeds the routine practice of a well-run clinical collaboration or a modern replication project. Its cost would be a rounding error beside the sums the field's universities spend in a year. What it requires is not money but surrender: the builders of the rival series would have to let a joint rulebook, and possibly a hostile verdict, carry their names.

### Could a single study settle the testability question?

No single study could, because the question is a bundle of component questions with different answers. A commissioned adversarial study could settle particular components, the profit tendency under joint rulebooks above all, and would be the most valuable single project the field could undertake.

The limits of the thought experiment are as instructive as the design. Even a flawless execution settles components, not the programme; whole economies cannot be randomised, history runs each configuration once, and the framework's core, the priority of value to price, would remain exactly where the map's bottom row puts it, beyond the reach of the exercise. Decisiveness in economics is always local. What the study would change is the location of the argument. Disagreement would move from the rulebooks, where it lies marooned, to the interpretation of a shared body of numbers, which is where empirical disagreements can actually be settled by further work. A field that knows the difference between its unsettled components and its unsettleable core, and that spends its authority accordingly, has answered the testability question in the only form in which it was ever really posed.

## The people problem: training, journals, and careers

Beneath the methodological weaknesses sits a sociological one that this article has touched repeatedly and should finally state directly. Empirical work of the kind the upper rows require is a craft, learned through apprenticeships in data construction that this tradition's training structures seldom provide. A young economist formed in a Marxian programme typically receives deep instruction in the history of theory and in textual interpretation, and is then left to acquire national accounting, input-output technique, and econometric practice alone or not at all. The neighbouring mainstream, whatever its intellectual limits, operates an industrial system for producing empirical craftsmen: methods sequences, data laboratories, dissertation workshops where concordances of its own kind, the cleaning and merging of datasets, are taught as the discipline's core skill. The predictable result is a division of labour in which the tradition's most talented empiricists are frequently hired away, retrained, and lost to its questions, while the questions themselves are taken up by scholars whose formation equipped them better for commentary than for computation. Journals compound the pattern. A journal that cannot find referees able to rebuild a concordance cannot police one, and a field whose flagship outlets publish measurement papers rarely will produce few of them, since careers are made where prestige sits. None of this is a conspiracy or a failing of individual will; it is the ordinary reproduction of a research community organised around other excellences. But it means the standards proposed in this article will not implement themselves through exhortation. They require the boring institutional acts by which fields change their own reproduction: methods training inside the tradition's programmes, journal requirements with referees to match, and prestige reallocated, deliberately, toward the unglamorous work of building instruments that strangers can trust.

## The untestable centre, examined closely

Return, finally, to the bottom row of the map, because its exact anatomy matters and loose talk about it has damaged both camps. The claim that resists testing is specific: that value magnitudes exist as real magnitudes prior to, and independently of, the prices in which they appear, governing those prices from behind. Notice first what is not claimed by the serious versions of the doctrine. Nobody denies that labour coefficients can be computed; the input-output literature computes them by the thousand. Nobody denies that computed values correlate with prices; the top row of the map is built on the correlation. The untestable residue is the priority claim itself, the assertion of an underlying magnitude of which price is the form of appearance. Every procedure for exhibiting the magnitude builds it from technical coefficients and labour inputs recorded inside the same statistical order that records prices, so the exhibition, however rigorous, cannot certify that the magnitude was there before the procedure convened it. A magnitude whose only access route is a construction cannot have its pre-construction existence confirmed by the construction. That is the precise sense in which the centre is untestable, and it is a matter of logic, not of temporary technical limitation awaiting a cleverer instrument.

### Why does the existence of value resist measurement?

Because any procedure that computes values works from technical coefficients and labour inputs recorded in the same statistical system that records prices. The computed magnitude is real and useful, but the claim that value exists prior to price, as its hidden governor, outruns every procedure that could check it.

Two consequences follow, one for each camp. For defenders, the honest course is to classify the priority claim correctly, as a framework commitment, a proposition about how to organise inquiry, comparable to the commitments other programmes carry at their cores, and to stop borrowing the prestige of the testable rows to lend it a certainty it cannot earn on its own. Framework commitments are not disgraced by being untestable; they are disgraced only by being mislabelled. For critics, the symmetrical honesty is to recognise that an untestable core does not contaminate a programme's testable periphery, any more than the untestable axioms of a statistical method contaminate the regressions run under it. The judgement that matters is the one Lakatos taught: whether the belt built around the core keeps generating claims that meet data honourably. On the evidence of the map, this programme's belt has done so unevenly, brilliantly at the cycle, laboriously at the rates, scarcely at all around crisis. That unevenness, and not a slogan in either direction, is the answer this article was assembled to give. It also explains the article's title discipline: a survey of testing should itself be testable in spirit, should say which findings would change its own verdicts. Three would. A profit tendency surviving adversarial classification would raise the map's most contested row. A discriminating test that killed the bargaining mechanism inside the cycle would lower its firmest one. And a maintained public concordance, used by rival teams in print, would dissolve the article's central diagnosis into history, which is the fate its author would most welcome for it.

## The data were built for a different theory

One further layer of the translation problem deserves excavation, because it explains why the problem cannot be solved by goodwill alone. The national accounts that every empiricist in this field must use were not assembled as a neutral mirror of economic life. They were designed, in the nineteen thirties and forties, by economists answering the questions of a rival research programme: how much demand the state must inject to restore employment, how the war economy could be planned, how income divides between consumption and saving in the short run. Richard Stone's architecture, which became the world's standard, sorts the economy by the categories that Keynesian management needs. Simon Kuznets, the other founding figure, fought and partly lost a parallel battle over whether the accounts should measure welfare or merely production, and his reservations about the finished instrument, its exclusion of household production, its indifference to distribution within its aggregates, were recorded and then largely set aside as the instrument hardened into infrastructure. The categories that greet the Marxian researcher, compensation of employees, operating surplus, final consumption, are therefore not raw facts awaiting any theory's use. They are a previous theory's answers, frozen into statistical law, and the concordance problem is at bottom a negotiation between two theories conducted across the frozen one's territory.

This genealogy carries a consolation and a warning. The consolation is that every programme working with these accounts performs translations, whether it admits the fact or not, and the Marxian field's necessity of publishing its concordances makes its translations more visible, hence more criticisable, than the silent translations performed inside concepts like the aggregate capital stock or the natural rate of unemployment, whose conventional content is protected by familiarity. The warning is that visibility cuts both ways. A field whose every number arrives with its rulebook attached will always look less certain than a field whose conventions are old enough to be invisible, and the appearance is not evidence. Readers comparing the confidence of the two literatures should discount accordingly: some of the mainstream's firmness is the firmness of settled convention, and some of this programme's hesitancy is the honesty of unsettled convention displayed in the open. The remedy proposed throughout this article is not to make the Marxian conventions invisible by age, which would take a century, but to make them shared, which could take a decade of deliberate editorial and institutional work.

## The revisionist controversy: the first great data fight

The programme's acquaintance with testing did not begin with econometrics. It began with a scandal. In the final years of the nineteenth century, Eduard Bernstein, a leading theorist of German social democracy and Friedrich Engels's literary executor, published a series of articles and then a book arguing that the empirical expectations of the doctrine he had inherited were failing in fact. Capitalism was not polarising into two camps with a vanishing middle; the middle strata persisted and in places grew. Joint-stock companies were diffusing share ownership rather than concentrating it in fewer hands. Real wages, on the statistics he assembled, had risen across the advanced countries. Credit and cartels were giving the system adaptive capacities that the collapse scenarios had not allowed for. Bernstein's conclusion, that the movement should revise its expectations and its strategy together, detonated the first great internal controversy of Marxism, and the controversy was conducted, to a degree its participants would not have predicted, as an argument about statistics.

### What did the revisionist controversy test?

Whether capitalism was heading where the doctrine said: toward polarisation, immiseration, and collapse. Bernstein marshalled wage statistics, ownership records, and the persistence of middle strata against those expectations, and the orthodox replied in kind. It was the tradition's first great argument conducted substantially with data.

The replies are as instructive as the challenge. Karl Kautsky answered with counter-statistics and with reclassifications, arguing that Bernstein's middle strata mixed together declining artisans and a new salaried class dependent on capital, that share diffusion concealed control concentration, and that wage gains were real but compatible with a rising rate of exploitation when productivity rose faster. Rosa Luxemburg answered from the theory's other flank, conceding little to the statistics and disputing the framework, in an exchange that already contains, in embryo, the two responses every later disappointment would receive: rebuild the categories, or defend them. Three lessons from the episode belong in this survey. First, the tradition's empirical conscience is as old as its organised existence; data arguments are not a foreign import. Second, the episode shows component testing working as it should at least once: the crude expectation of absolute immiseration, the claim that workers' living standards must fall as capitalism develops, did not survive the wage statistics of the following half-century and was quietly pensioned off by the tradition itself, surviving mainly in the rhetoric of its enemies. Claims in this programme have died of evidence before. Third, the episode also shows the protective reflex in its earliest form, since a share of the response consisted of reclassifying the disappointing strata rather than revising the expectation, and the same share reappears in the profit literature a century later. The field's methodological virtues and vices were both present at the creation, and its subsequent history is the story of their uneven race.

## The probabilistic reconstruction: rebuilding for testability

Among the attempts to move a row of the map by rebuilding the claim rather than remeasuring it, the boldest remains the probabilistic political economy proposed by Emmanuel Farjoun and Moshé Machover in their 1983 book Laws of Chaos. Their diagnosis was that the tradition had posed its central relation wrongly. Prices and values had been treated as point magnitudes standing in exact correspondence, so that every deviation of a price from its value registered as a problem to be explained away. Treat them instead as distributions, populations of magnitudes scattered by the competitive process around centres set by labour content, and the theory's claims transform into claims about the shapes of those populations: the spread of prices around values, the distribution of profit rates across firms and sectors, the statistical signature of exploitation at the level of aggregates where individual correspondences dissolve. Claims of that form are the native language of econometrics. They can be confronted with data without first agreeing on the exact value of anything, and several of the programme's subsequent price studies quietly absorbed the probabilistic outlook even when they declined the philosophy that came with it.

### What did the probabilistic reconstruction change?

It treated prices and values as distributions rather than point magnitudes, so that the theory's claims became claims about the shape of statistical populations. Claims of that form meet data naturally, and the reconstruction showed the tradition could rebuild its core for testability when it chose.

The reconstruction's reception is itself a datum for the map. Here was a version of the core theory rebuilt, by insiders of formidable technical competence, expressly so that its claims could be tested, complete with implications definite enough to fail. The mainstream of the tradition responded with interest, with criticism of its treatment of the transformation relation, and then, in large part, with a return to business as usual, in which the deterministic vocabulary the book had challenged continued to organise the field's self-understanding. A programme's testability, the episode suggests, is not only a property of its claims. It is a property of its choices, renewed or refused by each generation, about which of its available self-understandings to inhabit. The same pattern, of an available rigorous reconstruction taken up partially and late, marks the temporal interpretation of the value theory associated with Andrew Kliman and his collaborators, who rebuilt the transformation arithmetic on sequential rather than simultaneous valuation, drew from it a defence of the falling rate's coherence, and produced their own profit rate series for the United States whose behaviour they set against the simultaneist reconstructions. Friends and critics dispute the construction vigorously. What is not disputable is the form of the contribution: a claim rebuilt until data could vote on it, followed by data. The map's rows rise, when they rise, by exactly such acts of reconstruction, and the field's tragedy, if the word is not too large, is that the acts remain individual where they should be institutional.

## The instrument that got built elsewhere

Comparisons sting most when they are close, and the closest comparison to this programme's predicament is a neighbouring heterodoxy that escaped it. Ecological economics began, like Marxist economics, with magnitudes the official statistics did not record: the physical throughput of materials and energy that underlies monetary production. Its researchers faced the same choice between translating the monetary accounts into their categories and building instruments of their own. Unusually, they did both, and the instrument-building succeeded beyond the field's own borders. Material flow analysis, developed in the programme's institutes as a heterodox accounting of tonnes rather than currency, was taken up by statistical offices, standardised by international manuals, and is now compiled for whole economies by the same agencies that publish the national accounts. A heterodox category thereby acquired what this article has argued the Marxian categories lack: an official concordance, maintained by public bodies, revised in the open, and restated historically when its rules change. The episode does not prove that the Marxian categories could win the same adoption; the political obstacles differ, and tonnes are easier to standardise than the functional classification of labour. It proves something narrower and more useful. The absence of an instrument is a historical outcome, not a law of nature, and fields that treat instrument-building as first-rank theoretical work occasionally get their instruments built. The Marxian programme has produced no comparable institutional victory, and the reason is not that its categories are uniquely resistant. It is that its prestige economy rewards the interpretation of texts and the construction of systems over the decade-long committee work by which an accounting standard is actually born. Until that reward structure shifts, the classification convention problem will persist not as a fate but as a choice the field keeps making.

## The census of a literature

A survey of testing should include, as one of its exhibits, the shape of the literature itself, since a field's allocation of its own labour is evidence about its real, as opposed to professed, relation to testing. Sample the programme's journals across any recent decade and the proportions announce themselves without need of precise counting. Textual and theoretical work predominates: reconstructions of arguments, adjudications between schools, applications of the categories to new phenomena in essay form. Measurement papers form a small minority, and within that minority, papers that replicate or extend another team's series are rarer still, while papers that introduce a new series under a new concordance are the standard form. The shelf of book-length empirical reconstructions, the works a specialist would name as the field's data monuments, is short enough to be memorised: the Shaikh and Tonak reconstruction of the American accounts, Moseley's alternative reckoning of the same economy, the price and value studies gathered around a handful of input-output teams, the cycle estimations scattered across journals rather than gathered in volumes. Set beside the shelf of commentary on any single canonical text, the proportion is an X-ray of the field's incentives. Nothing in the theory requires the proportion. Everything in the field's career structure sustains it, because a commentary can be produced from a library while a reconstruction requires years of data labour, institutional patience, and tolerance for the possibility that the result will embarrass the hypothesis that funded the effort. A literature's testability, in other words, is partly a budget line. Programmes get the evidence their reward systems pay for, and a reader who wants to predict this field's empirical future should watch its hiring and its journal requirements more closely than its manifestos.

## The afterlife of a number

The final stage of a result's career begins when it leaves the literature that produced it, and the stage deserves scrutiny because it is where the damage catalogued in this article is either repaired or compounded. A profit rate series is born, in the honest cases, wearing its concordance like a name tag: computed under stated rules, bounded by stated sensitivities, valid for stated purposes. Watch it travel. The survey article cites it with the rulebook summarised in a clause. The textbook cites the survey and drops the clause. The polemic cites the textbook and promotes the figure to a fact about capitalism. The journalist, working from the polemic under deadline, reports the fact. At each relay the number grows more certain as it grows less qualified, until a magnitude that its own builder would defend only inside a paragraph of conditions circulates as a premise in arguments its builder would not sign. The process is not a Marxian pathology; it is the standard entropy of citation, and it operates on mainstream magnitudes with equal fidelity, as anyone who has traced a newspaper's use of a productivity statistic back to its methodological appendix can testify. But its consequences are asymmetric. A programme whose evidential base is thin cannot afford to have its few solid results and its many soft ones melted into a single circulating certainty, because the eventual correction, when a critic traces the number home and finds the conditions, discredits the solid results in the same bath as the soft ones. Citation discipline is therefore not pedantry in this field. It is the conservation of scarce credibility. Quote directions, name rulebooks, and let levels travel only in the company of their conditions: that rule, observed by the programme's friends, would do more for its standing than a decade of fresh confirmations, because it would make the confirmations believable when they arrive.

## The deflator problem: a fight both traditions share

A compact case, drawn from territory both camps inhabit, shows the convention problem operating at full strength inside a magnitude nobody considers exotic. A real wage is not observed; it is manufactured by dividing a money wage by a price index. Everything therefore depends on the index, and the advanced economies maintain several, constructed on different baskets, different formulas, and different treatments of quality change and new goods. In the United States, the consumer price index and the consumption deflator drawn from the national accounts have diverged persistently across the postwar decades, the first rising faster than the second, and an official commission of economists argued in the nineteen nineties that the index then in use overstated the growth of living costs by failing to credit quality improvements and substitution between goods. Deflate the same money wages by the faster index and the postwar era becomes an age of stagnation for the typical worker. Deflate by the slower and a substantial share of the stagnation dissolves into measurement. The political argument about whether ordinary workers shared in postwar growth, an argument in which Marxian claims about the wage bargain are directly implicated, turns out to be partly an argument about index construction conducted by other means.

### Why does the choice of price index change the wage story?

Because a real wage is a money wage divided by a price index, and rival indices disagree about how fast the cost of living rose. A faster index erases gains a slower one records, so the same pay packets support opposite histories of the postwar standard of living.

The case earns its place in this survey for three reasons. It demonstrates that convention-dependence at trend-reversing scale is not a Marxian deformity but a property of distributional measurement as such, since no one accuses the Bureau of Labor Statistics of running a machine for manufacturing confirmations. It demonstrates the correct professional response, which the mainstream literature in fact supplied: rival series published side by side, the sources of divergence itemised, and conclusions restated as ranges conditional on the deflator, precisely the sensitivity discipline this article prescribes for the profit rate wars. And it locates the exact difference between the two fields' predicaments. The deflator dispute is arbitrated, slowly and imperfectly, by institutions with the authority to revise the official index and restate the past. The Marxian concordance disputes have no such arbiter, which is why identical professional behaviour, rival series honestly published, produces cumulation in one field and stalemate in the other. The disease is the same. The immune system is what differs.

## Forecasting: the test nobody can dodge

Every programme prefers the tests it can schedule, and history schedules one test without asking permission: the future arrives, and dated expectations meet it. The tradition's record under this unscheduled examination is mixed in ways its defenders should state before its critics do. The clearest failures are the breakdown timetables. Henryk Grossmann's 1929 reconstruction of Marx's accumulation scheme extended the reproduction arithmetic across decades of simulated growth and derived, from stated assumptions about rising capital composition, a point at which the surplus would no longer suffice to sustain accumulation at the required rate, a computed appointment with collapse. The appointment passed. Defenders replied, with some justice, that the exercise was a tendency demonstration whose assumptions, frozen technology valves and a fixed real wage among them, were never meant as a calendar. The reply is sound and insufficient, because the book had been received in its time as stronger than a parable, and a programme that lets parables circulate as predictions cannot complain when the world grades them as predictions. Ernest Mandel's later work on long waves attempted the opposite discipline, dating the phases of postwar accumulation and arguing, in the early nineteen seventies, that the long expansion was exhausting itself on schedule; the downturn that followed gave the argument a partial and much-cited vindication, though the elasticity with which long waves are dated makes their prophecies soft targets, as the next section's discussion of periodisation will show.

### What is the tradition's record when it has dated its expectations?

Mixed, and worse than its defenders admit. Breakdown timetables computed in the nineteen twenties failed on schedule, long-wave forecasts scored partial hits amid elastic dating, and the record's honest summary is that the programme predicts mechanisms better than moments, like every other macroeconomic tradition.

Fairness demands the comparative sentence be completed. The official forecasting record of the mainstream across the same century is a chronicle of missed turning points, of recessions predicted by almost no model in real time and of a financial crisis, in 2008, that the representative frameworks had excluded by assumption. Whole-economy prediction is a test that every macroeconomic programme fails with some regularity, because the object under study is reflexive, institutionally unstable, and observed through revisions. The grading in this article therefore treats forecasting the way it treats the other rows: not as a referendum on the programme, but as a component with a record. On that record the tradition neither shames nor distinguishes itself. Its mechanisms have repeatedly identified the pressures that mattered in a crisis after, and occasionally before, the fact. Its calendars have been unreliable whenever it was unwise enough to issue them. A reader who encounters a dated collapse prediction carrying this tradition's authority should apply the field's own best lesson: ask for the mechanism, ask for the conditions, and decline the date.

## The long waves: periodisation as classification in time

The long wave hypothesis, that capitalist development moves in great surges of expansion and stagnation lasting roughly half a century, is the tradition's most seductive empirical claim and its most instructive methodological specimen. Kondratiev assembled the original case statistically in the nineteen twenties, from price series, interest rates, wages, and trade figures reaching back to the late eighteenth century, and the hypothesis has been inherited, revised, and re-dated by every generation since, with Trotsky disputing its mechanical regularity from inside the tradition almost immediately, on the ground that the surges were conditioned by wars, revolutions, and the incorporation of new territories rather than by any internal clock. The methodological interest for this survey is the form the testing took. Researchers applied the statistical machinery of cycle analysis to the long series and asked whether a rhythm of the claimed period stood out from the noise. The answers depended, with embarrassing fidelity, on where the analyst placed the turning points and which series were admitted, because periodisation is classification conducted along the axis of time: to date a wave is to decide which years belong to expansion and which to stagnation, and the decision, like the productive boundary, is guided by the theory and capable of reversing the result.

Spectral methods promised escape from the eyeballing of charts, and delivered a chastening lesson instead. Long samples contain few complete waves, so the statistical power to distinguish a genuine half-century rhythm from a wandering random walk is feeble, and analysts of unimpeachable mainstream standing, examining the same price histories, have alternately detected the rhythm and dissolved it. The long wave therefore sits in this article's taxonomy exactly where the profit tendency sits, and for the same reason: a claim whose evidence is real but whose adjudication is hostage to a prior act of classification, here the dating of phases, there the drawing of boundaries. What can be salvaged is also parallel. Beneath the contested rhythm lie component claims that fare better: that clusters of basic innovations bunch in time, that investment in long-lived infrastructure imparts a generational pulse to accumulation, that the postwar expansion and its exhaustion form a coherent period with distinctive distributional behaviour. Each component can be, and largely has been, examined on its own evidence. The wave as a single majestic object remains a periodisation in search of a test that its own elasticity keeps postponing, and the honest map marks it accordingly: a heuristic of proven fertility, a hypothesis of unproven rhythm.

## Leontief's tables: the instrument the tradition built

There is an irony at the foundation of the programme's best row, and a survey of its testing fortunes should not leave the irony uncollected. The input-output table, the instrument that made price and value computations possible, descends in part from the tradition's own theoretical furniture. Marx's reproduction schemas posed the question the table answers: what does each branch of production buy from every other, and what proportions must hold for the whole to reproduce itself? The first attempt to answer it statistically for a real economy was the balance of the national economy constructed in the Soviet Union in the mid nineteen twenties under the statistician Pavel Popov, an exercise mounted by the planning apparatus of a state that claimed the tradition's authority. Wassily Leontief, who participated in that milieu before emigrating, carried the idea to the United States and rebuilt it as the input-output method, for which he received economics' highest honours and which every statistical office on earth now maintains. The data infrastructure on which the price-value literature stands is thus the tradition's own grandchild, naturalised by the mainstream and repatriated by the empiricists who compute values from its grids.

The genealogy matters beyond its charm. It demonstrates that the traffic between this programme and the world's statistical infrastructure has not been one-way parasitism; the tradition has, at least once, contributed an instrument that every rival programme now uses without thinking of its provenance. It also sharpens the puzzle of the classification problem by contrast. Where the tradition's contribution was a technique for organising observables, it conquered the world's statistical offices within a generation. Where its categories required the offices to sort labour by function rather than by industry, no conquest followed, because the second demand asked the infrastructure to change its questions rather than its arithmetic. Instruments travel when they serve the questions already being asked. The concordance the field needs would serve questions only it is asking, which is why it will have to be built, and maintained, by the field itself or not at all. The Leontief precedent shows the building is possible. It also shows the price: a generation of unglamorous statistical labour by people whose names the theory seminars rarely speak.

## How a claim migrates under pressure: the Okishio episode

A programme's honesty is visible less in its confirmations than in what happens to a claim after a strong result lands against it, and the profit tendency's biography is the best case study the tradition offers. The claim in its classical form held that the rising mass of machinery per worker would drag the rate of profit downward. In 1961 the Japanese economist Nobuo Okishio proved a theorem that cut the ground from the classical mechanism: when capitalists adopt only techniques that reduce their costs at current prices and wages, the equilibrium rate of profit in the system that results cannot fall; it rises, unless real wages increase enough to absorb the gain. The proof was short, general, and conducted entirely within the value and price categories the tradition itself supplied. It deserves emphasis that Okishio was no outside raider; he worked inside the Japanese Marxian economics that was then among the most technically accomplished in the world, and his theorem was received there as a contribution to the theory, not an attack on it.

Watch what the claim did next, because migration, not death or denial, is the interesting behaviour. One line of defence relocated the tendency's engine from mechanisation alone to the wage side of the theorem: if accumulation tightens labour markets and real wages rise, as the cycle mechanism predicts, then the theorem's escape clause becomes the tendency's vehicle, and profitability is squeezed by the very success of accumulation in employing labour. A second line rebuilt the analysis on temporal rather than simultaneous valuation, arguing that the theorem's equilibrium comparison abstracts from the path along which capitals are actually revalued, and that along the path the rate can fall even as each adopted technique cuts costs. A third line shifted the claim's centre of gravity from a monotone tendency to a cycle of over-accumulation and crisis, in which the tendency operates as pressure rather than as destiny. Each migration preserved something and surrendered something, the something surrendered being, in each case, a portion of the original claim's simplicity and scope. Sceptics will call the sequence a retreat dressed as refinement, and the description is not baseless. But the comparison class matters: this is what every mature programme does when a theorem lands, from the Keynesian reconstruction after the stagflation of the nineteen seventies to the repair of efficient-market claims after the anomalies literature. Claims migrate under pressure or they fossilise. The methodological sin is not migration; it is migration without bookkeeping, the quiet substitution of the revised claim for the original in the programme's advertising. The tradition's bookkeeping on this episode has been better than its reputation, conducted in journals where the theorem, the replies, and the counter-replies remain on permanent record, and a student who reads the sequence learns the most valuable lesson the empirical wing teaches: how a claim with genuine content behaves when the world, and its own mathematics, push back.

## Degrees of confirmation: a taxonomy for readers

The survey has used the vocabulary of testing loosely across very different evidential objects, and a short taxonomy will let readers grade future claims for themselves. At the strongest grade stand formal results: theorems, like Okishio's and Morishima's, which settle what a theory implies with certainty inside stated assumptions, and which data cannot overturn because data never entered them. Their certainty is real and their reach is conditional, since the world is under no obligation to satisfy the assumptions. Next stand pattern confirmations: regularities of shape, sequence, and co-movement, the cycle's circuit and the price-value association among them, which survive across datasets and rulebooks and which rival explanations must work to accommodate. Pattern confirmations are the workhorse evidence of macroeconomics, a field whose systems cannot be experimented upon, and their strength is proportional to the variety of conditions under which the pattern persists. Below them stand directional findings under a single rulebook: a series that falls or rises as a claim expects, inside one concordance, which confirms the conjunction of claim and concordance and neither conjunct separately. Below those stand episodes: historical cases narrated in a claim's terms, the profit squeeze in its first telling among them, whose evidential force depends entirely on how many rival narrations the same facts sustain, and which harden into pattern evidence only when the narration survives hostile re-examination, as the squeeze, in narrowed form, did. At the bottom stand citations of levels and isolated figures, which this article has argued should be treated, in this field, as carrying no evidential weight at all until their rulebooks are produced. The taxonomy is not a courtesy ranking. It is a reading instrument. A claim announced at a high grade and supported at a low one has been mis-sold, and most popular argument about this programme, favourable and hostile alike, consists of exactly such mis-selling: theorems quoted as if they were measurements, episodes quoted as if they were patterns, levels quoted as if they were facts. The map at the head of this article was drawn so that a reader can refuse the mis-selling systematically, by asking of any result the only question that matters: which component does it belong to, and at which grade does its evidence actually stand?

## Simulation: the halfway house between theorem and test

A practice has grown up between proof and estimation that this survey should place on its scale, because a rising share of the programme's younger empiricists work there. Simulation takes a formal model, feeds it magnitudes drawn from the record, and examines the behaviour it generates: whether cycles emerge with plausible periods, whether a crisis can be produced by the specified mechanism without outside blows, whether distributional shares wander in the observed ranges. Stock-flow consistent models, built so that every monetary flow is accounted twice and every stock accumulates its flows, have become a shared instrument between this tradition and the post-Keynesian neighbours with whom it shares journals and conferences, and agent-based constructions, in which thousands of simulated firms and households interact under simple rules, have begun to appear in the tradition's workshops as computing power democratised what once required a mainframe and a ministry.

### What can a calibrated simulation prove?

That a mechanism, fed with plausible magnitudes, can generate behaviour resembling the record, no more and no less. Simulation tests the sufficiency of a mechanism inside a model world. It cannot certify that the same mechanism, rather than another, drove the events the record describes.

The discipline of placing simulation correctly matters because the practice tempts its users to overclaim, and the field's journals have not always resisted the temptation on their behalf. A model that reproduces a stylised fact has shown that its mechanism is sufficient, inside its assumptions, to generate that fact. Since several rival mechanisms are usually sufficient for the same fact, the demonstration discriminates among none of them, and the honest report of a simulation exercise is a possibility result, kin to the theorems graded at the top of this article's taxonomy, not a measurement. Where simulation earns genuine empirical wages is in exploration: tracing which assumptions a mechanism's behaviour depends on, discovering the parameter ranges in which a cycle lives or dies, and thereby telling the estimators where to look and what would surprise them. The tradition has an honourable ancestor for the practice in the planning models descended from the reproduction schemas, the two-sector constructions through which Feldman, and later Mahalanobis in the design of India's second five-year plan, explored how the allocation of investment between machine-making and consumption-goods sectors governs a poor economy's growth path. Those models were run, in effect, on real economies, and the ensuing historical argument about their performance is a reminder that a model's career as an instrument of policy is the harshest simulation audit of all, conducted by events rather than referees. The modern computational versions are safer and weaker at once: safer because no nation's bread depends on them, weaker because nothing outside the screen grades their homework. The map therefore files simulation under formal work with empirical manners, a genuine good, and not a substitute for the estimations whose scarcity remains the field's limiting fact.

## The village surveys: primary data in the agrarian debates

The survey has so far treated the programme's empirics as a literature of national accounts, and one substantial exception corrects the picture. In the study of agrarian change, above all in India, Marxian researchers built their evidence from the ground up, designing village surveys, returning to the same settlements across decades, and classifying households by criteria drawn from the theory itself. The context was the great mode of production debate of the nineteen seventies, in which scholars argued over whether Indian agriculture was capitalist, semi-feudal, or some articulated combination, an argument that could have remained a contest of labels. What kept it empirical was that the participants agreed, unusually, to fight with field data. Utsa Patnaik proposed a measurable criterion of class position based on the ratio between labour hired in from outside the family and labour the family itself performed, a ratio constructible from a household schedule. Teams of researchers then carried such schedules into villages in several states, and later foundations institutionalised the work, resurveying settlements across generations so that differentiation, the splitting of a peasantry into rich peasants hiring labour, middle peasants working their own land, and labourers selling their work to others, could be watched as a process rather than inferred from a snapshot.

### What did village surveys settle in the Indian debates?

They settled that the countryside could be classified household by household according to how much labour a family hired in, sold, or worked itself, and they showed differentiation proceeding along those lines. What the classification meant for the path of development remained, and remains, disputed.

For this article's argument, the agrarian literature is a controlled experiment in the classification problem conducted at human scale. The classification criterion was stated in advance, in print, by its proposer. It was applied by researchers who disputed its meaning and by some who disputed its validity, to data collected by schedules any later team could re-administer, and later teams did exactly that, returning to the same villages with the same instruments and arguing about what had changed. The level problem persisted in miniature, since borderline households migrate between classes as the criterion's thresholds are set differently, and the interpretation problem persisted entire, since the same differentiation tables were read as capitalism triumphant, as semi-feudalism adapting, and as a distinct colonial mode, by scholars looking at identical figures. But the replication norm that the national accounts literature lacks was present here from the start, because the instrument was cheap, public, and designed for repetition. The lesson generalises uncomfortably for the rest of the field. Where the tradition built its own measuring instrument for its own question, it got cumulative, checkable, arguable-over evidence. Where it borrowed the state's instruments and translated them in private, it got the concordance wars. The difference was never the cleverness of the researchers. It was who owned the ruler.

## The critics' case, in the critics' terms

Neutrality in a survey of this kind is not a balance struck by praising and blaming in equal weights. It is the discipline of letting each position appear in the form its holders would recognise, and the critics of this programme's empirical standing deserve their paragraph in that form, gathered rather than scattered. Their case, at its most coherent, runs as follows. The programme announces laws of motion for the most complex object in social science and supports them, after a century and a half, with one well-estimated toy model of a cycle, a correlation between prices and labour contents that its own defenders concede is weaker evidence than it looks, a shelf of distributional series that cannot agree with one another, and a core magnitude that is untestable by its friends' own admission. Its journals are edited by its believers, its referees are drawn from its congregations, and its standards of success are internal enough that a claim can enjoy a long career inside the tradition without once facing a hostile replication. When its predictions have been dated, they have failed; when its measurements have been checked, they have dissolved into their conventions; and the tradition's characteristic response to both events has been to revise the interpretation rather than the doctrine, which is the behaviour Popper identified and which no quantity of methodological sophistication about joint tests should be allowed to launder. A programme, the critics conclude, should be judged by the ratio between the size of its claims and the size of its verified content, and by that ratio this one is the most over-leveraged enterprise in economics.

That case has been answered in pieces throughout this article, and the pieces should now be gathered with the same candour. The ratio argument is sound as far as it goes, and the honest response is to shrink the claims to the size of the verified content rather than to inflate the content by rhetoric, which is the operation the testability map performs. The insulation argument is substantially true of the field's institutions and is a reason for the standards proposed here, not a refutation of any particular result, since results stand or fall by their evidence and not by the sociology of their producers, a principle that protects mainstream findings from guilt by association with a complacent establishment and must protect these findings equally. The Popperian argument is the deepest of the three and the answer to it is the Lakatosian one given earlier: revision in the face of disappointment is the universal behaviour of research programmes, and the discriminating question is whether the revisions generate new testable content or only absorb shocks. On the record assembled here, the answer varies by component, brilliant in one row, discreditable in another, and a verdict that averages them into a single adjective, scientific or unscientific, tells the reader less than the map does. What the critics' case establishes beyond reply is the burden of proof's location. It sits with the programme, and this article has been written on the assumption that the programme's friends serve it best by shouldering the burden in public, component by component, rather than disputing its address.

## Reconstructing one number: a field trip

Abstractions about concordances acquire their proper weight only when the reader has watched a single published figure being rebuilt from its sources. Take, as the specimen, a mid-century value for the American rate of profit of the kind the rival series dispute, and follow the labour its reconstruction demands. The journey begins in the national accounts as published by the Bureau of Economic Analysis, whose tables arrive organised by the statisticians' purposes: industry value added, compensation, and profits in categories that assume the productive boundary lies nowhere because the question is never asked. The rebuilder must first construct the concordance as a working document, assigning each industry's wage bill and surplus between the productive and unproductive columns according to the chosen rule, a labour of weeks when first performed and of days once the template exists. The capital stock must then be summoned from a different statistical family entirely, the fixed asset tables, whose valuations rest on the perpetual inventory method, a century of investment flows depreciated under assumed service lives, and whose two published variants, historical and current cost, embody precisely the fork described in this article's miniature. Income components must be adjusted for the self-employed, whose earnings blend wage and profit; for the inventories whose valuation shifts with prices; for the financial sector's imputed output; and for the taxes that decide whether the rate is struck before the state takes its share or after. At every seam between statistical vintages, the rebuilder meets the quiet catastrophe of the craft: the accounts are revised, benchmarks shift whole decades, categories are renamed and redefined, and a series spanning the postwar era is a patchwork whose stitches must be located and documented before any trend drawn across them means anything at all.

The field trip explains the literature's sociology better than any accusation can. A competent reconstruction is months of skilled work. Its replication is another such investment, rewarded by no journal's prestige and no hiring committee's metric, undertaken only by a rival with a series to defend. The figures that result are therefore few, precious, and fragile, known intimately to perhaps a dozen living people, and the arguments among those people have the density and the bitterness of all disputes in which the participants can see one another's errors in high resolution while the audience sees only the conclusions. It also suggests the cheapest reform available to the field, cheaper than any convention congress: the deposit of reconstruction files, concordance, code, and transformed data together, as a condition of publication in the field's journals, so that the next researcher's months become days and replication stops requiring an act of scholarly heroism. The mainstream's empirical journals learned this reform through their own replication scandals and now enforce versions of it as a matter of routine. The tradition that taught economics to count the social relations inside its statistics can afford to learn, in return, the bookkeeping by which counting is made cumulative.

## The instrument that got built next door: distributional accounts

One neighbouring field faced a version of the concordance problem and solved it in the open, and the comparison is the most instructive in this article because the fields share so much else. Students of income inequality in the late twentieth century worked from sources that refused to agree: household surveys that missed the rich, tax records that covered only filers and defined income as the revenue law pleased, and national accounts totals that exceeded the sum of what either source could find. For decades the literature's findings were hostage to the choice among them, and practitioners knew it. The response, built over the following generation by Thomas Piketty, Emmanuel Saez, Gabriel Zucman, Anthony Atkinson, and their collaborators, was to construct distributional national accounts: a complete allocation of the whole of national income to individuals, assembled from tax data, surveys, and the accounts themselves under rules that were published, debated, and versioned, and housed in a public database where any researcher could rebuild the series under variant assumptions. Where judgement calls were unavoidable, allocating undistributed corporate profits to shareholders being the deepest of them, the builders documented the choice, defended it, and in several cases published the series under the main alternatives side by side.

### What did distributional national accounts prove possible?

That a field can build the concordance it needs. Researchers constructed distributional series from tax records, surveys, and national accounts under published rules, housed them in a public database, and made their conventions arguable in the open. The instrument preceded the cumulation of results.

The consequences for that field were transformative, and they were institutional before they were intellectual. Disagreement migrated from conclusions to conventions, where it could be adjudicated; replication became an afternoon's download rather than a season's archaeology; entrants could begin at the frontier instead of at the concordance; and critics outside the project could attack its rules with its own data, which several did, to the project's improvement. None of this required the field's questions to be easier than the Marxian ones, because they are not: the treatment of retained earnings, of capital gains, of income in kind from public services, are classification decisions every bit as consequential as the productive boundary, and they remain contested with vigour. The difference lies in where the contest happens. In the distributional literature the contest happens upstream, at the instrument, in documentation that outsiders can inspect. In the Marxian measurement literature it happens downstream, series against series, with the instruments half-buried in methodological appendices. This article has argued that the missing Marxian accounting convention is the programme's chief empirical deficit. The distributional precedent shows the deficit is not a fate imposed by the subject matter. It is a project that a determined generation, with funders and a database, completed next door, on a problem of the same shape, within living memory. The testability map's middle rows are waiting for their equivalent act of construction, and the history of the neighbouring field supplies both the blueprint and the rebuke.

## The social wage: the state inside the rate

One fork in the concordance has a literature of its own, fought in the open under its own name, and it deserves separate treatment because it shows the classification problem operating on the state rather than on industry. Exploitation measures built from production accounts stop at the boundary of the firm: wages on one side, surplus on the other. But workers also pay taxes and receive public services, transfers, and pensions, and firms also receive subsidies and pay taxes of their own, so the division of the social product between classes is not settled until the state's flows are allocated too. In Britain in the nineteen seventies this question became the social wage debate. Ian Gough and others attempted to compute, from fiscal incidence data, whether the working class received more from public expenditure than it surrendered in taxation, in which case the state partially refunds exploitation, or less, in which case the state is a further extractor standing behind the employer. The question mattered politically, since it bore on whether defending public services defended workers' living standards or their exploitation's machinery, and it mattered methodologically for exactly this article's reasons.

### What was the social wage dispute about?

Whether the services and transfers of the welfare state add more to workers' real income than the taxes funding them take away, and so whether the state softens or deepens the exploitation the wage conceals. Both sides computed the balance from fiscal incidence data.

For the computation turns on incidence assumptions that are classification decisions wearing fiscal dress. Whether employer social contributions count as wages deferred or as a tax on the firm, whether corporation tax falls on shareholders, consumers, or workers, whether a hospital's services are valued at their cost or at the price a market would charge, each choice moves the balance, and the rival computations of the period differed chiefly in these assumptions while agreeing on the underlying expenditure data. The debate's afterlife is again instructive. Fiscal incidence analysis professionalised into an international practice with documented conventions, standardised by institutes that publish their allocation rules and the sensitivity of findings to them, so that a finance ministry and its critics can argue over a shared ledger. The Marxian exploitation series, by contrast, still differ silently over the state: some strike the rate before all taxes, some net of them, some assign state activity to the unproductive column entire, and the reader is rarely told which treatment produced the figure quoted. The social wage literature's bequest is the demand that the treatment be argued in the open, as a thesis about what the state does, rather than buried as a concordance convenience.

## The Japanese exception: a national school with a division of labour

The map's firmest results cluster in a few places, and the clustering is itself evidence about the conditions under which this programme produces cumulative knowledge. Postwar Japan sustained the strongest institutional base Marxian economics has enjoyed anywhere: university chairs, a large scholarly association, journals of standing, and generations of students trained in both the texts and the quantitative methods of the mainstream. Within that base the school founded by Kozo Uno rebuilt the theory's architecture into levels of analysis, distinguishing the pure theory of a fully capitalist society from stage theories of particular historical configurations and from the empirical analysis of the present, an arrangement that assigned measurement its own level, its own standards, and its own practitioners rather than treating data as theory's illustration. From this milieu came the two formal results this article graded highest, Okishio's theorem on technical change and profitability and Morishima's demonstration linking surplus labour to positive profit, and a steady traffic of technically accomplished work that engaged the international profession on shared ground.

The exception proves the article's institutional rule. Where the tradition possessed departments, examinations, journals with refereeing ambitions, and a recognised internal division of labour between those who formalise and those who measure, it produced the results that fill the strong rows of the map. Where it survived as commentary attached to political movements, or as a specialism tolerated at the margins of departments organised around other questions, it produced commentary. The difference between the Japanese school's output and the output of the tradition in countries where its presence was thinner is not a difference in the intelligence of individual scholars. It is the difference between a field, with the reproductive machinery of a field, and a literature, with only authors. Every prescription this article has offered, the shared concordance, the deposited data, the adversarial estimation, the rewarded replication, is in the end a proposal to build the machinery the Japanese case enjoyed by endowment. Programmes do not test themselves. Institutions test them, or nobody does.

## The long run and the humility of spans

Epochal claims demand epochal data, and the mismatch between the span of the programme's largest assertions and the span of its usable statistics deserves a section of its own. The profit tendency, the falling or stagnating real wage, the concentration of capital, are claims about capitalism across its whole career. Statistical systems capable of bearing them, national accounts with stable definitions, run back only to the middle of the twentieth century for most countries and to the interwar years for a favoured few. Everything earlier must be reconstructed: from tax assessments and probate records, from production censuses taken for forgotten administrative purposes, from price currents and trade returns, in the heroic manner of the Maddison project's long-run estimates of output and population. Such reconstructions are genuine scholarship and fragile evidence at once. Their early segments rest on strata of assumptions, about coverage, about the boundary between market and household production in economies where most output never met a market, about price relatives across centuries of changing baskets, that later readers can inspect only through the reconstructor's documentation.

The century-spanning profit rate series that a few ambitious researchers have assembled for Britain and the United States make the problem vivid. A line drawn from the age of the railway to the age of the microchip crosses at least four statistical regimes, each with its own coverage and conventions, joined by splices the author must design. Whether the line trends downward across the whole span can depend on the treatment of a handful of nineteenth-century decades in which the capital stock is inferred from fire insurance valuations and company balance sheets of surviving firms, a sample biased toward success by the manner of its survival. None of this makes the exercise worthless. It makes its honest unit of reporting the segment rather than the span: findings within a statistical regime, where definitions hold still, deserve more confidence than the grand slope across regimes, whose apparent precision is an artefact of the draughtsmanship. The programme's epochal claims should therefore be read as hypotheses about mechanisms whose tests must be conducted inside regimes and compared cautiously across them. A tendency that operates within every well-measured generation, even if the stitched line across centuries wobbles with its stitches, has been given the strongest support the archive allows. A tendency visible only in the stitching has been given none.

## The wage and productivity gap: the classification problem goes public

Once in a generation, a measurement dispute escapes the journals and becomes a civic argument, and the separation between productivity growth and typical pay growth in the United States has been this generation's instance. The chart at the argument's centre is simple to state. For roughly a quarter century after the Second World War, the output produced per hour of labour and the hourly compensation of typical workers climbed together. Thereafter the lines diverge: productivity kept rising, at a slower pace, while the pay of the median worker and of production workers crept. The Economic Policy Institute's presentations of this divergence became the most widely reproduced exhibit in the distributional politics of the age, cited wherever the fairness of the growth model is argued, and the exhibit matters for this article because its critics attacked it on exactly the grounds this survey has been anatomising. The fight is a public seminar in the classification convention problem, conducted before an audience of millions.

The objections were three, and each will be familiar. The first concerned deflators: the pay series was adjusted for inflation with a consumer price index while output was measured with an implicit price deflator that rises more slowly, so part of the gap is the distance between two rulers rather than between two fortunes. Use one deflator for both lines, the critics argued, and a portion of the divergence dissolves; the reply held that consumer prices are the right ruler for living standards and output prices the right one for product, so the gap between rulers is itself a fact about workers' experience of the economy. The second objection concerned the pay concept: compensation including employer-provided health insurance and pension contributions grows faster than cash wages, and choosing the broader measure narrows the gap further, at the cost of counting as pay some sums workers never see and cannot spend. The third concerned the typical worker: medians, production-worker averages, and all-worker means trace different paths through a distribution whose upper tail stretched, and the choice among them selects the story. Under the most deflationary combination of choices the gap shrinks to modest size; under the exhibit's original choices it is dramatic; and the honest summary, reached slowly by the more careful combatants on both sides, is that a real divergence survives every combination, smaller than the famous chart and larger than its dismissal, its exact width permanently convention-dependent. This article could ask for no better public demonstration of its central claim. A measurement can be robust in direction, contested in magnitude, politically enormous, and methodologically instructive all at once, and the correct public response to such a measurement is neither credulity nor contempt but the discipline of asking which choices built it. That discipline is the whole of the empirical method in this field, and the gap debate's service is to have taught it, inadvertently, to everyone who watched.

## The founder as empiricist: instruments before doctrine

One persistent myth about this tradition, shared oddly by its devotees and its detractors, holds that its empirical thinness descends from its founder, as if the theory began as pure deduction and data were a later embarrassment. The record runs the other way, and correcting it matters because it relocates the field's measurement problem from doctrine to institutions, where this article has insisted it belongs. Open the first volume of Capital at the chapter on the working day and the texture is not Hegelian at all: it is a dossier. Marx built the chapter from the reports of factory inspectors, the testimony of medical officers, parliamentary commissions, and the evidence of operatives, quoting the state's own investigators against the state's own laws at a length that Victorian reviewers remarked on. The inspectors, Leonard Horner chief among them, function in the text as something close to co-investigators: officials whose statutory duty took them onto factory floors, whose reports recorded hours, ages, and injuries with bureaucratic patience, and whose findings a theorist with a library ticket could assemble into an indictment no single witness could have carried. Engels had set the pattern a generation earlier in his account of the English working class, a book built from walking the districts of Manchester, from the reports of commissions and medical men, and from the newspapers of the mill owners themselves.

The most striking exhibit is an instrument. In 1880 Marx drafted a workers' enquiry, a schedule of a hundred questions about hours, wages, conditions, and the organisation of work, printed in a French journal and distributed to workers to complete about their own workplaces. It is a recognisable ancestor of the social survey: standardised questions, addressed to informants selected for their knowledge, intended to aggregate individual reports into a picture no employer or inspector could supply. The returns were meagre and the project faded, but its existence rearranges the genealogy. The tradition's founder did not merely consume the statistics of others; he attempted, once, to manufacture primary data for questions the official instruments were not asking, the same manoeuvre the Indian agrarian researchers would later execute with household schedules and the same manoeuvre whose absence in the national accounts field this article has been lamenting. What the founder lacked was not respect for measurement but machinery: no institute, no staff, no journal with referees, no students assigned to the counting. The machinery is what the Japanese school later supplied for a time, what the distributional accountants built for their field, and what this programme has never durably possessed. Read in that light, the empirical record's unevenness stops looking like a verdict on the theory's content and starts looking like what it is: the predictable output of a programme whose testing division was never staffed, punctuated by demonstrations, from the working day chapter onward, of what its arguments can do when evidence is placed in their hands.

## The hardest critique of the strongest correlation

The price and value row of the map carries the programme's most impressive association and should therefore carry its most searching technical criticism, which it has received, from inside and outside the tradition, in a debate worth following in detail. The findings report that prices and labour values, computed independently for the sectors of whole economies, correspond closely, far more closely than the distance between either and a random allocation would suggest. The criticism begins with scale. Large sectors have large values and large prices because both measures accumulate the same underlying size, so two series can track each other impressively while tracking nothing but bigness. A correlation between the heights of parents and the heights of their adult children is evidence about heredity; a correlation between a sector's value and its price may be, the critics argue, evidence only that elephantine industries are elephantine in every currency. Adjusted measures, comparing deviations and ratios rather than levels, shrink the association, and the argument then turns on how much shrinkage the finding can survive and still testify to anything.

The defenders' replies were methodological and, in the better instances, exemplary of how this field can work. Alternative distance measures were proposed that a scale artefact cannot inflate in the same way. Monte Carlo constructions showed what a purely spurious correspondence, generated by size alone under realistic distributions, would look like, giving the empirical association a null to beat rather than a suspicion to endure. Multi-country replications tested whether the pattern was a peculiarity of one statistical system. The association that survives this gauntlet is thinner than the headline version and different in meaning: not a near-identity of prices and values but a systematic gravitational pull, with deviations patterned by capital intensity in the direction the transformation analysis leads one to expect. Observe what the episode demonstrates about the programme at its best. A strong claim was made in measurable form. A lethal-sounding objection was pressed with technical care. The rejoinders did not reach for interpretation or authority; they built better instruments and ran them. And the claim that emerged was more modest, more precise, and more interesting than the one that entered. If a visitor from another science asked where in this tradition the scientific method may be seen working rather than merely praised, the honest guide would bring them here, to a quarrel about correlation coefficients, and would then have to confess how much of the map remains unvisited by quarrels of this quality.

## The convention that governed: material product accounting

Once, a classification convention recognisably descended from this theory's own distinctions was not a proposal but a state apparatus, complete with manuals, ministries, and decades of published series, and its career is the closest thing history offers to a controlled trial of the concordance idea. The planned economies of the twentieth century kept their accounts under the material product system, a framework that divided the economy by a principle Marxian in ancestry: production meant material production, the making and moving of physical goods, while most services, from health and education to finance and administration, were classified as belonging to the non-productive sphere, sustained by redistribution of the material product rather than contributing to it. National income in that system meant net material product. Whole statistical offices enforced the boundary, enterprises reported through it, and five-year plans were drafted, monitored, and judged in its magnitudes. Here, if anywhere, the productive and unproductive distinction escaped the seminar room and acquired an institution of exactly the kind this article has wished upon the field.

The trial's results cut in several directions, and honesty requires taking them all. The system demonstrated that an alternative accounting framework can be operated at national scale for generations, with trained cadres, stable manuals, and time series long enough to argue over; the concordance problem is not a problem of feasibility. It also demonstrated what classifications do when they are wired to power. Gross output measures, summed across enterprises without netting out intermediate goods, invited double counting and rewarded the production of heavy, input-hungry goods whose value swelled with their own costs; the celebrated pathologies of plan fulfilment, the chandelier too heavy for the ceiling and the nail factory judged by weight, were classification artefacts before they were jokes. Most instructive for this survey is the system's death. When the planned economies dissolved, their statistical offices converted to the System of National Accounts, and the conversion itself manufactured a discontinuity that historians of the transition still dispute: measured output collapses across the early nineteen nineties are partly real catastrophe and partly the shadow cast by changing the instrument mid-crisis, with services suddenly entering the product and material sectors shrinking on paper as relative prices were freed. Scholars arguing over how deep the transition recessions truly were are, knowingly or not, arguing over a concordance. The episode leaves the field a double inheritance: proof that its distinctions can run a statistical state, and a warning that a convention maintained by authority rather than by criticism will eventually measure the authority's needs. A Marxian accounting convention worth having would need the distributional accountants' openness more than the planning ministries' power.

## Concentration: the measurable tendency hiding in plain sight

Among the programme's tendencies, the concentration and centralisation of capital is the one with the richest ready-made evidence, and its absence from the field's own empirical self-image is a small mystery worth correcting. The claims are venerable: competition itself drives capitals to grow, larger capitals absorb or ruin smaller ones, and the share of production commanded by the largest firms rises over time. Unlike value magnitudes, every element of those claims has a statistical shadow. Firm size distributions are recorded in business registers. Merger and acquisition activity is chronicled exhaustively because money attends it. Market concentration is computed routinely by competition authorities, whose indices and ratios exist because regulators need them, maintained to professional standards, published on schedules, and indifferent to the theoretical quarrels of economists. A researcher asking whether industry after industry has passed into fewer hands does not need to build a concordance or plead for a convention. The antitrust state has been keeping the relevant ledgers for a century.

What the ledgers show, examined by industrial economists with no stake in this tradition's fortunes, is a pattern too interesting for either slogan. Concentration within narrowly defined industries has risen in many sectors of the American economy across the late twentieth century, a finding documented in the industrial organisation literature and debated there over the usual suspects, technology, regulation, and the economics of intangible assets. Yet the largest firms simultaneously diversified across industries, so the economy-wide weight of the biggest corporations measures something different from their grip on any single market, and national concentration can climb while local concentration, in the markets where buyers actually meet sellers, moves otherwise. Mergers arrive in waves synchronised with credit conditions and stock valuations, which fits a centralisation story driven by finance as readily as one driven by production. The tradition's claim, in other words, survives translation into the regulators' data as a family of definite, partly confirmed, partly confounded propositions, and the field's relative neglect of this row of its own map says more about its habits than about the evidence. Here was a tendency whose testing required no heroism, no concordance, and no permission, only the willingness to read the ledgers of a neighbouring discipline. That the programme's empiricists mostly walked past an open door, while labouring heroically at the locked ones, is a datum about academic attention that a candid survey must record: fields test what their training consecrates, and this one's training consecrated the transformation problem. The map is offered, among other purposes, as a redistribution of that attention toward the doors that open.

## The physical ledger: metabolism measured in tonnes

One branch of the tradition escaped the money of account altogether and built its evidence in tonnes, and the escape deserves recording because it produced the longest continuous datasets any Marxian research community maintains. The root is in the founder's reading of agricultural chemistry. Marx followed Justus von Liebig's work on soil exhaustion and argued that capitalist agriculture, shipping food and fibre to distant cities whose wastes never returned to the fields, progressively robbed the soil, a rift in the metabolic interchange between society and nature. For a century the passage lived as philosophy. Then researchers in Vienna, Marina Fischer-Kowalski and her colleagues foremost among them, rebuilt it as accounting. Material flow analysis inventories an economy's physical throughput: the biomass harvested, the fossil fuels and ores extracted, the construction minerals moved, the imports and exports weighed, the stocks accumulated in buildings and infrastructure, the wastes and emissions discharged. The units are tonnes rather than currency, which buys the method a unique immunity: no deflator, no exchange rate, and no price revolution can manufacture a trend in a column of weights.

### What do physical accounts measure that money accounts cannot?

The weight of the economy: tonnes of biomass, fossil fuel, ore, and construction material extracted, traded, and accumulated as stocks. Physical accounts track matter and energy through the system in units no price movement can inflate, revealing growth in throughput even where value measures stall or fall.

The Vienna school and its collaborators have carried the accounts back across two centuries for the world and for individual economies, work published in ecological economics journals and absorbed into the statistical programmes this article has already noted, so that the global series now describe extraction multiplying many times over since 1900 while the material intensity of each unit of output declined, a distinction between absolute and relative decoupling that only a physical ledger can draw cleanly. For the testability map, the metabolic literature earns a distinctive place. Its central claims, that accumulation commandeers growing physical throughput and exports ecological burden across distance, are stated in magnitudes that statistical practice can actually deliver, and its research community behaves like the communities this survey has held up as models: shared databases, documented boundaries between domestic extraction and trade, published revisions. The classification problem has a physical analogue, in the system boundaries drawn around an economy's metabolism and in the aggregation of unlike tonnes into single totals, so the immunity is partial rather than complete. But the episode extends the article's central lesson into a second element. Given a magnitude the world will register honestly, this tradition can build cumulative science. Its tragedies have occurred where the magnitude it needed was one the world's instruments were built to hide.

## The historians' empirics: evidence from the archive

A final province of the map belongs to the tradition's historians, whose empirical practice is older than its economists' and judged by a sterner external profession. Historical materialism made claims about the past that archives could embarrass, and its practitioners learned to fight with records. The debate over the transition from feudalism to capitalism, ignited by Maurice Dobb's studies and sharpened by Robert Brenner's intervention, turned on manorial and village evidence: whether demographic cycles or class relations between lords and peasants governed the divergent paths of Eastern and Western Europe was argued from court rolls, rents, and the tenures of identifiable communities, in journals where medievalists with no sympathy for the framework refereed the footnotes. Whatever the verdict on Brenner's thesis, the debate's conduct is the point: propositions about the fourteenth century were specified tightly enough for charters and custumals to count against them, and counted they did, in a literature whose corrections accumulated publicly.

### What did the standard of living debate settle?

Fought over wage series, heights, mortality, and consumption records, it settled that material life for the industrial working class improved slowly and unevenly, with deterioration in the early factory decades in several measures. The timing and distribution of gains remain contested by historians using the same archives.

The controversy over working-class living standards during the industrial revolution shows the same culture operating on quantitative series. Pessimists in the tradition, Engels their founder and Eric Hobsbawm their most formidable modern champion, marshalled real wage indices, urban mortality, and the testimony of commissions; optimists answered with revised wage series, consumption evidence, and eventually anthropometric data, the heights of soldiers and recruits standing proxy for the nutrition of the cohorts that raised them. The argument ran for generations, generated archives-based findings neither camp had expected, and narrowed progressively toward a shared account of slow improvement punctuated by early deterioration, an account written into the textbooks of a profession that includes few Marxists. The historians' province thus returns the survey's verdict by a second route. Where propositions meet records under rules outsiders enforce, the tradition's claims have been tested, corrected, and occasionally absorbed into the common stock of knowledge. The map's weak rows are not weak because the tradition cannot do evidence. They are weak where its economists attempted alone, with borrowed instruments and private rules, what its historians accomplished in company.

## Class counted by questionnaire: the Wright project

Sociology supplies the map with its most methodologically orthodox province. Beginning in the nineteen seventies, Erik Olin Wright rebuilt the concept of class for survey research, defining positions by their relations to productive assets, to organisational authority, and to scarce skills, and then did what theorists rarely do: he raised the money, fielded the questionnaires, and counted. The Comparative Project on Class Structure and Consciousness administered standardised instruments to probability samples in the United States, Sweden, and eventually a widening circle of countries, asking respondents about the property they owned, the decisions they controlled, the labour they supervised, and their views of the arrangements that employed them. The results were published in the general journals of sociology, refereed by scholars who owed the framework nothing, and argued over in the open literature of the discipline for two decades.

The findings rewarded the effort with precisely the mixture this survey has taught the reader to expect from honest testing. The class map itself proved drawable: populations sorted into the constructed categories in proportions that varied across countries in intelligible ways, with Sweden's structure differing from America's along lines the two nations' politics made plausible. The link between class position and class consciousness, the proposition the whole exercise existed to probe, emerged weaker and more conditional than the tradition's rhetoric supposed, mediated by unions, parties, and national institutions to a degree that forced theoretical revision, which Wright undertook in print, complicating his own categories across successive books. Critics disputed the category boundaries, the treatment of managers, the credential rents of experts, and each dispute was conducted with the shared dataset on the table, reanalysable by the disputants. Set beside the national accounts literature, the contrast is the agrarian one repeated in a richer key. Wright owned his instrument. He designed the questionnaire the theory required, published the codebook, archived the data, and invited the profession to break his findings with his own numbers, which it duly attempted. The programme's most cumulative empirical literatures, on three continents and in three disciplines now, share that single biography: somewhere, a researcher stopped translating other people's statistics and built the measuring device the question needed. The map's redrawing, with which this article will close, is in the end a plea for more such biographies.

## The map redrawn: what the evidence permits a careful person to say

The survey can now be folded back into the shape from which it began, and the folding is worth doing slowly, because the distance between the opening question and the closing answer is the article's whole content. Asked whether Marxist economics can be tested, the evidence assembled here permits a careful person to say the following. A mechanism at the programme's heart, the distributional cycle of employment and wage share, has been given exact form and taken to the data of multiple economies, where its predicted pattern appears, imperfectly and disputably, as honest patterns do. Relations between prices and the labour embodied in commodities have been computed for entire economies and found systematic, surviving a technical assault that improved them, while carrying an interpretation their friends and enemies will contest indefinitely. The great distributional rates have been measured with devotion and remain prisoners of the rulebooks that produced them, so that their levels testify to conventions and only their hardiest directions testify to the world. Whole provinces of the tradition that the slogans never mention, agrarian classification, class structure by questionnaire, the physical metabolism of economies, the standard of living across an industrial revolution, have accumulated findings under instruments the researchers owned and rules that outsiders enforced. And the commitment around which the entire structure is organised, the existence of value as a substance behind price, stands where it has always stood, beyond the reach of any counting house, believed or disbelieved on grounds that evidence cannot supply.

Nothing in that summary vindicates the theory, and nothing in it licenses the theory's dismissal, and the refusal of both shortcuts is not timidity but accuracy. The programme that emerges from its own testability map is neither a science in the honorific sense its founders claimed nor a pseudoscience in the dismissive sense its prosecutor coined. It is a research tradition with a tested periphery, a convention-bound middle, and an untestable centre, whose proportions resemble those of other ambitious frameworks for understanding society more closely than either camp enjoys admitting. What distinguishes it, unfavourably, is not the structure but the housekeeping: the missing concordance, the private datasets, the unrewarded replication, the smallness of the company willing to do the counting under hostile eyes. Those are corrigible failings, and the neighbouring fields whose stories thread this article, distributional accounting, fiscal incidence, material flow analysis, show them being corrected by deliberate construction within living memory. Whether the tradition's institutions want the correction badly enough to fund it is a question about the tradition, not about the world, and the world, for its part, keeps generating the data from which an answer could be built: every statistical office that publishes, every census that classifies, every archive that opens adds material to a test that remains, after a century and a half, more promised than performed. The map is offered so that the promise can at last be audited row by row, and so that the next generation of the programme's empiricists, whose existence the map presupposes and whose standards it has tried to state, can begin their work at the frontier of what is known instead of at the concordance, where too many of their predecessors were obliged to spend their strength.

## The limits of this survey, stated

A reference article that has spent its length demanding candour about method owes its readers the same candour about itself, and three limitations should be entered on its record. The first is linguistic and geographic. The literatures surveyed here are overwhelmingly those published in English or in the languages of the North Atlantic academy, supplemented by the Japanese school whose work reached that academy in translation. Substantial empirical traditions inside this programme have lived in other languages, in the political economy institutes of Latin America, in the planning literature of the formerly socialist world, in Chinese scholarship whose engagement with Western datasets follows its own course, and a map drawn from Anglophone shelves will misplace their weight. The second limitation is a bias toward the published. A survey of findings can only grade the tests that reached print, and the file drawers of every field hold the estimations that were abandoned when they refused to sing; how much of this programme's quiet null results never entered the record is unknowable from the record, which is itself a finding about the field's institutions. The third limitation belongs to the directional protocol this article imposed on itself. Refusing precise figures protected the survey from false authority, at the price of withholding from readers the very numbers around which the specialists argue, and a reader who wants the quarrels at full resolution must go to the component dossiers, where the figures live with their rulebooks attached. These limits do not overturn the map. They mark its scale, and a map that marks its own scale is, by the standards this article has enforced on everyone else, the only kind worth drawing.

A fourth limitation is temporal, and it cuts in the surveyed field's favour often enough to deserve separate mention. Much of the literature graded here was produced before the standards this article applies had been articulated anywhere in economics: before data deposit became a journal requirement, before pre-analysis plans had a name, before replication studies could be published as contributions rather than as discourtesies. Judging a nineteen seventies concordance by the bookkeeping norms of a later century risks the anachronism of expecting filing systems from scholars who worked with card indexes and photocopied tables, and whose data files, where they survive at all, survive as printouts in festschrift appendices. The grading in this article should therefore be read as a statement about the programme's accumulated position, the state of the evidence a reader inherits, rather than as a report card on the dead. The living inherit a different situation entirely. The instruments whose absence excused their predecessors have been built next door and demonstrated in public; the datasets sit open; the standards are written down in this article and in a dozen methodological statements besides. What the map chiefly measures, in the end, is the distance between the excuses available to one generation and the excuses available to the next.

There is finally the limitation that any survey shares with the literature it surveys: dependence on the combatants' own accounts of their battles. Where rival series disagree, this article has taken the disagreement itself as the datum and declined to re-referee the underlying computations, a restraint that keeps a reference piece honest but leaves open the possibility that one side in a given quarrel is simply right, its concordance defensible in detail and its opponents' objections answerable, in ways a survey cannot adjudicate without becoming a monograph. Readers who need a verdict inside a particular row, rather than the row's grade, are therefore sent onward with directions rather than destinations: to the dossiers, to the deposited data where it exists, and to the methodological appendices where this field hides its true autobiography. A map that knows where its own knowledge ends has done the work maps are for.

The formal results surveyed along the way carry a parallel caveat. Theorems have been graded here on the testimony of the literature that uses them, their proofs taken as the profession's referees left them, and a reader with the training to re-derive Okishio's result or Morishima's will find the originals short and rewarding. That division of labour is standard in reference writing and harmless where the results are old, multiply checked, and embedded in textbooks. It is recorded here because this article has made a principle of declaring the provenance of every claim it repeats, and its own claims should not be exempt from the bookkeeping it recommends to others.

The same declaration covers the article's architecture. Its centrepiece was assigned by the structure of the evidence rather than chosen for drama: the cycle stands at the front because it is the component that has travelled furthest toward testability, and the untestable centre stands at the back because a reader should meet a programme's strength before its mystery. A different architecture, organised by century or by country, would have told true stories this one omits, and the omission is acknowledged rather than disguised.

## For students and exam candidates

Your examination risk on this topic is a specific one: the question invites a slogan, and the slogan, in either direction, is marked as the failure it is. The candidate who writes that Marxist economics is unfalsifiable has reproduced a charge without meeting the cycle estimations; the candidate who writes that its claims have been confirmed has confused the existence of a literature with the success of its contents. The safe and creditable form is the map's form. Attribute every result to its component, grade it as pattern, direction, or level, and attach the classification caveat to every magnitude you cite. Where a number must appear, write the direction of movement and the span of years rather than the height of a rate, since directions are what the evidence in this field can actually carry. The companion treatment of [exam technique for Marxist economics answers](/2016/08/15/marxist-economics-exam-answers/) works through model phrasings for exactly these questions, including how to present the classification convention problem in the compressed form an answer requires, and how to concede the untestable centre without surrendering the tested periphery. Learn the Goodwin circuit as your worked example: it is the one case where you can name a model, a mechanism, a body of estimations, and a live dispute in four consecutive sentences.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The testability map was designed to be taught from, and a seminar built around it has a natural arc. Begin with the slogan and let the group defend it, then set the Goodwin estimations against it and watch the slogan require its first qualification. Move down the rows and let each qualification accumulate, until the class discovers that the interesting question was never the slogan but the gradient. The strongest objection, that the finding is built into the setup, makes an excellent formal debate, since both sides can be argued from materials inside this article and the verdict turns on the genuinely instructive distinction between constraint and dictation. For assessment design, the article's standards convert directly into exercises: give two groups the same published accounts and different concordances, and let the collision of their results teach the classification problem more durably than any lecture. Teachers assembling question banks on political economy will find the [ReportMedic explorer of previous-year examination questions](https://reportmedic.org/tools/upsc-pyq-explorer.html) a useful quarry for how these topics have actually been examined, which helps align seminar ambitions with the forms in which students will eventually be judged.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Researchers entering this field should begin from the component dossiers rather than from generalities: the [series investigation of price and value evidence](/2010/10/01/labour-theory-of-value-evidence/) for the strongest association in the programme, the [reconstruction literature on exploitation rates](/2011/03/01/measuring-rate-of-exploitation/) for the classification problem at its most instructive, and the [dossier on profit rate evidence](/2013/12/01/rate-of-profit-empirical-evidence/) for the field's central unresolved measurement war. Read each with the three grading questions from this article in hand: was the rule fixed in advance, is sensitivity reported, and was a defeat condition named? Historians of economics will find in the same materials a case study of how a research programme's fate can turn on metrology rather than on ideas, and the agrarian survey literature offers the control case in which the tradition built its own instrument and reaped cumulative results. Journalists face the sharpest version of the citation problem, since a deadline strips caveats the way a relay race strips a baton's wrapping. The rules that survive deadline pressure are three. Report directions, not heights. Name the component, never the whole theory, as what a result supports or damages. And where a striking figure is offered, ask for its rulebook before printing it; a source who cannot produce the rulebook has not given you a finding, whatever the figure's pedigree. Evidence in this field exists, and the public deserves it at its true strength, neither inflated into vindication nor dismissed into folklore.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Can Marxist economics be tested empirically?

In part, and the parts differ in kind. One component, the model of the wage and employment cycle, has been written as a formal system and fitted to the records of multiple economies, with the predicted circuit appearing in the data. Other components, the rates of exploitation and profit among them, have been measured repeatedly, though every measurement passes through a contested translation of the official statistics. One commitment, the existence of value magnitudes behind prices, admits no measurement procedure at all. A single verdict covering all three situations misdescribes each of them.

### Q: What is the Goodwin growth cycle and has it been tested?

Richard Goodwin published the model in 1967, turning a mechanism Marx had sketched in prose into a two-variable feedback system. High employment strengthens wage bargaining, the wage share climbs, profitability is squeezed, accumulation falters, employment falls, and the weakened bargaining position of labour then restores the margin from which accumulation recovers. Estimations followed for the postwar records of several economies, carried out by scholars inside the tradition and by specialists in economic dynamics outside it. The circuit the model predicts shows up in the data broadly as specified, while its amplitude, timing, and inner mechanism remain under dispute.

### Q: Which parts of the programme have been tested?

The relation between prices and labour values has been computed for whole economies from input-output tables and compared with market outcomes across hundreds of sectors. The distributional cycle has been estimated as a formal model. Exploitation and profit rates have been reconstructed from national accounts by rival teams working to rival rulebooks, yielding agreement on some broad movements and disagreement on heights and, for profit, occasionally on direction. The labour share is tracked by a large neighbouring literature. Crisis tendencies have been examined mainly through episodes, and the priority of value over price has not been tested at all.

### Q: Which parts cannot currently be tested?

The chief case is the claim that value magnitudes exist prior to the prices that express them. Every procedure for exhibiting such a magnitude constructs it from technical coefficients and labour records drawn from the same statistical system that registers prices, so the construction cannot certify an existence independent of itself. Claims about whole-system breakdown, stated as tendencies without form or horizon, also resist testing in their usual shape, since any quiet decade can be absorbed as postponement. Testability fails in this programme at exactly two places: where the object is defined out of observation's reach, and where the claim is phrased to survive every outcome.

### Q: Why does data classification matter so much here?

Because the theory's categories sort economic life by function, by the role an activity plays in producing, realising, or consuming value, while official statistics sort the same life by industry and institution. Translating between the two grids is unavoidable, and the translation is interpretive labour rather than clerical copying. Where the productive boundary is drawn, how the capital stock is valued, and whether finance and the state sit inside or outside the productive economy are decisions that precede all counting, and they are weighty enough to fix the range inside which every result must fall, occasionally reversing a trend outright.

### Q: Is there a Marxist national accounting convention?

No shared convention exists. National accounting in general works through one: an internationally maintained system, revised at long intervals, with historical series restated when the rules change. Marxian measurement has no maintaining body and no agreed concordance between official categories and its own. Individual schools and research groups settle their own translation rules, publish series under them, and defend them against rivals, so a figure from one team cannot be set beside a figure from another with any known margin of comparability. Proposals for common standards recur in the literature. None has acquired an institution capable of enforcing it.

### Q: Do Marxist economists test what would falsify their claims?

The record is uneven. The cycle literature behaves as testing should: a formal model, estimations by unsympathetic as well as sympathetic hands, and disputes conducted over observable features of the results. Elsewhere the falsification condition is seldom declared in advance, tests cluster where support is likely, and disappointing outcomes are too often met by adjusting the classification rather than by revising the claim. That pattern is not unique to this field; protective revision is a general temptation of research programmes. But the field's small size gives it fewer independent results with which to correct itself, so the discipline of naming defeat conditions in advance matters here more, not less.

### Q: What would count as progress in this programme?

Progress would look less like a new theory than like plumbing. A published concordance that rival teams actually use would make results comparable for the first time. Deposited datasets and reconstruction files would turn replication from heroism into routine. Adversarial estimation, in which a tendency is computed under a hostile rulebook and reported either way, would give the contested rows of the testability map their first independent verdicts. Findings stated as directions and patterns would accumulate where level claims have cancelled one another out. None of this requires the theory to change. It requires the field's institutions, its journals above all, to reward the work that makes evidence cumulative.

### Q: Why is replication limited in this literature?

Several causes compound. The community of active empiricists is small, spread across few journals, so the pool of potential replicators is tiny to begin with. Replication is unrewarded across economics generally, and here it carries a surcharge: before a result can be checked, the checker must rebuild the original author's classification of the raw accounts, usually from a compressed methodological description, using data files that were historically kept privately. A failed attempt then proves little, since it may only show that two concordances differ. Systematic deposit of data and code, standard elsewhere, would remove much of the surcharge at a stroke.

### Q: What standards should this research be held to?

Four standards follow from the field's own difficulties. The classification rule should be fixed and published before results are computed, so that it cannot be tuned to the answer. Sensitivity to the main rival rules should be reported as a matter of course, so readers can see which findings are robust and which are artefacts. Tests should aim at directions, turning points, and patterns, the quantities that survive translation, rather than at levels that belong to rulebooks. And every study should state in advance what outcome would count against its hypothesis. These are attainable standards, approximated already by the best work in the literature.

### Q: How does the classification convention problem change a profit rate series?

A profit rate is a ratio between a constructed flow and a constructed stock, and construction decisions enter both. Value the capital stock at replacement cost and the denominator grows with inflation; value it at historic cost and the rate rides on prices paid in cheaper money, manufacturing booms no manager remembers. Count finance inside the productive economy and both terms change again; strike the rate before taxes rather than after and the level shifts once more. Across decades these choices are large enough, jointly, to reverse the sign of the trend the series is built to reveal, which is why the same economy owns rival series pointing in opposite directions.

### Q: What should a researcher separate between testing a claim and testing a vocabulary?

A claim can fail while the vocabulary that framed it remains useful, and a vocabulary can organise fruitful work for decades without its boldest claims surviving. Researchers should therefore report results against the specific proposition under examination, not against the tradition as a whole, and should judge a vocabulary by a different standard: whether it keeps generating propositions definite enough to test. Confusing the two ledgers produces the field's characteristic exaggerations, in which one estimated model is advertised as the theory confirmed, or one failed magnitude is mourned as the theory refuted. Neither advertisement survives contact with the component structure of the programme.

### Q: Why do the same data yield different rates under different conventions?

Because a rate is never read off the data; it is computed from aggregates that must first be assembled, and assembly is where conventions live. National accounts report industries, sectors, and income types, not Marxian magnitudes. Turning those reports into a rate of exploitation or of profit requires assigning categories, valuing stocks, and placing whole industries on one side or another of the productive boundary. Two competent researchers applying different defensible assignments to identical published tables will obtain different rates as surely as two surveyors using different datums obtain different heights for the same hill. The disagreement is about the instrument, and only incidentally about arithmetic.

### Q: How does the testability map order the tradition's claims?

By their distance from direct observation. At the near end stand relations among magnitudes that statistical offices publish and theory barely needs to translate, prices against computed values, employment against the wage share; these have been modelled and estimated and carry the programme's firmest results. In the middle stand the great rates, reachable only through a contested concordance, whose evidence arrives permanently entangled with the rulebook used. At the far end stand commitments about what exists behind the observables, for which no measurement route has been produced. The ordering is a gradient, not a wall, and components can move along it when someone rebuilds a claim into testable form.

### Q: What separates a direction of movement from a point estimate in this literature?

A point estimate, the height of a rate in a stated year, is manufactured jointly by the data and by the classification rule, so a rival rule manufactures a different height from the same statistics. A direction of movement, a rise or fall sustained across a long span, often persists when the rule is changed, because rival rules tend to shift heights together while preserving the broad shape of the path. That asymmetry is why the strongest citations in this field report directions and patterns, and why a point estimate quoted without its rulebook should be treated as an artefact rather than a finding.
