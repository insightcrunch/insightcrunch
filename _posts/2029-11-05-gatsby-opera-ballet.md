## The Hardest Adaptation Question

The Great Gatsby as opera and ballet represents the outer limit of the adaptation problem, and that is exactly why these works are worth studying rather than filing under curiosities. A film can keep Fitzgerald's dialogue. A stage play can keep his narration. An opera keeps neither in recognizable form, because sung text moves at a fraction of the speed of spoken text, and a ballet keeps no words at all. These adaptations therefore test the sharpest version of the question every other version asks: how much of this novel survives when the prose is taken away entirely?

![The Great Gatsby as opera and ballet: musical and dance adaptations of Fitzgerald's novel - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-67.webp)

The usual reaction is that the answer must be very little, and that a novel this dependent on its sentences cannot survive translation into music and movement. That reaction is understandable and it is wrong in an instructive way. What the opera and the ballets demonstrate is that a substantial portion of the novel is not verbal at all. Gatsby standing on his lawn with his arms stretched toward the dark water is a posture. Daisy weeping into a heap of shirts is an action. Tom leaning aggressively forward is a physical fact. The reunion, the confrontation, the parties, and the drowned mattress are all events that a body or an orchestra can carry.

What cannot survive is Nick, and the wordless adaptations make this clearer than any film ever has, because they remove him completely and let the reader see what is missing.

The claim this article defends is that opera and ballet function as a controlled experiment on the novel. By stripping the prose entirely, they isolate exactly which elements of The Great Gatsby are structural and which are verbal, and the answer is more surprising than most readers expect: the plot, the symbols, the class antagonism, and the emotional architecture all transfer. The irony does not.

## The Opera: Harbison at the Metropolitan

The most significant musical adaptation of the novel is John Harbison's opera, which the Metropolitan Opera commissioned and premiered on December 20, 1999, conducted by James Levine and directed by Mark Lamos. Harbison, an American composer with a substantial concert catalogue, wrote his own libretto, which is unusual and consequential, because it means the adaptation decisions and the musical decisions were made by the same person.

The premiere cast included Jerry Hadley as Gatsby, Dawn Upshaw as Daisy, Susan Graham as Jordan Baker, and Lorraine Hunt Lieberson as Myrtle Wilson. Harbison revised the work after the initial run and it has been staged in other houses since.

The single most interesting compositional decision is the presence of period popular songs inside the opera. Harbison composed pastiche 1920s dance numbers, with lyrics by Murray Horwitz, and embedded them in the score, so that the work contains two musical languages: a modernist idiom for the drama and a period popular idiom for the parties. This is not decoration. It is a structural solution to a problem the novel poses.

Fitzgerald's own text works the same way. The party music in the book is functional, commercial, and slightly ridiculous, described as yellow cocktail music, delivered by a band that is no thin five-piece affair, but a whole pitful of oboes and trombones and saxophones and viols and cornets and piccolos, and low and high drums, and culminating in a fictional composition with a grandiose title that Nick admits he did not listen to. The novel keeps popular music at an ironic distance while conducting its serious business in a different register, which is Nick's prose. Harbison reproduces that stratification exactly, using pastiche where Fitzgerald uses commercial song and using his own idiom where Fitzgerald uses commentary. The relationship between the era's real music and the novel's treatment of it is traced in [the article on music and jazz culture in The Great Gatsby](/2027/05/10/jazz-music-culture-gatsby/).

### What is the Great Gatsby opera?

John Harbison composed it on a commission from the Metropolitan Opera, writing his own libretto, and it premiered there on December 20, 1999 under James Levine. Its distinctive feature is a double musical language: a modernist idiom for the drama and pastiche 1920s dance numbers for the parties.

## The Musical-Adaptation Table

Setting the major musical and danced versions against their solutions makes the pattern legible. This table pairs each with its approach to the novel's narration, its handling of the parties, and what the approach costs.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Harbison, Metropolitan Opera, 1999 | Opera with self-written libretto | Nick becomes a participant rather than a frame; interiority moves into vocal line and orchestra | Pastiche 1920s dance numbers embedded in a modernist score | Gains the novel's musical stratification; gives up the retrospective irony |

The last column is the one to read. Every version buys emotional directness and pays with ambiguity, because both music and dance declare feeling where Fitzgerald's prose withholds it.

## What a Ballet Can Do With This Novel

The ballets are less discussed than the opera and they succeed at something the opera cannot, which is class.

The Washington Ballet staged a full-length Gatsby choreographed by Septime Webre in 2010, with an original score. Northern Ballet, in Britain, presented a version choreographed by David Nixon in 2013, with music by Richard Rodney Bennett. Both are narrative ballets in the traditional sense: full evenings that tell the story in sequence, with characters given movement signatures and the central relationships carried by duets.

The reason dance suits this material better than it should is that Fitzgerald wrote a novel about social bodies. Class in The Great Gatsby is not a matter of stated opinion. It is carried in posture, gesture, and physical entitlement. Tom is introduced through his stance, standing with his legs apart on the front porch, with two shining arrogant eyes that had established dominance over his face and the appearance of always leaning aggressively forward. Jordan is introduced through containment, extended full length at her end of the divan, completely motionless, with her chin raised a little as if she were balancing something on it which was quite likely to fall. Daisy is a voice, but she is also a woman who does not stand up when Nick enters. Gatsby's entire project is a project of learning how to hold himself.

All of that is choreography. A ballet can establish in ninety seconds, without a word, that Tom takes up space and Gatsby has learned to, and that the learning is visible. The distinction between old money and new money, which prose has to argue for, is legible in a dancer's line. This is the single strongest case for the form, and it is why the ballets often communicate the novel's social analysis more efficiently than the films do.

Dance also has a natural affinity for the parties. Fitzgerald describes them as movement rather than as conversation: there was dancing now on the canvas in the garden, couples holding each other tortuously, fashionably, and old men pushing young girls backward in eternal graceless circles. That last phrase is a choreographic note. It specifies a shape, an ugliness, and a repetition, and a choreographer can execute it exactly. The novel's parties are, on the page, already partly danced.

### How does ballet render The Great Gatsby?

Through the body's social vocabulary. Fitzgerald establishes class through posture rather than statement: Tom leaning aggressively forward, Jordan holding her chin raised as if balancing something, Gatsby learning how to hold himself. A dancer's line can carry that distinction immediately, and the party scenes are already written as movement.
## The Novel's Own Musical Vocabulary

Before assessing how well music and dance serve this novel, it is worth registering how consistently Fitzgerald reaches for musical and choreographic language when he needs to describe something otherwise unsayable. The habit is systematic and it explains a good deal about why these adaptations work as well as they do.

Daisy is the clearest case. Her defining quality is a voice, and Fitzgerald refuses to describe its sound directly, offering instead a musical analogy: it is the kind of voice that the ear follows up and down, as if each speech is an arrangement of notes that will never be played again. That is a comparison to performance, and it carries a specific implication, which is that Daisy's speech is composed rather than spontaneous. Nick later hears in her voice a singing compulsion, and Gatsby delivers the verdict that her voice is full of money, which Nick immediately confirms. The character is built out of musical terms because Fitzgerald wanted an attribute that a reader could feel and not verify.

Gatsby's transformation gets the same treatment at its most important moment. Before he kisses Daisy he waits, listening for a moment longer to the tuning-fork that had been struck upon a star. A tuning-fork is an instrument for establishing a reference pitch, and one struck upon a star is a reference pitch from an impossible source. That is Fitzgerald describing an idea of perfection in the only vocabulary available to him, which happens to be the vocabulary of music.

The parties are described as sound and motion before anything else. The lights grow brighter as the earth lurches away from the sun, the orchestra plays yellow cocktail music, and the opera of voices pitches a key higher. That last phrase is remarkable: the crowd's noise gets the operatic label while the actual orchestra gets a cocktail label, so the registers have been deliberately swapped. Then the dancing arrives: there was dancing now on the canvas in the garden, couples holding each other tortuously, fashionably, and old men pushing young girls backward in eternal graceless circles.

Even the novel's structure has been described by readers in musical terms, and with reason. It is built on recurrence rather than accumulation. The green light appears three times with a shifted meaning each time. Gatsby's history arrives in a false version and then a corrected one. Chapter nine returns to images from chapter one. That is a theme-and-variation architecture, and it is why a composer working with the material finds the shape already congenial.

None of this means Fitzgerald wanted an opera. It means the novel's own descriptive resources are heavily musical and physical, which is why the wordless forms find more to work with than a first glance suggests.

### Does the novel itself use musical language?

Constantly. Daisy's voice is an arrangement of notes that will never be played again. Gatsby waits, listening to a tuning-fork that had been struck upon a star. A party's noise is an opera of voices pitching a key higher. Fitzgerald reaches for music whenever he needs an effect a reader must feel rather than verify.

## Scene by Scene: What Each Form Can Carry

The general argument sharpens when applied to specific scenes, and running the novel's six load-bearing moments past each form shows precisely where the strengths and failures fall.

The first party in chapter three is dance's easiest win and opera's harder one. The material is already choreographic, the social sorting is visible, and a corps can produce scale that no stage play can. What neither form supplies is Nick's estrangement, since he is the only person there having a bad time and a crowd on stage cannot easily contain a man who is not participating. The best productions solve this by isolating him physically, giving him stillness while everything around him moves, which is a genuinely elegant translation of the prose position.

The reunion in chapter five belongs to dance. Gatsby is reclining against the mantelpiece in a strained counterfeit of perfect ease when the clock tilts under the pressure of his head and he turns and catches it with trembling fingers. The scene's content is physical embarrassment resolving imperfectly into contact, and a pas de deux is built for exactly that trajectory. The shirts follow, with the soft rich heap mounting higher until Daisy bends her head into them and cries stormily, and an accumulating pile of cloth is a stage property that reads instantly.

The Plaza confrontation in chapter seven belongs to opera and to nothing else. Four people need incompatible things simultaneously in a hot room while Mendelssohn's wedding march drifts up from a ballroom below. Prose delivers this in sequence, film in cutting, but an ensemble delivers it as a single sound. The diegetic wedding march is a gift: Fitzgerald wrote a musical irony directly into the scene, and an opera does not have to invent it.

The accident and its aftermath belong to neither form comfortably. The novel withholds the collision itself and reconstructs it through witnesses, and both music and dance are poor at reconstruction, since they occur in a continuous present. Every version has to show what Fitzgerald refused to show, and something is lost each time.

The pool in chapter eight belongs to dance, unexpectedly. Fitzgerald keeps the killing offstage and gives the reader the laden mattress moving irregularly down the pool, disturbed in its accidental course by a small gust of wind and the touch of a cluster of leaves. That is an image of motion without agency, which choreography can render precisely, and stillness on a stage after sustained movement carries weight no film shot achieves.

Nick's closing meditation belongs to music alone. It is not a scene and there is nothing to photograph or dance. It widens from the green light to a fresh, green breast of the new world, then to the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us, and closes on the boats against the current. An orchestra performs a widening reflection natively. This is the one passage the wordless forms handle better than the films, which have all struggled with it, and it is not a coincidence that it is the passage most often quoted.

## What the Body Already Says

A final observation belongs to anyone studying the danced versions, because it changes how the novel reads afterwards.

Fitzgerald characterizes almost everyone through physical bearing before he characterizes them through speech, and the pattern is so consistent that it functions as a system. Tom stands with his legs apart on the front porch and always seems to lean aggressively forward. Jordan lies extended full length at her end of the divan, completely motionless, chin raised as if balancing something quite likely to fall. Daisy and Jordan are first seen with their dresses rippling and fluttering as if they had just been blown back in after a short flight around the house, and Fitzgerald notes that the only completely stationary object in the room was an enormous couch. Wilson is a blond, spiritless man, anaemic, and faintly handsome. Myrtle carries her flesh sensuously as some women can.

Read those together and a grammar emerges. Old money is either aggressively planted or weightlessly suspended; both are forms of not having to hold on. Working people are drained or overfull; both are forms of the body registering strain. Gatsby is the only character described as having acquired his bearing, which is what the compound elegant young roughneck records, and his most exposed moments are all failures of bearing: the trembling arms on the lawn, the tilting clock, the trembling fingers.

A ballet does not need to argue any of this. It shows it, and it shows it in the first minute. That is the reason the danced versions convey the novel's class analysis more efficiently than the films, and it is the reason a reader who has seen one goes back to the book and starts noticing how much of Fitzgerald's characterization is postural. The lesson survives the performance, which is the best thing an adaptation can give a reader.

## The Sincerity Problem

The single finding that unites every wordless adaptation is that they are all more sincere than the novel, and it is worth stating why this happens, because the mechanism is not obvious.

Both music and dance are committed forms. An orchestra playing a passage of longing is longing; it cannot simultaneously suggest that the longing is faintly absurd, because irony in music requires a text or a convention to push against and neither is reliably available. A dancer executing a gesture of reaching is reaching. The forms can be ambiguous about meaning, which is different, but they cannot easily be duplicitous about feeling.

Fitzgerald's method is duplicitous about feeling as a matter of course. Consider what he does with Gatsby's admiration for Daisy in chapter five. He gives the reader a scene of overwhelming emotional payoff and then, in the middle of it, has Nick observe that there must have been moments even that afternoon when Daisy tumbled short of his dreams, not through her fault but because of the colossal vitality of his illusion. The sentence arrives while the scene is still going. The reader is asked to feel the fulfillment and to know it is insufficient at the same instant.

Or consider the treatment of Gatsby's dream in the last paragraphs, where Nick writes that Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us, and then adds that it eluded us then, but that is no matter, before the famous cadence about boats and current. The passage is simultaneously an endorsement and an obituary for the endorsement. It says the belief was beautiful and the belief was futile without ranking them.

There is no orchestral or choreographic equivalent for that operation, and the adaptations do not find one. What they find instead is a version in which the feeling is real and the audience is invited to share it, which is a legitimate reading, is the reading most casual readers already hold, and is not the novel.

That gap is the most valuable thing these works give a student, because it converts a vague claim about Fitzgerald's tone into a demonstrable one. Anyone can assert that the novel is ironic. Watching a serious professional company, working for years with substantial resources, produce a sincere Gatsby and discover that something essential has gone missing is a much harder piece of evidence to argue with.
## What Music Can Do That Prose Cannot

The opera's advantages over every other adaptation are real and are usually overlooked because the form is unfamiliar.

The first is simultaneity. Prose is sequential: Fitzgerald can only tell you one thing at a time, and the reader assembles the layers afterward. Opera can present four people singing different things at once and let the audience hear the contradiction directly. The Plaza Hotel confrontation in chapter seven is written for this. Tom is accusing, Gatsby is insisting, Daisy is failing to say what is required of her, and Nick is present, silent, and complicit while registering that he has turned thirty. On the page these arrive in sequence. In an ensemble they arrive together, and the scene's actual structure, which is four incompatible needs colliding in a hot room, becomes audible in a way no other medium can manage.

The second is the orchestra as commentary. This is the closest any form comes to replacing Nick. An orchestra can undercut a singer, and the audience will register the undercutting without being told. When a character sings of love over music that refuses to agree, the listener experiences precisely the double awareness Fitzgerald builds through Nick's suspicion. It is not the same instrument, but it performs a similar operation, and it is the one genuine answer anyone has found to the irony problem.

The third is that music is good at obsession. Fitzgerald's Gatsby does not develop; he repeats. He has been maintaining one image for five years and the novel is explicit that the image has outgrown its occasion, since there must have been moments even that afternoon when Daisy tumbled short of his dreams through no fault of hers but because of the colossal vitality of his illusion. A recurring motif that returns unchanged is the exact musical form of a fixed idea, and a composer gets this for free where a novelist has to argue for it.

The fourth is that music can render the novel's most abstract passage. Nick's closing meditation is not a scene. It is a widening reflection that moves from Gatsby's green light to Dutch sailors' eyes on a fresh green breast of the new world, then to the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us, and finally to the boats against the current. No film has staged this successfully because there is nothing to photograph. An orchestra can do a widening reflection. It is one of the few things music does better than any other art.

### Can music replace Nick's narration?

Partly. An orchestra can undercut a singer, and an audience registers the contradiction without being told, which reproduces the double awareness Fitzgerald builds through Nick's suspicion. It is the closest any adaptation has come to the irony problem, though it supplies mood rather than a specific unreliable person.

## What Dance Can Do That Music Cannot

Ballet's advantages are different and, for two particular passages, decisive.

The first is that dance can show interiority without declaring it. This sounds counterintuitive, since the assumption is that a wordless form must be less precise about inner life. The opposite is closer to true. A sung emotion is a stated emotion: when a character sings of longing, the audience is told what the character feels, and the telling is sincere by convention. A danced emotion is inferred from movement, which means the audience does interpretive work, which is the same work a reader of Fitzgerald does. Dance withholds where song declares, and the novel withholds.

The second is that ballet handles the reunion better than any other form. Chapter five is a scene of physical awkwardness resolving into something else: Gatsby reclining against the mantelpiece in a strained counterfeit of perfect ease, his head tilting the defunct mantelpiece clock until he turns and catches it with trembling fingers, the long silence, the rain. A pas de deux is built for exactly this, an encounter between two bodies that begins wrong and finds its shape. Choreography can show two people learning how to stand near each other again, and it can show that the learning does not fully succeed, which is the scene's actual content.

The third is that dance can render the novel's most famous image without explaining it. Gatsby stretches out his arms toward the dark water in a curious way, and Nick, glancing seaward involuntarily, distinguishes nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock. That is a body in a posture directed at almost nothing. A dancer can hold it. A film has to decide how brightly to light the light, which risks explaining the symbol before the audience has met it, and a novel has to say something about it. A ballet can leave a man reaching into darkness and let the audience find the meaning, which is closest to the reader's experience on first encounter.

The cost is severe and worth stating plainly. Ballet cannot tell you that Gatsby's money is criminal. It cannot stage Wolfshiem's function, the bond business, or Tom's research. The economic machinery that makes the novel a social analysis rather than a romance is largely unrepresentable in movement, and every danced version therefore tilts toward the love story. That tilt is not a choreographic failure; it is a property of the medium, and recognizing it is part of reading the adaptation intelligently.

## What Is Lost When the Prose Goes

Four things disappear from the novel in these versions, and cataloguing them is the most useful thing a reader can take from studying them.

The first is Nick as an unreliable narrator, and this is the largest loss. In every wordless or sung version, the events become simply what happened. The novel's structure, in which a man reconstructs a summer while telling you on his first page that he is inclined to reserve all judgements and then judging continuously, has no equivalent. Opera can approximate the doubleness through orchestral irony; ballet cannot approximate it at all. The result is that these adaptations are, without exception, more sincere than the book.

The second is the sentence-level texture that constitutes most of the novel's reputation. The tuning-fork that had been struck upon a star, the incomparable milk of wonder, the colossal vitality of his illusion: these are effects with no non-verbal equivalent because they work by combining incompatible registers within a phrase. Music can be grand and dance can be beautiful, but neither can be grand and self-mocking in the same gesture, which is what Fitzgerald's best sentences do.

The third is the economic argument. A ballet cannot show a bond scheme. An opera can mention one in a line of libretto that will pass in four seconds. The novel's insistence that Gatsby's dream is financed by crime, and that Tom's contempt is partly accurate, requires exposition that these forms cannot afford.

The fourth is the timing of information. Fitzgerald controls what the reader knows and when with great care: Gatsby is a rumor for two chapters, his history arrives in a false version and then a true one, and the accident's details are withheld and then supplied. Opera and ballet must present characters as present and legible from their first entrance, because an audience needs to know who is dancing. The mystery flattens.

### What does the novel lose in opera and ballet?

Four things: Nick's unreliability, which has no wordless equivalent; the sentence-level texture, since no gesture can be grand and self-mocking at once; the economic argument connecting Gatsby's dream to crime; and Fitzgerald's control over when the reader learns things, since a danced character must be legible on entrance.
## The Curiosity Charge

The most common dismissal of these works holds that they are marginal, that a Gatsby opera and a Gatsby ballet are novelties for subscribers rather than serious interpretations, and that a student can safely ignore them. That view is comfortable and it is mistaken on three counts.

The first is a matter of resources. An opera commissioned and premiered by the Metropolitan Opera is not a fringe event. It represents years of a major composer's work, a large institutional investment, and a company assembling a premiere cast of that stature. Whatever one concludes about the result, the effort was not casual, and the interpretive decisions embedded in it were made by people with a deep professional stake in getting them right.

The second is a matter of evidence. These versions are the only ones that answer the question of what happens to the novel without its prose, and that question is genuinely important for understanding what the prose is doing. A reader who wants to argue that Fitzgerald's sentences carry the book's meaning has, in the wordless adaptations, an actual experiment rather than an assertion. The plot, the class antagonism, the symbols, and the emotional architecture all survive. The irony does not. That is a finding, and no film provides it, because films keep enough language to blur the test.

The third is a matter of the novel's own preoccupations. This is a book saturated with music and dance. It contains an orchestra, a tenor, a contralto, a fictional symphonic work, a pianist playing commercial songs, dancing on canvas in a garden, and a narrator who describes a party's rising noise as an opera of voices pitching a key higher. Fitzgerald reached for musical and choreographic vocabulary constantly when he needed to describe how his world felt. An adaptation into music and dance is therefore not an alien imposition. It is a return to the terms the novel keeps using about itself.

### Are opera and ballet adaptations marginal curiosities?

No. A Metropolitan Opera commission represents years of a major composer's work and substantial institutional investment. More importantly, these are the only versions that remove the prose entirely, which makes them the one real experiment in what the novel's language is actually carrying and what its structure carries independently.

## Translating the Dream

The most demanding passage for any wordless adaptation is chapter six, where Fitzgerald explains what Gatsby lost by kissing Daisy, and examining how music and dance handle it shows the two forms at their limits.

The passage is a piece of abstract argument dressed as memory. Gatsby, on a sidewalk with the moon out, senses that if he climbed alone he could reach a place where he might suck on the pap of life and gulp down the incomparable milk of wonder. Then Daisy's white face comes up to his own, and he knows that kissing her will wed his unutterable visions to her perishable breath, so that his mind would never romp again like the mind of God. He waits, listening for a moment longer to the tuning-fork that had been struck upon a star. Then he kisses her, and at his lips' touch she blossomed for him like a flower and the incarnation was complete.

Nothing in that paragraph is an event. It is a claim about what an event cost, made retrospectively, in metaphors drawn from religion, physics, and music. And yet the last of those metaphors is a gift to a composer: a tuning-fork struck upon a star is a sustained pitch from an impossible distance, which an orchestra can produce as an actual sustained pitch. The passage hands music its own instrument.

Dance takes the other half. The incarnation was complete, and she blossomed for him like a flower, describe a transformation of a body, and choreography is the art of transformed bodies. A pas de deux can show a man exchanging an infinite possibility for a finite person, and can show that the exchange is both a fulfillment and a diminishment, because two dancers coming together necessarily closes the space around them.

Between them the two forms cover the passage more completely than any film has, and neither can supply the thing that makes it devastating on the page, which is that Nick is telling us this and cannot possibly know it. He is reconstructing a moment he did not witness, from a confession delivered years later by a man with reasons to romanticize. The passage is a beautiful account of an event that reaches the reader through two layers of unreliability, and no orchestra and no dancer can put those layers back. The general problem is worked through in [the article on why The Great Gatsby resists adaptation](/2029/12/10/why-gatsby-resists-adaptation/).

## The Public Domain and the New Commissions

The novel entered the United States public domain on January 1, 2021, and the consequences for music and dance have been substantial though slower to arrive than in theater, because operas and full-length ballets take years to commission and build.

Before 2021, any company wanting to stage the novel needed permission from the Fitzgerald estate, and permission for a musical setting was granted rarely. That scarcity explains why the record before the copyright expired is short: one major opera and a small number of narrative ballets. It does not indicate a lack of interest.

Since 2021 the barrier has gone, and companies have moved into the space at the pace their production cycles allow. What can be said with confidence is that the new work tends toward stylization rather than reconstruction. Where the earlier ballets built period Long Island with period social dance, more recent approaches are readier to abstract the material, treating the parties as pattern and the relationships as geometry rather than as costume drama. That shift matters interpretively, because a stylized Gatsby is a Gatsby about a structure rather than about a decade, and the novel supports that reading well.

The theatrical parallel is close, and the two histories are best read together, since the immersive productions and the recitation experiments described in [the survey of The Great Gatsby on stage](/2029/10/29/gatsby-on-stage/) are responding to the same underlying problem from the other direction. Where the stage keeps the words and struggles with the spectacle, opera and ballet keep the spectacle and abandon the words, and the two bodies of work between them map the full territory. The film versions, which try to hold both and compromise on each, are assessed against the novel in [the comparison of the novel against its film adaptations](/2028/11/27/gatsby-novel-vs-films/).

## The Question These Works Leave Open

Every adaptation of The Great Gatsby has been an attempt to solve the same difficulty, and the opera and the ballets are the only ones that have solved it by declining to fight it. They accept that Nick cannot be carried and build something that works without him, which is more honest than the film versions' habit of installing voiceover and hoping the irony survives the delivery.

That acceptance raises a question the adaptation record has not yet answered, and it is the most interesting one available. Is there a musical or danced form that could reintroduce unreliability rather than abandoning it?

There are hints that the answer is yes. An orchestra contradicting a singer is the beginning of one. A narrator figure danced as a separate presence, watching the action and visibly declining to intervene, is the beginning of another, and it maps onto the novel unusually well, since Nick's defining behaviour is watching and not intervening while a woman is struck, a car is driven away from a body, and a man waits all night outside a house guarding nothing. A choreographer who built the piece around that figure, giving the audience a body on stage whose stillness is the point, would have something no version has yet attempted.

The materials for it are in the text. Nick is present at almost every event and participates in almost none, and his few actions, arranging the reunion and organizing the funeral, are both services to Gatsby rather than assertions of his own. He describes himself as within and without, simultaneously enchanted and repelled by the inexhaustible variety of life, which is a description of a physical position as much as a mental one. A dancer can be within and without. It is one of the few things a dancer can do that no other performer can, because presence on a stage is unambiguous and non-participation is therefore visible.

Whether anyone builds it is a matter for the coming decades, and the conditions are favourable. The copyright is gone, the commissions are flowing, and the newer work is already tending toward abstraction rather than period reconstruction. The novel's reception history has generally rewarded the versions that took its formal problem seriously rather than its surfaces, and the surfaces have now been thoroughly explored by everyone. What remains unclaimed is the interior, and it is not obvious that music and dance cannot get there.
## Writing About an Opera or Ballet Adaptation

These versions are the least written-about adaptations of a heavily written-about novel, which makes them unusually productive essay material, provided the essay uses them as instruments rather than as subjects.

The weak use is the survey. Reporting that an opera exists, naming its composer and premiere, and observing that a ballet also exists produces a paragraph of information with no argument in it.

The first strong use is the subtraction argument, and it is the strongest available. Wordless adaptation is a controlled experiment: it removes the prose and holds everything else constant. An essay can therefore ask what survives, answer that the plot, the class antagonism, the symbols, and the emotional architecture all transfer while the irony does not, and conclude that Fitzgerald's language is doing something specific and identifiable rather than merely being admired. That conclusion is difficult to reach from the text alone and easy to reach with this evidence.

The second strong use is the medium-affinity argument. Identify a passage and show that a particular form is built for it. The Plaza confrontation is written for operatic ensemble, because four incompatible needs collide simultaneously in one room and prose can only deliver them in sequence. Chapter five's reunion is written for a pas de deux, because it is an encounter between two bodies that begins in awkwardness and does not fully resolve. Chapter six's kiss hands a composer a tuning-fork struck upon a star, which an orchestra can produce. Arguing from affinity demonstrates close attention to the structure of the scene, not just its content.

The third strong use is the reversal. Note that music and dance declare emotion while Fitzgerald withholds it, then argue that the adaptations' greater sincerity exposes how much of the novel's effect depends on suspicion rather than sympathy. An audience at a ballet knows what Gatsby feels. A reader of the novel is never quite sure whether to trust the man reporting it.

## Verdict

Opera and ballet are the most revealing adaptations of The Great Gatsby and the least consulted, and the imbalance is worth correcting.

They are revealing because they run the experiment nobody else runs. Films and plays keep some of Fitzgerald's language and therefore cannot show what the language is carrying. Remove it entirely and the results are unambiguous: the story survives, the symbols survive, the class analysis survives and in dance survives better than in any other medium, and the emotional architecture survives with room to spare. What vanishes is Nick, and with him the irony, the timing, the unreliability, and the reader's uncertainty about whether to admire the man at the centre.

That result should change how a reader thinks about the novel. The common claim that Gatsby is a great book because of its beautiful sentences is imprecise. The sentences are not doing decorative work; they are doing epistemological work. They are what makes the reader unsure, and the wordless versions prove it by producing a certain Gatsby every time.

The practical verdict on the works themselves is that Harbison's opera is the most ambitious response the novel has received in any medium, that its double musical language is a genuinely elegant solution to the stratification Fitzgerald built into his own treatment of music, and that the narrative ballets communicate the book's social analysis with an efficiency the films have never matched. Their common limit is not a failure of craft. It is that they must be sincere, and the novel is not.

A reader who studies them comes away knowing something specific: that The Great Gatsby is a story anyone can tell and a book only Fitzgerald wrote, and that the difference between those two things is a narrator who does not entirely trust himself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What opera and ballet adaptations of The Great Gatsby exist?

The principal opera is John Harbison's, commissioned and premiered by the Metropolitan Opera on December 20, 1999, conducted by James Levine and directed by Mark Lamos, with Harbison writing his own libretto. The premiere cast included Jerry Hadley as Gatsby, Dawn Upshaw as Daisy, Susan Graham as Jordan, and Lorraine Hunt Lieberson as Myrtle. On the dance side, the Washington Ballet staged a full-length version choreographed by Septime Webre in 2010 with an original score, and Northern Ballet in Britain presented one choreographed by David Nixon in 2013 with music by Richard Rodney Bennett. Since the novel entered the public domain in 2021, further commissions have followed and the record is still being written.

### Q: What is the Gatsby opera?

John Harbison composed it on commission from the Metropolitan Opera, writing the libretto himself, which is unusual and means the adaptation choices and the musical choices came from the same mind. It premiered at the Met in December 1999 under James Levine and was subsequently revised for later stagings. Its most distinctive feature is a double musical language: Harbison wrote pastiche 1920s dance numbers, with lyrics by Murray Horwitz, and embedded them inside a modernist score, so the parties and the drama speak in different idioms. That stratification is not decoration but a structural solution, since Fitzgerald himself keeps popular music at an ironic distance while conducting his serious business in Nick's prose.

### Q: How does ballet render the novel?

Through the body's social vocabulary, which is more suited to this book than it sounds. Class in The Great Gatsby is carried in posture rather than in stated opinion. Tom is introduced standing with his legs apart on the front porch, with arrogant eyes that had established dominance over his face and the appearance of always leaning aggressively forward. Jordan is introduced as containment, extended full length at her end of the divan, completely motionless, with her chin raised as if balancing something. Gatsby's whole project is learning how to hold himself. A dancer's line establishes those distinctions in seconds. The parties are already written as movement, including old men pushing young girls backward in eternal graceless circles, which is a choreographic instruction.

### Q: How do music and dance test the limits of adaptation?

By removing the element every other version keeps. A film retains Fitzgerald's dialogue; a play can retain his narration. Sung text moves at a fraction of the speed of spoken text, so an opera cannot carry the prose, and a ballet carries no words at all. These works therefore answer the sharpest possible version of the adaptation question, which is what remains when the language is gone. The answer turns out to be substantial: the plot, the symbols, the class antagonism, and the emotional architecture all transfer intact. What does not transfer is Nick's unreliability, and since that is what makes the reader uncertain, the wordless versions are without exception more sincere than the book.

### Q: Are opera and ballet adaptations marginal curiosities?

No, on three grounds. A Metropolitan Opera commission represents years of a major composer's work and a large institutional investment, so the interpretive decisions inside it were made carefully by people with professional stakes in getting them right. More importantly, these are the only versions that remove the prose entirely, which makes them the sole real experiment in what Fitzgerald's language carries as distinct from what his structure carries. And the novel itself is saturated with music and dance: an orchestra, a tenor, a contralto, a fictional symphonic work, a pianist, dancing on canvas, and a narrator who calls a party's rising noise an opera of voices pitching a key higher.

### Q: How are the dream and the parties translated into music and dance?

The parties translate almost directly, because Fitzgerald wrote them as movement and sound rather than as conversation. There was dancing now on the canvas in the garden, couples holding each other tortuously, fashionably, and the band was no thin five-piece affair but a whole pitful of instruments. A choreographer executes that. The dream is harder and is handled by division of labour. Chapter six gives a composer a tuning-fork that had been struck upon a star, which an orchestra can produce as an actual sustained pitch as a sustained pitch from an impossible distance. It gives a choreographer the moment when she blossomed for him like a flower and the incarnation was complete, which is a transformation of a body and therefore dance's native subject.

### Q: Who composed the Gatsby opera and when did it premiere?

John Harbison, an American composer with a substantial concert catalogue, wrote both the music and the libretto. The work premiered at the Metropolitan Opera in New York on December 20, 1999, conducted by James Levine with staging by Mark Lamos, and Harbison revised it afterwards for subsequent productions. Writing his own libretto gave him unusual control: the decisions about what to cut, how to compress Fitzgerald's chapters into scenes, and where to let the music rather than the text carry the meaning were all made by the person who would then set them. That integration is visible in the score's structure, particularly in the way the period pastiche numbers are placed against the modernist material.

### Q: Did the Metropolitan Opera commission it?

Yes. The Met commissioned the work and gave the premiere in December 1999, which places it among the more significant American opera commissions of its era. The scale of that undertaking is worth registering when the adaptation is dismissed as a novelty, since a house of that standing does not assemble a premiere cast of that quality, engage its music director to conduct, and mount a new production for a passing curiosity. The commission also reflects a broader institutional interest of the period in building American opera from American literary sources, and Fitzgerald's novel, with its compact plot, small cast, one enormous party scene, and unresolved central relationship, is unusually well suited to the form on paper.

### Q: Who sang the leading roles at the premiere?

Jerry Hadley created the role of Gatsby and Dawn Upshaw sang Daisy, with Susan Graham as Jordan Baker and Lorraine Hunt Lieberson as Myrtle Wilson. The casting is worth noting because it assigns real vocal weight to the women, and that redistribution has an interpretive effect. In the novel Daisy's power is located in a voice Fitzgerald describes only through metaphor, calling it low and thrilling and the kind of voice the ear follows up and down, and Gatsby's verdict that her voice is full of money is endorsed by Nick immediately. Opera is the one form that can give her an actual voice of demonstrated power, which changes her from a described effect into a present force.

### Q: How was the opera received?

Reaction to new operas from major houses is rarely unanimous and this was no exception, with responses divided on the score's idiom and on the adaptation's compressions. What is more useful than the verdict is the nature of the disagreement, which centred on whether the work could carry Fitzgerald's tone rather than on whether it told the story. The story transfers easily; the tone is the difficulty, and reviewers argued about the tone because that is where the real problem sits. The work was revised after the initial run and has been staged since, which is the ordinary life of a serious new opera and is not consistent with the dismissal of it as an experiment that failed.

### Q: Which ballet companies have staged Gatsby?

The Washington Ballet presented a full-length narrative version choreographed by Septime Webre in 2010, using an original score rather than existing period music. Northern Ballet, one of Britain's principal narrative dance companies, premiered a version choreographed by David Nixon in 2013 with music by Richard Rodney Bennett. Both are full evenings in the traditional narrative-ballet form, telling the story in sequence with movement signatures for the characters and the central relationships carried by duets. Since the copyright lapsed in 2021, further commissions have entered development, and the newer work tends toward stylization rather than period reconstruction, treating the parties as pattern and the relationships as geometry.

### Q: What music do the Gatsby ballets use?

The two principal versions took different routes. The Washington Ballet commissioned an original score rather than assembling period recordings, and Northern Ballet used music by Richard Rodney Bennett, a composer with an extensive film and concert catalogue and a genuine facility with jazz idioms, which suits a work that must move between drawing rooms and dance floors. Both approaches avoid the trap that catches film adaptations, which is that authentic 1920s recordings now read as quaint and heritage-flavoured rather than as new and slightly disreputable. A newly composed score can gesture at the period without inheriting the antique sound, and can also supply the recurring motifs that a narrative ballet needs to identify its characters.

### Q: How does a libretto handle Nick's narration?

Badly, and unavoidably so. Sung text moves at roughly a quarter the speed of spoken text, which means a libretto has perhaps a tenth of a novel's word budget. Nick's commentary is the first thing to go, because it is not dramatic action and cannot be staged as dialogue. He therefore becomes a participant rather than a frame: present at the events, involved with Jordan, witness to the confrontation, but no longer the man reconstructing all of it afterwards while insisting he is inclined to reserve all judgements. What partially compensates is the orchestra, which can undercut a singer and let an audience feel a contradiction without being told. That is not the same as an unreliable narrator, but it is the nearest equivalent any medium has found.

### Q: Can dance show what a character is thinking?

It can show it more faithfully than song can, which is counterintuitive. A sung emotion is a declared emotion: when a character sings of longing, the audience is told what they feel and the telling is sincere by convention. A danced emotion must be inferred from movement, which puts the audience in the position of interpreting surfaces, and interpreting surfaces is exactly what a reader of Fitzgerald does. The novel almost never enters a character's head; it gives gestures, postures, and Nick's suspicions and lets the reader assemble the interior. Dance withholds in the same way. Where it fails is not in interiority but in exposition, since no gesture can convey that a fortune came from bootlegging.

### Q: How do these works stage the green light without words?

By trusting the posture, which is what Fitzgerald does. The image as written is a body in a position: Gatsby stretches out his arms toward the dark water in a curious way, and Nick, glancing seaward, distinguishes nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock. Nothing in that requires language. A dancer can hold the reach, and a director can keep the light small and unexplained. The advantage over film is real, since a camera must decide how brightly to light the object and any emphatic choice explains the symbol before the audience has encountered it. The stingiest treatment is the most faithful.

### Q: What is lost when the prose goes?

Four things, and they are consistent across every wordless version. Nick's unreliability disappears entirely, so events become simply what happened rather than what one interested party reports. The sentence-level texture goes, because effects like the colossal vitality of his illusion or the incomparable milk of wonder work by combining incompatible registers inside a phrase, and no gesture can be grand and self-mocking at once. The economic argument goes, since a bond scheme cannot be danced. And Fitzgerald's control over the timing of information goes, because Gatsby is a rumour for two chapters in the novel and must be a legible figure from his first entrance on a stage.

### Q: What is gained when the prose goes?

Simultaneity and physical argument. Prose is sequential, so Fitzgerald can only deliver one strand at a time and the reader assembles the layers afterwards. An operatic ensemble presents four incompatible needs at once, which is the actual structure of the Plaza confrontation, where Tom accuses, Gatsby insists, Daisy fails to say what is required, and Nick stands silent and complicit. Dance supplies the other gain, which is that class becomes visible rather than argued. The difference between a man who has always taken up space and a man who has learned to is legible in a line and takes a novelist paragraphs. Music also renders obsession for free, since a motif that returns unchanged is the exact form of a fixed idea.

### Q: Are there other musical settings of the novel?

The record was constrained until recently by copyright rather than by interest. Before January 1, 2021, staging the novel in any form required permission from the Fitzgerald estate, and permission for musical settings was granted rarely, which is why the pre-2021 catalogue amounts to one major opera and a small number of narrative ballets. Once the work entered the public domain, the barrier vanished, and musical adaptation has expanded across several forms at once, most visibly in musical theatre. Opera and ballet move more slowly because commissions and full-length productions take years to build, so the effects of the copyright lapse in those forms are still arriving rather than fully visible.
