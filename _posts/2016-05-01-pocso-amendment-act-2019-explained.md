The POCSO Amendment Act 2019 is a study in how far a statute can travel in a single direction. The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 had been in force for less than seven years when Parliament returned to it, and the returning legislature changed almost nothing about how offences are defined, reported, investigated or tried. What it changed, with near completeness, was the price attached to conviction. Minimum sentences rose at every point the amendment touched. A new and higher minimum was built for the youngest victims. The gravest offence in the statute acquired the death penalty. A provision on stored material that had punished one commercial act was rebuilt as a ladder of graded offences reaching down to a failure to delete a file. Anyone who wants to understand the amendment has to hold two thoughts together: that its textual work is precise, extensive and in places genuinely clarifying, and that the question it declined to engage is the question the evidence keeps asking, which is whether offences committed overwhelmingly by persons known to the child are reduced by raising the sentence that follows the small minority of cases that end in conviction.

This article follows the amendment as a trail. It states the position the 2012 Act had reached before the amendment, change by change, and then the position the amendment substituted, because an amendment trail is only intelligible as a series of before and after positions and most published summaries fail exactly there, reporting the death penalty in the headline and omitting the restructuring of the storage offences that affects a far larger number of prosecutions. It then takes up the evidence question directly, states the case for enhancement and the case against it at full strength in the voices of those who have actually made them, and closes with a verdict that separates what the amendment demonstrably did from what it was never designed to do.

## The statute the amendment inherited

The principal Act, Act 32 of 2012, received assent in June 2012 and came into force on 14 November 2012. It was a purpose-built code for sexual offences against persons below eighteen, constructed because the general penal law of the time reached such offences only partially and without any child-specific procedure. Its architecture matters for understanding what the 2019 amendment did, because the amendment operated on the sentencing storey of a building whose other floors it left exactly as built. The offences are graded by the nature of the act and by aggravating circumstance. Penetrative sexual assault is defined by Section 3 and punished by Section 4. Aggravated penetrative sexual assault, defined by the long list of circumstances in Section 5, is punished by Section 6. Sexual assault short of penetration is defined by Section 7 and punished by Section 8, and its aggravated form by Sections 9 and 10. Sexual harassment is dealt with by Sections 11 and 12, the use of a child for pornographic purposes by Sections 13 and 14, and the storage of pornographic material involving a child by Section 15. Around those offences the Act builds a procedural shell that is its second half: mandatory reporting under Section 19 with a punishment for failure to report in Section 21, child-friendly recording of statements and medical examination, designated Special Courts under Section 28, presumptions in Sections 29 and 30 that reverse the ordinary burden once the foundational facts are proved, and statutory timelines in Section 35 that direct the child's evidence to be recorded within thirty days of the court taking cognizance and the trial to be completed within a year, as far as possible.

The 2012 sentencing scheme, which is the baseline for everything that follows, was severe by the standards of the penal code it supplemented but measured beside what replaced it. Penetrative sexual assault carried imprisonment of either description for a term of not less than seven years, extendable to imprisonment for life, with a fine. Aggravated penetrative sexual assault carried rigorous imprisonment of not less than ten years, extendable to imprisonment for life, with a fine. Using a child for pornographic purposes carried up to five years on a first conviction and up to seven on a second or subsequent conviction, each with a fine. Storing pornographic material involving a child was an offence only when the storage was for a commercial purpose, punishable on a first conviction with up to three years or a fine or both, and on a second or subsequent conviction with up to five years and a fine. The statute contained no definition of the material itself, and it contained no death penalty anywhere. The full anatomy of that statute, its definitions, its presumptions and its procedure, is the subject of [the pillar account of the POCSO Act 2012](/2016/04/15/pocso-act-2012-explained/), and this article does not retake that ground; its subject is narrower, being the eleven sections of one amending Act and what each of them moved.

The 2012 floors had not been set carelessly, and understanding the 2019 choices requires understanding what the original legislature thought it was doing. The bill that became the 2012 Act was debated against the background of the Ministry of Women and Child Development's 2007 study on child abuse, which had documented the scale of sexual abuse of children and the near absence of any legal instrument addressed to it, and the Act that emerged was deliberately constructed as a protective code rather than a purely punitive one: its long title and its chapter structure give as much space to the child's treatment inside the process as to the offender's sentence, and its sentencing floors were set at levels meant to end the pattern, familiar under the general law, of short sentences for offences against children treated as lesser violations. Seven years for the basic penetrative offence was, in 2012, a substantial floor, roughly double the practical starting point for comparable conduct under the Penal Code as it then stood, and ten years for the aggravated offence marked the legislature's judgment that the catalogue of trust, authority and injury in Section 5 placed those cases in a different order of gravity. The floors were also uniform across the two offences in one respect that later became important: neither section graded the sentence by the age of the child beyond the Act's own threshold of eighteen, except in the single respect that assault on a child below twelve was itself an aggravating circumstance under Section 5. Age-grading of the basic offence is a 2019 idea, and it arrived only after the Penal Code had adopted it in 2018.

Two features of the inherited position deserve emphasis because the amendment's choices are unintelligible without them. The first is that the 2012 Act's punishments were expressed, for the two principal offences, as a floor and a ceiling with judicial discretion between them: a court sentencing for penetrative sexual assault could impose anything from seven years to life, and the distance between the floor and the ceiling was where proportionality reasoning lived. The second is that the Act was already, by design, a statute of mandatory minimums; there was no question in 2019 of introducing minimums to a statute that lacked them. The question was how high the floors should be set, what should sit at the top of the scale, and whether the offences at the margins, the storage offences in particular, should be graded by intention or punished as one undifferentiated act.

## The road to the POCSO Amendment Act 2019

The amendment cannot be separated from the legislative mood of the two years that preceded it. A sequence of widely reported cases involving very young children, above all the gang rape and murder of an eight-year-old in Kathua in January 2018 and the Unnao case in Uttar Pradesh, produced a public demand for capital punishment for the rape of children that the government of the day answered first by ordinance. The Criminal Law (Amendment) Ordinance was promulgated on 21 April 2018 and replaced later that year by the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2018, Act 22 of 2018, assented to on 11 August 2018 and deemed in force from the ordinance date. That Act worked on the general criminal law: it raised the minimum for rape in the Indian Penal Code, inserted Section 376(3) for rape of a girl below sixteen, and inserted Section 376AB, which made rape of a girl below twelve punishable with rigorous imprisonment of not less than twenty years, extendable to imprisonment for the remainder of natural life, or with death. Its single amendment to the POCSO Act itself was consequential: it updated the list of Penal Code provisions in Section 42, the rule that awards the punishment greater in degree where an act offends both statutes, so that the list included the new sections. The 2019 POCSO amendment is the second instrument of the same turn, and the relationship between the two is examined below and in [the comparison of the criminal law amendments on women](/2016/03/15/criminal-law-amendments-women-compared/), because the POCSO amendment's central choices, the twenty-year floor and the death penalty at the top of Section 6, were calibrated to the scale the 2018 Act had already set for the same conduct under the Penal Code.

The parliamentary route of the POCSO amendment itself was doubled, which is part of its record. A first amendment bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 8 January 2019 by the Minister of State for Women and Child Development, Virendra Kumar, and lapsed when that House was dissolved for the general election. The bill was then introduced afresh in the Rajya Sabha on 18 July 2019 by the Minister of Women and Child Development, Smriti Irani, was passed by the Rajya Sabha on 24 July 2019 and by the Lok Sabha on 1 August 2019, and received the assent of the President on 5 August 2019 as Act 25 of 2019. The amending Act left its own commencement to notification, and the Central Government brought it into force on 16 August 2019. The debate, as the record of it shows, was not a contest between a punitive majority and a resistant minority; members across parties supported the bill, and the disagreements voiced were about what punishment alone could not do. The Congress member Vivek Tankha, welcoming the bill, observed that the stringent provisions were not stopping such incidents and pointed to the poor conviction rate. The AIADMK member Vijila Sathyananth supported the bill and demanded chemical castration of convicts, a proposal the bill did not carry and no Indian statute has enacted. Jaya Bachchan of the Samajwadi Party warned that amendments by themselves would not solve the problem and pressed for time-bound disposal. The minister, replying, pointed to the sanctioning of 1,023 fast track courts for rape and POCSO cases and to a national database of sexual offenders, and resisted a suggestion to exempt consensual adolescent cases from the Act, a question that returns later in this article because the amendment made it sharper.

The speed of the bill's passage is itself part of the trail. Introduced in the Rajya Sabha on 18 July 2019, passed by that House six days later, passed by the Lok Sabha on 1 August and assented to on 5 August, the bill completed its parliamentary journey in eighteen days and became law in the form in which it had been introduced, so the text examined in this article is the text the ministry drafted, unamended by either House. The statement of objects and reasons framed the case in terms of rising incidence and the barbarity of the offences, and members were reminded in the debate that an estimated 1.6 lakh cases of child abuse stood pending across the states and Union territories, a figure that cut in both directions at once: it evidenced the scale of the problem the bill addressed, and it evidenced the incapacity of the process, untouched by the bill, to dispose of the cases already inside it. The data complaint surfaced openly, with members noting that the National Crime Records Bureau's published series on crimes against children left Parliament legislating on incomplete figures, and the minister's reply leaned on the institutional programme around the bill, the fast track courts, the offenders' database and witness protection, rather than on any claim that the higher tariff by itself would reduce offending. That distribution of emphasis in the government's own presentation is worth recording, because later discussion has often attributed to the amendment a deterrent promise that its defenders in Parliament stated more cautiously than its critics assumed.

Behind the minister's resistance stood an older and more considered refusal. After the Delhi gang rape of December 2012, the committee appointed to examine criminal law reform, chaired by Justice J. S. Verma with Justice Leila Seth and Gopal Subramanium, reported on 23 January 2013 and addressed the death penalty directly. The committee had received strong representations from women's organisations against capital punishment for sexual offences, and it was not inclined to recommend the death penalty for rape even in the rarest of rare cases, reasoning that there was considerable evidence that the death penalty was not a deterrent to serious crime and that enhanced imprisonment up to the remainder of natural life was the proportionate response. That recommendation shaped [the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act 2013](/2011/02/15/criminal-law-amendment-act-2013/) only partially, since the 2013 Act did introduce death for rape causing death or a persistent vegetative state and for repeat offenders, but for six years the Verma position remained the considered counterweight in every debate on the subject. The 2018 and 2019 amendments represent the point at which Parliament, in two successive sessions and with broad cross-party support, legislated past it. An honest trail has to record both halves of that sentence: the recommendation was expert, evidence-based and made after public consultation, and the departure from it was deliberate, debated and democratically carried.

The trail between the committee's report and the 2019 amendment has one intermediate entry that explains the slope. The Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013, which followed the Verma report within months, accepted most of the committee's architecture but not its restraint at the very top: it introduced the death penalty into the Penal Code for rape that causes the death of the victim or leaves her in a persistent vegetative state, and for a repeat conviction of rape. The specialist statute was not touched in 2013; the POCSO Act was barely a year into force, and the 2013 legislature's capital experiment was run on the general law for adult victims. The sequence that followed is therefore a ratchet with three clicks. In 2013, death for the fatal and repeat cases under the general law. In 2018, death for the rape of a girl below twelve under the general law, decoupled from any requirement that the victim die. In 2019, death carried into the specialist statute and attached to the aggravated offence as such. Each step was defended at the time by the incompleteness of the step before, and the trail as a whole shows how a punishment introduced for the narrowest class becomes, within six years, the stated maximum for a catalogue offence covering conduct of very unequal gravity. Whether that progression is read as a legislature converging on the right grading or as escalation feeding on itself is the value question this article has declined to adjudicate; the mechanical fact of the ratchet, and the dates of its clicks, are matters of record and are the amendment trail's central exhibit.

## The changes, provision by provision

The amending Act has eleven sections, and its footprint across the principal Act is wider than its reputation suggests. It amended Sections 2, 4, 5, 6, 9, 14 and 15 substantively, and it made consequential amendments to Sections 34, 42 and 45. It did not touch the offence definitions in Sections 3, 7 and 11, the punishment for sexual assault in Section 8, the punishment for aggravated sexual assault in Section 10, the reporting machinery, the presumptions, or the trial procedure. That pattern, punishment provisions rewritten while the procedural text stood still, is the single most informative fact about the amendment's theory of change, and the sections that follow take the substantive amendments in the order of the statute.

### Which sections of the POCSO Act did the 2019 amendment actually amend?

The amendment substantively rewrote the punishment provisions in Sections 4, 6, 14 and 15, inserted a definition into Section 2, and adjusted the aggravating circumstances in Sections 5 and 9, with consequential corrections in Sections 34, 42 and 45. It left the offence definitions, Sections 8 and 10, and the procedural code untouched.

That distribution is worth pausing on, because commentary on the amendment habitually describes it as if it had recast the Act. It recast the tariff. The definitions of the principal offences were already seven years old and had generated their own case law, and the amendment did not reopen them; the one definitional insertion, examined next, filled a gap rather than revising a settled term. The procedural chapters, which govern how a case moves from report to judgment and which determine almost everything about whether the tariff is ever reached, were not amended at all in 2019; the changes that strengthened the child's position inside the process came a year later and by subordinate legislation, in the Rules of 2020, and are treated separately below.

### The definition the statute had lacked

Section 2 of the principal Act acquired a new clause (da), defining the expression the Act had used since 2012 without defining. Child pornography, in the inserted definition, means any visual depiction of sexually explicit conduct involving a child, which includes a photograph, a video, a digital or computer generated image indistinguishable from an actual child, and an image created, adapted or modified but appearing to depict a child.

### What does the definition inserted in 2019 actually cover?

It covers any visual depiction of sexually explicit conduct involving a child: a photograph, a video, a digital or computer generated image indistinguishable from an actual child, and an image created, adapted or modified so that it appears to depict a child, whether or not a real child was used in making it.

Three features of the drafting deserve notice. The definition is confined to visual depiction; it does not extend to text or audio. It expressly captures synthetic material, the computer generated image indistinguishable from an actual child and the image created or adapted to appear to depict one, so the absence of an identifiable real child in the production of an image does not by itself take the image outside the definition. And it defines the material by reference to sexually explicit conduct, which ties the definition to the conduct depicted rather than to the purpose of the person holding the image; purpose enters the offence structure later, in Section 15, where it does the grading work. The definition's afterlife has been terminological as much as substantive: the Supreme Court held in 2024 that the expression itself misdescribes the material and directed that it be replaced in court usage by the expression child sexual exploitative and abuse material, a development taken up below because the 2019 definition is the text the Court was reading.

### Section 4: the floor rises, and a second floor is built beneath sixteen

The amendment's treatment of the basic penetrative offence is the template for its method. Section 4 was renumbered as Section 4(1), and in that sub-section the words seven years were replaced by ten years, so the minimum for penetrative sexual assault rose from seven years to ten, with the ceiling of imprisonment for life and liability to fine retained. The amendment then inserted a new sub-section (2), creating a distinct and higher sentencing band for the same offence committed on a child below sixteen years of age: imprisonment for a term of not less than twenty years, extendable to imprisonment for life, which the statute glosses expressly as imprisonment for the remainder of the natural life of the person, with liability to fine. A further inserted sub-section (3) provides that the fine imposed under sub-section (1) shall be just and reasonable and paid to the victim to meet the medical expenses and rehabilitation of the victim.

Each element repays attention. The rise in the general floor, from seven to ten, is a fifty per cent increase in the lowest sentence a court may impose for the offence in its unaggravated form; whatever view is taken of enhancement generally, this is the change that operates in the largest number of penetrative assault convictions, because most convictions under the Act are not of the aggravated offence. The below-sixteen band is a different kind of change: rather than raising one floor, it splits the offence by the age of the victim for sentencing purposes, so that the identical act carries a floor of ten years where the child is sixteen or seventeen and a floor of twenty where the child is younger. The age line chosen, sixteen, tracks the line the 2018 Act had drawn in Section 376(3) of the Penal Code, and the twenty-year floor tracks Section 376AB's floor for the below-twelve offence there, so the two statutes were being kept in rough calibration. The drafting of the fine direction is the one imperfect note: sub-section (3), as inserted, speaks only of the fine imposed under sub-section (1), and says nothing in terms about the fine under the new sub-section (2), although the parallel direction inserted into Section 6 is comprehensive for that section. No court is likely to read the omission as a licence to divert a below-sixteen fine away from the victim, but the asymmetry is in the text and an accurate account should record it.

The below-sixteen band has a second consequence that operates in every contested case rather than in the text: it makes the proof of the child's age a sentencing fact of the first importance. Under the 2012 scheme, age mattered to the offence itself, since the Act applies only where the victim is below eighteen, and to aggravation where the child was below twelve; the band between those lines carried no sentencing consequence. After 2019, the difference between a child of fifteen and a child of sixteen doubles the floor from ten years to twenty, and the difference is litigated accordingly. The determination of age follows the documentary hierarchy the Supreme Court approved for this field in Jarnail Singh v. State of Haryana, matriculation or school records and the birth certificate taking precedence, with medical opinion as the last resort where documents are absent or unreliable, and the benefit of a genuine margin of error in that exercise is contested ground in case after case, because on it rides a decade of the sentence. Schools' records, municipal birth registers and the absence of either in the poorest households therefore became, by the operation of this amendment, part of the sentencing apparatus of the Act, and the practitioner who treats age proof as a formality in a case near the line misunderstands where the case will be fought. The amendment did nothing to regularise the underlying records; it raised the stakes that ride on them, which is the recurring pattern of this trail in miniature. Before 2019, nothing in Section 4 directed the fine toward the victim at all; the fine was simply part of the punishment, and the victim's route to money lay through the compensation provisions elsewhere. The amendment's direction that the fine be just and reasonable and paid to the victim for medical expenses and rehabilitation converts the fine, in the two sections where the direction appears, into a compensatory instrument riding on the sentence, and it did so in the same words the 2018 Act had used for the new Penal Code offences.

### Section 5: two additions at the margins of the aggravated list

Section 5, the catalogue of circumstances that convert penetrative sexual assault into its aggravated form, received two changes, and both are precise. Clause (j) lists the consequences to the child's body and mind that aggravate the offence: physical incapacitation or mental illness or impairment, pregnancy of a female child, and infection with HIV or another life threatening disease. The amendment added a fourth sub-clause, so that a penetrative assault which causes the death of the child is within clause (j) and therefore aggravated, a position that had previously been reachable, if at all, only by charging the killing separately. Clause (s) covered penetrative sexual assault committed in the course of communal or sectarian violence; the amendment substituted a wider formula, communal or sectarian violence or during any natural calamity or in similar situations, so that an assault committed in the disorder of a flood, an earthquake or a comparable breakdown of ordinary protection is aggravated by that circumstance. Commentary on the bill connected the widened clause to the conditions of relief camps and displacement during floods, where children are separated from their ordinary protectors and housed among strangers under the supervision of temporary authority, and the drafting's tailpiece, in similar situations, deliberately leaves the category open to comparable breakdowns of the protective environment rather than attempting an exhaustive list of disasters. The clause is a circumstance of setting rather than of conduct or relationship, like the communal violence clause it extends, and its presence in the catalogue is a reminder that the Section 5 list is not confined to who the offender is and what he does, but extends to the situation he exploits. These are gap-filling amendments of a recognisable kind: neither alters the centre of gravity of the list, and each answers a scenario that prosecutors and commentators had identified as falling outside the letter of the earlier text. The death of the child addition has a further consequence that only becomes visible in Section 6, because it brings the fatal case squarely within the offence that carries the death penalty under the substituted Section 6, rather than leaving the capital question to the law of murder alone.

### Section 6: twenty years, natural life, or death

Section 6 was not amended piecemeal but substituted wholesale, and the substituted text is the centre of the amendment. Whoever commits aggravated penetrative sexual assault is punishable with rigorous imprisonment for a term which shall not be less than twenty years, but which may extend to imprisonment for life, meaning imprisonment for the remainder of the natural life of that person, and shall also be liable to fine, or with death. A second sub-section repeats the fine direction in the same terms as Section 4: the fine shall be just and reasonable and paid to the victim to meet the medical expenses and rehabilitation of the victim.

Against the 2012 baseline, every element moved. The minimum doubled, from ten years to twenty. The description of the imprisonment changed in kind: the 2012 text prescribed rigorous imprisonment between ten years and life without defining life, while the substituted text defines the life term expressly as the remainder of natural life, removing the possibility of reading the sentence as an ordinary life term subject to the usual remission framework in the same way. A fine direction with a compensatory destination was added. And the death penalty entered the statute for the first time, placed by the drafting not as a separate grade but as an alternative at the head of the provision, so that the sentencing court faces a range that runs from twenty years through whole-life imprisonment to death, with the choice among them governed not by anything in the amendment but by the capital sentencing jurisprudence of the Constitution, the rarest of rare framework laid down in Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab, which the amendment neither mentions nor displaces. That last point is the one most often lost in coverage: the amendment made death available for aggravated penetrative sexual assault; it did not make death mandatory, presumptive or ordinary, and a court imposing it must still bring the case within the constitutional discipline that governs every capital sentence in India, weighing aggravating and mitigating circumstances and treating the sentence as the exception. The structure of the substituted section also tells the reader something about legislative intent that the debate confirmed: the twenty-year floor and the natural-life ceiling were the operative deterrent instruments in the government's presentation, and death was the expressive apex, the statement of where the offence sits in the legislature's scale of gravity.

There is a sequencing consequence in the substitution that practitioners noticed at once. Because the aggravated list in Section 5 is long and includes circumstances of very different weight, from assault by a person in a position of trust to assault causing death, the single substituted Section 6 range must cover all of them: the same twenty-year floor applies to the relative who commits the offence in a shared household and to the offender whose assault kills the child, and the differentiation between such cases is left entirely to the court's movement within a range whose bottom is already twenty years. The amendment thus compressed judicial discretion at the bottom of the aggravated scale at the same moment as it expanded the scale at the top, and the tension between those two movements is the practical form of the mandatory minimum problem examined later in this article.

The presence of death at the head of the range also imports a procedural apparatus that the rest of the Act does not use, and a complete account of the substitution should name it. A trial court that imposes a sentence of death does not have the last word: under the procedure code, a death sentence passed by a court of session must be submitted to the High Court for confirmation and does not take effect unless confirmed, and the convicted person is entitled to appeal in that proceeding as a matter of right, so every capital sentence under Section 6 begins a second, mandatory round of scrutiny. The sentencing decision itself must be taken after hearing the accused on the question of sentence, and it is in that hearing that the mitigating material, age, background, mental condition, the circumstances of the offence and the probability of reform, is marshalled against the aggravating case. The practical consequence is that the capital limb of Section 6 will operate in a small number of heavily litigated cases at the very end of long trials, which is consistent with how its defenders presented it, as the apex of the scale rather than its working centre. The working centre of the substituted section is the twenty-year floor and the natural-life alternative, and it is those two elements, not death, that will appear in the great majority of Section 6 sentences. A reader who understands the substitution as primarily a doubling of the floor with a capital alternative attached has understood it as the courts will experience it.

One more feature of the substituted text deserves record, because it distinguishes the Indian drafting from the way the same policy was executed in the Penal Code a year earlier. Section 376AB of the Penal Code confined its capital range to rape of a girl below twelve. Section 6 of this Act attaches the capital alternative to the aggravated offence as such, across the whole Section 5 catalogue and for a child of any age below eighteen, so that the capital question can arise, in principle, in a case involving a seventeen-year-old where the aggravating circumstance is the offender's position of trust. Whether a court would ever bring such a case within the rarest of rare discipline is a question the constitutional framework answers case by case, but the legislature's choice to define the capital field by aggravation rather than by age is a real difference between the two instruments of 2018 and 2019, and it widens the class of cases in which the prosecution may seek death even as the courts' discipline narrows the class in which it may be given.

### Section 9: the calamity clause and the hormone clause

Section 9, the parallel catalogue for aggravated sexual assault short of penetration, received two changes that mirror and extend the Section 5 amendments. Its clause (s) was widened in the same words, so that sexual assault committed in the course of communal or sectarian violence or during any natural calamity or in any similar situations is aggravated. And a new clause (v) was inserted, in terms that have no equivalent anywhere else in the Act: whoever persuades, induces, entices or coerces a child to get administered, or administers, or directs anyone to administer, or helps in getting administered any drug or hormone or any chemical substance to a child, with the intent that such child attains early sexual maturity, commits aggravated sexual assault.

The clause deserves a careful reading, because it is the most unusual provision the amendment wrote. Its conduct element is not a sexual act at all: persuading, inducing, enticing or coercing a child to take a substance, administering it, directing another to administer it, or helping in its administration. Its mental element is specific and demanding: the administration must be done with the intent that the child attains early sexual maturity. What the clause is aimed at is a documented pattern in the commercial sexual exploitation of children, in which children, girls in particular, are given hormonal or other chemical preparations to accelerate physical maturation so that they present as older, a practice reported in the context of trafficking and of children held in exploitative establishments. The legislature's chosen instrument was to classify the practice as aggravated sexual assault, which carries the punishment in Section 10, rather than to create a standalone offence; the classification is debatable, since the administration may occur without any sexual contact by the person administering, but the mischief it addresses is the preparation of a child for sexual exploitation, and the Act has always treated acts preparatory to exploitation as within its concern. Two drafting features should be recorded exactly. The clause sits in Section 9 alone; there is no corresponding clause in Section 5, so the practice is aggravated sexual assault even where the same course of conduct is directed at enabling penetrative abuse, in which case the penetrative offence itself would ordinarily be charged when it occurs. And the clause was inserted after clause (u), at the very end of the Section 9 list, after the words by which clause (u) closes the definition, so that in the bare text it reads as an appendage to a completed list; courts read it as a full member of the catalogue, but its position in the printed section is a small monument to the speed of the drafting.

The clause also marks the point at which this Act's catalogue reaches furthest beyond the sexual act itself, and that reach has a logic worth stating because it will govern how prosecutors use the provision. The persons who administer such substances to children in exploitative settings are typically not the persons who commit the eventual sexual acts; they are the keepers of the establishment, the suppliers and the intermediaries who prepare the child as a commodity. A sexual offences statute that punished only the eventual act would leave that preparatory layer to the general law of abetment, with its requirement of tying the preparation to a specific offence committed in consequence. By classifying the administration itself as aggravated sexual assault, complete when the substance is administered with the stated intent, the amendment gives the preparatory layer its own offence and its own sentence under Section 10, independent of proof that any particular subsequent act occurred. The evidentiary price of that independence is the intent element: the prosecution must prove that the administration was done with the intent that the child attain early sexual maturity, and not for any therapeutic or accidental reason, and in practice that proof will be assembled from the setting, the substances used, the absence of medical indication and the commercial character of the establishment. The clause is therefore best understood as a trafficking-facing provision written into a sexual offences statute, aimed at the economics of exploitation rather than at an individual act of lust, and its rarity in prosecutions, relative to the contact offences, reflects the rarity with which the preparatory layer is itself investigated.

One asymmetry in this corner of the amendment should be recorded, because it qualifies any description of 2019 as a general raising of the Act's floors. The new clause (v) conduct is punished under Section 10, and Section 10 was not amended: the keeper of an establishment who has a child chemically matured for exploitation faces the same five to seven year range that has applied to aggravated sexual assault since 2012, with no enhancement for repetition, organisation or profit written into the punishment provision. A legislature intent on a uniform upward revision would presumably have revisited Section 10 when it widened Section 9, and its failure to do so is consistent with the rest of the trail's evidence that the enhancement was concentrated where public attention was concentrated, on the penetrative offences and the material offences, and that the non-penetrative aggravated offence, for all its catalogue's growth, was left at its original tariff. The point is not that five to seven years understates the clause (v) conduct in every case; it is that the amendment's severity map has a contour, and the contour follows salience rather than any measured ordering of harms.

### Section 14: ceilings converted into floors

The amendment substituted Section 14, the punishment for using a child for pornographic purposes under Section 13, and the substitution works a change of sentencing logic that the headline figures conceal. Under the 2012 text, a first conviction carried imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to five years, with a fine, and a second or subsequent conviction carried a term which may extend to seven years, with a fine. Those were ceilings: the court could impose anything up to the stated term, including a short sentence or, within the general law, a sentence below any floor because there was no floor. The substituted Section 14 provides that whoever uses a child or children for pornographic purposes shall be punished with imprisonment for a term which shall not be less than five years and shall also be liable to fine, and in the event of a second or subsequent conviction, with imprisonment for a term which shall not be less than seven years and also be liable to fine. The figures five and seven survived the amendment unchanged; their grammatical direction reversed. What was the maximum became the minimum. A first conviction that could once have attracted a sentence of a year must attract at least five, and the amendment prescribes no maximum in the substituted text at all, leaving the upper end to the general principles that govern a term expressed by its floor. Sub-section (2), retained in substance, provides that a person who, in the course of using a child for pornographic purposes, himself commits an offence under Section 3, 5, 7 or 9 by directly participating in the pornographic acts is punished for those offences under Sections 4, 6, 8 and 10 respectively, in addition to the Section 14 punishment, so the production offence does not absorb the contact offence committed in making the material. For practitioners this substitution, not the death penalty, is the amendment's most consequential sentencing change after Section 6, because prosecutions for using children in the production of material are far more numerous than capital-eligible aggravated convictions, and every one of them begins at five years.

The offence to which the substituted punishment attaches, defined by Section 13, is wider than the phrase pornographic purposes suggests to a lay reader. Section 13 covers using a child in any form of media, including programmes and advertisements telecast by television channels, internet content and any other electronic or printed form, for the purposes of sexual gratification, and its explanation provides that use of a child includes involving a child through any medium for the preparation, production, offering, transmitting, publishing, facilitation and distribution of the material. The production offence therefore reaches the whole chain of making and moving the material, and the substituted Section 14 sets the floor for everyone convicted within it, from the person who films to the person whose role is facilitation or distribution of the finished product. Read with the graded Section 15, which punishes the keeping of the material at the consumption and circulation end, the 2019 scheme for material offences is complete in a way the 2012 scheme was not: production punished from a floor of five years, commercial keeping from a floor of three, circulation keeping up to three, and passive keeping with intent and default in deleting or reporting by fine. Whatever is said of the penetrative tariff, this part of the amendment is a coherent tariff for a market, constructed on the theory that the market is supplied by producers, moved by circulators and sustained by keepers, and that each layer should be priced. That theory, and not deterrence of the individual assault, is the amendment's most defensible piece of penal design.

### Section 15: one offence becomes three

The substitution of Section 15 is the amendment's most accomplished piece of drafting and the part of it that public discussion has almost entirely ignored. The 2012 Section 15 punished a single, narrowly drawn act: storing or otherwise possessing pornographic material in any form involving a child for a commercial purpose, on a first conviction with imprisonment of up to three years or a fine or both, and on a second or subsequent conviction with imprisonment of up to five years and a fine. Storage for any other purpose, however deliberate, was outside the section, and the person who kept such material for transmission to others, or who simply kept it, committed no offence under the Act by that keeping alone.

The substituted Section 15 builds three offences graded by what the keeping is for and what the keeper does. The first, in sub-section (1), addresses the person who stores or possesses such material but fails to delete or destroy it or report it to the designated authority, as may be prescribed, with an intention to share or transmit child pornography; the liability is a fine of not less than five thousand rupees, and for a second or subsequent offence a fine of not less than ten thousand rupees. The second, in sub-section (2), addresses the person who stores or possesses the material for transmitting or propagating or displaying or distributing it in any manner, except for the purpose of reporting, as may be prescribed, or for use as evidence in court; the punishment is imprisonment of either description which may extend to three years, or a fine, or both. The third, in sub-section (3), preserves the original commercial offence in strengthened form: storage or possession for a commercial purpose carries, on a first conviction, imprisonment of not less than three years and up to five years, or a fine, or both, and on a second or subsequent conviction imprisonment of not less than five years and up to seven years, with liability to fine.

The architecture is a deliberate ladder. At its foot is a regulatory offence of omission, failure to delete, destroy or report, which is punishable only where the keeping is accompanied by an intention to share or transmit, and which is fined rather than imprisoned; the amendment accompanied it with changes to Section 45, the rule-making power, directing rules on the manner of deleting, destroying and reporting material to the designated authority, so the machinery for compliance was contemplated by the same Act that created the duty. In the middle is the dissemination grade, possession held for the purpose of transmission, propagation, display or distribution, carrying up to three years, with express protection for the two innocent reasons a person might hold such material, reporting it and using it as evidence in court. At the top is the commercial grade, where the 2012 ceilings were converted, as in Section 14, into floors for repeat convictions and a floor and ceiling for the first. The graded structure matters beyond its own cases, because it embodies a legislative judgment that the wrong in this area is not possession in the abstract but possession oriented toward circulation or profit, a judgment whose edges the Supreme Court would have to test five years later when the breadth of sub-section (1) came before it. That later reading is the property of the pillar article's account of the Act's judicial life; the position it produced on the terminology, which the brief for this article directs to be stated, is given in the section on the storage provision's afterlife below.

The first grade is also, on close reading, the most legally novel offence the amendment created anywhere in the Act, because it punishes an omission. Every other offence in the statute is built on an act: a touching, a use, a keeping for a stated purpose. Sub-section (1) of the substituted Section 15 is built on a failure, the failure to delete or destroy material in one's possession or to report it to the designated authority, and the omission is criminal only when combined with a specified intention, the intention to share or transmit. The prosecution in such a case must therefore prove two things that sit awkwardly together, a negative and a mental state: that the accused did not delete, destroy or report, and that the keeping was accompanied by the intention the sub-section names. The drafting places the fine at this grade rather than imprisonment, which suggests a regulatory intention, a duty of digital housekeeping owed by anyone who comes to hold such material, with the criminal law's heavier instruments reserved for the grades where the keeper means to circulate or to profit. It also explains the otherwise curious coupling in the amending Act between Section 15 and Section 45: a duty to report is workable only if the manner and destination of reporting are prescribed, and the amendment took care to enlarge the rule-making power in the same breath as it created the duty. The practitioner advising a client who has received such material unsolicited, a situation the design of messaging platforms makes commonplace, works directly from this text: delete it, destroy it or report it to the designated authority through the prescribed route, and the first grade is not engaged, because the omission on which it rests has not occurred.

### The pattern across the changes: age as the organising line

Standing back from the provision-by-provision account, the amendment has a shape that the table in the next section displays in figures. Before 2019, the Act's sentencing for penetrative offences turned on a single distinction, between the basic offence and its aggravated form, and the age of the child mattered to sentence only because assault on a child below twelve was one of the aggravating circumstances. After 2019, age operates as an independent organising line with three bands. A child below twelve stands inside the aggravated offence by force of Section 5(m), so the substituted Section 6 range, twenty years to natural life or death, governs. A child of twelve to fifteen stands inside the new Section 4(2), with its twenty-year floor and natural-life ceiling, unless an aggravating circumstance carries the case into Section 6. A child of sixteen or seventeen stands inside Section 4(1), with the ten-year floor, again subject to Section 6 where the catalogue applies. The same conduct, proved to the same standard, therefore attracts three different floors depending on the victim's age, and the prosecution's selection among the basic, the below-sixteen and the aggravated charges becomes the single most consequential decision in the case, taken at the stage of the charge sheet and largely invisible to public discussion.

The calibration against the Penal Code explains where the numbers came from without wholly justifying the fit. The twenty-year floor of Section 4(2) is the floor the 2018 Act set in Section 376(3) of the Penal Code for rape of a girl below sixteen, and the capital alternative of Section 6 answers Section 376AB, although, as noted, the Penal Code confined death to the below-twelve offence and this Act extends the capital field across the aggravated catalogue at any age below eighteen. Section 42, the greater-in-degree rule, then arbitrates where the same facts are charged under both statutes, awarding the greater punishment. The result is a pair of statutes kept in rough symmetry by deliberate borrowing, and a sentencing map in which the specialist Act is, at every age band, at least as severe as the general law and at the aggravated level more widely capital. Whether that symmetry was worth its price in discretion is the question the mandatory minimum discussion below takes up; what the trail establishes is that the figures in the 2019 amendment were not chosen within the four corners of this Act at all, but were imported from the scale Parliament had set for the Penal Code the previous year, which is why the amendment reads, in retrospect, as the second half of a single legislative act performed in two sessions.

## The compensatory turn inside the punishment sections

The fine directions inserted into Sections 4 and 6 are easy to pass over as boilerplate, since the same formula appears in the Penal Code amendments of 2018, but in this Act they complete a three-part compensatory structure whose other parts the amendment left standing, and the interaction is where practitioners earn their fee. The first part is the fine itself, directed by the new sub-sections to be just and reasonable and paid to the victim for medical expenses and rehabilitation; a sentencing court fixing the fine is therefore required to reason about the victim's medical and rehabilitative needs rather than about the offender's means alone, although the offender's means remain relevant to what can in fact be recovered. The second part is the Special Court's independent power under Section 33(8) of the Act, untouched by the amendment, to direct payment of compensation to the child for physical or mental trauma or for immediate rehabilitation, in addition to the punishment and of a kind that does not depend on the fine at all. The third part is the state-funded layer: the victim compensation schemes maintained under the procedure code, to which Rule 9 of the 2020 Rules connects the interim and final compensation orders described later in this article. The three layers answer different failures. The fine answers the offender's debt and fails where he is impecunious, as offenders in these cases very often are. The Section 33(8) direction answers the trauma as assessed by the trial court at sentencing and fails where the case ends in acquittal or the accused is never traced. The scheme layer answers both failures by paying from public funds, at the price of administrative process. The 2019 amendment strengthened only the first layer and only in the two gravest offence provisions, leaving the architecture's weight where it had always rested, on the court-directed and scheme-funded layers. An account of the amendment that treats the fine direction as a new victim benefit therefore overstates it; it is better described as the alignment of the fine with a compensatory purpose the rest of the Act had already adopted, and its practical yield will be measured in the small class of cases where a convicted offender has assets worth fining.

## The amendments nobody quotes

Three further changes complete the trail, and each is small in the way that consequential amendments usually are. Sections 2(2) and 34 of the principal Act referred to the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2000; the amendment substituted references to the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, bringing two cross-references that had survived the 2015 replacement of the juvenile justice statute into line with it. Section 34 is the provision that routes the procedure where the accused is a child, and its correction matters to the boundary this article touches later: offences committed by children are governed by the juvenile justice legislation, whose own account is given in [the account of the Juvenile Justice Act 2015](/2016/05/15/juvenile-justice-act-2015-explained/), and nothing in the 2019 amendment altered the position of a child accused. Section 42, which directs that where an act is an offence under both this Act and specified provisions of the Penal Code the offender is liable to the punishment greater in degree, and which the 2018 Act had already amended to take in the new Penal Code sections, was extended to include Section 67B of the Information Technology Act, 2000, the provision that punishes publishing or transmitting material depicting children in sexually explicit acts electronically; the extension closed an overlap question between the two statutes that deal with the same material from different directions. Section 45, the rule-making power, was enlarged so that the rules could prescribe the manner of deleting, destroying or reporting material under Section 15(1) and the manner of reporting under Section 15(2), giving the new storage offences their administrative plumbing. None of these changes attracted a speech. Together they show an amendment that was drafted as a working instrument and not only as a statement, which makes its silence on the larger machinery of reporting and trial, to which this article turns next, a choice rather than an oversight.

## What the amendment did not touch

An amendment trail is defined as much by its omissions as by its text, and the omissions here form a coherent set. The amendment did not alter the definition of a child as a person below eighteen, and so did not disturb the line that brings consensual adolescent activity within the Act, a line whose consequences are examined below. It did not alter mandatory reporting under Section 19, which obliges any person who has knowledge or apprehension of an offence to report it to the Special Juvenile Police Unit or the local police, on pain of imprisonment under Section 21; the reporting duty therefore continues to fall, in the run of cases, on the family members and professionals among whom the knowledge first surfaces. It did not alter the presumptions in Sections 29 and 30, the Special Court scheme, the thirty-day direction for recording the child's evidence or the one-year direction for completing trial in Section 35, even though the gap between those directions and actual disposal times was already notorious and was raised in the debate. It did not alter Section 8 or Section 10, so the sentencing position for sexual assault and aggravated sexual assault short of penetration stands exactly where 2012 left it, a reminder that the enhancement was selective and driven by the gravity ordering of the penetrative and pornographic offences rather than by a general retariffing of the Act. And it appropriated nothing and created no institution; the implementation instruments that were announced alongside it, the fast track special courts sanctioned under the Nirbhaya Fund and the national database of sexual offenders maintained by the National Crime Records Bureau under the Ministry of Home Affairs, are executive schemes, not text of the amendment. The fast track scheme is the instrument most often cited as the process half of the 2019 settlement, and its published position is worth stating exactly: the Department of Justice's scheme sanctioned 1,023 fast track special courts including exclusive POCSO courts, and the Ministry of Women and Child Development has published that 754 of them, including 404 exclusive POCSO courts, stood functional across thirty states and Union territories at the end of January 2025, having disposed of more than three lakh cases. Those are the government's own figures, offered here because they are the figures, and they describe an executive programme running in parallel with the statute rather than any obligation the amendment imposed. The distinction matters for the verdict at the close of this article: where the process improved, it improved by scheme, funding and administrative direction, all of which can lapse or be discontinued without any legislature voting, whereas the tariff the amendment wrote into Sections 4, 6, 14 and 15 binds every court until Parliament returns. The wider frame within which this single-statute trail sits, the family of child protection laws of which the Act is one member, is mapped in [the guide to child protection laws in India](/2016/04/01/child-protection-laws-india-guide/); within that frame, 2019 is the year the frame's central statute had its tariff rewritten and its process left alone.

## Reading the amendment with its neighbours

No provision of the amended Act operates alone, and the trail is incomplete without the overlap map, because the same facts in this field routinely constitute offences under three statutes at once. A penetrative assault on a child is an offence under Section 4 or Section 6 of this Act and, where the child is a girl, rape under the Penal Code as recast in 2013 and 2018, with the two tariffs kept in the rough symmetry described earlier. Section 42 arbitrates the overlap for punishment: where the act constitutes an offence under both this Act and the listed Penal Code provisions, the offender is liable to the punishment greater in degree, and the 2019 extension of that section to Section 67B of the Information Technology Act brought the electronic material offences inside the same arbitration. Over the whole field stands Section 42A, inserted in 2013 and untouched in 2019, which provides that the Act's provisions are in addition to and not in derogation of other law, and that in case of inconsistency the provisions of this Act have overriding effect on the matters the Act contains. The practical consequence for the amendment's operation is that its higher floors cannot be evaded by charging the general statute instead: the greater-in-degree rule pulls the sentence up to the specialist scale wherever the specialist Act applies, and the child-specific procedure of the specialist Act governs the trial. The same map explains the one boundary the amendment respected. Where the accused is himself a child, the proceeding leaves this Act's sentencing entirely and enters the juvenile justice framework, and the 2019 amendment, for all its severity toward adult offenders, made no move across that boundary beyond the citation correction in Section 34. The severity of 2019 is therefore a severity of the adult tariff within a specialist code, applied through an arbitration rule that prefers the greater punishment, and it is at the charging stage, where the statutes are selected and combined, that the map is drawn in each individual case.

## How the amended scale meets the courts

Between the text of an enhancement and its operation stands the charging decision, and the record of the Act's first years contains a finding about that decision that conditions everything the amendment can achieve. A study of judgments of the Special Courts in five states by the Centre for Child and the Law at the National Law School of India University, published in 2018, found that aggravated charges had not been invoked in a substantial share of cases whose facts disclosed aggravating circumstances, with the gap recorded at fifty one per cent of such cases in Maharashtra and thirty five per cent in Andhra Pradesh. The finding matters to the 2019 trail for a precise reason. The amendment's largest single sentencing movement, the doubling of the Section 6 floor and the addition of death, operates only on cases that enter through the Section 5 gateway, and the gateway is operated by police officers framing charges on facts that are often incompletely recorded at the first information stage. An enhancement addressed to the tariff reaches only as far as the charging practice carries it, and a charging practice that under-invokes the aggravated form, whether from caution, workload or imperfect fact-gathering, dilutes the amendment at its point of entry. The same study tradition records the opposite error as well, the invocation of aggravated charges on unclear facts as a precaution, which exposes accused persons to the doubled floor on allegations the evidence may not sustain until trial sorts them. Neither pattern is an argument against the amendment's figures; both are reminders that a sentencing statute's real content is co-authored by the charging sheet.

The amendment's commencement generated a second, narrower body of litigation that an amendment trail should record because it is the standard fate of enhancements in Indian criminal law. Offences committed before 16 August 2019 but tried after it raised the question whether the enhanced punishments could be applied to them, and the answer is the constitutional one. Article 20(1) forbids a penalty greater than the law in force at the commission of the offence allowed, and the Supreme Court has set aside a sentence in which a trial court applied the substituted Section 6 to an offence committed in May 2019, months before the amendment commenced, holding the bar absolute and the sentence unsustainable to the extent it rested on the enhanced provision. Cases straddling the date are therefore sentenced on the 2012 scale, and the amendment's practical ascendancy in the courts was necessarily gradual, growing as the stock of pre-commencement prosecutions worked through the system. Practitioners dating an offence in a POCSO case do so against two commencement lines as a result, the original Act's in 2012 and the amendment's in 2019, and the second line will remain a live sentencing question in appeals for as long as the older cases persist.

## The evidence question the amendment did not address

The amendment's stated object, in the statement of objects and reasons that accompanied the bill, was deterrence: the incidence of child sexual abuse demonstrating inhumane and barbaric treatment of young victims was rising, and more stringent punishment was presented as the response. The difficulty with that theory is not that punishment is irrelevant but that the cases in which punishment can operate are a small and unrepresentative residue of the offences committed, and the features that decide whether an offence enters that residue were all left where they stood. The National Crime Records Bureau's relationship tables for the penetrative offences under Sections 4 and 6 have consistently shown the offender to have been known to the child in roughly ninety six per cent of cases; in the Crime in India 2022 tables the figure recorded is 96.8 per cent, with about half of those known offenders being family members, friends, employers or other persons in the child's immediate world, and the Bureau registered 63,414 cases under the Act in that year. A reporting system in which the reporter is very often reporting on a relative, a neighbour, a teacher or a member of the household is a system under strain at its first step, and the strain increases with the severity of the consequence the report sets in motion, because the family deciding whether to report an offence by one of its own members is also deciding whether to expose that member to a floor of twenty years or, in the aggravated case, to a capital charge. Attrition then continues through the process: survivors and families turn hostile under pressure, trials outlast the statutory year by multiples, and convictions result in a minority of the cases that reach judgment. The causes of that attrition are documented in the implementation literature and none of them is a sentencing variable. They are the delay in forensic examination and reporting, on which prosecution cases in this field unusually depend because the child's testimony is often the principal evidence and corroboration is perishable; the absence, in district after district, of the support persons and interpreters the Act and its Rules contemplate, without whom a young child's evidence degrades across adjournments; the pressure brought to bear on families by the accused's household and by community intermediaries toward compromise, which the Act forbids but cannot police; and the sheer pendency that members cited in the debate itself, the estimated 1.6 lakh cases standing in the system when the bill was passed, which guarantees that the statutory year of Section 35 operates as an aspiration in the ordinary district rather than a timetable. A sentencing amendment operates at the end of that funnel. It can change what happens to the small number who are convicted; it cannot, by its own force, change the number who are reported, charged or tried, and the amendment contained nothing addressed to any of those stages.

The point is sometimes answered by observing that reporting and registration have risen rather than fallen, and the Bureau's figures do show registrations under the Act rising year on year across the period, from roughly thirty two thousand cases in 2017 to the 63,414 recorded for 2022. That rise is real, but it does not carry the deterrent conclusion, for two reasons that the careful on both sides of this debate accept. Registration measures the working of the reporting system and of public awareness at least as much as it measures the incidence of offending, so a rising series is consistent with offending that is stable, rising or falling; and the series rose through tariff regimes of very different severity, before and after both the 2018 and 2019 enhancements, which is what one would expect if the drivers of registration lie in awareness, institutional response and the mandatory reporting duty rather than in the sentence at the end. What the record does not contain, from any official source, is a demonstrated fall in offending attributable to the higher tariff. That is the evidence position, stated as the evidence stands, and the debate that follows is best understood as a disagreement about how much weight that absence should carry against the other things a sentence does.

There is a reason the deterrent claim faces its hardest test in this particular statute, and it lies in the identity of the marginal offender. Deterrence theory, in the form in which the Verma Committee and the Law Commission received it, works on a calculating actor who knows the tariff, estimates the chance of being caught and convicted, and discounts the threatened sentence by that chance. The offender profile this Act addresses fits that model poorly at every point. The offences are committed overwhelmingly inside relationships of family, neighbourhood and institutional trust, in private, by persons who do not consult sentencing tables and whose dominant expectation, formed by the observed reality that most such offences are never reported, is concealment rather than conviction. Where the probability term in the calculation is close to zero, multiplying the sentence term changes the product very little, and the variable that moves the product is the probability itself, which is set by reporting, investigation quality and trial speed. This is the analytical core of the position the two expert bodies took, and it does not depend on any tenderness toward offenders: it is an argument about which lever is connected to the outcome. The amendment pulled the lever that was not connected to reporting, and the case for it, to which this article turns, must therefore rest on the other things a sentence legitimately does.

## The case for enhancement, at full strength

The case for the amendment begins with proportionality, and it is a stronger case than its critics sometimes allow. A legislature confronted with penetrative assaults on children below sixteen, and in the aggravated catalogue with assaults by persons in trust, assaults causing grievous injury, pregnancy, life threatening infection or death, is making a judgment about the relative gravity of wrongs when it sets the tariff, and that judgment is constitutionally its own to make. On this view the 2012 floors had been set for a statute feeling its way, and by 2019 the Assembly of evidence about the prevalence and character of the offences justified the conclusion that seven years at the bottom of the basic offence understated the wrong. The expressive function of punishment is a real function and not a euphemism for vengeance: a sentence announces to victims, to families deciding whether the system takes their child's violation seriously, and to offenders calculating risk, what the community understands the act to be, and for offences that destroy a child's development there is a respectable position that anything short of the gravest available response misdescribes the harm. The minister who carried the bill put the government's position in those terms in Parliament, presenting stringent punishment as the necessary answer to offences of demonstrated barbarity against very young victims, and pointing to the surrounding apparatus of fast track courts and an offenders' database as evidence that punishment was one element of a wider response rather than a substitute for one. The argument from symmetry supports her on one point at least: the 2018 Act had already placed death at the top of the Penal Code scale for rape of a girl below twelve, and it would have been an odd legislative order in which the specialist child protection statute, whose aggravated catalogue includes the same conduct and worse, offered less. Nor is the deterrence claim empty in its weaker and more defensible form, which is not that a announced tariff measurably deters the impulsive and the intimately placed offender, but that long incapacitating sentences certainly prevent the convicted offender's own repetition during custody, and that a twenty-year floor with a natural-life ceiling guarantees that prevention in a class of case where release on an ordinary life term was a genuine possibility under the earlier text. Finally, the storage offences answer the expressive argument's strongest objection, that the amendment was gesture rather than law: the graded Section 15 is careful, proportionate, fine-first drafting aimed at a digital harm of enormous volume, and it is not plausibly described as theatre.

There is also a constituency argument that the amendment's defenders pressed and that an evenhanded account must let stand in its own voice. The demand for a capital response to the rape of young children did not originate in a ministry; it arose from the cases themselves, from Kathua and Unnao and the long roll of assaults on children of four, six and eight that filled the news in those years, and it was carried into Parliament by families and by members who regarded a seven-year floor for the penetrative assault of a child as an indefensible understatement of the wrong whatever the deterrence literature said. On this view, a legislature that had declined to move would not have been following the evidence; it would have been declining to perform the elementary legislative act of grading the worst wrongs it prohibits at the top of its own scale, and leaving the specialist statute visibly milder than the general law for the same act against the same child. The 2018 asymmetry was real, and it embarrassed the 2012 settlement independently of any deterrent calculation. The amendment's defenders are entitled to the point that coherence and expression are legislative goods in themselves; the question the next section presses is not whether those goods exist but what was traded for them.

## The case against, at full strength

The case against the amendment is evidentiary, and its strongest holders are not sentimental about offenders. The Verma Committee's refusal to recommend death for rape rested on the evidence available to it that capital punishment does not deter serious crime more effectively than long imprisonment, and on the submissions of women's organisations that a capital tariff would worsen the position of victims. The Law Commission of India, in its 262nd report on the death penalty in 2015, recommended the abolition of capital punishment for all crimes other than terrorism related offences and waging war, after examining the deterrent claim and the arbitrariness of capital sentencing in Indian practice. Child rights organisations made the reporting argument directly when the 2018 ordinance was promulgated: UNICEF India cautioned publicly that introducing the death penalty for child rape risked deterring reporting and driving abuse further underground, since the offender is so often within the family, and organisations working with survivors, among them the HAQ: Centre for Child Rights in Delhi, argued that the certainty and speed of punishment, not its severity, was the variable the system was failing on. The mechanism is concrete and requires no speculation. Where the offender is a family member, the household that reports him under a capital and twenty-year-floor regime is choosing destitution and stigma along with prosecution, and the child knows it; where the offender is outside the family, the defence is given every incentive to contest the case to the last procedural inch, and the child testifies for longer under greater pressure. A further risk, raised before the Verma Committee by women's groups and repeated by child rights organisations in 2018 and 2019, is that equalising the punishment for the assault and for the killing that silences its only witness removes the marginal reason not to kill; the proposition is not capable of direct proof either way, which is exactly why the committee that heard the evidence treated it as a reason for caution rather than a conjecture to be run as an experiment on the class of victims least able to bear it. And beneath all of these arguments sits the attrition arithmetic: in a system where the overwhelming majority of offences are never reported and convictions follow in a minority of prosecuted cases, raising the sentence redistributes severity onto the few cases at the end of the funnel without enlarging the funnel, so that the amendment's certain effect is longer imprisonment for a small number of convicted persons and its claimed effect, less abuse, depends on the deterrent premise its own expert committees declined to endorse. This article does not adjudicate the death penalty question itself, which turns on judgments about the proper objects of punishment on which reasonable legislatures differ; what the record does support, and what this article does say, is that the amendment's deterrent presentation of itself was stronger than the evidence available to its authors, and that the reporting-side risk was identified to Parliament, by name and in advance, by the bodies that work with the children the Act protects.

### Did the 2019 amendment change anything for offences committed by children themselves?

Nothing. The amendment amended the punishment of offences against children and did not touch the separate question of offences committed by children, who are dealt with under the juvenile justice legislation as children in conflict with law, with its own procedures and its own bar on capital and certain other sentences for that class.

That separation is worth stating plainly because public discussion habitually merges the two questions, and because the minister's own expressed concern in the debate about the rising number of juvenile perpetrators might suggest otherwise. A child accused of an offence under the Act is not sentenced under Sections 4 or 6 at all; the juvenile justice framework governs, including its preliminary assessment procedure for older children accused of heinous offences. The 2019 amendment's only contact with that framework was the correction of the cross-reference in Section 34 to the 2015 juvenile justice statute, which is to say a citation update, not a policy change.

## Mandatory minimums and the case that does not fit

The most consequential practical objection to the amendment is not the death penalty, which will be sought and imposed in a very small number of cases, but the height of the floors in the ordinary run of prosecutions, and it arises from a feature of the Act that predates 2019: the Act makes no exception for consensual activity between adolescents. A child is a person below eighteen; consent is legally irrelevant; and a relationship between a seventeen-year-old and a partner of nineteen is, in the statute's terms, penetrative sexual assault carrying, after 2019, a minimum of ten years under Section 4 where the younger party is sixteen or seventeen and twenty years where that party is below sixteen. An analysis of 7,064 cases decided under the Act in Assam, Maharashtra and West Bengal by Enfold Proactive Health Trust, supported by UNICEF India, found that 24.3 per cent of the cases in its dataset were what the researchers classified as romantic cases, prosecutions arising from consensual relationships, very often initiated by the girl's family after an elopement. In such a case the mandatory minimum confronts the sentencing court with a choice the legislature did not design for: impose a decade or two decades of imprisonment for conduct the younger party describes as chosen, or strain to acquit against the evidence, as courts in these cases demonstrably do, since the dataset also records very high acquittal rates in the romantic category, driven by the prosecutrix and family turning hostile or the court finding reasons of doubt. Either outcome damages the statute: the first by disproportion, the second by teaching that the law's stated floor is negotiable in practice, which corrodes the certainty on which any deterrent theory depends.

The judiciary noticed the problem early and has said so in terms. The Madras High Court, in Sabari v. Inspector of Police in 2019, suggested that the Act's application to consensual adolescent relationships called for legislative reconsideration, and benches of several High Courts have since recorded the same unease while applying the Act as written, because a sentencing court has no lawful route below a statutory minimum that Parliament has prescribed in unqualified words. The institutional answer on the table comes from the Law Commission of India. Its 283rd report, Age of Consent under the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012, submitted on 27 September 2023 under the chairmanship of Justice Ritu Raj Awasthi, declined to recommend any reduction of the age of consent, reasoning that lowering it would bear directly and negatively on the fight against child marriage and child trafficking and would strip protection from the very class the Act exists to protect. But the Commission accepted that cases involving the tacit approval, in fact though not in law, of a child between sixteen and eighteen do not merit the same severity as the cases the Act was framed for, and it proposed the surgical remedy of guided judicial discretion in sentencing: amendments to Sections 4 and 8 that would empower the Special Court, on recorded grounds and within a structured set of considerations, to impose a sentence below the statutory minimum in that defined class. The proposal is squarely addressed to the difficulty the 2019 floors aggravated, since a guided discretion that might have been a refinement against a seven-year floor becomes a necessity of proportionality against a floor of ten or twenty. It remains a recommendation. Parliament has not enacted it, and the reader should understand the resulting position exactly: the floors are unqualified, the courts are bound by them, the one official body to have examined the question in depth has proposed a controlled exception, and the class of case that needs the exception is not marginal but roughly a quarter of the decided caseload in the largest dataset assembled.

The interim position deserves one further precision, because it is often misstated in both directions. A statutory minimum binds the sentencing court as to the sentence it may impose on conviction; it does not bind the court's anterior decisions, and the system's actual responses to the misfit case have grown up at those anterior points rather than at sentencing. They are the decision to charge a lesser or non-aggravated form where the facts allow a choice, the appreciation of the evidence at trial, where the hostility of the prosecutrix and her family in the romantic cases produces acquittals the evidence might not otherwise have yielded, and the appellate power to interfere with conviction itself. None of these is a sentencing discretion, and each carries its own cost: charging choices made to avoid a floor are invisible and unreviewable, and acquittals recorded against the evidence teach their own lesson about the law's demands. The Law Commission's proposal is significant precisely because it would move the safety valve to the one point in the process where it would operate openly, on recorded reasons, subject to appeal: the sentence. Until it is enacted, the valve operates where it cannot be seen, and the amendment's floors are at once absolute in the text and negotiable in the system's behaviour, which is the least defensible of the available combinations.

### Why does the amended Section 6 offer death as an alternative rather than as a grade of the offence?

Because aggravated penetrative sexual assault is a single offence defined by the presence of any one of the Section 5 circumstances, and the amendment chose to give that offence a single sentencing range, running from the twenty-year floor through whole-life imprisonment to death, rather than to subdivide the aggravated offence into capital and non-capital grades of its own.

The drafting choice has a logic. The Section 5 catalogue is long and its circumstances combine freely, so grading by circumstance would have required the legislature to rank combinations it has never ranked, and would have invited appellate litigation on the grading in every case. A single range leaves the ranking to the sentencing court under the constitutional discipline for capital cases. The cost of the choice is the one already noted: the floor of the single range must be set for the least grave case in the catalogue, and twenty years is the floor the amendment chose.

## The storage provision after 2024

Section 15's ladder was tested sooner than its drafters can have expected. Prosecutions under it raised a question the 2019 text had left genuinely open: whether the first grade, the failure to delete, destroy or report material kept with an intention to share or transmit, reaches the person who views or downloads such material and simply keeps it, and how the section sits beside Section 67B of the Information Technology Act, 2000, which punishes a range of acts in relation to the same material in electronic form. The Madras High Court, in proceedings arising from a prosecution of a man in whose phone such material was found, quashed the case on a reading that mere downloading and watching, without more, was not an offence under either provision. On 23 September 2024 a bench of the Supreme Court comprising Chief Justice D. Y. Chandrachud and Justice J. B. Pardiwala, in Just Rights for Children Alliance v. S. Harish, set that order aside. The Court read Section 15 as a set of graded offences under which keeping such material, in the circumstances each sub-section specifies, is punishable, treated viewing with the requisite attribute as capable of amounting to possession within the section, and restored the prosecution. Two features of the reading matter for the amendment trail. The Court preserved the 2019 ladder rather than flattening it: the three grades, with their distinct purposes and their descending and ascending punishments, were construed as a coherent scheme in which the first grade operates close to an inchoate liability, attaching at the point of keeping with the specified intention and the failure to delete, destroy or report. And the mental element, which the ladder's critics had treated as a charter for the passive holder, was connected to the Act's own presumption provision, Section 30, under which a Special Court presumes the culpable mental state the offence requires and the accused must prove its absence, so that the intention to share or transmit is not a fact the prosecution must extract from the defendant's own mouth in every case. The judgment is, in short, an endorsement of the 2019 architecture by the court that dismantled the narrower reading below it, and it leaves the amendment's drafting reputation higher than it found it. Those propositions are stated here narrowly and their full analysis belongs to the pillar article's account of the Act's judicial interpretation; what this amendment trail must record is the terminology position the same judgment established and the legislative invitation it issued. The Court held that the expression child pornography, the very expression the 2019 amendment had inserted into Section 2, misdescribes the material, because it borrows the vocabulary of consensual adult material for what is a record of the sexual exploitation and abuse of a child; it directed that courts use the expression child sexual exploitative and abuse material instead, and it suggested that Parliament amend the Act to substitute that expression in the statute itself, proposing that the Union consider bringing an ordinance in the interim so that the statutory vocabulary need not await a full amending bill. The position, therefore, is that the defined term of the 2019 amendment remains the statutory term while the courts have been directed to abandon it in their own orders, and the amendment that would conform the statute book to the direction has been invited but is a matter for Parliament. Files in this corner of practice, charge sheets, forensic reports, medical records and the material's own chain of custody, are among the most sensitive documents any office holds, and they are handled on the principle that [a confidential file belongs in a single encrypted workspace, as VaultBook describes in its account of how journalists protect their sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), rather than scattered across inboxes and shared drives.

## A note on method: reading an amending Act

A short methodological passage belongs in an amendment trail, because the errors in public writing on this amendment are mostly errors of reading rather than of opinion. An amending Act of this kind does three different things in three different grammatical moods, and confusing them produces false law. Some of its sections substitute words, as Section 4 of the principal Act had seven years replaced by ten, and the resulting text must be read in the principal Act as substituted, not in the amending Act. Some insert new provisions, as the below-sixteen band and the fine directions were inserted, and those provisions have no pre-2019 existence at all. Some replace whole sections, as Sections 6, 14 and 15 were replaced, and there the entire prior text is gone, so that a commentary quoting the old Section 6 as if it survived in part misleads. The discipline this article has followed, and the discipline the table above embodies, is to state every position from the principal Act as it stood on the two sides of 16 August 2019, using the amending Act only as the instrument of change. The same discipline exposes the amendment's one true oddity of form, the insertion of clause (v) into Section 9 after the closing words of the list it extends, as the kind of defect that arises when a bill is drafted, introduced and passed inside a single month of sittings. None of this is pedantry. In a field where a wrong floor in a charge sheet or a judgment can cost or save a decade of a sentence, the difference between a substituted word, an inserted band and a replaced section is the difference between three different laws.

## The sequel in the Rules of 2020

The amendment trail of the Act has a subordinate chapter that a reader of only the amending Act would miss. The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Rules, 2012 were replaced by the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Rules, 2020, notified and brought into force on 9 March 2020, and the 2020 Rules did the kind of work the 2019 amendment had not attempted: they strengthened the position of the child inside the process. Rule 9 empowers the Special Court, of its own motion or on an application by or on behalf of the child, to order interim compensation to meet the child's needs for relief or rehabilitation at any stage after the registration of the first information report, with the amount adjusted against any final compensation, and requires payment by the state government within thirty days of the order. Rule 8 provides for special relief for immediate contingencies such as food, clothing and transport, arranged through the Child Welfare Committee. The Rules elaborate the role of the support person, the trained individual assigned to accompany the child through the investigation and trial, and they strengthen the monitoring of the Act's working by the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights and the State Commissions, alongside detailed provisions on awareness, on the vetting of staff in institutions that house or serve children, and on the care of the child through the process. The detail of the 2020 Rules rewards the same close reading the amending Act received, because it shows the system working on its own friction points. Rule 3 addresses the institutional setting in which a large share of offences occur, requiring every institution housing children or in regular contact with them, schools, creches and sports academies among them, to ensure police verification and background checks of staff, teaching and non-teaching, regular and contractual, and periodic training to sensitise staff on child safety and protection. The support person provisions address the trial from the child's side of the room: a support person is to be provided through the Child Welfare Committee with the consent of the child and the guardian, to accompany the child through the investigation and the proceedings, to keep the family informed of the progress of the case and to stand between the child and the process at the points where children are most often lost to it. The monitoring provisions work on the statute's own supervision clause, Section 44, under which the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights and the State Commissions monitor the implementation of the Act, and the Rules strengthened the periodic character of that monitoring. None of this is sentencing law. All of it operates at the stages where, on the evidence assembled earlier in this article, cases are actually decided: whether an institution's staff were vetted before an offence, whether a reported child stays inside the process long enough to testify, and whether anyone is watching the system's performance against its own directions.

The contrast within the trail is instructive and it is this article's considered observation rather than any body's slogan: in 2019 the legislature raised the price of conviction and stopped; in 2020 the executive, using the rule-making power, addressed money in the child's hands within weeks of a report, accompaniment through the trial, and supervision of the system's own performance, which are the points at which cases are actually won, lost or abandoned. Neither instrument is a substitute for the other, but the sequence exposes which half of child protection each branch of the state reached for first.

## What the trail predicts

Amendment trails in this series are read for what they predict, and this one supports a specific prediction. The pressure on the statute is no longer concentrated on the tariff, which has reached the constitutional ceiling at the top and high floors below; it is concentrated on the two doors the 2019 amendment closed its eyes to, sentencing discretion at the bottom of the scale and terminology in the material offences. Both corrections exist in finished form outside the statute book. The guided discretion proposal sits in the Law Commission's 283rd report with its amending language reasoned out, awaiting introduction; the terminology substitution sits in a Supreme Court judgment that proposed the interim ordinance route precisely so that the change need not wait for a bill's full passage. Neither has been taken up, and the durable way to state the position is that the statute's two most discussed defects are defects with drafted remedies, which distinguishes them from the open-ended problems of reporting and delay for which no drafted remedy exists. A legislature that returns to this Act will therefore not be choosing whether to think, but which of two prepared corrections to enact, and the order in which it takes them will say as much about the politics of the statute as the 2019 amendment did. The Law Commission's proposal of guided judicial discretion for the adolescent cases is drafted, reasoned and awaiting a legislature, and the Supreme Court's invitation to substitute the statutory expression for the material is an ordinance away if the government chooses speed. Either change, when it comes, will arrive characterised as a softening by the same debate that produced 2019, and each will in fact be a correction of a different kind: one restoring to sentencing courts a measured part of the discretion the floors removed, the other conforming the statute's words to the Supreme Court's direction. The verdict on the 2019 amendment itself can be stated in one sentence, which is the namable claim of this article. The sentence rose and the reporting problem did not move: Parliament doubled the floor of the gravest offence, built a twenty-year floor beneath the youngest victims, converted ceilings into floors across the pornographic offences and graded the storage offence with real skill, and it did all of that at the end of a process that the evidence shows most offences never enter, because they are committed by persons the child knows, reported, if at all, by the household that shelters both parties, and decided years after the timelines the statute prescribes. One further entry in the trail confirms the amendment's durability. The general criminal law around the Act was replaced in 2024, when the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita took the place of the Penal Code and re-enacted the sexual offences against children in its own sections, with capital punishment retained for the rape of the youngest girls and for gang rape of a girl below eighteen. The specialist statute was left exactly where 2019 had set it: its tariff, its age bands and its graded storage offences all survived the codification untouched, and the cross-references in Section 42 are read with the new code through the ordinary repeal and savings mechanism. A sentencing structure that survives a wholesale replacement of the surrounding penal law without a single consequential adjustment has a claim to be treated as settled legislative policy rather than as a passing response to a season of cases, which strengthens the case for reading the 2019 amendment as the considered position of the Indian legislature on the punishment of these offences, and strengthens equally the obligation on its critics to engage it as policy and not as episode.

Judged as drafting, the amendment is substantial and in Section 15 exemplary. Judged as an answer to the question its own statement of objects posed, the reduction of abuse, it operated on the variable the record least supports and left the variables the record indicts, reporting, speed and support, to a set of rules made the following year by a different instrument. That is the trail, and the reader who has followed it provision by provision is in a position to test any future amendment against it: ask not how high its sentences run, but which stage of the funnel it touches.

The three readers this article set out to serve can each take a different instrument from it. The advocate takes the tariff as it stands after 16 August 2019 and the discipline of its application: the three age bands, the substituted Section 6 range with its constitutional capital discipline, the converted floors of Section 14, the graded Section 15 with the Section 30 presumption behind its mental elements, the fine directions with their compensatory destination, and the Article 20(1) line that keeps the older scale alive for older offences. The child rights professional takes the evidence position: the known-offender structure of the caseload on the Bureau's own tables, the reporting burden that mandatory reporting places on the households least able to bear it under a capital tariff, the quarter of the decided caseload that the largest study classifies as romantic, and the two drafted corrections, guided discretion and the terminology substitution, that await a legislature. The journalist takes the discipline of accuracy: the death penalty is available and not mandatory, the storage offences are the amendment's widest practical change and its least reported, the 2012 rules are gone and the Rules of 2020 govern compensation and support, and the statute's own expression for the material is under a Supreme Court direction of abandonment in court usage. An amendment that changed this much law deserves coverage as exact as its drafting, and a debate conducted at the level of its text rather than its headline.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the POCSO Amendment 2019 change?

The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (Amendment) Act, 2019, Act 25 of 2019, changed the sentencing structure of the 2012 Act and its material offences. It raised the minimum for penetrative sexual assault from seven years to ten, created a twenty-year minimum where the child is below sixteen, rewrote Section 6 so aggravated penetrative sexual assault carries twenty years to imprisonment for the remainder of natural life or death, converted the ceilings in Section 14 into floors of five and seven years, rebuilt Section 15 as three graded storage offences, inserted a definition of child pornography into Section 2, and widened two aggravating circumstances. It left the offence definitions and the trial procedure untouched.

### Q: Does POCSO now carry the death penalty?

Yes, but in one place and as a maximum rather than a requirement. The substituted Section 6 makes aggravated penetrative sexual assault, the offence defined by the circumstances in Section 5, punishable with rigorous imprisonment of not less than twenty years extending to imprisonment for the remainder of natural life, with a fine, or with death. No other offence under the Act carries death. The sentence remains discretionary and governed by the constitutional discipline for capital cases, so death may be imposed only where the sentencing court brings the case within the rarest of rare framework. The 2012 Act contained no death penalty anywhere.

### Q: How were punishments for storing child sexual abuse material changed?

Section 15 was replaced wholesale. The 2012 provision punished only storage for a commercial purpose, with up to three years on a first conviction and up to five years on a second. The substituted section grades the conduct by purpose. Failing to delete, destroy or report material kept with an intention to share or transmit it is punishable with a fine of at least five thousand rupees, rising to at least ten thousand for a repeat offence. Keeping material for transmitting, propagating, displaying or distributing it carries up to three years, with protection for keeping it in order to report it or use it as evidence. Commercial storage carries three to five years on a first conviction and five to seven years subsequently.

### Q: What new aggravating circumstances were added in 2019?

Three. Section 5, the catalogue for aggravated penetrative sexual assault, gained a sub-clause making an assault that causes the death of the child aggravated, and its communal or sectarian violence clause was widened to cover an assault committed during any natural calamity or in similar situations. Section 9, the parallel catalogue for aggravated sexual assault, received the same natural calamity widening and a wholly new clause (v): persuading, inducing, enticing or coercing a child to be administered, administering, or helping to administer any drug, hormone or chemical substance with the intent that the child attains early sexual maturity is aggravated sexual assault, punishable under Section 10.

### Q: Has a harsher sentence reduced sexual offences against children?

No official evidence demonstrates that it has. Registrations under the Act have risen steadily across the period, from roughly thirty two thousand cases recorded by the National Crime Records Bureau in 2017 to 63,414 in 2022, and registration reflects reporting and awareness as much as incidence, so the series cannot settle the question either way. What can be said is that the figure rose through very different tariff regimes, before and after the 2018 and 2019 enhancements, and that no official study attributes a fall in offending to the higher sentences. The Verma Committee in 2013 and the Law Commission in 2015 both recorded that the deterrent evidence for capital punishment was not made out.

### Q: Why do child rights groups oppose harsher POCSO sentences?

Their objection is practical rather than sympathetic to offenders. The National Crime Records Bureau's relationship tables show the offender to have been known to the child in roughly ninety six per cent of the penetrative cases, very often within the family or household. A household deciding whether to report one of its own members weighs that report against a twenty-year floor or a capital charge, and organisations such as UNICEF India and the HAQ: Centre for Child Rights have argued that the heavier the consequence, the stronger the pressure to stay silent. They add that certainty and speed of punishment, which the system delivers poorly, deter more reliably than severity, and that a tariff equal to the punishment for murder weakens the marginal reason to leave the child alive.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court say about the term child pornography?

In Just Rights for Children Alliance v. S. Harish, decided on 23 September 2024 by a bench of Chief Justice D. Y. Chandrachud and Justice J. B. Pardiwala, the Supreme Court held that the expression child pornography misdescribes the material, because pornography suggests consensual adult content whereas the material is a record of the sexual exploitation and abuse of a child. The Court directed that courts use the expression child sexual exploitative and abuse material, and suggested that Parliament amend the Act to substitute that expression in the statute, proposing that the Union consider bringing an ordinance in the interim. The statutory term inserted by the 2019 amendment remains in the Act until Parliament acts.

### Q: What do the POCSO Rules 2020 provide?

The Rules of 2020, which replaced the Rules of 2012 and came into force on 9 March 2020, work on the child's position inside the process rather than on punishment. Rule 9 empowers the Special Court to order interim compensation for the child's relief or rehabilitation at any stage after the first information report is registered, adjusted against final compensation and payable by the state government within thirty days. Rule 8 provides special relief for immediate needs such as food, clothing and transport through the Child Welfare Committee. The Rules also elaborate the role of support persons who accompany the child, provide for the vetting of staff in institutions serving children, and strengthen periodic monitoring of the Act by the national and state commissions for child rights.

### Q: What was the punishment for penetrative sexual assault before and after the 2019 amendment?

Before the amendment, Section 4 punished penetrative sexual assault with imprisonment of either description for not less than seven years, extendable to imprisonment for life, with a fine, and made no distinction by the child's age. After the amendment, Section 4(1) carries not less than ten years up to imprisonment for life, with the fine directed to be just and reasonable and paid to the victim for medical expenses and rehabilitation. A new Section 4(2) adds a separate band where the child is below sixteen: not less than twenty years, extendable to imprisonment for life meaning the remainder of the offender's natural life, with a fine.

### Q: When did the POCSO Amendment Act 2019 come into force?

The amending Act, Act 25 of 2019, received the assent of the President on 5 August 2019 and was published in the Gazette the following day. The Act did not commence on assent; its first section left commencement to a date appointed by the Central Government by notification in the Official Gazette. The government appointed 16 August 2019, and every change the Act makes, to Sections 2, 4, 5, 6, 9, 14, 15, 34, 42 and 45 of the principal Act, operates from that date. An earlier amendment bill introduced in the Lok Sabha in January 2019 had lapsed on the dissolution of that House and forms no part of the law.

### Q: What does imprisonment for the remainder of natural life mean in the amended Act?

The 2019 amendment wrote the meaning into the provisions themselves. Where Sections 4(2) and 6 speak of imprisonment for life, the text adds that it shall mean imprisonment for the remainder of the natural life of that person. The gloss removes the ambiguity that attaches to an ordinary life sentence in Indian penal law, where life imprisonment is treated in the case law as imprisonment for the whole of the remaining period of the convict's natural life but is in practice subject to the remission and commutation powers of governments. By spelling the meaning into the section, the amendment signals that the sentence is intended to run for the convict's whole life, subject to the constitutional powers of pardon and remission that no statute can exclude.

### Q: Can the enhanced punishments of 2019 be applied to an offence committed before the amendment?

No. Article 20(1) of the Constitution forbids subjecting a person to a penalty greater than the one that could have been inflicted under the law in force when the offence was committed, so an offence committed before 16 August 2019 is sentenced under the 2012 scale even if the trial concludes long afterwards. The Supreme Court has applied exactly that bar to this amendment, setting aside the use of the enhanced Section 6 against an offence committed in May 2019 and holding that the trial court had subjected the convict to a greater penalty than the law in force at the time permitted. Charging and trial procedure for earlier offences uses the pre-amendment punishments throughout.

### Q: What is the fine under Sections 4 and 6 of the POCSO Act meant to pay for?

The amendment gave the fine a stated destination for the first time. Sections 4(3) and 6(2) provide that the fine imposed shall be just and reasonable and paid to the victim to meet the medical expenses and rehabilitation of the victim. Before 2019 the fine in these sections was an undifferentiated part of the punishment, payable in the ordinary way, and the victim's monetary route lay in compensation schemes. The direction borrows the formula the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2018 had used in the Penal Code. One drafting unevenness should be noted: Section 4(3) speaks in terms of the fine under sub-section (1), although sub-section (2) also imposes a fine.

### Q: How did the 2019 amendment change the punishment for using a child for pornographic purposes?

It reversed the direction of the figures without changing the figures. Under the 2012 Section 14, using a child for pornographic purposes carried imprisonment up to five years on a first conviction and up to seven years on a second or subsequent conviction, each with a fine; those were maximums, and a court could sentence well below them. The substituted Section 14 makes the same figures minimums: not less than five years on a first conviction and not less than seven years on a second or subsequent conviction, with a fine in each case. The substituted section retains the rule that a person who directly participates in the acts and thereby commits an offence under Section 3, 5, 7 or 9 is punished for that offence in addition.

### Q: Did the 2019 amendment change the punishment for aggravated sexual assault?

No. Section 10, which punishes aggravated sexual assault as defined in Section 9, was not amended, and it continues to prescribe imprisonment of either description for not less than five years and up to seven years, with a fine. What changed in that part of the Act was the catalogue of aggravating circumstances in Section 9 itself, which gained the widened natural calamity clause and the new clause covering the administration of a drug, hormone or chemical substance to bring about a child's early sexual maturity. A case falling within the new clause is therefore aggravated, but it is sentenced within the same five to seven year range that has applied since 2012.

### Q: Is the death penalty under POCSO mandatory for aggravated penetrative sexual assault?

No, and the error of describing it as mandatory is the commonest error in coverage of the amendment. Section 6 places death at the top of a discretionary range that begins at twenty years and runs through imprisonment for the remainder of natural life. The sentencing court chooses, and for the capital choice it must apply the constitutional framework from Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab, under which death is reserved for the rarest of rare cases where the alternative is unquestionably foreclosed, after weighing aggravating and mitigating circumstances of both the crime and the offender. The amendment made death available for the offence; it did not direct any court to impose it in any case.

### Q: How does Section 15 of the POCSO Act differ from Section 67B of the Information Technology Act?

The two provisions approach the same material from different directions. Section 67B of the Information Technology Act, 2000 is built around electronic acts: publishing or transmitting such material in electronic form, creating text or digital images, browsing, downloading and related conduct, and it belongs to a statute about electronic records and intermediaries. Section 15 of the POCSO Act is built around the keeping of material in any form and grades that keeping by purpose, from failing to delete or report it, through holding it for transmission or distribution, to holding it for a commercial purpose. Section 42 of the POCSO Act, amended in 2019 to add Section 67B to its list, directs that where conduct is an offence under both statutes the punishment greater in degree applies.

### Q: Why do sentencing courts face a problem in POCSO cases involving adolescents close in age?

The Act defines a child as a person below eighteen and treats consent as legally irrelevant, so a consensual relationship between a seventeen-year-old and a partner slightly older is penetrative sexual assault carrying, after 2019, a minimum of ten years, or twenty if the younger party is below sixteen. An Enfold Proactive Health Trust study supported by UNICEF India, covering 7,064 decided cases in Assam, Maharashtra and West Bengal, classified 24.3 per cent of them as romantic cases of this kind. The sentencing court cannot go below the floor Parliament has set, however ill the case fits the predatory pattern the floor was designed for, so judges must choose between a disproportionate sentence and the acquittal routes the data shows they often take.

### Q: What did the Law Commission recommend about sentencing in cases involving children between sixteen and eighteen?

In its 283rd report, Age of Consent under the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012, submitted on 27 September 2023, the Law Commission declined to recommend lowering the age of consent from eighteen, holding that reduction would damage the fight against child marriage and trafficking. It instead proposed guided judicial discretion: amendments to Sections 4 and 8 that would let the Special Court impose less than the statutory minimum where a child between sixteen and eighteen had given tacit approval in fact, though not consent in law, within a structured set of considerations recorded by the court. The recommendation addresses the severity the 2019 floors created in adolescent cases. It has not been enacted.

### Q: How did the Criminal Law Amendment Act 2018 differ from the POCSO Amendment Act 2019?

The two Acts are companion instruments of the same legislative turn, but they work on different statutes and in different ways. The 2018 Act, Act 22 of 2018, amended the general law: it raised the minimum punishment for rape in the Indian Penal Code, created Section 376(3) for rape of a girl below sixteen and Section 376AB making rape of a girl below twelve punishable with twenty years to natural life or death, and tightened investigation and trial timelines in the procedure code. The 2019 Act worked only on the specialist statute: it raised the POCSO tariff to a comparable scale, extended death to aggravated penetrative sexual assault of a child of any age, and restructured the material offences in Sections 14 and 15, which the 2018 Act had not touched.
