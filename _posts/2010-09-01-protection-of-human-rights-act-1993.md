A family reaches a police station to collect a relative taken in for questioning the night before, and is told that the relative has died, or has been beaten so badly that hospital admission must come first, and in that moment the question is not abstract, because the family must decide where a complaint can go, who will hear it, and whether any institution exists whose only job is to look at what the state has done to a person in its custody, and the Protection of Human Rights Act 1993 is the statute built to answer exactly that question.

![A complainant's route through the Protection of Human Rights Act 1993, from the first complaint to the final remedy - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-54.webp)

The same doorway is meant to serve the person whose home has been demolished by a public authority without a hearing, the person who was picked up and then cannot be traced, the prisoner who says that food, medical care or protection from violence inside custody has been denied, and the worker who says that a public employer has treated her in a way that strips away equality and dignity at once. Yet a doorway is not a guarantee of entry, and a hearing is not a guarantee of relief. Every complaint in this system is governed by two hard limits, the one-year cliff and the recommendation ceiling. The one-year cliff means that a complaint must be brought within one year of the act complained of, so that a grievance left unfiled while a family pursues informal assurances, departmental promises or a slow local inquiry can fall out of the system on time alone, even when the underlying facts are grave. The recommendation ceiling means that the best outcome a commission can produce is a recommendation, not an order, so that even a complaint that is fully heard, fully investigated and fully believed ends in a direction that a government or authority is asked to accept rather than a command that a court bailiff can enforce in the ordinary way. Choosing a forum without accounting for both limits is the commonest reason a genuine grievance goes nowhere, because a complainant who files late in the wrong place, or who files in time but expects a binding decree from a body that can only recommend, has spent energy, money and hope on a route that was never designed to deliver what was asked of it.

That is why the Act repays close reading before any complaint is drafted. After reading this account, a reader should be able to decide where to take a grievance, whether the complaint belongs before the National Human Rights Commission, a State Human Rights Commission, a court or some combination of those routes pursued in a sensible order. The reader should also know what to file, in plain terms, which facts must be stated, which period must be covered, which documents help and which assertions waste the limited space a complaint allows. The reader should know within what period the complaint must move, why the one-year rule shapes every tactical choice from the first week onwards, what the commission can do during an inquiry, and what it cannot do however sympathetic its members may be. Above all, the reader should know the realistic next step when a recommendation is ignored, because that step, whether it is a further representation, a press of the record before a government, or an approach to a constitutional court, is part of the remedy from the beginning and not an afterthought invented after disappointment.

## Why India built a human rights commission in 1993

The Protection of Human Rights Act 1993 did not appear because a single government woke up to a new idea in isolation. It appeared because an international expectation had taken a firm shape, because a world conference had spoken in unmistakable terms, and because India's own record was being questioned in a way that made silence harder to defend than action. To understand the Commission that the Act created, a reader must hold those three pressures together, for the institution carries the marks of all of them. It was promised in the language of universal standards, it was hurried into existence through an ordinance, and it was given a wide field to watch over alongside a deliberately limited hand with which to act. That combination was not an accident of drafting. It was the product of the moment that produced it.

The international expectation had a name and a text. The Paris Principles on national human rights institutions were drawn up at an international workshop in Paris in October 1991, and they set out what a credible national institution should look like if it was to be more than a department with a new signboard. The principles concerned a broad mandate grounded in law, independence from the government of the day, pluralism in membership, adequate powers of investigation and adequate resources, for without those elements an institution could neither earn trust at home nor stand comparison abroad. The United Nations General Assembly endorsed the Paris Principles in resolution 48/134 of 20 December 1993. For a state creating such a body in 1993, the principles supplied both a template and a test, the standard against which other states and non-governmental organisations would judge whether what had been built deserved the name.

The World Conference on Human Rights at Vienna in June 1993 sharpened that expectation into a direct appeal. The conference urged states to establish and strengthen national institutions, pressing governments to turn declarations into machinery. A declaration without machinery leaves a victim with words, and a right without an institution to receive a complaint, call for a record or publish a finding leaves enforcement to chance. Vienna mattered for India because the timing left little room for delay dressed up as deliberation. A state that accepted the language of Vienna and then produced nothing at home would face an obvious question in every later forum. The ordinance that followed in September must be read against that calendar, for the speed of the response was part of its message to an external audience as well as to a domestic one.

The domestic pressure was harder and less comfortable. India's human rights record, particularly the counter-insurgency in Punjab and the conflict in Kashmir, faced sustained criticism in international fora and at home, and the absence of a national institution was itself a standing point of criticism. That sentence contains two distinct embarrassments that reinforced each other. The first concerned conduct, the operations of security forces and police in areas of armed conflict, where allegations of custodial death, disappearance, torture and extrajudicial killing were pressed by families, by lawyers, by journalists and by organisations that documented cases one by one. The second concerned structure. Even a government that disputed a particular allegation had to answer a prior question, which was where an aggrieved person was supposed to go if the complaint was against the state itself. A departmental inquiry conducted by the same hierarchy that was accused offered little public confidence. A court offered a real remedy but at a cost, a pace and a distance that placed it beyond many complainants. The absence of a specialised national institution, independent in appearance and charged only with human rights, was therefore not a neutral gap. In debate after debate, the missing institution became evidence in its own right, a sign that the state preferred to answer criticism with denial rather than with a forum.

Punjab and Kashmir did different work in that criticism. Punjab raised questions from a counter-insurgency fought largely through police power, where the complaint concerned what happened after a person had been taken into custody. Kashmir raised questions from a conflict in which the armed forces, special laws and a prolonged military presence shaped daily life, where the complaint could concern a search, a detention, a killing during an operation or a disappearance no local office would resolve. The two situations differed in law and politics, yet both produced grievances in which the respondent was the state, the evidence was largely in state hands and the complainant began from weakness. A commission able to call for records, inquire on its own motion and publish a finding spoke to that weakness in a way that assurances could not.

The route chosen tells its own story about urgency and hesitation. The Act began as the Protection of Human Rights Ordinance promulgated on 28 September 1993 and was given effect from 28 September 1993. An ordinance is the route a government takes when it wants law in force at once and Parliament is not in a position to pass the bill first. The groundwork had not been absent, for the bill had been introduced in the Lok Sabha in May 1993 and sent to a standing committee, the ordinary path by which a major bill is examined before enactment. The ordinance pre-empted that wait. The government wanted the institution in place and Parliament was not in session to pass the bill first, so the executive legislated for the interval. The choice allowed the National Human Rights Commission to be constituted on 12 October 1993, within a fortnight, rather than leaving the announcement empty for months, though a human rights institution born by ordinance begins life under a small cloud, since accountable and deliberative forms sit uneasily beside creation by executive fiat.

Enactment followed in due course. The Act received the President's assent on 8 January 1994, and the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 is Act 10 of 1994, with effect from 28 September 1993, the same date from which the ordinance had operated, so that there was no gap between the ordinance regime and the statute that replaced it. The dating often puzzles a first reader, who sees a statute called the Act of 1993 that is numbered as an Act of 1994 and wonders whether a mistake has crept into the citation. No mistake has occurred. The year in the short title preserves the year of the ordinance and of the institution's creation, while the Act number records the year in which the enacted statute received assent and entered the statute book. That continuity mattered for the Commission itself, because acts done, appointments made and proceedings begun under the ordinance needed the shelter of a statute that spoke from the same starting date, and the retrospective effect from 28 September 1993 supplied that shelter in a single move.

The Commission that resulted was constituted on 12 October 1993, and its first Chairperson was Justice Ranganath Misra, a former Chief Justice of India. The choice signalled the authority the new body was meant to carry, for a commission headed by a figure of that judicial standing could summon records, address governments and publish findings with a weight no ordinary administrative body could match. It also foreshadowed a lasting feature of the design, a body whose authority rests on reputation, standing and reasoning, precisely because the statute withheld the coercive final word that a court possesses. Moral and institutional authority had to do work that legal compulsion was not assigned to do. The state wanted a commission credible enough to answer criticism at home and abroad, and one that stopped short of issuing orders to the state itself.

That is the analytical point on which the whole history turns. The Act was born of external expectation and internal embarrassment at once, and that dual parentage explains both its breadth of promise and its narrowness of power, which the rest of this article tests. External expectation pushed towards breadth, because a commission modelled on the Paris Principles and announced after Vienna had to speak the language of life, liberty, equality and dignity, cover the covenants and look like a national institution rather than a narrow tribunal. Internal embarrassment pushed towards narrowness, because a government under criticism for Punjab and Kashmir retained an interest in a forum that could expose and recommend without being able to command and punish. Together they produced a statute whose promise must be read generously and whose powers must be read exactly. The Commission was designed to be heard, respected and answered, which is a real form of power, and also designed to depend on governments accepting its recommendations, which is a real limit upon that power.


## What the Protection of Human Rights Act 1993 counts as a human right

Before limitation, before forum choice and before any question about evidence, a complaint must pass through a definition. Section 2(d) of the Act defines human rights, and that definition does the quiet, decisive work of the whole statute, because it decides which grievances the commissions may entertain at all and which grievances, however painful, must find their remedy elsewhere. A complainant who reads Section 2(d) carelessly will treat the commissions as general courts of fairness, open to any story of hardship or misconduct, and will then be surprised when the complaint is returned at the threshold. A complainant who reads the definition closely will draft differently from the first line, tying each allegation to a right that the definition recognises, naming the source of that right and showing why a court in India could enforce it. The definition is short enough to quote in full in argument and structured enough to reward that close reading, for it is built from three limbs that must be held together, and the third limb is the filter through which the first two must pass.

### What counts as a human right under the Protection of Human Rights Act 1993?

Under Section 2(d), a human right means a right relating to life, liberty, equality or dignity that the Constitution guarantees or an International Covenant embodies, and that an Indian court can enforce. A grievance without that constitutional or covenant anchor falls outside the Act.

Section 2(d) provides that human rights means the rights relating to life, liberty, equality and dignity of the individual guaranteed by the Constitution or embodied in the International Covenants and enforceable by courts in India. Each element carries weight. Life, liberty, equality and dignity are broad words, deliberately so, for they reach the body in custody, freedom against unlawful detention, treatment without arbitrary discrimination by public power and treatment as a person whose worth does not depend on office or the favour of an official. The second element names the sources, and the word or matters, because a right may enter through the Constitution or through the International Covenants, so the field is not confined to the fundamental rights alone. Section 2(f) defines the International Covenants as the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights adopted by the United Nations General Assembly on 16 December 1966, together with other covenants or conventions the Central Government may notify, so the covenant limb has defined content rather than being an invitation to invoke any international text. The third element then narrows what the first two promise, for the right must be enforceable by courts in India.

The third limb is the operative filter, and a reader who grasps it grasps the Act. Enforceability by an Indian court ties the commissions to the legal order they supplement rather than allowing them to float above it as roving commissions of justice. If a court can enforce the right, because the Constitution guarantees it in terms a court will act upon or because a covenant right has a recognised place in the law courts apply, the grievance has the anchor Section 2(d) demands. If no court could enforce the claimed right, because the claim rests on a general hope, a policy preference or a provision speaking to governments as an aspiration for legislation, the definition does not open, and the commission has no business converting sympathy into jurisdiction. A claim with no enforceable anchor belongs in a legislature, in an administrative representation, in a civil suit framed on a recognised cause of action or in a constitutional petition, and filing it before a commission in the hope that a wide mandate will cure the missing anchor wastes the one year the Act allows.

Worked examples make the filter concrete. A custodial assault fits without strain. A person beaten in a police station alleges a violation of life, liberty and dignity at once, for Article 21 protects life and personal liberty in terms that courts have enforced against torture and custodial violence, and the covenant right against torture in the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights covers the same conduct, so both source limbs point to the same wrong and enforceability is satisfied. Any later failure on such facts will turn on proof, delay or the limits of recommendation, not on the doorway. A complaint that a neighbour has grabbed land does not fit. Land grabbing by a private neighbour may support a civil suit, an injunction or a criminal complaint for a specific offence, yet the wrong is private, the respondent exercises no public power and the grievance alleges no enforceable denial by the state, so a commission complaint repeating the private dispute asks the Act to do the work of a civil court. A different result could follow only if police refused to act on grounds denying equality before the law, for then the complaint would concern the public failure, not title to the land.

A claim about the quality of a government school sits between those poles and shows why drafting matters. A parent who writes that teaching is weak or buildings are neglected states a legitimate concern, yet in that loose form the complaint has no enforceable anchor and a commission may treat it as a general representation. The same facts must be tied to an enforceable right before the Commission can touch the matter, which means identifying the constitutional guarantee said to be breached, showing how the condition denies that guarantee to identifiable children and explaining why a court could act. That sorting applies across the field, to a denial of medical care in custody, which can be anchored in life and dignity, and to a dismissal from public employment said to offend equality, which must show the constitutional content of the equality claim.

Dignity deserves separate attention because it does real work in the definition and is too often treated as decoration. In the Supreme Court's Article 21 jurisprudence, dignity is treated as part of the right to life, so that protected life is not mere existence but a life lived with the basic respect owed to a human being, a reading that carries into the treatment of prisoners, persons in custody and workers in degrading conditions. The covenant limb imports the same content, for the International Covenants proceed on the premise that rights derive from the inherent dignity of the human person, and the prohibition of degrading treatment gives that premise a hard legal edge. Dignity therefore widens the definition, capturing harm that takes no life but degrades the person in custody or in a public office, while still requiring a connection to a guarantee a court can enforce. It does not convert every discourtesy or harsh word from an official into a violation, for the enforceability limb remains in place.

The screening consequence follows, and it should be stated without softening. The definition is the first reason complaints fail, before limitation or forum choice ever arise. A complaint can be filed within the year, addressed to the correct commission, supported by documents and pressed by a diligent family, and still go nowhere because the grievance, as framed, never entered the statutory meaning of human rights at all. Conversely, a complaint that passes Section 2(d) has cleared only the first gate and has earned no more than the right to be tested against the one-year cliff, against the choice between the national and state commissions and against the recommendation ceiling that waits at the end of a successful inquiry. Keeping those gates in order is the habit this article asks of its reader throughout. First ask whether the Act counts the wrong as a human rights violation. Then ask whether the complaint is in time. Then ask which forum should hear it and what that forum can actually deliver. A grievance handled in that order stands a chance. A grievance handled in any other order, however sincere, is likely to spend its strength at a door that was never open to it.

## The human rights complaint route map

A person whose rights have been violated by a public servant faces a decision before any question of evidence or argument arises, and that decision is the choice of forum. The Protection of Human Rights Act 1993 does not create a single doorway through which every grievance passes to a single remedy. It forms part of a wider landscape in which five distinct doors stand open, each leading to a different kind of proceeding, a different kind of power and a different kind of result. A complainant who knocks on the wrong door does not merely waste time. Time itself is rationed under the Act, and months spent pursuing a forum that can only recommend, when what was needed was a forum that can command, may consume the period within which another and stronger remedy remained available. Equally, rushing to a constitutional court may choose the most demanding route when a commission inquiry would have served.

The five doors differ along practical rather than theoretical lines. They differ in what they can do, because a commission that inquires and recommends is not the same instrument as a court that tries and sentences or a constitutional court that issues binding directions. They differ in what they cost, because the commissions charge no fee while litigation before the higher judiciary carries the ordinary burdens of representation, preparation and attendance. They differ in how long the complainant has, because the commissions are fenced by a statutory period of one year from the act complained of, while a writ petition under Article 226 or Article 32 carries no such statutory bar, though unexplained delay still counts against the petitioner. They differ, most sharply, in whether the outcome binds anyone, because a recommendation invites a response but does not compel obedience in the manner of a court order. The table that follows is the working map for this segment, compressing the five routes into the questions a complainant should ask of any forum before entrusting it with a grievance.

**The human rights complaint route map**

| Forum | What it can do | Limitation | Cost | Outcome and enforceability | Choose it when |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| National Human Rights Commission | Inquire into a violation with the powers of a civil court, recommend compensation, prosecution or other action, recommend interim relief, and approach the Supreme Court or a High Court for directions | Complaint within one year of the violation under Section 36(2), and no inquiry into a matter pending before a State Commission or another commission under Section 36(1) | No fee | Recommendation only, not binding or enforceable as a court order | A free and accessible inquiry is needed and the complaint is within one year |
| State Human Rights Commission | Inquire into a violation and recommend compensation, prosecution or other action, and recommend interim relief | Complaint within one year, and inquiry only into matters relatable to entries in List II and List III of the Seventh Schedule under Section 21(5) | No fee | Recommendation only, not binding or enforceable as a court order | The matter concerns a State or concurrent subject and a State level inquiry is appropriate |
| Human Rights Court (district) | Try offences arising out of violation of human rights in a speedy criminal trial, with a special public prosecutor under Section 31 | Reached through prosecution rather than by complaint, and confined to offences arising out of violation of human rights | No complaint fee under the Act, prosecution led by the State | Criminal judgment of a Court of Session, binding on the parties to the trial | The violation amounts to a criminal offence and punishment through trial is the object |
| High Court, Article 226 | Issue directions on a writ petition and award compensation for violation of rights | No statutory one year bar, though unexplained delay weighs with the court | Ordinary litigation costs of a writ petition | Binding and enforceable orders of a constitutional court | A binding direction or compensation is needed, or the one year period for a commission has expired |
| Supreme Court, Article 32 | Enforce fundamental rights on petition, issue binding orders and award compensation in public law | Petition must concern violation of fundamental rights | Ordinary litigation costs of a Supreme Court petition | Binding and enforceable orders of the Supreme Court | A fundamental right is violated and the highest constitutional remedy is sought |

How the map is read matters as much as what it contains, and three columns repay a slower look because each corresponds to a recurring error. The enforceability column is the one readers skip and regret. A complaint to the National Human Rights Commission or to a State Human Rights Commission ends, at its most successful, in a recommendation. Section 18 frames the steps open to the National Commission at the conclusion of an inquiry in those terms. The Commission may recommend that compensation be paid to the victim or the family, that proceedings for prosecution or such other action as it considers fit be initiated, or that interim relief be granted. It may also approach the Supreme Court or the High Court concerned for such directions, orders or writs as that court may deem necessary. The language is the language of recommendation and approach, not of command. A finding recorded after an inquiry in which the Commission exercised the powers of a civil court carries institutional weight, and governments do act on such recommendations, but weight is not enforceability. A victim who requires certainty that payment will follow must understand that a binding outcome lies through the courts in the last two rows.

The limitation column is the one that kills claims before they are read. For both commissions the operative provision is Section 36(2), which provides that neither the National Commission nor a State Commission shall inquire into any matter after the expiry of one year from the date on which the act constituting the violation is alleged to have been committed. A complaint filed within the year may be inquired into on its merits. A complaint filed after the year has expired falls outside the power of inquiry altogether, however serious the violation. Beside that bar stand two further limits. Section 36(1) forbids the National Commission from inquiring into a matter pending before a State Commission or any other commission duly constituted under a law in force, so parallel pursuit is not a way of multiplying chances but a ground to stay its hand. Section 21(5) confines a State Commission to matters relatable to entries in List II and List III of the Seventh Schedule, the State List and the Concurrent List, so subject matter decides whether a State Commission may proceed at all.

The cost column is the one where the commissions win outright. No fee is charged for a complaint to the National Human Rights Commission, and the complaint may be written in English, Hindi or any language included in the Eighth Schedule to the Constitution, sent by post, delivered in person, sent by email or filed through the online complaint portal. The proceeding is an inquiry conducted by the Commission itself, armed with the powers of a civil court, rather than an adversarial contest the complainant must finance alone. Against that position the writ routes offer a different bargain. A petition under Article 226 or Article 32 can yield a binding and enforceable order, and the Supreme Court has used that power to award compensation itself, as in Rudul Sah v State of Bihar in 1983, where compensation followed detention that continued after acquittal, and in Nilabati Behera v State of Orissa in 1993, where compensation was awarded for a custodial death as a public law remedy distinct from a private damages claim. Those outcomes explain why the constitutional courts remain the ultimate destination for grave violations, despite the greater demands they make in money, representation and endurance.

One matter the map deliberately leaves to another part of this series is the institutional anatomy of the National Commission itself, its composition, appointment and record, because that anatomy is treated in full in [the companion institutional account of the Commission](/2010/09/15/nhrc-powers-functions-explained/) and repetition here would crowd out the route guidance a complainant needs first. What matters for forum choice is not who sits on the Commission but what it can and cannot do with a complaint once it arrives, and it is to the mechanics of that arrival that attention turns.

## Filing the complaint: who, what and where

The first liberating feature of the procedure is how widely the door stands open. Section 12(a) empowers the Commission to inquire on a petition presented by the victim or any person on the victim's behalf. The words any person do real work. A victim who is dead cannot write, a victim who is detained may be unable to write freely, and a victim who is injured, ill or frightened may be unable to write at all. A relative may therefore file for a person held in custody. A lawyer may file for a client who described the incident but cannot prepare a formal document. An organisation working with labourers, with prisoners' families or with residents of an institution may file for a victim who lacks the means or confidence to approach a national body alone. The standing in Section 12(a) recognises that the people most likely to suffer violations at the hands of the State are often the people least able to complain unaided.

Who may file being settled widely, what the complaint should contain decides whether the wide door leads anywhere. A complaint should carry the complainant's name and address, the identity of the victim where complainant and victim differ, the date, place and particulars of the incident, the public servants or authority involved, and the relief sought. The name and address give the Commission a person to acknowledge and correspond with, and distinguish the complaint from anonymous or pseudonymous communications liable to threshold dismissal. The victim identity fixes whose rights are said to have been violated. The place and particulars allow the Commission to identify the authority whose report will be called for. Naming the public servants or authority involved, by name, office, station or institution so far as the complainant can, directs the inquiry to the hands holding the relevant records. The relief sought tells the Commission what to recommend, whether compensation, prosecution, interim relief or another form of action under Section 18.

Among those particulars, the date of the incident carries more consequence than any other line. Because neither Commission may inquire after the expiry of one year from the date on which the act constituting the violation is alleged to have been committed, under Section 36(2), the date written in the complaint is the date against which the Commission measures its own power to proceed. A vague date invites a threshold difficulty that a precise complaint avoids, and a misstated date risks worse, because the inquiry will test the chronology against records held by the very authority complained against. The date should be given exactly as the complainant believes it to be, with care given to surrounding events that fix it in sequence, such as an arrest, a production before a magistrate, a release, a medical examination or the moment a custodial death came to the knowledge of the family. Where the exact day is uncertain, the complaint serves itself best by saying so honestly and giving the narrowest range the complainant can support.

The channels match the openness of the standing rule. A complaint may be sent by post, delivered in person, sent by email or filed through the Commission's online complaint portal, and no fee is charged at any point of entry. A complaint may be written in English, Hindi or any language included in the Eighth Schedule to the Constitution, and a complainant should use the language in which the facts can be stated most accurately, because accuracy matters more to an inquiry than elegance. On receipt the complainant receives an acknowledgment bearing a diary or case number. That number should be preserved and quoted in every later communication, because the institution can trace a file reliably only through the reference it itself assigned.

A substantial number of complaints go no further than the threshold. The Commission's procedure regulations treat complaints that are vague, frivolous, anonymous or pseudonymous, matters pending before a court or another commission, and events older than one year as ordinarily liable to dismissal at the threshold. Each class has a reason. A vague complaint gives an inquiry nothing to act upon. A frivolous complaint discloses no violation and burdens finite resources. An anonymous complaint cannot be acknowledged, clarified or answered. A pending matter belongs to the forum already seized of it, for the reason Section 36(1) supplies. An event older than one year falls outside the power conferred by Section 36(2). The word ordinarily marks the threshold as regulated practice rather than a fresh statutory bar, but no complainant should draft in reliance on an exception. The safe course is a complaint that is specific, serious, signed with a real name and address, directed to one commission and dated within the year the statute allows.

Practical discipline after drafting protects the complaint thereafter. The complainant should keep copies of everything filed, including the complaint, every enclosed document and any later representation, so that the account of what was submitted never depends on memory or on the completeness of an official file. The complainant should record the date of posting or submission and preserve whatever proof the channel affords, whether a postal receipt, a sent email or the confirmation from the online portal, because limitation under Section 36(2) is a question about dates and proof of filing strengthens the complainant's position. The complainant should also state the facts in sequence, with dates attached wherever dates are known, because sequence turns allegations into a narrative an inquiry can test. That habit matters because the Commission's first act in many complaints is usually to call for a report from the very authority complained against. A precise chronology makes that report hard to falsify. A vague complaint can be met with a vague denial, while a complaint fixing who was taken, at what hour, to which place, before which officer and with what recorded consequence forces the authority either to confirm the sequence, to dispute it at a specific and checkable point, or to fall silent where its own records should speak.

## The complaints the Act will not let the Commission touch

### Which complaints can the commission not take up at all?

The Commission cannot inquire into a matter pending before a State Commission or another duly constituted commission, under Section 36(1), and cannot inquire into any matter after one year from the date of the alleged violating act, under Section 36(2), for which the Act provides no condonation power.

For all the openness of its standing rule, languages and channels, the Act draws two hard boundaries around the power of inquiry. Section 36(1) provides that the Commission shall not inquire into any matter which is pending before a State Commission or any other commission duly constituted under any law for the time being in force. Section 36(2) provides that the Commission shall not inquire into any matter after the expiry of one year from the date on which the act constituting the violation of human rights is alleged to have been committed, and it speaks of the National Commission and the State Commissions together, so the bar binds both tiers. Neither boundary is discretion dressed as a rule. Each is expressed as prohibition, shall not inquire, and each goes to power rather than preference. A Commission proceeding in defiance of either provision would be acting outside the statute that created it, and a complainant who does not know the boundaries may invest hope, labour and the irreplaceable months of the limitation year in a proceeding the Commission was never permitted to conduct.

The pending elsewhere bar rests on a clear logic. A State Commission exists alongside the National Commission, and other commissions duly constituted under laws in force may also be seized of matters overlapping a human rights grievance. If the same matter proceeded before two commissions at once, authorities would face parallel inquiries into identical facts, commissions might reach findings pulling against each other, and the complainant would gain nothing except duplication. Section 36(1) prevents that duplication at the National Commission door. Its practical teaching is about choice and sequence. The matter must be placed before one forum and pursued there, and the choice, guided by the route map and in the State Commission case by the subject matter limit in Section 21(5), should be made deliberately at the outset rather than undone later by a dismissal.

The trap in practice is natural and sympathetic. A complainant, or a family acting for a victim who cannot act, may reason that filing the same complaint with both the National Commission and the State Commission doubles the chance of being heard, or that a complaint sent to every available address cannot be ignored by all of them. Under the Act that reasoning defeats itself. The filing with the State Commission, once pending there, becomes the very fact that forbids the National Commission to inquire, and two filings may disable the forum of choice rather than create two inquiries. The same difficulty arises where a matter is already pending before another commission, because the bar depends on the matter being pending elsewhere, from whatever cause, not on who created the overlap. A complainant or an assisting organisation should therefore establish whether the same matter is already before a State Commission or another commission, choose the forum the route map indicates, and commit the complaint to that forum alone, keeping the acknowledgment and case number as its single thread.

The one year bar in Section 36(2) is the sterner boundary, because it runs against time and the Act provides no power to condone delay beyond the year. The year runs from the date on which the act constituting the violation is alleged to have been committed. It does not run from discovery of the act, from receipt of legal advice, from the conclusion of another proceeding or from the gathering of persuasive documents. A complainant who waits until every paper is in hand spends the same year that a complainant who files promptly and supplements the record later would have preserved. The act in question is also the act alleged, which places the complainant's own dating at the centre of the limitation question. Above all, there is no condonation power. Many limitation regimes allow delay to be excused for sufficient cause. The Act contains no such softening, and neither Commission may extend the year for a good reason, a grave violation, illness, detention or any other circumstance, however compelling. The year is a cliff rather than a slope, and a complaint arriving after it has expired arrives after the power to inquire has ended.

The hardest question concerns the difference between a single dated act and a continuing situation. A concluded beating on a stated day is a single act. Its date is the date of the beating, the year runs from that date, and a complaint filed after the year has passed is barred, whatever evidence survives. An ongoing detention presents a different shape. A person remaining in custody day after day under a detention alleged to be unlawful is not complaining only about a moment of arrest in the distant past. Each fresh day of an unlawful detention is arguably a fresh act, because the State on each day continues to hold the person without lawful authority, so a complaint filed during the detention, or within one year of a recent day of it, speaks of an act inside the statutory year. The argument follows from the text of Section 36(2) and from detention as a continuing State action, and it is not a licence to revive concluded acts in continuing language. A beating does not become continuing because its injuries continue to hurt, and a dismissal does not become continuing because its financial consequences continue to be felt. The distinction lies between consequences continuing after an act has ended, which do not restart the year, and a violation the State is still committing, which arguably presents a fresh act on each day of its continuance.

When the year has run, the complainant is not left without law, because Section 36 limits only the commissions. It does not limit the constitutional courts, a criminal proceeding or a departmental representation, none of which is an inquiry by a commission under the Act. The writ route is the principal survival strategy. A petition under Article 226 before a High Court may seek directions and compensation, and no statutory one year bar applies, though unexplained delay weighs with the court. A petition under Article 32 before the Supreme Court lies for violation of fundamental rights and likewise carries no bar drawn from Section 36(2), and the public law awards in Rudul Sah and Nilabati Behera came from that constitutional route. The difference should be stated exactly. Before a commission, delay beyond one year is fatal regardless of explanation. Before a constitutional court, delay is weighed, so delay explained by detention, illness, pursuit of another remedy or the circumstances of the violation may be excused, while unexplained delay may still damage or defeat the petition.

Two further routes stand outside Section 36 altogether. A criminal complaint concerning conduct amounting to an offence is not governed by Section 36, and the Act itself contemplates prosecution and, under sections 30 and 31, trial before a Human Rights Court, being a Court of Session specified for each district by the State Government with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court, conducted by a special public prosecutor who has been an advocate for not less than seven years. That route is reached through prosecution rather than a complaint desk, but expiry of the commission year does not extinguish the criminal character of the conduct. A departmental representation to the employing authority likewise stands outside the limitation scheme, depends on service discipline rather than any power conferred by the Act, and creates a record other proceedings may later use. Forum choice was the first decision, and Section 36 makes it a decision with a deadline. Filing within the year preserves every option, including a later approach to a constitutional court for binding relief. Letting the year pass retains the writ, criminal and departmental routes, but loses the free inquiry the Act designed as its most accessible remedy, and no explanation, however honest, can restore it.

## What the Commission does with a complaint

Section 12 of the Protection of Human Rights Act 1993 lists the functions of the National Human Rights Commission, and the function placed first is the one that matters most to a complainant. The Commission may inquire, on its own motion or on a petition presented by the victim or by any person on the victim's behalf, into a complaint of violation of human rights or abetment of such violation, or into negligence in the prevention of such violation by a public servant. Each part of that clause widens the door. A victim who is dead, detained, ill, illiterate or afraid to approach an authority need not petition in person, because any person may petition on the victim's behalf. A matter that reaches the Commission through its own information need not wait for a petition at all, because the power to act on its own motion allows an inquiry to begin without a formal complainant.

The scope of the inquiry is wider than a search for a single deliberate act. Violation covers the direct wrong. Abetment covers the official who encouraged, instigated or facilitated the wrong without personally carrying it out. Negligence in the prevention of a violation by a public servant is a separate ground, and its inclusion changes the kind of complaint that can be sustained. Many failures of protection involve no order that can be produced and no blow that a witness saw. A police station that ignored warnings of an attack, an officer who delayed medical care for a prisoner, or an administration that failed to act on repeated complaints may have caused grave harm by omission. Section 12 allows the Commission to examine that omission as the substance of the complaint, and to follow responsibility upward rather than stopping at the most junior person present.

A second function in Section 12 concerns cases already before the courts. The Commission may intervene in any proceeding involving an allegation of violation of human rights pending before a court, with the approval of that court. The approval requirement preserves the court's control over its own proceedings and prevents competing directions in the same matter. When approval is granted, intervention brings into the proceeding an institutional voice that belongs to neither the prosecution nor the defence nor the government as a party. That voice can place the human rights dimension of the case before the court, including a wider question raised by an individual dispute, such as the treatment of a class of prisoners or the conduct of a pattern of investigation. Its presence in the list shows that Parliament did not design the Commission as a sealed parallel system, but as a body able to assist the judicial process where a pending case turned on a human rights allegation.

The jail visit function in Section 12 operates through a different channel, systemic rather than individual, although its benefits return to individuals over time. The Commission may visit, after intimation to the State Government, any jail or other institution under the control of the State Government where persons are detained or lodged for purposes of treatment, reformation or protection, in order to study the living conditions of the inmates and make recommendations. Such a visit does not depend on a prisoner managing to send a petition beyond the walls, and it does not require proof of a single incident. Overcrowding, sanitation, food, medical access, the separation of undertrials from convicts, the handling of women prisoners and the condition of children lodged with their mothers can be observed as conditions of a system. The same applies to institutions for treatment or protection, where residents may be even less able to complain than prisoners. The requirement of prior intimation marks the limit plainly, since the visit is announced and the findings are naturally directed to conditions an announced inspection can document.

The remaining functions in Section 12 form the background against which individual inquiries proceed. The Commission is to review the safeguards provided by or under the Constitution or any law in force for the protection of human rights and recommend measures for their effective implementation. It is to review the factors, including acts of terrorism, that inhibit the enjoyment of human rights and recommend appropriate remedial measures. It is to study treaties and other international instruments on human rights and make recommendations for their effective implementation. It may undertake and promote research in human rights, spread human rights literacy among various sections of society, and encourage the efforts of non-governmental organisations and institutions working in the field. A residual clause adds such other functions as the Commission may consider necessary for the promotion of human rights.

The procedure for an individual complaint is governed in part by Section 17, and its sequence allocates power between the Commission and the government complained against at each step. While inquiring into a complaint, the Commission may call for information or a report from the Central Government, a State Government or any other authority or organisation subordinate to that government, within such time as the Commission specifies. In ordinary course, therefore, a complaint taken up for inquiry is registered and then sent outward to the respondent side for a report. The government or authority whose servants are complained against is asked to state what happened and what action has been taken. The time for answering is fixed by the Commission rather than by the respondent, and that allocation matters. Delay weakens inquiries into official conduct as officers are transferred, records are mislaid and recollection hardens into competing certainties. A power to specify the time for a report does not guarantee a prompt or truthful answer, but it denies the respondent a legal right to answer at leisure.

Section 17 then provides for default. If the information or report is not received within the time specified, the Commission may proceed to inquire into the complaint on its own. Silence does not create a veto, and the Commission is not required to close the file for want of the respondent's account or to wait through an indefinite series of reminders. It may proceed on the material available, including the petition and its supporting documents and any material gathered through its own powers. The provision also shapes conduct before any default occurs. A government that knows the inquiry can continue without its report has a strong reason to file the report and to file it in a form that can withstand testing, because an absent official account leaves the complainant's version as the principal narrative on the record.

The same section defines a point of early closure. If, on receipt of the information or report, the Commission is satisfied either that no further inquiry is required or that the required action has been taken by the concerned government or authority, it may not proceed with the complaint further and must inform the complainant accordingly. Two different satisfactions can end the matter at this stage. The Commission may conclude that the material discloses no violation calling for further inquiry, that the matter falls outside its remit, or that another process has dealt with the substance. It may also conclude that required action has already been taken, whether through a completed departmental proceeding, the release of a detained person, a medical examination, or the restoration of a safeguard, so that a parallel inquiry would add nothing. The duty to inform the complainant is part of the provision rather than a courtesy. A communicated ending can be questioned, represented against or carried elsewhere. A file that disappears without explanation cannot.

This design must be described honestly, because its strength and weakness are the same feature seen from opposite ends. In most complaints the first fact finder is the respondent government itself. The initial account of a beating in custody, a disappearance after arrest, a denial of treatment or a failure to prevent violence will usually be prepared by the administration whose servants are accused, from records held by that administration and statements taken within its own hierarchy. The arrangement has administrative logic, since the state holds the custody record, the duty roster, the medical register and the file notings without which an incident in a police station or prison cannot be reconstructed. It also carries an obvious risk of minimisation, omission and rationalisation in polished institutional language. Parliament cannot be treated as unaware of that risk, because the rest of the Act gives the Commission powers whose purpose is to test and, where necessary, displace the respondent's account. For the same reason, the complainant's own documentation matters greatly. Dates, names, the sequence of custody, copies of applications made at the time, medical papers from the first examination outside custody, photographs and the names of witnesses give the Commission material against which the official report can be measured. A bare allegation invites a bare denial and makes closure easy. A detailed petition changes the questions that the report must answer.

## The powers behind the inquiry

Section 13 gives the inquiry its compulsory force. While inquiring into complaints under the Act, the Commission has all the powers of a civil court trying a suit under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, in respect of specified matters. The Commission does not become a court for all purposes and does not try offences or pass decrees, but in gathering and testing material it holds instruments that separate a tribunal from a correspondent. It may summon and enforce the attendance of witnesses and examine them on oath. Attendance can be compelled rather than invited, and evidence on oath carries a discipline and a legal exposure that an informal statement to a superior officer does not. An official account that might otherwise arrive as an unsigned institutional summary must, when this power is used, be defended by a named person answering in person.

The documentary powers in Section 13 address the materials on which inquiries into official conduct usually turn. The Commission holds the civil court power in respect of discovery and production of any document, reaching papers that a holder would prefer not to volunteer. It may receive evidence on affidavits, allowing sworn written testimony to be placed on record where oral examination on every point would add time without adding clarity. It may requisition any public record or a copy of such a record from any court or office, a power of particular value where the decisive file sits in a registry, a hospital office or a prison record room. It may issue commissions for the examination of witnesses or documents where a witness or record cannot conveniently be brought before the Commission itself. Proceedings before the Commission are deemed to be judicial proceedings for specified purposes of the Indian Penal Code, and the Commission is deemed to be a civil court for specified purposes, attaching legal consequences to false evidence and obstruction. The Commission may also enter any building or place where it has reason to believe that documents relating to the subject matter of the inquiry may be found, a power tied to documents and to reason to believe.

### How does the commission gather evidence during an inquiry?

The Commission gathers evidence through summons and enforced attendance, examination on oath, discovery and production of documents, affidavits, requisitioned public records, and commissions for examination. It may also enter premises believed to hold relevant documents and use government investigation officers, with concurrence, to investigate, summon witnesses and record evidence formally.

The contrast with the position of a private litigant gives these powers their practical meaning. A family pursuing a complaint of torture, custodial death or disappearance after arrest begins in informational weakness. Its members cannot compel production of the station diary for the night in question. They cannot insist on being handed the post-mortem record, the inquest papers, the arrest memo or the lock-up register. They cannot summon the sentry, the investigating officer or the doctor who conducted the first examination and require answers on oath. Applications can be made and a court may in time order disclosure, and a determined lawyer may reconstruct much from fragments, but each step costs money, time and procedural knowledge while the records remain with the institution whose conduct is in issue. Section 13 inverts that starting position. The institution can be required to produce, the witness can be required to attend, and the record can be requisitioned from whichever office holds it. The inversion is not complete, since the Commission must choose to use its powers, frame its demands with precision and manage a heavy inquiry load. The legal capacity is nevertheless present from the outset, and departments answer a body that can compel production in a different tone from a body that can only request it.

Section 14 provides the field investigation that powers of summons and production alone cannot supply. For conducting an investigation pertaining to an inquiry, the Commission may, with the concurrence of the Central Government or the concerned State Government, utilise the services of any officer or investigation agency of that government. An officer so utilised may investigate the matter, summon and record evidence in the manner contemplated by the section, and must report back to the Commission. Material gathered by such an officer is gathered for the inquiry rather than merely asserted by the respondent department in its own defence, although the officer will often come from the same broad governmental structure as the persons complained against. The concurrence requirement is the visible limit. The Commission cannot commandeer a state agency at will, and a reluctant government can make agreement slow in a sensitive case. Against that limit stands the Commission's own machinery. Under Section 11 the Commission has its own staff, including police and investigative staff working under an officer not below the rank of Director General of Police, with a Secretary-General as its chief executive. That internal capacity allows the Commission to plan inquiries, evaluate outside reports and maintain continuity across cases without depending entirely on borrowed officers.

Read together, Sections 17, 14 and 13 form a sequence of escalating answers to official silence or self-protection. Section 17 begins with a call for a report and permits the Commission to proceed if the report does not arrive. Section 14 permits investigation through borrowed officers with concurrence or through the Commission's own investigative staff. Section 13 permits witnesses to be summoned, documents to be discovered and produced, public records to be requisitioned and premises believed to hold relevant documents to be entered. The existence of a power is not proof of its exercise in a given case. What the scheme does establish is that, in an ordinary complaint against civil authorities, an inquiry cannot credibly be said to have failed for a complete absence of legal means covering oral testimony on oath, documentary and public records, and field investigation reported back to the Commission.

Section 16 protects the person on the other side of the inquiry. A person likely to be prejudicially affected by the inquiry, or whose conduct the Commission considers it necessary to inquire into, or against whom a risk to reputation arises in the course of the inquiry, must be given a reasonable opportunity of being heard and of producing evidence in defence. Inquiries of this kind name officials, often junior officials with limited control over the events in which they took part, and a finding or a widely reported allegation can damage or end a career before any criminal or departmental process begins. The hearing guarantee answers that danger. It also strengthens the complainant's position in a less obvious way. Findings reached after the affected person has been heard carry greater weight with a government asked to act on them and with a court asked to respect them, because the charge of one-sidedness has been met inside the process. Fairness in this scheme is not a concession outside the human rights purpose. It is one of the conditions that makes adverse findings usable.

## The armed forces carve-out: Section 19

All of these powers meet a statutory wall when a complaint concerns the armed forces. In the Act, armed forces means the naval, military and air forces and includes the other armed forces of the Union. Section 19 provides that, in dealing with complaints of violation of human rights by members of the armed forces, the Commission shall adopt a prescribed procedure only. That procedure replaces the ordinary inquiry model rather than trimming it. The breadth of the definition matters, because the carve-out reaches beyond the regular army on a battlefield to naval and air personnel and to the other armed forces of the Union, including personnel deployed on internal duties in aid of civil authority and in counter-insurgency roles. Those internal deployments generate the civilian complaints about search, arrest, firing, custody and disappearance that most need examination, and in those complaints the petitioner receives not a reduced version of the ordinary process but a different process with a ceiling of two steps.

The first step is a report from the Central Government, which the Commission may seek either on its own motion or on receipt of a petition. No call issues under this procedure to a subordinate commander, no personnel are summoned, and no unit record is requisitioned through the ordinary powers. The account returns from the Central Government as an institution, after passing through the chain of the force concerned and the ministry that answers for it. After receiving the report, the Commission may either not proceed with the complaint or make its recommendations to that Government. Those are the only two outcomes the section offers. The Central Government must inform the Commission of the action taken on the recommendations within three months or such further time as the Commission may allow, a duty that prevents a recommendation from vanishing into an unacknowledged file. The Commission publishes its report together with its recommendations made to the Central Government and the action taken by that Government, and provides a copy of the published report to the petitioner. Publication and the copy to the petitioner close the scheme. The petitioner learns what was recommended and what the government states it did, without having had access to an inquiry of the kind available against the civil police.

The absences in Section 19 should be stated plainly. There is no power under Section 19 to summon the personnel involved, to investigate independently, or to call for evidence as Section 13 permits in other cases. There is no enforced attendance, no examination on oath, no discovery and production of documents, no requisition of a public record and no entry into a place believed to hold relevant documents for the purposes of this procedure. These powers are not held in reserve and used cautiously. They are absent from the procedure prescribed. A Commission dissatisfied with the Central Government's report could ask institutionally for a fuller account, recommend strongly, publish fully and control any extension of time for the action taken report. It could not, within Section 19, convert disbelief into evidence through its own compulsory acts. This carve-out operates in exactly the theatres where the [Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act framework](/2010/08/15/afspa-1958-explained/) applies, where extraordinary powers have been conferred for disturbed conditions and where the case for independent fact finding is at its strongest rather than its weakest.

The government's case for the arrangement, in its strongest form, rests on discipline and on the nature of operational judgment. Forces deployed in counter-insurgency conditions act on fragmentary information, under severe time pressure, in places where a concealed weapon or a false signal can kill within seconds. Operational judgment formed in such conditions, the argument runs, cannot fairly be second-guessed by a civilian body with summons powers, reconstructing a decision taken in seconds from documents and hindsight in a hearing room distant from the ground. If patrol leaders, sentries and commanding officers faced compulsory attendance, examination on oath and production of operational papers whenever a complaint followed an operation, command would hesitate and soldiers would bear risks that their judges would never bear. Military discipline also possesses internal machinery, including courts of inquiry and service law, framed by doctrine and tactical context that a general human rights body does not command. On this view, the report and publication route preserves accountability without compromising operations, since the Central Government must account to the Commission, answer recommendations within the prescribed period and accept publication of the recommendations and the action taken.

The critics' case, in its strongest form, rests on the logic of evidence that the rest of the Act accepts. Everywhere else, a report from the respondent is treated as a starting point to be tested, which is the reason Sections 13 and 14 exist. Under Section 19 the body complained against authors the only evidence the Commission sees, which inverts the logic applied everywhere else in the Act. The force whose member is accused supplies, through the Central Government, the factual narrative. The Commission cannot summon a civilian witness whose account differs, cannot requisition the record that might resolve the conflict and cannot send investigators to the place. It must then choose between closure and recommendation on a single authored record, and the government that authored the record decides what action to take on any recommendation, subject to reporting that action and accepting publication. Publication exposes a conclusion without exposing the underlying facts to independent testing. The proportion is also reversed, since a complaint against a local police station attracts summons, production and investigation, while a complaint arising from an operation under extraordinary statutory powers, conducted by personnel equipped for lethal force, attracts a request for a report.

A verdict is available from the structure of the section itself. The government's argument explains, at its strongest, why caution is needed, why operational context must be heard, why sensitive material may require protection and why tactical findings should be framed with an understanding of the conditions in which decisions were taken. None of those concerns requires the complete removal of summons, investigation and evidence-calling powers. Caution could have been expressed through examination away from public view, protection for operational detail, a requirement to hear the commanding officer before any adverse observation, or limits on publication of sensitive material, each preserving an independent capacity to test facts. Section 19 adopts none of those intermediate forms. It adopts a report, a choice between closure and recommendation, a time limit for reporting action taken, and publication. The defining feature of that choice is not caution in the exercise of compulsory powers but their absence. On its own terms, therefore, the section supports the critics' description of how it works. It trusts the Central Government's account in armed forces cases in a way the Act refuses to trust the account of any other respondent. Whether that trust is justified by military necessity remains open to political and constitutional argument. That the trust is complete within the prescribed procedure, and that the complainant under Section 19 is weaker by design than a complainant in any other class of case under the Act, follows from the words and structure of the section.

## What the Commission can deliver at the end of an inquiry

Section 18 of the Protection of Human Rights Act 1993 sets out the steps the Commission may take during or upon the completion of an inquiry. The provision is not confined to a single moment at the end of a file, because harm disclosed in the middle of an inquiry may call for a response before findings are final. A complainant therefore experiences the parts of Section 18 in an order that differs from the order in which a reader meets them in the statute book.

Interim relief is the part that may arrive first. The Commission may recommend, at any stage of the inquiry, the grant of immediate interim relief to the victim or to the family of the victim. The Commission does not have to wait for a final report or for every disputed fact to be resolved before it identifies an urgent need. For a family that has lost an earning member or a victim who needs support in the aftermath of a violation, delay can empty a later remedy of much of its value. Interim relief answers that problem in principle, even though it remains a recommendation. It is directed to pressing need, and it does not depend on the final allocation of responsibility in the report.

The central final power arises where the inquiry discloses the commission of a violation of human rights, or negligence in the prevention of a violation of human rights, or abetment of such a violation, by a public servant. That threshold links the strongest recommendations to a defined factual basis. Violation covers an act that breaches human rights. Negligence in prevention covers a failure to stop a violation that ought to have been prevented. Abetment covers participation that encourages or assists a violation committed by another. Each route leads to the same gateway, but each describes a different form of official fault, and the Commission has no roving authority under this head to recommend payment or prosecution in the abstract.

Once that gateway is crossed, the Commission may recommend to the concerned Government or authority the payment of compensation or damages to the complainant, to the victim, or to the family of the victim. The recommendation places responsibility for payment on the Government or authority to which it is addressed. It does not frame payment as a private bargain between the victim and the individual officer. It frames payment as an institutional response to conduct by public servants and to failures within systems of prevention for which the state apparatus must answer.

The same head allows a recommendation for the initiation of proceedings for prosecution or such other suitable action against the persons concerned, and a recommendation for such further action as the Commission thinks fit. Prosecution and compensation stand side by side without either displacing the other. A payment may acknowledge harm and support a victim, but payment alone does not answer a case that calls for criminal accountability. A prosecution may address culpability, but prosecution alone does not repair material loss. The wider words allow the Commission to shape a recommendation to the conduct found, while the specific heads give complainants and governments clear notice of the principal remedies the provision has in view.

A separate strand in the history of Section 18 concerns the place of compensation. In the Act as originally enacted, compensation was not listed as a separate express head, and recommendations rested on the general power to recommend prosecution or other action. The 2019 amendment restated Section 18 to name compensation first, removing doubt about whether compensation stood at the centre of the remedial scheme. Interim relief, by contrast, stood in the original scheme. The contrast shows a scheme that recognised urgent need from the beginning but acquired, through later amendment, a clearer statement of monetary redress as a principal final outcome.

Section 18 gives the Commission another route that points away from the executive and towards the courts. The Commission may approach the Supreme Court or the concerned High Court for such directions, orders, or writs as the court may deem necessary. The Commission itself recommends, while a court directs, orders, and issues writs. Where the Commission considers that the situation calls for a judicial command, it may place the matter before a court that possesses that authority, and the decision on what, if anything, should be directed then rests with the court. The power belongs to the Commission rather than to the complainant under Section 18, and its significance lies in the connection it creates between an inquiry body and the constitutional courts.

Transparency to the petitioner forms another part of the scheme. The Commission shall provide a copy of the inquiry report to the petitioner, who is therefore not left to learn the outcome through rumour or through the government response alone. Possession of the report matters for every later step, because the petitioner can see the findings and the recommendations, compare the government response with the reasoning of the Commission, and take advice on a further legal route.

The report then travels in the other direction as well. The Commission shall send a copy of its inquiry report, together with its recommendations, to the concerned Government or authority. That sending starts a defined period of accountability, because a duty to respond follows, with a time limit, a requirement to state what has been done or what is proposed, and publication that brings the exchange into the open.

### What must a government do after receiving a recommendation?

The concerned Government or authority must, within one month or such further time as the Commission allows, forward its comments on the inquiry report, including the action taken or proposed to be taken on the recommendations, after which the Commission publishes the report with those comments attached.

The one month period is the benchmark, subject to such further time as the Commission may allow. The allowance of further time is a discretion held by the Commission, not an open ended liberty held by the government. The comments must address the inquiry report and must include the action taken or proposed to be taken on the recommendations. A bare acknowledgment of receipt does not satisfy the design of the provision, because the provision asks for a substantive account of response. Action taken records a step already completed. Action proposed records a step the government states it will take, even where the step has not yet been completed. Both forms of statement create a record that can be tested, and a government that reports neither in any meaningful sense leaves a gap that is itself visible.

Publication completes the Section 18 sequence. The Commission shall publish its inquiry report together with the comments of the concerned Government or authority and the action taken or proposed to be taken on the recommendations. A published report places the findings, the recommendations, the comments, and the response in a form that the victim, the legislature, a court, a journalist, or a civil society organisation can read. It also disciplines a government response, because an authority that knows its comments will appear alongside the report has reason to write comments that can bear public reading. Reasons that look thin beside detailed findings are exposed by proximity, and a promise of action can be measured against later conduct.

Read as a whole, the section gives interim relief, final recommendations for compensation or damages, prosecution, other suitable action, or further action the Commission thinks fit, a possible approach to the Supreme Court or a High Court, a copy of the report to the petitioner, a government duty to comment in time, and publication. None of those things is a decree. Its strength and its weakness arise from the same design.

## The recommendation ceiling

A recommendation of the Commission binds no one. No execution machinery follows a recommendation, and a victim cannot levy it like a decree. A decree belongs to a court process that can move from judgment to enforcement through recognised coercive steps. A recommendation belongs to a different legal world. It is a reasoned demand for action addressed to a government or authority, supported by the findings of a statutory inquiry, but it does not itself compel payment, compel prosecution, or compel any other act through execution. A government may accept it, may act on it in part, may propose a different course, or may decline it, and the recommendation alone supplies no officer of a court to enforce the outcome. The gap between a finding and an enforced remedy is the space in which delay, pressure, and litigation occur.

Yet a recommendation is more than advice offered by a private body. Three pressures work on it, and each comes from the statute rather than from goodwill alone. The first pressure is the duty of the concerned Government or authority to respond within one month, or within such further time as the Commission allows, with comments including the action taken or proposed to be taken. The duty converts a recommendation from a document that can be shelved in silence into a document that calls for a written answer on the record.

The second pressure is publication. Section 18 requires the Commission to publish the inquiry report together with the comments of the government and the action taken or proposed. Publication attaches the response to the findings in a single public account, so a government cannot easily present its conduct in a more favourable light elsewhere while its own comments sit beside the report. Publication does not enforce, but it remembers, and institutional memory in public form is a pressure of a real kind.

The third pressure comes from Section 20(2). Under that provision, the annual and special reports of the Commission are laid before Parliament or the State Legislature together with a memorandum of action taken or proposed to be taken on the recommendations and the reasons for non-acceptance of any recommendation. The individual case therefore enters a wider system of legislative accounting, and the pattern of acceptance, delay, partial action, and refusal across reports is placed before elected representatives in documentary form. A government that declines a recommendation must explain the decline in that memorandum. A refusal therefore has to be explained twice over, in writing, on the record, first in the Section 18 exchange published with the report and again in the Section 20(2) memorandum.

### Why is a commission recommendation not the same as a court order?

A recommendation binds no one and carries no execution machinery, so a victim cannot enforce it like a decree. A court order, by contrast, commands obedience and can be executed through the court, while the Commission must rely on response duties, publication and parliamentary scrutiny to secure compliance.

A court order speaks in the language of command and is supported by processes for enforcement. A recommendation speaks in the language of institutional judgment and is supported by duties of response, by publication, and by legislative scrutiny. A victim who holds a court order holds an instrument that the legal system can execute. A victim who holds a recommendation holds findings and a reasoned demand that the state answer and act, but not an instrument of execution. That distinction should not be softened, because softening it misleads complainants. Nor should it be inflated into a claim that a recommendation has no legal consequence, because the consequence operates through administrative law and through the legislative record, and a government remains bound to act rationally and fairly even when it deals with a non binding recommendation.

No court treats a recommendation as a decree, and the jurisprudence accepts that a recommendation is not binding. The review position nevertheless gives the recommendation a second life in a courtroom. A government that ignores a recommendation, or rejects it without reasons, exposes its inaction to judicial review on ordinary administrative law grounds, because unexplained disregard of a reasoned finding by a statutory body is the kind of arbitrariness that Article 14 condemns. A court does not need to convert the recommendation into a decree to ask whether the government acted arbitrarily. The target of review is the government decision, and the questions concern whether the government considered the report and gave reasons that address the findings.

A reasoned rejection stands in a stronger position for the government. Where a government engages with the evidence and gives reasons why a recommendation cannot be accepted or why a different course will be taken, a court faces a harder task in dislodging that decision on review. Judicial review is not an appeal on the merits, and a court does not substitute its own preference merely because another course might have been preferable. Thin reasons expose a decision to challenge, while substantial reasons give it a defence.

For that reason, the practical fight is usually about forcing reasons into the open. A complainant gains ground when a government is made to state whether it accepts a recommendation, what action it has taken, what action it proposes, and why any part is not accepted. A stated reason can be tested against the report, a stated proposal can be followed to its completion, and a stated refusal can be carried into the legislative record and, where reasons are absent or inadequate, into a writ petition.

The compliance pattern has to be stated with care and without invented precision. Governments comply with compensation recommendations in the ordinary run of cases, often after delay. Recommendations for prosecution of named officers meet the stiffest resistance. The published reports of the Commission and the action taken memoranda laid before legislatures form the record on which that pattern is visible. Money can be paid through an administrative decision that does not require the state to proceed against its own officers, while prosecution asks the state to set the criminal process in motion against named individuals inside its own apparatus. Delay occupies a middle ground between compliance and refusal.

## When the recommendation is ignored: the realistic ladder

A complainant whose recommendation is ignored needs a sequence of steps that matches the design of the Act. The first step is to press the Commission itself, which holds the report, the recommendations, the government comments where comments exist, and the power of publication under Section 18. A complainant can place the failure to act before the Commission, point to action proposed and not completed, or point to the absence of any adequate response. The aim is to make the default part of the record rather than a private grievance.

Publication gives that pressure a public form. The Commission publishes the inquiry report together with the comments of the government and the action taken or proposed. Where a government has answered, the answer travels with the report. Where a government has remained silent beyond the period allowed, or has answered without addressing the substance, the published record shows the recommendation beside the response or beside the silence. The documents sit together, and the gap between them can be seen.

The next step uses the legislative record created by the action taken memorandum. Under Section 20(2), annual and special reports are laid before Parliament or the State Legislature with a memorandum of action taken or proposed and the reasons for non-acceptance of any recommendation. A memorandum does not enforce a payment, but the laying of the report changes the forum in which the default is preserved. Questions can be asked on the basis of a laid document, patterns across cases can be drawn from laid documents, and a refusal that might pass unnoticed inside a department becomes part of a record available for public and political scrutiny.

Where those steps do not produce action, the writ petition becomes the central legal route. A petitioner may approach the High Court under Article 226 or the Supreme Court under Article 32, with the findings of the Commission as evidence developed through a statutory inquiry and the unexplained refusal as the review target. The court is not asked to execute the recommendation as if it were a decree. It is asked to examine the legality of the state response, or the absence of a lawful response, in the light of the findings. The report supplies a reasoned account, the Section 18 exchange supplies the government comments or the silence that followed, and the memorandum supplies the reasons for non-acceptance or exposes their absence. A court can then test the state conduct against Article 14 and ordinary principles of review.

The writ route is not a consolation prize, because the constitutional courts possess a compensation jurisdiction of their own. That jurisdiction does not depend on Section 18. The courts award compensation in public law on their own authority where fundamental rights have been infringed, and the leading cases show the route in operation across custody, detention, and death at the hands of the state. A complainant who reaches a constitutional court enters a forum that can make an award carrying the authority of a court.

In Rudul Sah v State of Bihar, decided in 1983, the Supreme Court granted compensation to a man who had been kept in prison for years after his acquittal, in a habeas corpus petition. A person whose liberty was taken away beyond the point at which the law allowed detention was not confined to a declaration that the detention was wrong. The court used its constitutional jurisdiction to grant compensation, and the petition that began as a challenge to imprisonment became the vehicle for that award.

In Nilabati Behera v State of Orissa, decided in 1993, the Supreme Court awarded compensation for a death in police custody and explained that the public law remedy under Article 32 and Article 226 is distinct from, and additional to, a private law claim in damages. A private law claim and a public law claim answer different juridical concerns, even where both arise from the same death. The public law remedy responds to the infringement of a fundamental right by the state. The case also shows why inquiry findings can matter in a writ petition without being treated as a decree, because a court grants relief on its own authority, while inquiry material can inform its decision.

In D K Basu v State of West Bengal, decided in 1997, the Supreme Court laid down requirements governing arrest and detention and recognised monetary compensation as an acknowledged remedy for an established infringement of Article 21. The case joined preventive requirements with a remedial principle for cases in which the protection had already failed, so a complainant who invokes D K Basu invokes both a standard by which official conduct can be judged and an authority for compensation where Article 21 is infringed.

Read together, Rudul Sah, Nilabati Behera, and D K Basu explain why the move to a constitutional court is a move upwards in coercive authority rather than a sideways move to another advisory forum. The Commission can recommend compensation, while a constitutional court can award it. The ladder holds because each step places the complainant in a stronger position than the step before, even though no step guarantees success.

A parallel criminal process runs beside the ladder and does not wait for a recommendation at any point. A first information report and a complaint before a magistrate run on their own tracks, with their own thresholds, procedures, and courts. A recommendation for the initiation of prosecution may add institutional weight to a demand for criminal action, but its absence does not close the criminal route, and a victim does not need permission from the Commission to set the criminal law in motion. Equally, a recommendation for compensation does not extinguish a criminal complaint, and a recommendation for prosecution does not replace the need for the criminal process to satisfy its own requirements.

The debate over the strength of the institution gathers these themes into a single phrase. Justice H L Dattu, a former Chief Justice of India who served as Chairperson of the Commission, described the institution as a toothless tiger in a newspaper interview in 2016, saying that after painstaking investigation the Commission could only recommend remedial measures or compensation. The phrase carries force because of its source and because of its economy. A tiger suggests an institution expected to inspire respect and fear, while toothless suggests that the expected means of compulsion are missing.

The Commission also described itself in terms of helplessness in an affidavit before the Supreme Court in the litigation brought by families of victims of alleged extra judicial executions in Manipur, and the Court in that litigation took note of the inability of the Commission to take coercive measures. That episode deepens the significance of the remark by Justice H L Dattu, because the sense of limitation appeared in a formal statement in litigation concerning grave allegations, and the Court recorded the limitation.

A different view came from a later Chairperson. Justice Arun Mishra publicly rejected the toothless tiger description, maintaining that the Commission has sufficient powers and that authorities implement the great majority of its recommendations. That characterisation belongs to Justice Arun Mishra and should be treated as his attributed assessment rather than as a free standing statistic. His rejection completes the exchange, because the institution has been described from within as lacking teeth and has also been defended from within as possessing sufficient power in practice.

The exchange itself shows where the position of the institution lies. The phrase came from inside the institution, which is precisely why it carries weight, because a criticism made by a former Chairperson who had conducted the work answers in advance the defence that an outsider has not seen that work. At the same time, the compliance pattern shows that the ceiling is a ceiling on compulsion, not a ceiling on effect. Governments comply with compensation recommendations in the ordinary run of cases, often after delay, even though no execution machinery stands behind the recommendation. Reports are published, memoranda are laid, reasons for non-acceptance must be given, courts can review unexplained disregard, and constitutional courts can award compensation on their own authority. The Commission cannot compel, but it can inquire, find, recommend, require a response, publish the exchange, feed a legislative record, approach a constitutional court, and supply material on which a writ petition can be built.

## Choosing the forum in real situations

A death in police custody presents the forum question in its starkest form, because the events that matter happened inside a closed institution, the records that matter were created by that institution, and the family that needs those records stands outside it with the least power to obtain them. The Act answers that imbalance with machinery that begins before any complaint is drafted. Deaths in police custody and deaths in judicial custody must be reported to the National Human Rights Commission by the district authorities within twenty-four hours, followed by the post-mortem report, the magisterial inquiry report and the record of the videographed post-mortem examination. The sequence is deliberate. A prompt report fixes the fact and time of death before accounts can settle into a convenient shape, the post-mortem record speaks to the condition of the body, the magisterial inquiry places an officer outside the police chain in charge of the questions, and the videograph guards the examination itself against later dispute. The Commission treats every custodial death as requiring an explanation from the state, and that posture changes the character of a family complaint. Such a complaint does not ask an indifferent office to take an interest in a closed file. It joins a process already designed to demand the post-mortem and magisterial record, and it adds the family's account and knowledge to a file the district authorities were obliged to open within a day. Its practical work is therefore specific. It identifies gaps between what the machinery should have produced and what has been produced, presses for reports that have not arrived, and places on record any injury, illness, threat or prior complaint that the official papers omit, before the official version hardens through repetition. The writ route remains in the background throughout, and it remains the compensation route of last resort, because a constitutional court can order what a commission can only recommend. A family that understands that division of labour uses the Commission for the record it is built to extract, and preserves the writ petition for enforceable relief, with the Commission's file strengthening the petition if that stage is reached.

An encounter death raises a related but distinct problem, because the state does not deny that its officers caused the death and instead justifies the killing as an act done in the line of duty. The argument is rarely about whether a death occurred. It is about whether the force was lawful, whether the account given afterwards is credible, and whether the investigation was conducted by hands clean enough to be trusted. Here the complainant is not left to invent a standard of scrutiny, because a settled checklist exists and the complaint's job is to hold the state to that checklist. The Commission first issued guidelines on deaths in police encounters in 1997, addressed to all Chief Ministers, and revised them in 2010. The revised guidelines require the police to register a first information report where a specific complaint makes out a cognizable case of culpable homicide against the police, investigation by an independent agency such as the state crime investigation department rather than the police station involved, a magisterial inquiry into every death in police action, a report of every such death to the Commission by the district Superintendent of Police within forty-eight hours, a second report within three months carrying the post-mortem report, the inquest report and the inquiry findings, and no out-of-turn promotion or instant gallantry award for the officers involved until the gallantry is established beyond doubt. The staged reports prevent silence from becoming a strategy, because the state must speak first within forty-eight hours and again within three months with documents in hand. In PUCL v State of Maharashtra in 2014, a Supreme Court bench of Chief Justice R M Lodha and Justice Rohinton F Nariman laid down sixteen requirements to be followed in investigating police encounters in cases of death, treated as law declared under Article 141 of the Constitution, including recording the intelligence or tip-off, registering a first information report where firearms are used and death results, investigation by the criminal investigation department or a team from another police station under a senior officer, a magisterial inquiry, informing the National Human Rights Commission or the State Human Rights Commission of the incident without delay, involving the next of kin, and suspension and disciplinary proceedings where an officer is found guilty of a wrongful encounter. The judgment absorbed much of the Commission's own guidelines into binding law, which is the clearest instance of the Commission's soft output hardening into enforceable doctrine. For a complainant the consequence is practical. The complaint can be framed as verifiable questions. Was the tip-off recorded. Was a first information report registered. Who investigated, and was that agency independent of the station involved. Was a magisterial inquiry held. Was the Commission informed without delay. Did the second report arrive within three months with the required papers. Were the next of kin involved. Was any officer honoured before gallantry was established beyond doubt. A complaint built in that shape converts suspicion into documented failure against a named standard, and documented failure is far harder for a government to file away without answer.

A person held in preventive detention whose family believes the detention is mala fide presents a different configuration, because the detainee is alive, the state asserts a lawful order on its face, and the complaint is not that no authority existed but that the authority was exercised for an improper purpose, on irrelevant material, or without honest application of mind. In that configuration the primary fight is the habeas corpus petition, and a commission complaint is no substitute for it. The writ court examines the detention order, can direct production of the detainee, and can set the person at liberty if the order fails; no recommendation carries that consequence, and time spent waiting for a commission while detention continues is time spent in custody. The commission complaint is the parallel record building step, and in that role it has real value. It places the family's account of the arrest, the sequence of detention, the condition of the detainee and any denial of access or medical care on an official record outside the detaining authority's own file, and it preserves dates, names and representations in a form that can be drawn upon if conditions deteriorate, if detention is extended, or if a compensation claim follows release. The reader who wants the detention side set out at length will find it in [a fuller guide to preventive detention law in India](/2010/06/01/preventive-detention-law-india-guide/), and the point for forum choice is one of sequence. Pursue the writ as the remedy that can free the person, and file the commission complaint alongside it as the remedy that can document, inquire and recommend. Confuse the two, and a family may win a sympathetic observation while the detention runs its full course untouched.

An atrocity against a member of a Scheduled Caste requires the same discipline about parallel tracks, approached from the opposite direction. The commission route remains open, and a complaint can bring inquiry, record and recommendation to bear on public servants who failed to prevent the offence, failed to register it, failed to investigate it or failed to protect the victim and the family. Alongside that route, however, a parallel statutory remedy with its own machinery exists for the atrocity itself, built for this class of offence and carrying its own offences, arrangements and relief, and that machinery is set out in [an explanation of the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes atrocities legislation](/2017/02/15/sc-st-atrocities-act-1989-explained/) for the reader who needs its detail. The forum error here is not usually the choice of a wrong forum in place of a right one. It is the assumption that one filing exhausts the matter, so that a family pursues a commission complaint while the criminal process under the special statute drifts, or pursues the criminal case alone and never places the failure of the local administration on any record beyond the police file. The two tracks answer different questions. The special statute asks who committed the atrocity and what punishment and relief follow. The Commission asks what the state and its servants did and failed to do around the atrocity, and what should change and be paid in consequence. A complainant who keeps both questions alive, in their proper forums, gives the grievance its full shape.

The fifth situation is quieter and in some ways the most instructive, because nothing dramatic has gone wrong except that an institution is missing. A grievance that belongs naturally to a State Human Rights Commission meets a commission that has not been constituted, or that exists in name while its posts lie vacant, or that sits so rarely that a complaint filed with it goes nowhere. Section 21 explains how that gap is possible, because the section says a State Government may constitute a State Human Rights Commission, and the permissive verb matters. The state tier exists only where a state has chosen to create it and keep it staffed, and states have gone long stretches with commissions unconstituted or posts vacant. A State Commission can inquire only into matters relatable to List II and List III of the Seventh Schedule under Section 21(5), and it cannot inquire into a matter the National Commission or another commission is already inquiring into. Sections 9 to 11 and sections 13 to 19 of the Act apply to State Commissions with the adaptations under Section 29, so the powers and the one year bar travel with the design. Where the state tier is absent or dormant, the national complaint becomes the operative one, filed with the National Commission because it is the institution that exists and can carry an inquiry forward, and the complainant should record on the face of the complaint that the state body is unconstituted or without members, so the file itself shows why the national forum was approached. A remedy drawn on paper as a two-tier structure delivers a two-tier remedy only in states that have built the second tier, and elsewhere the citizen's real map of forums is shorter than the Act's table of contents suggests.

That point leads to a confusion that costs complainants time they do not have. The National Commission for Women, the National Commission for Scheduled Castes, the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes, the National Commission for Minorities, the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights and the National Human Rights Commission are separate bodies, with separate statutes and separate complaint desks. Their names sound like departments of a single organisation, and a complainant acting on that impression may send a grievance about custodial violence to a body constituted for a different subject. Commissions do forward misdirected complaints in practice, but forwarding takes time, the receiving body must register and examine the matter afresh, and all of it happens against the one year bar. A complaint sent to the wrong commission therefore loses time against the one year bar even when it eventually arrives at the right one, and where the events are already some months old, the weeks spent at the wrong desk can be the difference between a complaint the Commission may entertain and one it is barred from touching. Identify the body whose statute covers the grievance, confirm its complaint desk, and file there first.

## The honest calculation: neither a court nor a waste of time

Two opposite errors dominate discussion of the Commission, and a complainant is served by neither. The first assumes that a human rights commission is a court in slightly informal clothing, so that a complaint well made will end in an order a government must obey as a decree or writ must be obeyed. It is not a court. Its characteristic output is a recommendation, and a complainant who files expecting an enforceable order, who waits for execution as a decree holder waits, and who takes no other step because the Commission has been moved, will be disappointed in a way the statute never promised to prevent. That disappointment is produced by mistaken expectation rather than by a broken promise. The Act grants inquiry, the power to call for information and reports from governments and authorities, the power to visit institutions, intervention in court proceedings with the court's approval, and the power to recommend compensation, prosecution, departmental action and changes of practice. A remedy of that shape does its work through exposure, record and pressure before it does any work through payment, and a complainant who files in ignorance of that shape will misjudge a favourable outcome, by counting it worthless because no officer was jailed on the spot, and a delay, by counting it final because no order has arrived.

The opposite error concludes, from the same recommendatory character, that the Commission is useless and that filing is an empty ritual. That conclusion does not follow, and it survives only by ignoring what a commission inquiry can do that private litigation, for all its superior enforceability, often cannot. The Commission can obtain records a private litigant cannot compel, because a department that may ignore or answer evasively a citizen's request for its internal papers faces a statutory body entitled to call for information and reports, and the papers produced enter a record the complainant can then use. Its findings carry evidentiary weight, in the practical sense that a considered finding by a statutory commission, reached after calling for the state's own reports, is read differently by a writ court, a legislature, a department and a newspaper from an allegation in a petition, however carefully drafted. Its findings also carry political weight, because a government may lawfully decline a recommendation in a way it cannot lawfully decline a court order, but declining a published recommendation on a documented death or failure is a public act with a public cost. Its reports and the memoranda of action taken that governments lay alongside them also create a legislative record in which a single complaint can appear as part of a pattern a legislature is asked to answer for. Compensation belongs in the same calculation, because compensation recommendations are in the ordinary run complied with, and a family that receives payment on a recommendation has received the same money it would have received on an order, by a route that demanded less in cost, in years and in exposure than full constitutional litigation would have demanded. None of this converts a recommendation into an order, and the cases in which a government resists, delays or answers with token compliance are part of the record too, which is why the writ route is kept in reserve rather than renounced. The honest position sits between the two errors. The Commission is a powerful instrument of a particular kind, weak exactly where a court is strong and strong exactly where a lone litigant is weak, and the complainant who grasps that shape can deploy it deliberately instead of discovering it by disappointment.

The Human Rights Courts provisions supply the cautionary counterweight that keeps this calculation honest, because they show what happens when a statute creates an institution in words and the institution is never built in fact. Implementation of Section 30 has been uneven. In many districts the designation of a Court of Session as a Human Rights Court exists on paper while the court continues to sit as an ordinary sessions court, no separate docket is maintained, and the special public prosecutor under Section 31 is not appointed, so the provision delivers less than its text promises. The gap between text and institution, which the State Commission gap under Section 21 already illustrates, can open inside a single section, in a court building that exists, staffed by judges who sit daily, under a designation formally made. What is missing is the separateness that was supposed to make the forum more specialised and more visible, and that missing element leaves no trace in any single answer, only in the accumulated experience of litigants whose cases move at the ordinary pace under an extraordinary name. A complainant should therefore test every forum the Act names by one question. Does the body exist, does it sit, is it staffed, does it keep its own record, and does its output reach anyone who must answer for it. Where the answer is yes, the forum deserves a careful complaint. Where the answer is no, the complaint belongs elsewhere, and the year should not be spent discovering the answer by experiment. This article describes the general position, which is no substitute for advice on an individual case, and limitation periods and forum rules are unforgiving, so a live grievance should reach a lawyer while the year still runs.

## What the amendments changed for a complainant

The Act a complainant meets is not the Act of 1993 in its original form, because Parliament amended it in 2006 and again in 2019, and the filing side of the counter asks one question. What did those amendments change for the person who brings the grievance, as distinct from the institution that receives it. The Protection of Human Rights (Amendment) Act, 2006, in force from 23 November 2006, was largely structural. It reduced a State Commission from five members to three, namely a chairperson who has been a Chief Justice of a High Court, one judicial member who is or has been a judge of a High Court or a district judge with at least seven years of service as a district judge, and one member with knowledge or practical experience of human rights. It also allowed two or more state governments to share a chairperson or member between their commissions, a provision that speaks directly to the staffing problem, since a smaller commission with a shared chairperson is easier for a state to keep constituted than a larger one that must be filled from that state alone. For a complainant the 2006 changes mattered at the margins of availability and composition. A three-member state body is leaner in its judicial element and more dependent on the quality of its single expert member, and the sharing provision offered a route by which a state without a functioning commission might acquire at least a chairperson. What the amendment did not do is as significant. It did not touch the recommendation ceiling, so both commissions remained bodies that recommend rather than order. It did not touch the one year bar, so a complaint about an event more than a year old remained outside reach however the membership was arranged. The two hard limits that define a complainant's experience survived the first amendment exactly as enacted in 1993.

The Protection of Human Rights (Amendment) Act, 2019 travelled further across the institution and no further across the complainant's position. It changed the national institution's composition and tenure, providing that a judge of the Supreme Court became eligible for the chairpersonship alongside a former Chief Justice of India, that the expert members rose from two to three with at least one woman, and that the chairpersons of the National Commission for Backward Classes and the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights and the Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities joined as deemed members. Tenure fell from five years to three years, with eligibility for reappointment, a change that alters the rhythm of the institution and the security of its members. The amendment retouched the State Commission's chairperson eligibility in parallel, and it made the compensation head of Section 18 express, so that the power to recommend payment of compensation stood stated in the section rather than resting on implication. The institutional detail of the 2019 amendment belongs to the companion institution article, and here only the complainant-facing significance matters. A wider eligibility for the chair, a somewhat more representative expert membership, and deemed members drawn from neighbouring commissions may each affect the quality and range of inquiries, and an express compensation head removes an argument a government might otherwise have taken against a payment recommendation. Against those gains the same ledger of untouched limits appears, because the amendment left the two hard limits, the one year bar and the recommendatory character, exactly where they stood.

Read together, the two amendments teach a complainant how to read amendment itself. Parliament adjusted who sits on the commissions, how long they sit, which other office holders sit with them, how states may share them and how one head of relief is worded. Parliament did not, on either occasion, make the Commission a court, and it did not lengthen the year. Those refusals represent a settled legislative choice about the kind of body the Commission is to be, strong in inquiry and weak in command, quick to start and barred from starting late. A complainant who internalises that choice stops waiting for the amendment that will convert the Commission into something else and starts using the Commission that exists, within the year that exists, for the relief that exists, so the two hard limits of 1993 remain the two hard limits of the amended Act.

## Verdict: use the Commission for what it is built to do

The statute promises a remedy, and the record shows what kind of remedy it is. It is not the remedy of a court, and a complainant who measures it by that standard will call it a failure even in cases where it has done everything it was built to do. It is a remedy of record, of inquiry and of recommendation, delivered by a body that can summon the state's own papers, test the state's account against guidelines and against law declared by the Supreme Court, publish what it finds, and place a figure of compensation and a course of action before a government that must then decide, in the open, whether to comply. Used for exactly that, inside the year, the Commission gives a complainant things no other single filing gives in combination. It gives an early and authoritative assembly of the official record in custodial and encounter deaths, where the twenty-four-hour and forty-eight-hour reports and the staged production of post-mortem, inquest and magisterial papers mean that delay itself becomes visible and answerable. It gives a parallel documentary track beside a habeas corpus petition or a prosecution under a special statute, so that administrative failure around a violation is recorded even where the central legal fight belongs elsewhere. It also gives findings other institutions can act upon, and a compensation recommendation governments in the ordinary run comply with.

The verdict is therefore one of fit rather than enthusiasm or dismissal. The Commission is built to document, to pressure and to recommend, and a complainant who uses it for exactly that, inside the year, while keeping the writ route in reserve, gets from it everything it has to give. The complainant who asks it to convict, to execute or to resurrect a grievance the year has closed asks for things outside its design, and the failure that follows belongs to the asking rather than to the institution. The reader who knows only the text files in the wrong place a year too late, filing confidently in a forum the text names and the state has never staffed, or at a desk whose name resembles the right one, or in a designated court that keeps no separate docket, and discovers the gap only when the year has run. For teachers and advocates who turn the route map into a presentation or handout, a presentation file can be opened and read in the browser with [the ReportMedic PPT viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), without uploading it anywhere, which suits material built from case papers.

A statute is not its text alone but the whole record around it, and in this Act the record is the difference between a recommendation that moves a government and a grievance that goes nowhere.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you file a human rights complaint with the commission?

A complaint is filed in writing, and no fee is charged at any stage. Under Section 12(a) of the Protection of Human Rights Act 1993, the petition may be presented by the victim or by any person on the victim's behalf, so a relative, a lawyer or an organisation can file for a person who is dead, detained, injured or unable to write. The complaint may be written in English, Hindi or any language in the Eighth Schedule, and may be sent by post, delivered in person, sent by email or filed through the Commission's online complaint portal. It should state the complainant's name and address, the victim's identity, the date, place and particulars of the incident, the public servants or authority involved and the relief sought. The Commission acknowledges the complaint with a diary or case number, which should be kept and quoted in every later communication, together with copies of everything filed and proof of the date of filing.

### Q: Is there a time limit for filing a human rights complaint?

Yes, and it is the hardest limit in the Act. Section 36(2) provides that neither the National Commission nor a State Commission shall inquire into any matter after the expiry of one year from the date on which the act constituting the violation is alleged to have been committed. The year runs from the act, not from the day the victim learns of it, takes advice or gathers documents, and the Act gives the commissions no power to condone delay for any reason, however compelling. A continuing violation, such as a detention the state goes on maintaining, is arguably a fresh act on each day it continues, but a concluded assault is not revived because its injuries continue. A complainant approaching the end of the year should file promptly and supplement the record afterwards. Once the year has run, the commission door closes, but the writ jurisdiction of the High Court and the Supreme Court carries no such statutory bar, though a court will weigh unexplained delay.

### Q: What relief can a human rights commission grant?

Strictly speaking, a commission grants no enforceable relief at all, because its powers under Section 18 are recommendatory. Where an inquiry discloses a violation of human rights, or negligence in its prevention, or abetment of it, by a public servant, the Commission may recommend to the concerned Government or authority the payment of compensation or damages to the complainant, the victim or the victim's family. It may recommend the initiation of proceedings for prosecution or such other suitable action against the persons concerned, and such further action as it thinks fit. At any stage of an inquiry it may recommend the grant of immediate interim relief. It may also approach the Supreme Court or the High Court for directions, orders or writs. The government must respond with its comments and the action taken or proposed within one month, or such further time as the Commission allows, and the report is published with that response, so relief arrives through compliance or through a court, never through execution of the recommendation itself.

### Q: Can you file a human rights complaint against the armed forces?

A complaint can be filed, but it travels by a different and narrower road. Section 19 of the Act provides that, for complaints of violation of human rights by members of the armed forces, meaning the naval, military and air forces and the other armed forces of the Union, the Commission shall adopt one procedure only. It may, on its own motion or on a petition, seek a report from the Central Government. After receiving the report it may either not proceed with the complaint or make its recommendations to that Government. It cannot summon the personnel involved, call for evidence or investigate independently, as it can in an ordinary case under Section 13. The Central Government must inform the Commission of the action taken on the recommendations within three months, or such further time as the Commission allows. The Commission then publishes its report with its recommendations and the action taken, and gives a copy to the petitioner. The complaint is therefore worth filing for the record and the recommendation it can produce, but no complainant should expect an inquiry of the ordinary kind.

### Q: Which cases go to a Human Rights Court?

Offences arising out of a violation of human rights go to a Human Rights Court, and they go by prosecution, not by complaint. Section 30 of the Act provides that, for the purpose of speedy trial of such offences, the State Government may, with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court, by notification specify for each district a Court of Session to be a Human Rights Court to try them. Section 31 provides that the State Government shall specify a public prosecutor, or appoint an advocate who has practised for not less than seven years, as a special public prosecutor for every Human Rights Court. A victim does not file a case there asking for compensation. The case arrives as a criminal trial, founded on investigation and charge, and ends in conviction or acquittal. In practice the designation has been implemented unevenly, and in many districts the specified court sits as an ordinary Court of Session with no separate docket, so a complainant whose object is punishment should understand that the criminal process, rather than the commission, is the road, while compensation and inquiry belong to the commission and writ routes.

### Q: Can you approach a State Human Rights Commission instead?

Yes, where the state has constituted one. Section 21 empowers a State Government to constitute a State Human Rights Commission, and Section 29 applies the operative provisions of the Act, Sections 9 to 11 and 13 to 19 among them, to the State Commission with adaptations, so it inquires with civil court powers and ends in recommendations, exactly as the National Commission does. Two limits define the choice. Under Section 21(5) a State Commission can inquire only into matters relatable to entries in List II and List III of the Seventh Schedule, the State List and the Concurrent List, so a matter of purely Union concern lies outside it. And the one-year bar in Section 36(2) binds the State Commission as firmly as the National Commission. There is also a sequencing trap: under Section 36(1) the National Commission cannot inquire into a matter pending before a State Commission, so the same complaint should not be filed with both. Choose one commission at the outset, guided by subject matter and by which body is functioning and staffed.

### Q: What can you do if the government ignores an NHRC recommendation?

The first step is to put the default on the record. Under Section 18 the government was bound to forward its comments, including the action taken or proposed, within one month or the further time the Commission allowed, and the Commission publishes its report with those comments, so silence or evasion is published. Under Section 20(2) the Commission's annual and special reports are laid before Parliament or the State Legislature with a memorandum of action taken or proposed and the reasons for non-acceptance of any recommendation, which carries the default into legislative scrutiny. The complainant can press the Commission to pursue the recommendation and record the failure. The decisive legal step is a writ petition to the High Court under Article 226 or the Supreme Court under Article 32. A recommendation is not a decree and no court will execute it as one, but a government that ignores a finding, or rejects it without reasons, exposes its inaction to judicial review for arbitrariness, and the courts can award compensation on their authority, as the Supreme Court did for a custodial death in Nilabati Behera v State of Orissa. The ladder runs from record, to legislature, to court.

### Q: Can you go to court if your human rights complaint is rejected?

Yes. Nothing in the Act makes a commission the only door, and a dismissal or closure by a commission decides nothing about the underlying rights. If the complaint was turned away at the threshold, for example because the year under Section 36(2) had expired, the constitutional courts are not bound by that bar: a writ petition under Article 226 of the Constitution lies to the High Court, and a petition under Article 32 lies to the Supreme Court for enforcement of fundamental rights, though the court will weigh unexplained delay on its own principles. If the complaint was closed after a report satisfied the Commission that no further inquiry was required, the writ petition challenges the violation itself, and the commission file, including the government's own report, becomes material the petitioner can place before the court. The criminal process is equally unaffected, since a first information report and a complaint before a magistrate run independently of the commission at every stage. A rejection therefore ends one route, not the grievance, and the commission record usually leaves the complainant better documented than a complainant who began in court.

### Q: Who can file a human rights complaint on behalf of someone else?

Any person can. Section 12(a) empowers the Commission to inquire on a petition presented by the victim or by any person on the victim's behalf, and the width of that phrase is deliberate, because the victims of the gravest violations are often dead, detained, hospitalised, illiterate or too frightened to approach a national body. A spouse, a parent or another relative may file for a person in custody or a person who has died. A lawyer may file for a client. A trade union, a prisoners' welfare group or another organisation may file for a worker, a prisoner or a resident of an institution. The person filing should identify himself or herself, because anonymous and pseudonymous complaints are ordinarily dismissed at the threshold, and should state the victim's particulars, the relationship or authority under which the filing is made, and the facts within the filer's knowledge. The filer becomes the petitioner to whom the Commission sends its acknowledgment and, at the end of an inquiry under Section 18, a copy of the inquiry report, so the choice of filer should be someone who can stay with the file.

### Q: What details should a human rights complaint to the NHRC include?

A complaint should carry the complainant's name and address, the identity of the victim where victim and complainant differ, the date and place of the incident, a narrative of the facts in the order they happened, the public servants or authority involved, identified by name, office, station or institution so far as they are known, and the relief sought, whether compensation, prosecution, interim relief or other action. Supporting papers strengthen it: medical records, copies of applications or representations made at the time, photographs, and the names of witnesses. The date deserves the greatest care of any line, because the Commission measures its own power against Section 36(2), and the inquiry will test the chronology against the respondent authority's records. Where the exact day is uncertain, the complaint should say so and give the narrowest range the complainant can support, fixing the sequence by surrounding events such as an arrest, a production before a magistrate or a release. A precise chronology matters for a practical reason: the Commission ordinarily begins by calling for the authority's report, and a sequence fixed at checkable points is far harder to answer with a general denial.

### Q: Is there any fee for filing a complaint with a human rights commission?

No. A complaint to the National Human Rights Commission or to a State Human Rights Commission carries no fee at filing, at inquiry or at any later stage, and no court fee or stamp duty attaches to it. The inquiry itself is conducted by the Commission using its own machinery and the reports it calls for, rather than by parties financing an adversarial contest. That design is one of the deliberate access features of the Act, and it is the sharpest contrast with the writ route, where a petitioner bears the ordinary costs of drafting, representation, travel and attendance over what may be years of litigation. The complainant's real expenses are incidental ones: preparing and copying documents, corresponding with the Commission and, where advice is taken, paying for it. Those costs should not be understated for a poor family, but they are costs of pursuit, not a price of admission. For a complainant of limited means, the commission route is frequently the only inquiry into official conduct that can be set in motion at all.

### Q: Can you file a human rights complaint in your own language?

Yes. A complaint to the Commission may be written in English, in Hindi or in any language included in the Eighth Schedule to the Constitution, which gathers the scheduled languages of the Union. The complainant should write in whichever of those languages allows the facts to be stated most accurately, because an inquiry lives on accuracy: dates, names, places and the sequence of events must survive translation without distortion, and a complaint written haltingly in English by a complainant fluent in another scheduled language serves the grievance worse than a precise complaint in the language of the events. Names of persons, institutions and places should be spelled consistently throughout, and figures and dates written with particular care, since those are the elements most easily garbled between scripts. The acknowledgment and later correspondence return through the Commission's own machinery, and the wideness of the language rule exists so that a complaint from a district, in the district's language, reaches the national body without a paid intermediary standing between the victim and the forum.

### Q: What happens after a complaint is filed with the NHRC?

The Commission first acknowledges the complaint and assigns it a diary or case number, which the complainant should quote in all later correspondence. The complaint then faces threshold screening, and complaints that are vague, frivolous, anonymous, concerned with matters pending before a court or another commission, or concerned with events more than a year old are ordinarily dismissed at that stage. If the complaint is taken up, the Commission ordinarily proceeds under Section 17 by calling for information or a report from the government or authority complained against, within a time it specifies. If no report arrives, the Commission may inquire into the complaint on its own, using the civil court powers in Section 13 and the investigation machinery in Section 14 where the case needs them. If the report satisfies the Commission that no further inquiry is required, or that the required action has been taken, it may close the complaint and must inform the complainant. Otherwise the inquiry proceeds to the steps in Section 18: recommendations, a copy of the report to the petitioner, the government's comments within one month, and publication.

### Q: Can the commission recommend interim relief before its inquiry ends?

Yes. Section 18 expressly separates interim relief from the final findings, providing that the Commission may recommend to the concerned Government or authority, at any stage of the inquiry, the grant of immediate interim relief to the victim or the members of the victim's family as it considers necessary. The power exists because the situations behind these complaints do not wait for a report: a family that has lost its earning member to a custodial death, or a victim carrying injuries that need treatment, faces costs in the weeks when the inquiry has barely begun, and relief that arrives only with the final report arrives after the need has done its damage. Interim relief remains a recommendation, so payment depends on the government's action rather than on any power of the Commission to compel it, and it is not a prejudgment of the final outcome, since responsibility is allocated only when the inquiry closes. A complainant with urgent need should ask for interim relief expressly in the complaint and state the need with figures and papers, rather than assuming the Commission will raise the point unaided.

### Q: Can the NHRC act when the violation is by a private person rather than a public servant?

Only where a public failure is part of the story. The inquiry ground in Section 12(a) is a complaint of violation of human rights or abetment of such violation, or of negligence in the prevention of such violation by a public servant. A purely private wrong, a neighbour seizing land, an assault by a private person with no official involvement, belongs to the civil courts and the criminal process, and the definition in Section 2(d) confines the Commission to rights guaranteed by the Constitution or embodied in the International Covenants and enforceable by courts in India. The position changes when the complaint is aimed at the state's part in a private wrong: police who refuse to register a case, officers who stand by during violence they were warned of, or an administration that denies a class of persons the protection it gives others. That complaint concerns negligence in prevention or a denial of equality by public servants, and it sits within the Commission's remit. The drafting lesson is to plead the public failure, with dates and warnings given, rather than to repackage a private dispute.

### Q: Should you go to the commission first or file a writ petition directly?

The answer turns on what the grievance needs and how much of the year remains. The commission route suits a grievance whose first need is an official record: the inquiry is free, the Commission can call for the state's papers and test the state's account, and its findings can then support every later step. It is the weaker instrument where liberty is immediately at stake, because a person in detention needs a habeas corpus petition that can end the detention, not a recommendation that leaves custody untouched, and it is the wrong first step where binding interim protection is required. The writ route is also the necessary route where the year under Section 36(2) has already expired, because the commissions cannot inquire at all while the courts weigh delay instead of applying a bar. The routes are not exclusive in sequence: a commission complaint and a writ petition answer different questions, and a commission inquiry does not have to conclude before a court is approached. What must never be duplicated is the same matter before two commissions, which Section 36(1) forbids. Where time is short, file in time first and choose forums in the order the deadlines impose.

### Q: Can the commission inquire into a case that is already pending in court?

The statutory bar is aimed elsewhere: Section 36(1) forbids inquiry into a matter pending before a State Commission or another commission duly constituted, not a matter pending before a court. Even so, the Commission does not run a parallel inquiry into the very questions a court is trying, and complaints raising matters under judicial consideration are ordinarily not entertained, because two forums reaching findings on identical facts would damage both proceedings. The Act instead builds a bridge between the two. Under Section 12(b) the Commission may intervene in any proceeding involving an allegation of violation of human rights pending before a court, with the approval of that court, and in that role it places the human rights dimension of the case before the court as an institutional voice rather than as a rival fact finder. A complainant with a case in court should disclose the pending proceeding in any complaint, and should understand the realistic options: the Commission may decline to inquire while the court is seized, may watch the matter, or may seek to intervene where its presence adds something the parties cannot supply.

### Q: Does every state have a State Human Rights Commission?

No. Section 21 of the Act says that a State Government may constitute a body to be known as the State Human Rights Commission for that state, and the permissive word does real work, because the state tier exists only where a state has created it and kept it constituted. The record of the state tier is uneven: states have gone long stretches with commissions unconstituted, with chairperson and member posts vacant, or with bodies that exist on paper and sit rarely, and the 2006 amendment, which reduced the State Commission to three members and allowed two or more states to share a chairperson or a member, was in part a response to the difficulty states found in staffing the larger body. The practical consequence for a complainant is direct. Before filing, establish whether the state in question has a functioning commission; where it does not, or where the body cannot carry an inquiry forward, the complaint belongs with the National Commission, which can inquire across the country. The one-year bar under Section 36(2) runs while the question is being researched, so the check should be made quickly and the filing made inside the year.

### Q: What does the commission do when someone dies in police custody?

A custodial death sets machinery in motion whether or not a family complains. The Commission requires a death in police custody or judicial custody to be reported to it by the district authorities within twenty-four hours, followed by the post-mortem report, the magisterial inquiry report and the record of the videographed post-mortem examination, and it treats every custodial death as one for which the state must account. On that file the Commission may call for further reports, hold an inquiry using its civil court powers, and at the end recommend compensation to the family, prosecution or other action against the officers responsible, with interim relief available at any stage. Deaths in police encounters carry a staged variant of the same discipline under the Commission's guidelines, first issued in 1997 and revised in 2010: a report of the death to the Commission by the district Superintendent of Police within forty-eight hours and a second report within three months carrying the post-mortem report, the inquest report and the inquiry findings, requirements the Supreme Court absorbed into binding law in PUCL v State of Maharashtra in 2014. A family complaint joins and strengthens a process that was designed to begin without one.

### Q: Can the NHRC help when the police refuse to register a first information report?

It can help, though registration itself is compelled through the criminal process rather than by the Commission. A refusal to register a first information report, where the information discloses a cognizable offence, is a failure of duty by a public servant, and a complaint to the Commission can set the Section 17 machinery in motion: a report is called for from the police authorities, the refusal has to be explained on an official record, and the Commission may recommend departmental or other suitable action and record a finding that follows the officers concerned. That pressure and that record are real assets. But a commission recommendation does not itself register the information, and the complainant who needs the criminal investigation started should also use the remedies the criminal procedure provides, an application to the Superintendent of Police and then to the magistrate, who can direct the police to investigate. The two routes run in parallel without obstructing each other: the magistrate's route starts the case, and the Commission's route answers for the refusal, and each file strengthens the other if the refusal is later examined.
