Citizenship law in India is not one statute, and the first service a guide can perform is to stop a reader from looking for it in one place. The field is a division of labour between a constitutional Part that settled who was a citizen on a single day in 1950 and then handed the subject to Parliament, a citizenship statute that has governed acquisition and loss since 1955, a separate statute book that regulates everyone who is not a citizen, a travel statute that governs the documents a citizen carries out of the country, and one conspicuous absence: no statute at all for the person who has fled a home country and cannot return to it. A reader who understands that division can answer the question this field actually turns on, which is never simply whether a person is a citizen, but which statute answers which question about that person, before which authority, and on what evidence.

The guide that follows is the map of that field rather than the territory of any one part of it. The Citizenship Act, 1955 is treated here at the level of its architecture: its modes of acquisition and loss, its special provision for Assam, its overseas cardholder scheme and its register provision. The section-by-section depth belongs to the pillar article of this cluster, [the article on the Citizenship Act, 1955](/2021/09/15/citizenship-act-1955-explained/), and the amendment history belongs to the trail article that follows it. The position of the person who is not a citizen is treated here through the succession of foreigners legislation that ended in a consolidating Act of 2025, whose own detailed treatment sits with the article on the foreigners statute. The amendment of 2019, which inserted a religion-referenced proviso into the definition of an illegal migrant and a fast track into the naturalisation schedule, is stated here at headline level only, because the controversy it generated is owned by the debate article of this cluster and a map that rehearsed the whole argument would fail at its own task. What this guide claims for itself is the orientation: the constitutional settlement, the statutory division, the proof problem, the register, and the gap where a refugee statute would otherwise stand.

## The shape of the field: three statute books and one absence

Indian law sorts every person physically present in the country into one of two legal classes, citizen and foreigner, and it maintains a different statute book for each class. For the citizen, and for the person who claims to be one, the governing instrument is the Citizenship Act, 1955, Act 57 of 1955, made by Parliament under the power the Constitution conferred in Article 11. That Act states how citizenship is acquired, how it continues, how it is lost, what an overseas cardholder is, and how a register of citizens may be prepared. For the foreigner, the governing instruments were for most of the period since independence a cluster of older enactments headed by the Foreigners Act, 1946, and they answer a different set of questions: entry, registration, presence, movement, and removal. Between the two statute books there is no third. A person who is not a citizen and who says that return to a home country would expose that person to persecution is processed through the foreigners book, softened or hardened by executive decision in the individual case or for the group, because Parliament has enacted no protection statute under which such a person could claim a status as an entitlement.

The distinction matters in practice because the two books allocate power differently. The citizenship book is rule-bound in form: it names modes of acquisition, conditions, authorities and certificates, and a dispute under it is a dispute about whether a statutory route was satisfied. The foreigners book is power-conferring in form: it arms the Central Government with wide discretionary authority over the presence of a non-citizen, and the courts have historically reviewed the exercise of that authority with restraint, because the Constitution gives a foreigner the protection of Article 21 but not the freedoms of movement, residence and settlement that Article 19 reserves to citizens. A single human situation can therefore pass through both books in sequence. A person whose claim to citizenship fails is, by the failure itself, remitted to the foreigners book, and the consequences that follow, including detention and removal, are consequences of the second book rather than the first. Much public confusion in this field comes from attributing to the citizenship statute outcomes that belong to the foreigners statute, and the reverse.

There is also a third, smaller book that readers habitually confuse with the first two: the law of documents. The Passports Act, 1967 governs the issue, refusal, impounding and revocation of the travel documents through which a citizen leaves and re-enters India. Aadhaar, the biometric identity number administered under the Aadhaar Act, 2016, is an identity infrastructure for residents, and the authority that administers it has stated repeatedly that it is not proof of citizenship. Birth certificates, electoral rolls, land records and school records each prove the fact they record, and none of them, alone, confers or conclusively establishes the status. The status is established by the statutory route, and documents are evidence tendered in support of a route. That single proposition, developed in the proof section of this guide, resolves a large share of the difficulty that people encounter when a status question is raised about them or about a parent or grandparent whose papers are incomplete.

## Part II of the Constitution: a settlement for one day

Part II of the Constitution, Articles 5 to 11, is the foundation of the field, and its most important characteristic is that it was designed to be temporary. The framers faced a population in violent motion: Partition had displaced millions across the new borders with Pakistan, the princely states were acceding, and a constitution that postponed the citizenship question would have left the first electoral rolls, the first legislatures and the first public services without a defined people. Part II therefore determined, exhaustively and for one moment only, who was a citizen at the commencement of the Constitution on 26 January 1950. Everything after that moment it left to Parliament. The articles are best read as a set of answers to the situations Partition and the princely settlement had created, followed by a deliberate constitutional exit.

Article 5 states the general rule for commencement: a person who had a domicile in the territory of India and who satisfied any one of three conditions, birth in the territory, birth of either parent in the territory, or ordinary residence in the territory for not less than five years immediately before commencement, was a citizen. Domicile does quiet work in that sentence, because it ties the founding citizenship to a settled home rather than to presence alone, and it explains why the citizenship statute and the private international law of domicile continued to matter to each other long after 1950. Article 6 then deals with the person who had migrated to India from the territory that became Pakistan. Such a person was deemed a citizen at commencement if a parent or grandparent was born in India as defined in the Government of India Act, 1935, and if the migration and residence conditions were met: residence in India since the date of migration for those who came before 19 July 1948, and, for those who came on or after that date, registration as a citizen by an officer appointed for the purpose, on application made before commencement and after six months of residence. The date of 19 July 1948 is the date on which the permit system for entry from Pakistan was introduced, and the article builds the permit system into the constitutional rule itself.

Article 7 answers the mirror question, the person who migrated from India to Pakistan after 1 March 1947. Such a person was not deemed a citizen notwithstanding Articles 5 and 6, unless that person returned to India under a permit for resettlement or permanent return issued by or under the authority of law, in which case the registration machinery of Article 6 applied with the six months of residence counted from the return. Article 8 reaches outward, to the person of Indian origin residing outside India: a person whose own birth, or a parent's or grandparent's birth, was in India as defined in the 1935 Act, and who was ordinarily resident in another country, could be deemed a citizen if registered by the diplomatic or consular representative of India in that country, on an application in the prescribed form made before or after commencement. Article 9 states the exclusion that still structures the whole field: no person is a citizen under Articles 5, 6 or 8 if that person has voluntarily acquired the citizenship of a foreign State. Article 10 provides that every person who is or is deemed to be a citizen under Part II continues to be a citizen, subject to the provisions of any law Parliament may make. Article 11 then completes the design by providing that nothing in Part II derogates from the power of Parliament to make any provision with respect to the acquisition and termination of citizenship and all other matters relating to citizenship.

Three consequences follow from that architecture, and each of them runs through the modern law. The first is that the constitutional provisions are transitional in the strict sense: they fix a starting population and then fall silent, so that a person whose status is questioned in any later decade is answered not by Part II but by the Citizenship Act, 1955 and its amendments, applied to the facts of birth, descent, residence and registration. The second is that Parliament's power under Article 11 is plenary within its field: the Supreme Court has treated the acquisition and termination of citizenship as a subject on which the legislature's word governs, which is why the great contests in this field are contests about the validity or construction of statutory provisions rather than about a freestanding constitutional definition of citizenship after 1950. The third is that Article 9's exclusion, carried forward into Section 9 of the 1955 Act, makes Indian citizenship exclusive in a way that many legal systems do not: the voluntary acquisition of a foreign citizenship terminates the Indian citizenship by operation of the statute, and the question whether that has happened is, under Section 9(2), a question for the Central Government to decide, on which the courts have accorded the executive determination a distinctive weight.

## One citizenship for a federal Union

India has a federal Constitution and a unitary citizenship. A person is a citizen of India or is not; there is no citizenship of Maharashtra or of Assam or of any other State, no State-level naturalisation, no State passport, and no State power to add to or subtract from the class of citizens. The point is sometimes obscured because States administer much of the machinery through which citizenship facts are recorded, because domicile within a State carries consequences for education and public employment preferences, and because one provision of the citizenship statute, Section 6A, applies to one State alone. None of that creates a second tier of citizenship. The register machinery, the enrolment authorities and the appellate authorities under the Citizenship Act are statutory authorities exercising a Union subject, citizenship being Entry 17 of the Union List in the Seventh Schedule, which covers citizenship, naturalisation and aliens together and places the whole field, status and foreigner alike, within Parliament's exclusive legislative competence.

### Does India recognise statelessness as a legal category?

No Indian statute creates a status of statelessness, defines a stateless person, or provides a determination procedure for one. A person in India whom no State recognises as a national is handled under the law applicable to foreigners, and any protection received comes from executive decision or from a court's enforcement of Article 21 in the individual case.

The single citizenship design was a deliberate departure from the federal model of the United States, where state citizenship exists alongside national citizenship, and the Constituent Assembly adopted it as part of a broader choice in favour of a strong Centre in the years after Partition. Its practical consequences are easy to underestimate. Movement between States never raises a citizenship question. A change of State residence changes domicile-related entitlements and documentary footprints, which is one reason the proof of citizenship so often turns on reconstructing a family's movements across States through school, land and electoral records, but it never changes the status itself. And when a State government conducts or proposes an exercise touching the identification of foreigners, the legal instruments it acts under are Union instruments, whether the foreigners legislation or the register provision of the citizenship statute, because no State possesses a citizenship power of its own to exercise. The same exclusivity explains why the citizenship consequences of State reorganisation are handled where they are: when States are divided or merged under Articles 3 and 4, no citizenship question arises at all, because the people of the divided State carry the one national citizenship through every redrawing of the internal map, and the reorganisation Acts therefore say nothing about status while saying a great deal about assets, services and legislatures. It is a silence that only a unitary citizenship makes possible.

## The Citizenship Act, 1955: the operative code

The statute that answers almost every citizenship question is the Citizenship Act, 1955, Act 57 of 1955, which received assent and commenced at the end of December 1955 and which Parliament has amended repeatedly since, most consequentially in 1986, in 2003 and in 2019. Its structure is a code in miniature. Sections 3 to 7 state the modes by which citizenship is acquired: by birth, by descent, by registration, by naturalisation, and by incorporation of territory. Section 6A, inserted in 1985, is a self-contained code for one class of migrants in one State. Sections 7A to 7D, inserted by the amendment of 2003 with effect from 3 December 2004, create and regulate the overseas citizen cardholder scheme. Sections 8, 9 and 10 state the three modes by which citizenship ends: renunciation, termination and deprivation. Section 13 provides for a certificate of citizenship in case of doubt. Section 14A, also inserted in 2003, empowers the Central Government to maintain a National Register of Indian Citizens and to issue national identity cards, and the Citizenship Rules, 2009 supply the procedural code beneath the Act, replacing the rules of 1956. The detailed exposition of each of those provisions, the schedules that condition naturalisation, and the case law on deprivation and on the burden in status disputes, is the work of the pillar article in this cluster, and this guide deliberately keeps to the level at which a reader can see how the parts connect.

Two features of the code deserve emphasis at map level because they organise everything else. The first is that the Act is exhaustive of its subject in the courts' reading of it: citizenship after commencement is acquired only in the modes the Act provides, and a claim that rests on equity, long residence alone, or possession of documents without a statutory route behind them does not succeed as a citizenship claim, however sympathetic the facts. Long residence matters enormously within the Act, because registration and naturalisation are built on residence qualifications, but it operates through those sections and not around them. The second feature is the Act's asymmetry between entry and exit. Acquisition is elaborately conditioned, with different rules for different classes of applicant and different evidentiary demands at different dates, while the termination provision of Section 9 operates automatically on the fact of voluntary foreign naturalisation, without any order needing to be made against the person. A field in which entry is procedural and exit is automatic produces a distinctive kind of dispute, about facts and dates rather than about discretion, and that is the character of most citizenship litigation.

### Can a person be a citizen of India without applying for it?

Yes, and most citizens are. Citizenship by birth under Section 3 and by descent under Section 4 operates by force of the statute on the facts of birth and parentage, without any application, order or certificate. Registration and naturalisation, by contrast, are granted on application, and for the citizen by birth or descent the status precedes every document.

The Act also allocates the deciding roles, and a reader of the field should hold the allocation clearly. Applications for registration and naturalisation are made to the prescribed authority under the Rules and are decided within a structure in which district-level authorities collect and verify, State governments transmit and recommend, and the Central Government, through the Ministry of Home Affairs, is the granting authority for the classes that matter most. The question whether a person has voluntarily acquired a foreign citizenship, which determines termination under Section 9, is reserved to the Central Government by Section 9(2), and the courts have treated that reservation as deliberate: in Bhagwati Prasad Dixit v Rajeev Gandhi and in the line of authority following the Constitution Bench's approach to Section 9, the determination of that particular fact is an executive determination, examinable on judicial review for legality but not re-decided by a court as an original question. Deprivation under Section 10, by contrast, is an order of the Central Government made only on stated grounds, with a right in most cases to have the grounds examined by a committee of inquiry. The map of authorities is therefore not uniform, and part of reading this field well is knowing which of its questions are judicial, which are executive, and which the statute answers by itself.

## Acquisition in outline: five doors, narrowing over time

The five modes of acquisition in Sections 3 to 7 are not five versions of one policy. Each embodies a different theory of belonging, and the history of the Act is largely the history of Parliament tightening the first of them. Citizenship by birth, Section 3, is the territorial theory: the soil confers the status. Citizenship by descent, Section 4, is the bloodline theory: the status descends from a citizen parent to a child born abroad. Registration, Section 5, is the relational theory: defined classes of persons with a stated connection to India, persons of Indian origin resident in India, spouses of citizens, minor children of citizens and others, may be registered on application after satisfying residence conditions. Naturalisation, Section 6 read with the Third Schedule, is the earned theory: a foreigner of full age who satisfies residence, character, language and intention qualifications, and who renounces any other citizenship, may be granted a certificate. Incorporation of territory, Section 7, is the sovereign theory: when territory becomes part of India, the Central Government specifies by order the persons who become citizens by reason of their connection with that territory, as was done for Goa, Daman and Diu, for Pondicherry and for Sikkim.

Citizenship by birth is the mode whose contraction defines the modern Act, and the guide states the three eras here because the pillar and trail articles build on them. As enacted in 1955, Section 3 conferred citizenship on every person born in India on or after 26 January 1950, subject to narrow exceptions for children of enemy aliens and of foreign diplomats. The Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 1986, with effect from 1 July 1987, required in addition that either parent be a citizen of India at the time of the birth. The Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2003, with effect from 3 December 2004, tightened the rule again: a person born in India is a citizen by birth only if both parents are citizens, or if one parent is a citizen and the other is not an illegal migrant at the time of the birth. The direction of travel is unmistakable, from a rule of soil to a rule of soil filtered through parentage and then through the legality of a parent's presence, and it means that the answer to whether a person is a citizen by birth depends on the date of birth in a way that surprises readers who assume a single timeless rule. The amendment trail article of this cluster takes each of those steps in turn.

Descent moved in the opposite direction in one respect. As enacted, Section 4 transmitted citizenship through the father; the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 1992 made the transmission gender-neutral, so that a child born abroad to either a citizen mother or a citizen father qualifies. The amendment of 2003 then attached a registration condition to births abroad occurring on or after 3 December 2004, requiring the birth to be registered at an Indian consulate within one year, or later with permission, together with a declaration that the child does not hold a foreign passport, a condition designed to prevent the quiet accumulation of a second nationality in tension with Article 9 and Section 9. Registration and naturalisation remain the discretionary doors. A certificate of registration or naturalisation is a grant, not an entitlement, and the Act clothes the granting authority with a discretion that the courts supervise for legality and fairness of process rather than by substitution of their own view of the merits. The naturalisation qualifications in the Third Schedule, twelve years of residence or service in the pattern the Schedule prescribes, good character, an adequate knowledge of a language in the Eighth Schedule, and the intention to reside or serve, were the qualifications into which the amendment of 2019 cut its shorter track for a defined class, a change this guide records and the debate article examines.

## Loss in outline: renunciation, termination, deprivation

The Act provides three doors out, and they differ in whose act closes them. Renunciation under Section 8 is the citizen's own act: a citizen of full age and capacity may make a declaration renouncing Indian citizenship, which takes effect on registration of the declaration, and the provision carries a family consequence in that a minor child of the person renouncing also ceases to be a citizen, with a right to resume on attaining majority by declaration within the period the section allows. Termination under Section 9 is the statute's own act: a citizen who voluntarily acquires the citizenship of another country thereupon ceases to be an Indian citizen, automatically and without any order, and the only adjudication the provision contemplates is the Section 9(2) determination by the Central Government of the anterior question whether the foreign citizenship was in fact acquired and acquired voluntarily. Deprivation under Section 10 is the State's act: the Central Government may by order deprive a citizen of the citizenship, but only a citizen who holds it by registration or naturalisation, and only on grounds the section enumerates, that the certificate was obtained by fraud or false representation, that the person has shown disloyalty toward the Constitution, that the person has traded or communicated with an enemy in wartime, that the person has been sentenced in any country to imprisonment of not less than two years within five years of the grant, or that the person has been ordinarily resident outside India for seven years continuously without the registrations or service the section recognises. For most grounds the order may not be made unless the Central Government is satisfied that it is not conducive to the public good that the person continue as a citizen, and the person affected is entitled, except where the government directs otherwise for stated reasons, to an inquiry by a committee presided over by a person who has held judicial office.

The distribution of those three doors is itself a statement about the field. The citizen by birth can leave through the first two doors but not the third: deprivation does not reach birth or descent citizenship, a limitation that marks the difference in the Act's eyes between a status that the statute conferred by operation of law and a status that the executive granted on an application it was entitled to scrutinise. The dual nationality question, which the seeded questions of this guide address, is answered by the second door. India does not permit a citizen to hold a foreign citizenship concurrently, and the overseas cardholder scheme, examined below, exists precisely because that prohibition bites on a very large diaspora: the scheme offers a bundle of rights to former citizens and their descendants without crossing the constitutional line that Article 9 drew in 1950.

## Section 6A and the Assam settlement

One provision of the Act applies to a single State, and it is the provision most often misunderstood outside the cluster that owns it. Section 6A was inserted by the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 1985, Act 65 of 1985, to give statutory effect to the Assam Accord of 15 August 1985, the memorandum of settlement that ended the six-year Assam agitation over the detection of foreigners whose names had entered the electoral rolls. The section creates date bands for persons of Indian origin who entered Assam from the specified territory, meaning Bangladesh, before 1 January 1966, between 1 January 1966 and 25 March 1971, and on or after 25 March 1971. The first band is deemed to be citizens. The second band is liable, on detection as foreigners through the prescribed process, to registration and to a defined disability, deletion from the electoral rolls for ten years, after which citizenship follows. The third band falls to be dealt with under the foreigners law. The date of 25 March 1971 is the commencement of the Bangladesh Liberation War, and the bands encode the Accord's political bargain: regularisation for the earlier movement, a waiting period for the middle movement, and no regularisation after the war began.

Section 6A matters to this guide for two structural reasons. The first is that it demonstrates the reach of Article 11: Parliament may legislate citizenship differently for a defined territory and a defined class, and the constitutional challenge to that differentiation, which argued that the section violated the equality guarantee and the constitutional protection of Assamese identity, was decided by a Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court on 17 October 2024, which upheld the section by a majority of four to one in the proceedings reported as In Re: Section 6A of the Citizenship Act, 1955. The detailed treatment of the section and of that judgment belongs to the pillar article; the map records only that the provision exists, that it is Assam-specific, and that its validity has been sustained. The second reason is that Section 6A is the statutory neighbourhood of the Assam register of citizens, because the register exercise in Assam proceeded under the register provision and rules of the same Act, supervised by the Supreme Court, and its output fed directly into the detection machinery the Accord contemplated. The register is treated in its own section below.

## The overseas cardholder: a status that is not citizenship

The Overseas Citizen of India cardholder is the figure most often misdescribed in this field, usually by being called a dual citizen. The scheme, created by Sections 7A to 7D inserted by the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2003 with effect from 3 December 2004, confers a registered status on a foreign citizen who was eligible to become a citizen of India at the commencement of the Constitution, or who belonged to a territory that became part of India after 15 August 1947, or who was a citizen of India on or after 26 January 1950, together with the children, grandchildren and spouses of such persons within the conditions the section prescribes, and it excludes persons who are or were citizens of Pakistan or Bangladesh or of such other country as the Central Government notifies. The cardholder is not a citizen. The cardholder has no vote, is not eligible for constitutional offices or for appointment to public services in parity with citizens, and holds instead a defined bundle of statutory privileges: a multiple-entry, multipurpose lifelong visa for visiting India, exemption from the registration and reporting requirements that the foreigners law imposes, and parity with non-resident Indians in financial, economic and educational fields, with a stated exception for the acquisition of agricultural and plantation properties. In 2015 the parallel Person of Indian Origin card scheme was merged into the OCI scheme, so that a single card carries the whole of this halfway status.

### Is there an Indian statute that defines who is not a citizen?

There is no definition section that lists non-citizens as a class. The law proceeds by inversion: the Citizenship Act, 1955 defines the citizen and the modes of acquiring that status, and the foreigners legislation then defines a foreigner residually, as a person who is not a citizen of India. Every other status is defined against that single division.

The scheme repays attention in a guide of this kind because it is the field's clearest example of a distinction that public discussion persistently blurs in both directions. To the diaspora it is sometimes sold as citizenship in all but name, which it is not, because the political rights and the security of tenure of citizenship do not attach to it: an OCI registration may be cancelled under Section 7D on grounds that include fraud, disaffection toward the Constitution, and a sentence of imprisonment of two years or more, a cancellation power that has no equivalent for the citizen by birth, whose status Section 10 cannot reach. To critics it is sometimes described as a mere visa, which understates it, because the lifelong visa and the parity provisions give the cardholder a durable, inheritable-in-effect connection that no visa regime creates. The accurate statement is the statutory one: it is a status created by the citizenship statute, held by a foreign citizen, terminable on stated grounds, and valuable precisely because it is less than citizenship and more than permission.

## The second statute book: the person who is not a citizen

For the person who is not a citizen, Indian law was organised for most of the period since independence by the Foreigners Act, 1946, Act 31 of 1946, an enactment of the colonial legislature that the Republic retained and that the consolidating legislation of 2025 has at last replaced. The 1946 Act was short and sweeping. Section 3 empowered the Central Government to make orders prohibiting, regulating or restricting the entry, presence and departure of foreigners, generally or by class, and the orders made under it built the entire administrative apparatus of protected and restricted areas, registration, and reporting. Section 9 placed the burden of proving that a person is not a foreigner on the person himself, a reversal of the ordinary burden that the Supreme Court sustained and that still explains the procedural character of status proceedings. Around the 1946 Act stood the Passport (Entry into India) Act, 1920, the Registration of Foreigners Act, 1939 and the Immigration (Carriers' Liability) Act, 2000, each governing a fragment of the same subject: documents for entry, registration of presence, and the liability of the carrier who brings a person without them.

That four-statute patchwork is no longer the law, and a guide that presented it as operative would mislead its reader. The Immigration and Foreigners Act, 2025, Act 13 of 2025, which received the assent of the President on 4 April 2025 and was brought into force on 1 September 2025, repealed all four enactments and consolidated their subject matter in a single statute of six chapters and thirty-six sections, with a Bureau of Immigration at its administrative centre and reporting obligations laid on carriers, keepers of accommodation, educational institutions and medical institutions. The detailed treatment of that succession, of what the new Act carried forward from the old powers and what it changed, belongs to the article in this cluster that owns the foreigners statute, [the article on the foreigners legislation](/2021/11/01/foreigners-act-1946-india-explained/); the map records the essential orientation, which is that a reader asking which law governs a foreigner in India must be answered with the Act of 2025 for the operative law, and with the Act of 1946 for the long period during which the case law, the tribunal jurisprudence and the administrative practice of the field were formed.

Two features of the second book survive every consolidation and belong in any guide. The first is the breadth of the executive power over presence. From 1946 to 2025 the form has been constant: the statute confers the power, orders and rules particularise it, and the individual's entitlements are those the Constitution gives to every person rather than those it gives to citizens. The second is the institution of the Foreigners Tribunal in Assam, constituted under the Foreigners (Tribunals) Order, 1964 made under the 1946 Act, which decides whether a person referred to it is a foreigner, in proceedings in which the burden lies on the person proceeded against. The tribunals are an Assam institution, born of that State's particular history with migration and of the repeal in 2005, in Sarbananda Sonowal v Union of India, of the gentler Illegal Migrants (Determination by Tribunals) Act, 1983 which the Supreme Court struck down as unconstitutional. Their workings belong to the foreigners article; their place on the map is at the junction of the two books, because a tribunal declaration is the point at which a failed citizenship claim converts into detention and removal consequences under the foreigners book.

### What separates the statute book for citizens from the statute book for foreigners?

The citizenship book confers and regulates a status, and its characteristic question is whether a statutory mode was satisfied on given facts. The foreigners book confers powers on the executive over persons admitted not to hold that status, and its question is whether an order was lawfully made. One is a code of entitlement; the other, of control.

## Travel documents: the citizen leaving and returning

The third book is the smallest. The Passports Act, 1967, Act 15 of 1967, governs the issue of passports and travel documents, the classes of passport, the grounds on which an application may be refused, and the variation, impounding and revocation of documents issued. It replaced an ordinance of 1967 and answered the Supreme Court's decision in Satwant Singh Sawhney v D. Ramarathnam, which had held that the right to travel abroad fell within the personal liberty of Article 21 and that a law was needed to regulate its restriction, a holding the Court built on a decade later in Maneka Gandhi v Union of India when it sustained the impounding power of the Act while reading a hearing into its exercise. For the map, the Act's significance is its position at the exit door of the citizenship field: the [article on the Passports Act, 1967](/2021/11/15/passports-act-1967-explained/) carries its detail, and what a guide needs to fix is that a passport is a document the State issues to a person it treats as its citizen for travel purposes, and that the issue, refusal or impounding of the document is an administrative act under the 1967 Act, distinct from the determination of the status itself under the 1955 Act. The two determinations usually travel together, and the cases in which they separate, a passport impounded while citizenship is undisputed, or citizenship disputed while a passport subsists, are the cases that teach the distinction.

## Proof of citizenship: the statute, not the document

The question the public asks most often in this field is which document proves citizenship, and the honest answer restructures the question. No document is named by the Citizenship Act as conclusive proof of the status, and the consistent position of the courts is that citizenship is determined by the statute: a person establishes citizenship by establishing the statutory basis on which it was acquired, by birth under Section 3 on the facts and dates the section prescribes, by descent under Section 4, or by a certificate of registration or naturalisation under Sections 5 and 6, whose evidentiary position is the strongest the Act knows. Documents enter as evidence of those facts. A birth certificate evidences birth and parentage as registered. A passport is treated by the courts as strong evidence, and the Bombay High Court in a line of cases has accepted a valid Indian passport as proof of citizenship for the purposes before it, while other courts, including the Gauhati High Court in the tribunal context, have held that no single document, passport included, is by itself conclusive. Electoral rolls evidence enrolment at a place and date, and enrolment itself presupposes citizenship under Article 326, which is why a name on an old roll carries weight as a supporting fact without being the status itself.

The identity documents sentence of this field is the one at which the [article on the Aadhaar Act, 2016](/2019/05/01/aadhaar-act-2016-explained/) becomes relevant, because Aadhaar is the document most often tendered and most often misunderstood. Aadhaar is a number issued to a resident, and residence for its purposes is a physical-presence test, not a citizenship test; the Unique Identification Authority of India has stated in terms that Aadhaar is not a proof of citizenship, and the Supreme Court's Aadhaar judgment proceeded on the same understanding. A person assisting a family member through a status question builds the file differently: the statutory route is identified first, and the papers are then assembled as evidence of the facts that route requires, birth and parentage records, older identity and electoral documents, land and school records, and any registration or naturalisation certificate in the family's possession. Because such papers are often held as scanned images and office files rather than as originals, a practical step in assembling the file is to open and check each scanned record for legibility before it is tendered, for which [a browser-based viewer for office and presentation files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) serves the purpose without installing software. The file is the evidentiary foundation on which everything else in a status proceeding rests, and its assembly is clerical work with legal consequences.

### Who issues certificates of registration and naturalisation in India?

Certificates under Sections 5 and 6 are granted by the Central Government under the Citizenship Act, 1955, through the machinery the Citizenship Rules, 2009 prescribe: applications are filed before the district-level collector of the place of residence, verified and forwarded through the State government, and decided at the Centre. The oath of allegiance in the Second Schedule completes the grant.

The burden in these matters completes the picture, and it is the point at which the two statute books interlock most tightly. In proceedings under the foreigners legislation, Section 9 of the 1946 Act placed the burden of proving that he is not a foreigner on the person alleged to be one, and the tribunals and courts applied that allocation for the whole life of the Act; the Supreme Court in Sarbananda Sonowal treated the burden provision as one of the features that made the foreigners scheme workable where the IMDT Act's contrary allocation had made it unworkable. Within the citizenship book, a person claiming a status by registration or naturalisation must satisfy the granting authority, and a person whose citizenship is questioned by reference to Section 9 must face a determination by the Central Government under Section 9(2) on the question of foreign acquisition. A guide states the allocation without softening it, because a reader who does not know where the burden lies does not yet understand the field: the State confers the status by statute and grant, but when the status is put in issue under the foreigners book, the person must prove it.

## The register of citizens: a statutory power exercised once

The National Register of Citizens rests on Section 14A of the Citizenship Act, inserted by the amendment of 2003, which empowers the Central Government to maintain a National Register of Indian Citizens, to establish a National Registration Authority, and to issue national identity cards to registered citizens. The Citizenship (Registration of Citizens and Issue of National Identity Cards) Rules, 2003, made under that provision, prescribe a house-to-house enumeration through local, tehsil, district and State registers rising to the national register, and provide a claims and objections process. Two things about that scheme must be held together. Its statutory basis is general and national: the power to prepare a register for the whole country exists on the face of the Act and the Rules. And its exercise has been singular: a register of citizens has been prepared for one State, Assam, under a process supervised by the Supreme Court and connected with Section 6A, and no corresponding national exercise has been carried out or completed. The nationwide National Population Register, prepared under the same 2003 Rules as a register of usual residents in connection with the Census, is a different instrument from a register of citizens, and the difference between a register of residents and a register of citizens is the difference between counting who lives in a place and adjudging who belongs to the country.

The Assam exercise shows what the register machinery involves when it is run to completion. The updating of the National Register of Citizens for Assam proceeded on an application model: residents applied with documents linking themselves to a person recorded in the legacy data, principally the 1951 register for Assam and the electoral rolls up to the midnight of 24 March 1971, the Section 6A date, and claims were verified against family trees and field checks, with claims and objections heard before publication. The final register was published on 31 August 2019, and the figures published with it recorded about 3.3 crore applicants and the exclusion of about 19 lakh of them, figures attributable to the office of the State Coordinator for the exercise. Exclusion from the register was not itself a declaration of foreigner status: the excluded person retained the route of appeal to a Foreigners Tribunal, and the legal position of the register's output, its finality and its consequences remained matters under consideration after publication, including the question of a re-verification sought by the State. A guide describes that position structurally and stops there, because the register's significance for the field does not depend on its Assam numbers. Its significance is that Parliament has placed on the statute book a complete machinery for converting the question of citizenship from a case-by-case inquiry into a population-wide documentary adjudication, and that the machinery has been operated once, in one State, under judicial supervision, for reasons rooted in that State's own provision of the Act.

## The missing statute: refugees and administrative protection

The structural finding of this guide is the absence at the centre of the field. India is not a party to the Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees of 1951 or to its Protocol of 1967, and Parliament has enacted no statute defining a refugee, conferring a protection status, or prescribing a determination procedure for a person who claims one. The consequence is not that India has received no refugees; the consequence is that reception has been an act of executive policy rather than of legal entitlement. Groups who arrived in large numbers across the decades, Tibetans from 1959, East Pakistanis in 1971, Chakmas and Hajongs from the Chittagong Hill Tracts, Sri Lankan Tamils from 1983, Afghans in successive waves, were each dealt with by a decision of the government of the day, under arrangements that differed group by group in documentation, camps, assistance and freedom of movement, and none of them under a statutory status that any individual in the group could claim as an entitlement. For asylum seekers from countries that do not share a border with India, the office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees in New Delhi conducts refugee status determination under its own mandate and issues documentation to those it recognises, and the Government of India tolerates and in practice respects much of that documentation, but the UNHCR has no statutory recognition in Indian law and its certificates are not a status under any Indian enactment. Protection in India is therefore administrative rather than legal, and the field's central debates take the shape they do because the protection question has to be argued through the citizenship statute and the foreigners statute, the only two books there are.

The courts have supplied the floor beneath that administrative structure, and the floor is Article 21. A foreigner in India is entitled to the protection of the right to life and personal liberty, though not to the rights the Constitution confers on citizens alone. The Supreme Court stated the division in Louis De Raedt v Union of India in 1991, holding that a foreigner enjoys Article 21 but cannot claim the Article 19(1)(e) right to reside and settle, so that the power to expel is not defeated by the liberty guarantee. In National Human Rights Commission v State of Arunachal Pradesh in 1996, the Court held the State bound to protect the life and liberty of every human being, citizen or otherwise, and restrained the threatened forcible expulsion of the Chakma residents of the State while their citizenship applications, made under Section 5, remained undecided. High Courts have at times read into Article 21 a protection against return to persecution, and at other times have declined to convert that reading into a bar on removal, and the Supreme Court's treatment of deportation challenges has kept the matter within the discipline that the right asserted must be found in the Constitution as it applies to non-citizens. The accurate summary is that the judiciary polices the manner and the constitutional minimum of executive action toward the unprotected person, while the status itself, protected or unprotected, remains an executive gift rather than a legal claim.

## The case for and the case against a refugee statute

The absence of a refugee statute is defended and criticised in India, and this guide states both cases at strength and adjudicates neither. The case for the present position, as it has been put by successive governments and their defenders, rests on the record and on flexibility. India, it is said, has in fact sheltered very large populations for decades without any statute obliging it to do so, which shows that the absence of a legal duty has not meant an absence of protection. Executive discretion has allowed differentiated responses to different movements of people, calibrated to the relationship with the country of origin, to numbers, and to the prospect of return, in a way a determination statute with uniform criteria could not. Accession to the 1951 Convention, framed for a European displacement problem, would expose a country with a very long and porous land border to obligations toward an open-ended future caseload whose scale no government can forecast, and the Convention's framework was not designed for the mixed movements, part flight and part economic migration, that characterise South Asian mobility. On this view the administrative model is not an oversight but a considered posture, maintained by governments of different parties across seven decades.

The case against, as put by jurists and scholars of refugee law, including B. S. Chimni in his writings on the international refugee regime and its treatment of the global South, rests on the character of discretion itself. Discretion without criteria, it is said, produces unequal treatment between groups in materially similar situations, one group documented and assisted, another undocumented and exposed, with the difference explained by foreign policy rather than by any feature of the people's need. A person with no status cannot lawfully work, study or access services on equal terms, and the insecurity is inherited by children born in India to such parents, whose position under Section 3 after 2004 may itself be compromised by a parent's classification as an illegal migrant. A statute would supply what discretion cannot: a definition, criteria, a determination procedure conducted by an identified authority, a reasoned decision, and an appeal. It is part of the record of this argument that a remedy was drafted and never used. An Eminent Persons Group on refugees for South Asia, chaired by Justice P. N. Bhagwati, prepared a Model National Law on Refugees, adopted at a consultation in Dhaka in 1997, which defined a refugee, affirmed non-refoulement, and provided for status determination; the model law was never introduced in Parliament, and neither was any government bill on the subject, so that the proposal exists in the field as a drafted answer to a question the legislature has not taken up. The guide records the two cases and the drafted model, and notes only that the debate's persistence is itself evidence for the structural finding: where no protection statute exists, the argument about protection is conducted through the citizenship and foreigners books, and takes on their categories, their burdens and their limits.

## The procedural code: the Citizenship Rules, 2009

Beneath the Act stand the Citizenship Rules, 2009, which replaced the Citizenship Rules, 1956 and which supply the field's procedural grammar: the forms of application for each section, the authorities to whom applications are made, the fees, the registers to be maintained, the manner of the oath of allegiance in the Second Schedule to the Act, and the procedure for the certificate of citizenship in case of doubt under Section 13. The Rules matter to a map because so much of the Act's operation is delegated to them. The Act states that a person of Indian origin ordinarily resident in India for seven years may apply for registration under Section 5(1)(a); the Rules state where the application is filed, before the collector or district authority of the place of residence, how it is verified, how it travels through the State government to the Central Government, and what the certificate looks like when it issues. The Act states that a naturalisation applicant must satisfy the Third Schedule; the Rules and the Schedule together state the residence computation, the language requirement's proof and the renunciation undertaking that accompanies the application. A practitioner reading the field therefore reads two instruments at once, and a historian of the field reads the Rules' changes, the digitisation of applications, the delegation of specified classes of decision, including the delegation made in 2024 to empowered committees and district authorities for applications under the 2019 amendment, as the administrative history of the statute.

The Rules also carry the register scheme's second half. The Citizenship (Registration of Citizens and Issue of National Identity Cards) Rules, 2003 are a separate instrument from the Rules of 2009, made specifically under Section 14A and Section 18 together, and they prescribe the Population Register and the National Register of Indian Citizens as distinct products of one enumeration architecture, with the Registrar General of India as the National Registration Authority. Keeping the two rule-books apart is a small discipline with large consequences for accuracy, because public discussion routinely attributes the 2003 Rules' register machinery to the ordinary application system, or treats the Population Register prepared alongside the Census of 2011 as if it were a stage of a citizens register. The map keeps them separate: one code for individual applications, one code for population-wide registration, both under the same short Act.

## Territory joining India: Section 7 in practice

Section 7 is the least litigated and most historically revealing of the acquisition modes. When a territory becomes part of India, the Central Government may by order published in the Official Gazette specify the persons who shall be citizens of India by reason of their connection with that territory, and those persons become citizens from the date specified. The power has been exercised at each enlargement of the Union. The Goa, Daman and Diu (Citizenship) Order, 1962, made after the liberation of the Portuguese territories, conferred citizenship on persons born in those territories before 20 December 1961 and on defined classes connected with them, subject to a window within which a person could elect the former nationality instead. The Citizenship (Pondicherry) Order, 1962 followed the treaty of cession with France and dealt with the French citizens of the former establishments, again with an option structure that respected the treaty. The Sikkim Citizenship Order, 1975 attended the merger of the protectorate, converting the subjects of the Chogyal, whose status had been governed by Sikkim's own subject regulation, into Indian citizens with provisions for those who had taken another nationality. Each order is a small negotiated settlement between sovereignty and biography: the State acquires territory, and the order decides, class by class, what happens to the people whose nationality the territory carried. The mode matters to the modern map for one further reason. It is the only acquisition route that operates on a population rather than on applicants, and it is the historical template against which the register idea becomes intelligible: a citizenship order for territory is a one-time, sovereign determination of a whole people's status, whereas a register is the same ambition pursued by enumeration and documents rather than by order.

## Citizens alone: the rights that mark the status

The value of the status, and the reason its determination matters so much to the people it is decided about, is fixed by the constitutional distribution of rights between citizens and persons. Article 14 guarantees equality before the law to any person. Articles 20 and 21 protect every person, citizen or not, against ex post facto criminal law, double jeopardy and self-incrimination, and against deprivation of life and personal liberty except by procedure established by law. Article 25 protects the freedom of religion of all persons. Against that person-wide floor, a defined set of entitlements is reserved to citizens alone: Article 15's protection against discrimination by the State on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex and place of birth; Article 16's equality of opportunity in public employment; Article 19's six freedoms, including movement, residence and settlement throughout the territory and the practice of professions; Articles 29 and 30's cultural and educational rights of sections of citizens and of minorities; and the political rights that the Constitution builds on citizenship, the franchise under Article 326, membership of Parliament and the State legislatures, and eligibility for the offices of President, Vice-President, judge of the Supreme Court and the High Courts, Governor, and Attorney General. Article 16(3) adds the single qualification that Parliament may prescribe residence within a State as a condition for certain State employments, the provision under which domicile-linked preferences survive inside a unitary citizenship.

This distribution explains two otherwise puzzling features of the field. The first is the asymmetry of the stakes in a status proceeding. A determination that a person is not a citizen does not merely deny a passport; it withdraws the Article 19 freedoms and the public employment guarantee, exposes the person to the foreigners book's regime of orders, and in Assam engages the detention and removal machinery, while leaving the Article 21 floor intact, which is why litigation about status is so often argued as litigation about liberty. The second is the position of the overseas cardholder, whose statutory bundle was drafted by selecting from this citizen list the items Parliament was willing to extend, economic and educational parity and freedom of movement for visiting, and withholding the political items entire. The card is best understood as a partial grant from the citizen column of the constitutional table, made by statute to a class of foreign citizens, and revocable in a way that nothing in the citizen column is.

## The amendment of 2019 at headline level

No map of this field can omit the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019, Act 47 of 2019, and no map should attempt to carry its controversy, which the debate article of this cluster owns. The headline is this. The amendment inserted a proviso into the definition of illegal migrant in Section 2(1)(b) of the 1955 Act, taking out of that definition persons belonging to the Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi and Christian communities from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan who entered India on or before 31 December 2014 and who are exempted by the Central Government from the passport and foreigners requirements; it reduced, for that class, the residence qualification for naturalisation in the Third Schedule from eleven years to five; it excluded the tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram and Tripura under the Sixth Schedule and the Inner Line Permit areas from its operation; and it added a ground for cancellation of OCI registration. The Act received assent on 12 December 2019 and was brought into force on 10 January 2020, and the Citizenship (Amendment) Rules, 2024, notified on 11 March 2024, supplied the application machinery under it. Challenges to the amendment's constitutional validity were brought before the Supreme Court and stand pending, and the arguments on both sides, the defence resting on the classification of three countries and six communities by reference to religious persecution and a cut-off tied to the pre-amendment position, the challenge resting on Articles 14 and 21 and on the place of religion in a citizenship law, are set out in [the article on the Citizenship Amendment Act, 2019 debate](/2021/10/15/citizenship-amendment-act-2019-debate/), whose questions this guide does not take. The map records the amendment as the event that made the structure of the whole field visible to the public: the protest and the litigation were about a proviso in the citizenship statute, because the citizenship statute is where Indian law keeps the questions that a refugee statute would have answered elsewhere.

## The illegal migrant: the definition that organises the modern Act

A single defined term does more work in the modern Act than any other, and the map must place it even though its detailed treatment belongs to the pillar article. Section 2(1)(b) defines an illegal migrant as a foreigner who has entered India without a valid passport or other travel documents and such other document or authority as may be prescribed, or with such documents but who remains in India beyond the permitted period. The definition, inserted by the amendment of 2003, is the hinge on which three doors swing. An illegal migrant is barred from citizenship by registration and by naturalisation, for which Sections 5 and 6 require that the applicant not be an illegal migrant. The child of an illegal migrant born in India after 3 December 2004 is kept out of citizenship by birth by the parentage condition of Section 3. And the amendment of 2019 operated by carving a class out of this definition rather than by amending the acquisition sections directly, which is why the debate about that amendment is, in statutory form, a debate about the definition. The definition also fixes the boundary between the two books with unusual sharpness: the illegal migrant is the person in whom the citizenship book's exclusions and the foreigners book's enforcement powers meet, the person whom the first book refuses to admit and the second book is entitled to remove, and whose only remaining legal shelter is the person-wide floor of Articles 14 and 21 and such executive protection as the government of the day extends.

## The diaspora road to the card

The overseas cardholder scheme has a prehistory in the diaspora policy of the decades after 1991, and the map records its outline because the scheme is unintelligible without it. Persons of Indian origin settled abroad pressed for a status that recognised the connection without offending the single citizenship rule, and a committee on the Indian diaspora chaired by L. M. Singhvi recommended a form of dual recognition that the constitutional text could not accommodate, since Article 9 and Section 9 forbid concurrent citizenship. The administrative answer came first: a Person of Indian Origin card, introduced in 1999 and revised in 2002, which conferred visa-free travel and a measure of parity on its holders as a matter of scheme rather than statute. The statutory answer followed in the amendment of 2003, which built the OCI scheme into the Act itself in Sections 7A to 7D, and the administrative and statutory schemes then ran in parallel for a decade until the PIO card was merged into the OCI card with effect from 9 January 2015, converting every surviving PIO cardholder into a cardholder under the Act. The trajectory is characteristic of the field: a political demand that the constitutional settlement of 1950 forecloses in its strong form is met first by executive improvisation and then by a statutory halfway house, drafted so as never to cross the line the settlement drew.

## The boundary of the second book: a citizen cannot be deported

One boundary between the books is absolute, and a guide should mark it because it disciplines everything the executive does in a status case. Deportation, expulsion and removal are powers over foreigners. A citizen of India cannot be deported from India, and the entire apparatus of the foreigners book, its orders, its tribunals, its detention pending removal, is lawful only on the premise that the person subjected to it is not a citizen. That is why the status question is logically prior to every removal, why the burden allocation in Section 9 of the 1946 Act mattered so intensely, since it decided who had to prove the premise on which the whole apparatus depended, and why the Supreme Court's citizenship jurisprudence returns so often to the fairness of the process by which the premise is established. Extradition is not an exception: it is a treaty process for the surrender of a person accused or convicted of an offence, it operates on citizens and foreigners alike under its own statute, and it ends in a trial rather than in exclusion from the national community. The citizen's exposure to the criminal law is unlimited; the citizen's exposure to the aliens power is nil, and the one-sentence test of a status case is whether the State is proceeding against the person as an accused, which it may always do, or as an alien, which it may do only if the status question is first answered against the person by the authority and in the manner the law prescribes.

## Three lists: census, population register, citizens register

Public discussion of the register habitually conflates three instruments that the law keeps apart, and the map separates them because the conflation is the single most common error in reporting on this part of the field. The Census, conducted under the Census Act, 1948, counts the population and collects the household data on which administration and delimitation rest; it records usual residents, asks no question that determines citizenship, and its returns are confidential by statute. The National Population Register, prepared under the Citizenship (Registration of Citizens and Issue of National Identity Cards) Rules, 2003, is a register of usual residents, compiled door to door in association with the house-listing phase of the Census of 2011, and intended by the Rules as the platform from which a register of citizens could be prepared by verification. The National Register of Indian Citizens is the third instrument: a register of citizens, prepared by scrutinising the population register through the claims, verification and objections process the 2003 Rules prescribe, and completed, in the event, only for Assam under its special history and the supervision of the Supreme Court. The three lists answer three different questions: how many people are there, who usually lives here, and who is a citizen. A debate that slides between the questions will misdescribe every answer, and a reader equipped with the separation can locate most published claims about a national register on the correct rung of the ladder within a sentence or two.

## A status question in practice: how a determination travels

The map closes its substantive account where the field meets the individual file. A citizenship question ordinarily surfaces in one of four settings: an application for registration or naturalisation, in which the applicant bears the task of satisfying the granting authority through the collector, the State government and the Centre in sequence; a reference to a Foreigners Tribunal in Assam, initiated by the police or the election authorities, in which the proceeding is adversarial in form and the burden rests on the person proceeded against; an election-roll or passport proceeding, in which the status question arrives incidentally and is decided by the authority for the purposes of that proceeding without determining the status generally; and a Section 9(2) reference, in which the question is the narrow one whether a foreign citizenship was voluntarily acquired, and the answer is the Central Government's to give. Each setting has its own standard of satisfaction, its own appellate or reviewing route, and its own consequences while the question is open, which range from the deferral of a grant to detention pending determination. The common thread is documentary: every setting is decided on records, the older the more valuable, because the statutory routes run through facts of birth, parentage, entry and residence that are proved by the paper trail of a life, school records, land records, electoral rolls across the decades, ration and employment records, and the certificates of an earlier generation whose own status was settled when the trail began. The practical law of citizenship in India is therefore, to a degree unusual even in a documentary legal culture, a law of archives, and the families who navigate it best are those who treat the file as the case.

## The founding population in practice

Part II's articles read as abstractions until they are set against the administrative machinery that operated them, and the machinery explains several features of the field that persist. Registration under Article 6 was a paper process conducted in the districts of a state managing refugee camps, transit points and a permit system introduced in July 1948 for entry from Pakistan; the permits, the registration certificates and the rehabilitation records generated at that time are the founding documents of the modern proof problem, because the descendants of the founding population establish their own position, generation by generation, by descent from persons whose status the permits and registers recorded. In the east the founding settlement met a different geography: the enclaves, the riverine chars and the porous Bengal border produced movements that the permit system captured imperfectly, and the constitutional categories of Article 6 and Article 7 have been litigated in the east, in cases concerning persons who crossed and recrossed in the years around 1950, for decades. The princely states added a third pattern. Their subjects became citizens through the accession and integration of their states and the operation of Article 5's domicile and birth conditions on the merged territories, a transition that left its own archival trail in state subject certificates and in the citizenship orders and notifications that attended particular mergers. The founding population was therefore not one population but three, settled by three different instruments, and the modern map inherits all three archives. A proof inquiry that begins in a district record room in Punjab works from permit and rehabilitation paper; one that begins in Assam works from the 1951 register and the rolls of 1971; one that begins in a former princely territory works from subjecthood records. The statute is one, and the evidentiary geography is plural, and that plurality is a founding fact of the field rather than an accident of its administration.

## Soil and bloodline: the two theories in the Indian settlement

Every citizenship law in the world is a negotiation between the two classical principles, jus soli, citizenship by the soil of birth, and jus sanguinis, citizenship by the bloodline of parentage, and the Indian settlement is instructive because it has travelled most of the distance between them within one statute's lifetime. The Constitution's founding rule in Article 5 was neither purely: it combined domicile with birth, parentage and residence, a composite suited to a population being sorted by migration rather than born into stillness. The Act of 1955 chose the soil for its first door, in the expansive manner of the birthright tradition, and then, as the politics of migration into Assam and the metropolitan cities sharpened, Parliament superimposed bloodline conditions in 1987 and again in 2004, until the birth door was narrower than the descent door is for the child of a citizen born abroad whose birth is duly registered. Comparative lawyers note the convergence: the United Kingdom travelled the same road in its Act of 1981, which ended unqualified birthright citizenship from 1983, and most European states condition birthright citizenship on a parent's settled status. The Indian peculiarity is not the direction but the third condition of 2004, the legality of the non-citizen parent's presence, which ties a child's status at birth to the immigration classification of a parent and thereby hard-wires the citizenship book to the foreigners book at the moment of birth. No account of the field is complete without seeing that hinge, because the litigation and the human difficulty of the modern subject concentrate there: the child born in India, educated in India, knowing no other country, whose status depends on the documents of a parent.

## Citizenship and the family

The Act treats the family as a transmission belt of status, and several of its quieter provisions decide the position of spouses and children in ways the map should record. Marriage to an Indian citizen confers nothing by itself; it qualifies the foreign spouse to apply for registration under Section 5(1)(c) after seven years of ordinary residence, a route that has generated its own case law on marriages of convenience and on the position of the spouse whose marriage ends before the grant. The minor child of a citizen may be registered under Section 5(1)(d) on the parent's application, a provision that functions as the family's tidying mechanism for children whose birth or descent position is doubtful or unrecorded. Renunciation under Section 8 carries the family consequence noted earlier, the minor child ceasing with the parent, balanced by the child's right to resume citizenship by declaration within one year of attaining majority, a right that also exists, in parallel form, for the person who lost citizenship as a minor through a parent's foreign naturalisation. Deprivation and termination do not cascade in the same way: a spouse's status is independent, and the Act's individualism at the point of loss is as deliberate as its familism at the point of transmission. Adoption, surrogacy and assisted conception press on these provisions from outside, and the courts and the Law Commission have from time to time noted that a statute drafted for the family forms of 1955 meets family forms it did not contemplate; the map records the pressure without pretending the Act resolves it, because the pillar article is the place for the cases and this guide's discipline is to show where the strain falls.

## The certificate in case of doubt

Between the confident routes of the Act and the hostile process of the foreigners book stands a small provision that the map should not omit, because it is the Act's own answer to uncertainty. Section 13 provides that the Central Government may, in such cases and subject to such conditions as may be prescribed, grant a certificate of citizenship to a person with respect to whose citizenship a doubt exists, and that a person to whom such a certificate is granted shall thereafter be deemed to have been a citizen from such date as may be specified, unless it is proved that the certificate was obtained by fraud. The provision is sparingly used and little reported, but its existence matters to the structure of the field: Parliament contemplated that doubt itself could be a settled state, to be resolved by a declaratory grant rather than by adversarial determination, and placed that resolution at the Centre. Beside it operates its mirror image in practice, the evidentiary weight given to a long-held passport, to inclusion in the Assam register, and to enrolment across successive electoral rolls, each of which can, in a tribunal or a court, discharge the practical burden in a given case without any doctrine declaring them conclusive. The law of proof in this field is therefore a law of accumulated paper, in which no item is sovereign and the whole file speaks, and Section 13 is the provision that acknowledges, from within the statute, that some files cannot speak clearly enough and must be answered by a grant.

## Open questions the map records without answering

A guide to this field earns its authority partly by marking the questions that the law has left open. Three stand on the record. The constitutional validity of the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 stands challenged before the Supreme Court, and the petitions, raising the equality guarantee, the classification of countries and communities, and the relationship between the amendment and the Assam settlement in Section 6A, remain pending; the guide describes them as pending and predicts nothing about their outcome. The register provision stands enacted and unexercised at national level: whether a National Register of Indian Citizens will be prepared for the whole country, on what timetable and with what consequences for persons omitted, are questions of government decision that no notification has finally answered, and the Assam register's own aftermath, including the fate of the excluded and the integration of the register with the tribunal process, remains incomplete on the record. And the protection question stands where the Eminent Persons Group left it in 1997, with a model law drafted and no statute introduced, so that the next chapter of the field, if there is one, will be written either by Parliament entering the silence at the centre of the map or by further decades of executive practice and Article 21 adjudication at its edges. The map's last service is to make those three openings visible in one view, because they are, between them, the agenda of Indian citizenship law. There is a fourth opening of a quieter kind, which is the condition of the archive itself. The proof regime of this field consumes records faster than the record system renews them: the legacy data of Assam ends in 1971, the founding permits of the west are three generations old, the district registers of births were incomplete for much of the population for much of the century, and each decade that passes widens the distance between the facts the statute asks about and the paper that survives to answer. Whether Indian citizenship law adapts to that distance, by presumptions, by evidentiary reform or by a register that fixes the record prospectively, is a question no judgment has yet had to answer in general terms, and it may in the end matter more to more people than any of the three openings above it, because it concerns not the boundaries of the status but the possibility of proving it at all.

## The courts on the citizenship book: deference, process and the one hard edge

Judicial review in this field follows the allocation the statute makes, and the map can state its pattern in three propositions. Where the Act commits a question to the Central Government, the courts review the decision for legality, rationality and procedural fairness but do not substitute their own finding: the exemplar is the Section 9(2) question of foreign acquisition, which the Supreme Court in Bhagwati Prasad Dixit v Rajeev Gandhi treated as the executive's to determine, in an election dispute in which the Court refused to convert a petition about a legislator's eligibility into an original trial of his citizenship. Where a status inquiry is conducted by an administrative authority, the courts insist on the fairness of the process with real strictness: in Lal Babu Hussein v Electoral Registration Officer, arising from the intensive revision of electoral rolls in Bombay in which large numbers of residents were called on to prove citizenship, the Supreme Court held that deletion of a name on a finding of non-citizenship required notice, a hearing and material with probative value, and that suspicion generated by language, appearance or neighbourhood could not substitute for evidence. Where the Act operates by itself, as in Section 9(1) termination on foreign naturalisation, the courts' role narrows to the anterior fact, and the automatic consequence follows. The pattern is coherent: Parliament governs the substance of citizenship, the executive governs the committed determinations, and the courts govern the fairness of every process that touches the status, a division that the Assam litigation of the register era tested at each of its three points.

## The Third Schedule: what naturalisation demands

Naturalisation is the door through which the field's philosophy is most fully stated, because the Third Schedule sets out, as a checklist, what India asks of the stranger who would become a citizen by grant. The applicant must not be a citizen of a country that prevents Indians from becoming its citizens by naturalisation, a reciprocity condition. The applicant must have renounced any other citizenship if the law of that country permits, or undertaken to renounce it on the grant. The residence qualification is the core: residence or service under the Government of India amounting to twelve months immediately before the application and, within the fourteen years before that, periods amounting in aggregate to not less than eleven years, the computation that the amendment of 2019 reduced to five years of the aggregate for its defined class. The applicant must be of good character, must have an adequate knowledge of a language specified in the Eighth Schedule, and must intend, if the certificate is granted, to reside in India or to enter or continue in service under the Government of India or an international organisation of which India is a member. The Central Government may waive the residence periods where the applicant has rendered distinguished service to science, philosophy, art, literature, peace or human progress. Read as a whole the Schedule describes naturalisation as the State's considered adoption of a person who has demonstrated attachment by presence, character and tongue, and the citizenship that results is, alone among the modes, preceded by an oath and shadowed for five years by the deprivation power in its sentencing ground. The map notes the Schedule here because its residence arithmetic is the provision the 2019 amendment altered, and a reader who has the arithmetic holds the measure of that alteration.

## Why Assam is different

No feature of the field puzzles an outside reader more than the concentration of its special machinery in one State, and the map owes that reader the explanation in structural form. Assam's position is the product of a specific sequence: a land frontier with East Pakistan and then Bangladesh across a riverine plain of historic movement; the growth of the movement's political salience through the electoral rolls, which converted a demographic anxiety into a constitutional one when the rolls for the Mangaldoi parliamentary constituency were revised in 1979 and the scale of objection petitions became a public event; the six-year agitation that followed, led by the All Assam Students' Union and the All Assam Gana Sangram Parishad; the Accord of 1985, which settled the agitation on the date bands that Section 6A enacted; the IMDT Act of 1983, which had applied a special, gentler determination procedure to Assam alone until the Supreme Court struck it down in 2005 in the Sarbananda Sonowal judgment, holding that the Act had failed the constitutional duty it was enacted to serve and that its burden and procedure provisions had made detection practically impossible; and the register exercise, undertaken under the Supreme Court's supervision from 2013 in the Assam Public Works proceedings, which applied the 2003 Rules to the State whose politics had made a register a live demand. Each element of the special machinery, the date bands, the tribunals, the register, answers an element of that sequence, and none of it generalises: the rest of India has no tribunals, no completed register and no Section 6A, and conducts its status business through the ordinary application and foreigners machinery. The guide states the sequence without adjudicating the politics, because the sequence is the explanation the map exists to give.

## Executive protection in outline: camps, settlements and long-term visas

The administrative protection described in the refugee section of this guide has instruments of its own, and a complete map names them at the level of form. The first instrument is the settlement: the Tibetan community that arrived from 1959 was accommodated in settlements administered with the Central Tibetan Administration, on land made available by State governments, with the residents holding registration certificates under the foreigners machinery and identity certificates for travel in place of passports, a durable arrangement that has carried a community across three generations without ever converting its members' presence into a statutory status. The second instrument is the camp: Sri Lankan Tamils who arrived from 1983 were received in camps in Tamil Nadu administered by the State's rehabilitation department, with subsistence support and restrictions on movement and employment that distinguished camp residents from the smaller number permitted to live outside, and Chakma and Hajong families in Arunachal Pradesh lived under yet another arrangement, long-settled residence shadowed for decades by the unresolved citizenship applications that the Supreme Court protected in 1996 and by the political opposition of the host State. The third instrument is the long-term visa: the executive's device, regularised through exemption orders made in 2015 under the passport and foreigners legislation and through the long-term visa policy notified for defined classes of minority-community nationals of Pakistan, Bangladesh and Afghanistan, of permitting a class of entrants to remain, work in defined ways and in time pursue the citizenship route, an instrument that the amendment of 2019 then built upon for part of the same population. The three instruments share the signature of the administrative model: each exists by executive act, each can be varied group by group, none confers a status a court can enforce as such, and each has sheltered real populations for real decades. A map that found the absence of a statute and stopped there would have missed the field's actual texture, which is not emptiness but a dense, discretionary, group-wise practice that the statute book does not describe.

## The numbers problem in a field without a count

The register note that governs this cluster, attribute every figure to a named source and period and assert no national figure without one, reflects a genuine feature of the field: Indian citizenship law operates without an official count of its own subject. The Census does not ask a citizenship question that yields a published count of citizens and foreigners. No authority publishes a figure for the number of persons whose status stands determined adversely in a given year across all forums, or for the number of persons present in India whom the government treats as refugees, in a single series over time. Figures exist in fragments, in parliamentary answers for particular groups and dates, in the publications of the Assam register exercise, in the reporting of the UNHCR for the population it recognises under its mandate, and each fragment is accurate, if at all, only for its source and its moment. The consequences for public discussion are visible everywhere: estimates of irregular migration vary by orders of magnitude between speakers, each citing a number whose provenance dissolves on inspection, and the debate proceeds as if the disagreement were about policy when it is partly about arithmetic that the State itself has never performed. The guide observes the discipline its brief imposes and extends it into a reading rule for the whole field: in citizenship, treat every unattributed number as rhetoric, including the reassuring ones, because the legal structure described in this article, a status determined person by person on documents, is precisely the kind of structure that generates no totals of its own.

## Three errors this guide exists to correct

Published writing on this field repeats three errors with such regularity that a guide can justify itself by correcting them alone. The first is the description of the overseas card as dual citizenship. It is not, and the correction is not pedantry, because the error misstates the cardholder's security: a dual citizen holds two statuses neither of which the other State's law can cancel for disaffection, while the cardholder holds one foreign citizenship and one Indian registration that Section 7D empowers the Central Government to cancel on grounds that include a two-year sentence of imprisonment passed anywhere. The second error is the treatment of a single document as proof of citizenship, an error with a human cost, because families discard or neglect the supporting trail, the school records, the old rolls, the parental certificates, in reliance on one card, and discover in a status proceeding that the card evidenced one fact and the proceeding required the route. The correction is the proof section of this guide: status by statute, documents as evidence of the route. The third error is the description of the Foreigners Act, 1946 as the operative law of foreigners, an error of currency that the consolidation of 2025 has made common in material written before it and copied after it. The correction is the succession this guide has recorded: the Act of 1946 explains the jurisprudence, the Act of 2025 states the law. A reader who carries away only those three corrections is already better oriented in this field than most of what search returns on it.

## Enclaves and the boundary settlement of 2015

Section 7's sovereign mode of acquisition had its most recent large exercise not in a merger but in a cartographic tidying. The India and Bangladesh enclaves, the fragments of each country's territory stranded inside the other along the Cooch Behar frontier, a legacy of princely and colonial boundaries, were exchanged under the Land Boundary Agreement of 1974 as implemented by the Constitution (One Hundredth Amendment) Act, 2015. The residents of the enclaves passing to India, persons who had lived for generations in a jurisdictional twilight without the ordinary documents of either State, were given the option to retain their existing nationality or to take the nationality of the State in which the enclave then lay, and citizenship was conferred on those who opted for India through the machinery of the Act, with camps, enumerations and documentation drives conducted for the purpose. The episode belongs on the map for the light it throws backward: it is the founding problem of Part II in miniature, a population whose nationality had to be settled by a sovereign act because no ordinary route of birth, descent or registration could reach facts that two States' record systems had jointly failed to capture, and its resolution by option and enumeration anticipated, at village scale, the logic of the register and the citizenship order alike.

## The exceptions inside the map: Sixth Schedule and the Inner Line

The field's geography contains two internal exceptions that a complete map must mark, because they determine where parts of the modern law run. The Sixth Schedule areas, the autonomous tribal districts of Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram and Tripura, are constitutionally distinct administrative country, with district councils exercising legislative and executive powers, and the amendment of 2019 was expressly kept out of them. The Inner Line system, descended from the Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation of 1873 and operating in Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Mizoram and Manipur, restricts the entry even of Indian citizens from other States without a permit, an internal frontier that coexists with single citizenship and qualifies, in practice, the Article 19 freedom of movement for the protection of indigenous land and demography, sustained as a reasonable restriction in the interest of the general public and of the Scheduled Tribes. Neither exception touches citizenship itself, no one is more or less a citizen within an Inner Line State, but both mark the places where the Union's uniform law of status meets its plural law of place, and both were written into the exclusions of the 2019 amendment for the same reason, that the demographic sensitivities which the Inner Line and the Sixth Schedule exist to manage are the very sensitivities that citizenship legislation in the north-east engages.

## Leaving the status: renunciation and the surrender of paper

The field's exit door generates its own documentary institution, and the map records it because consular practice is where most individuals actually meet this statute. A citizen who voluntarily acquires a foreign citizenship ceases to be an Indian citizen by operation of Section 9, and the Indian passport in that person's hands becomes a document to which the person is no longer entitled; its retention or use after the foreign naturalisation is an offence under the Passports Act, 1967, and the practice of the Indian missions is to require its surrender against a surrender certificate when the former citizen applies for the overseas card or for a visa. The renunciation route of Section 8 is the orderly alternative for the person who plans the change: a declaration in the prescribed form, registered by the prescribed authority, effective on registration, followed by the issue of a renunciation certificate that records the date from which the status ended. The distinction between the two exits matters in the files of real families, because the date on which Indian citizenship ended determines the position of children born around it, the validity of property and inheritance steps taken across it, and the eligibility of the person for the overseas card, whose scheme reaches the former citizen and the spouse and descendants of one. The map's point is structural: even exit from this status is a paper event in Indian law, certified, dated and filed, and the archive of a family's citizenship includes its renunciations as faithfully as its births.

## Status and residence: the distinctions the tax law does not draw

A persistent confusion at the edge of the field imports categories from other laws into the citizenship question, and the guide dispels it in a paragraph because the confusion corrupts advice and reporting alike. Non-resident Indian is not a citizenship category: it is a description used by the exchange control and income tax laws for a citizen of India who resides outside India for employment, business or an uncertain duration, and such a person remains a citizen with every political right, including the franchise, exercisable through the provisions for overseas electors on the electoral roll of the constituency of the Indian address. Resident and ordinarily resident are tax and census concepts. Domicile is a private-law concept governing succession and marriage jurisdiction. A person of Indian origin is a descent description used by schemes and by the overseas card's eligibility rules. None of these terms answers the citizenship question, each belongs to a different statute answering a different question, and the field's core discipline, knowing which statute answers which question, applies to vocabulary before it applies to anything else. The overseas elector point completes the circle neatly: the one political right the diaspora citizen exercises from abroad flows from citizenship retained, and the cardholder, who holds no citizenship, has no corresponding right, which is the whole difference between the two positions stated in a single franchise.

## The appellate map: where a decision can be taken next

A map of a legal field should end its substantive work by showing the exits from each of its rooms, because a reader in difficulty needs the next forum more than any other fact. From a refusal or cancellation under the citizenship book, the Act provides no departmental appeal for most grants, the application being a discretionary grant, and the remedy is judicial review before the High Court under Article 226, on the grounds of legality, procedural fairness and rationality, with the Supreme Court available under Article 136 by special leave and Article 32 for the enforcement of fundamental rights. From a Section 9(2) determination the same constitutional routes run, with the court reviewing the determination rather than re-making it. From a Foreigners Tribunal opinion in Assam the statute provided no appeal, and the Gauhati High Court's writ jurisdiction became the forum in which tribunal opinions were tested, a position the consolidating legislation of 2025 carried into its new appellate arrangements for the foreigners book, whose detail belongs to that cluster article. From a passport decision the Passports Act, 1967 supplies its own appellate authority and then the writ jurisdiction beyond it. Two features of this appellate map deserve the reader's notice. The first is that almost every road ends in a constitutional court exercising review rather than appeal, so that the quality of the record made before the first authority, the file again, decides most outcomes at the end of the road. The second is that the person-wide floor of Article 21 is available at every stage to every person, citizen or not, which is why the litigation of this field, for all its statutory particularity, keeps returning to the same article of the Constitution with which the substantive account began.

## The word foreigner: a short statutory genealogy

The second book is older than the first, and its genealogy explains its temper. The colonial state legislated for the foreigner before it legislated for the citizen, in the Foreigners Act, 1864, which empowered the executive to expel foreigners and to require security from them, and in the Registration of Foreigners Act, 1864's successors and the Passport (Entry into India) Act, 1920, which introduced the passport as the condition of lawful entry in the aftermath of the First World War. The Registration of Foreigners Act, 1939 added the machinery of registration on the eve of the Second World War, and the Foreigners Act, 1946 consolidated the executive powers in the form the Republic inherited: a definition by negation, a power to make orders on entry, presence and departure, a burden of proof on the person alleged to be a foreigner, and detention and removal as the sanctions at the end of the line. The Acts were instruments of a state that regarded the foreigner primarily as a security subject, and the independent Republic, occupied with the citizenship book's construction, retained the second book's architecture almost untouched for eight decades, amending its penalties, building the Foreigners Regional Registration Offices beneath it, and litigating its burden clause, while Parliament's creative energy went to the citizenship side of the line. The consolidation of 2025 is therefore best read as the second book's first full legislative reconsideration since 1946, and the guide's treatment of the foreigners article as the owner of that story reflects the size of what happened there: a wartime architecture was replaced, at last, by a statute drafted for the administrative state of the twenty-first century, which kept the executive's powers and systematised the duties of everyone who houses, teaches, treats or transports the foreigner.

## The identity card that the Act promised

Section 14A couples the register with a card: the Central Government may issue a national identity card to every registered citizen, and the pairing was the statutory seed of a project that ran for a decade in parallel with the register idea, the Multipurpose National Identity Card, piloted in selected districts from 2003 onward for citizens above a stated age, with a chip-based card recording the particulars the Rules prescribe. The national card was never generally issued. Into the space it left stepped a different instrument built for a different purpose: Aadhaar, conceived as a resident identity platform for the delivery of services and subsidies, which achieved near-universal coverage of the resident population while disclaiming, by design and by the repeated statements of its own authority, any determination of citizenship. The result is a documentary landscape of elegant perversity: the statute that governs citizenship authorises an identity card for citizens that does not exist, and the identity number that nearly every resident carries is issued under a statute that has nothing to do with citizenship and proves nothing about it. Readers who grasp that inversion understand the proof section of this guide without further argument, and understand also why the register, if it is ever prepared nationally, would have to be built from the citizenship book's own evidence and not read off from the resident identity infrastructure that the country actually built.

## The district as the unit of citizenship administration

For all its constitutional altitude, the field is administered in districts, and the map descends to that level before closing because the district is where the statute's abstractions become paper. The collector or district magistrate's office receives the application, causes the police and revenue verification of residence and antecedents that the Rules require, maintains the registers of declarations and certificates, and forwards the file with its recommendation into the State and central machinery described earlier. The district also keeps the archive on which the next generation's proof will depend: the birth and death registers maintained under the Registration of Births and Deaths Act, 1969, the electoral rolls prepared constituency by constituency under the Representation of the People Act, 1950, the land records of the revenue department, and the school admission registers that become, decades later, the linkage documents of a register exercise. Assam's register was built, in the end, by digitising and cross-matching exactly these district archives against the legacy data of 1951 and 1971. The administrative lesson generalises: the quality of a country's citizenship law in action is the quality of its district record-keeping across fifty years, and the field's hardest cases are disproportionately the cases of districts where the record was interrupted by flood, by insurgency, by the burning of a record room or by the simple failure of a poor family's encounters with the State to generate paper at all. A guide that stayed at the level of sections and benches would miss the material truth of the subject, which is that citizenship in India is proved out of the record rooms of its districts or it is not proved.

## The choice the Constituent Assembly made

The transitional design of Part II was a choice, made against alternatives, and the map records it because it governs everything that followed. Voices in the Constituent Assembly pressed for the Constitution itself to lay down the continuing law of citizenship, fearing that a subject so fundamental should not rest on ordinary legislation alterable by a bare majority. The Drafting Committee's design, defended in the Assembly by Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar among others, took the opposite course: the Constitution would settle the founding population, whose determination could not wait, and would equip Parliament in Article 11 with the whole subject thereafter, on the reasoning that the conditions of acquisition and termination would need revision as circumstances changed in ways no constituent body could foresee, and that a citizenship code frozen into the constitutional text would either be amended constantly or obeyed inadequately. The Assembly accepted the design, and the seventy years since have vindicated the prediction embedded in it if not every use of it: Parliament has rewritten the birth rule twice, created and abolished an overseas status in two forms, legislated for one State alone, armed a register and disarmed a determination statute, and fought its largest citizenship battle over a proviso, all by ordinary legislation under Article 11, with the courts reviewing the products against the rights articles rather than against any constitutional citizenship code that does not exist. The reader who understands that the absence of such a code is itself the founding decision holds the key to the field's politics as well as its law: every generation's argument about who belongs is an argument addressed to Parliament, and the Constitution's role is to police the manner and the limits of the answer, not to supply it.

## Detention at the junction of the books

Where the two books meet an individual case, the meeting point is frequently a place of detention, and the map marks it without entering the detailed treatment the foreigners article gives the subject. A person declared a foreigner by a tribunal in Assam, or found by the executive elsewhere to be present without authority, may be detained pending removal, in facilities that the States maintain for the purpose, and the detention is lawful as an incident of the removal power rather than as punishment, which is why it is not governed by the safeguards the criminal process provides and why its duration has drawn the courts in. The Supreme Court has held that detention pending deportation cannot be indefinite where removal is not possible within a reasonable period, and has directed the release on conditions of persons held for long periods whose deportation no country would effect, reasoning from Article 21. The position discloses the junction's logic in its purest form: the foreigners book supplies the power, the citizenship book's failure to recognise the person supplies the premise, and the Constitution supplies the only limit, a floor of liberty that operates even where the status question has been answered against the person. For the person who cannot establish citizenship and whom no State will receive, the three books exhaust themselves in sequence and the case rests, sometimes for years, on that floor, which is the human form of the structural gap with which this guide began.

## Gaps within the citizenship book itself

The absence at the field's centre is a missing statute; within the citizenship book itself there are smaller silences that the map should register for completeness. The Act contains no foundling provision: many citizenship codes deem a child found in the territory, whose parents are unknown, to be a citizen until the contrary is proved, and the Indian Act's omission leaves the foundling to the general proof regime, a lacuna that practitioners in child welfare law have noted. India is not a party to the Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness of 1961, so no treaty obligation fills the space either. The Act's provisions on descent were drafted for registration systems of an earlier era and sit awkwardly with births in countries that prohibit dual registration, a friction the consular declaration requirements manage rather than remove. And the Act says nothing about the citizenship consequences of adoption across borders, leaving the adopted child's position to be worked out through registration. None of these gaps has generated litigation on the scale of the field's great contests, and each is the kind of point on which the pillar article's section-by-section treatment is the authority; the map records them because a reader who knows where a code is silent reads its provisions more accurately than a reader who assumes the code is complete.

## The small print of the birth rule

Even the soil rule carries fine print that the map should not smooth away, because the exceptions mark the points at which sovereignty qualifies birthplace. Section 3 has never conferred citizenship on a child born in India whose father possesses such immunity from suits and legal process as is accorded to an envoy of a foreign sovereign power accredited to the President of India and who is not a citizen of India, nor on a child born in India whose father is an enemy alien and the birth occurs in a place then under occupation by the enemy. The two exceptions descend from the classical international law of diplomatic immunity and belligerent occupation, and they remind the reader that birthright citizenship, even at its most expansive in the original Act of 1955, was a rule of the municipal law operating inside a world of sovereigns. The descent door has its own corresponding small print in the consular registration regime described earlier, and the naturalisation door has its reciprocity condition, so that each of the Act's doors is fitted with a device that looks outward to the conduct or status of another State. A citizenship code is, among other things, a foreign policy instrument written in the grammar of personal status, and the map that omits the small print understates how deliberately this one was drafted.

## The citizen who is not a person

One boundary of the field is jurisdictional in a different sense: the status belongs to natural persons. A company incorporated in India is not a citizen of India for the purposes of the Constitution's citizenship provisions or of Article 19, a position settled by the Supreme Court in State Trading Corporation of India v Commercial Tax Officer in 1963, where the Court refused to lift the corporate veil to find citizen shareholders behind an Indian company claiming the Article 19 freedoms, a refusal maintained in Tata Engineering and Locomotive Co v State of Bihar. The corporation's remedies run through the rights its citizen shareholders and officers hold in their own names and through the person-wide articles, and the distinction matters beyond doctrine because a surprising volume of popular writing attributes citizenship to companies, airlines and ships, each of which in truth carries a nationality or a registration of a different legal kind. The field of this guide is the status of human beings in relation to the Indian State, and its boundary at the artificial person is one more instance of the discipline the whole map teaches: each legal word answers the question of its own statute, and citizenship answers the question of the person's membership of the Republic, which no incorporation can confer.

## A note on the method of this guide

Citizenship is the subject on which Indian public writing most often substitutes position for description, and a guide that hopes to be used by readers on every side of its questions must state its method and keep it. The method here has been to describe provisions, procedures and holdings; to state every contested position in the terms its own proponents use, at the strength at which they hold it, and to adopt none; to attribute every figure to the source and the moment that produced it, and to print no figure that lacks both; to describe every pending matter as pending; and to give no advice about any individual case, because a status question turns on facts, dates and documents that a published guide cannot see. Where the guide has taken a position, the position is structural rather than partisan: that the field is divided as the statutes divide it, that protection within it is administrative rather than legal, and that the absence of a refugee statute is the fact from which the modern controversies grow. Those are findings about the shape of the law, of the kind a map is for, and a reader who disputes every political conclusion ever drawn from them can still navigate by this chart, which is the test the guide sets itself.

## How to read the rest of this cluster

The cluster of which this guide is the map proceeds from the centre outward. The pillar article takes the Citizenship Act, 1955 section by section, including the schedules, the deprivation jurisprudence and the Section 6A judgment. The trail article takes the amendments of 1986, 1992, 2003, 2004, 2015 and 2019 in sequence and shows the birth rule narrowing and the overseas scheme evolving. The debate article states the case for and the case against the amendment of 2019 in their own strongest forms, the areas and communities it covers and excludes, and the state of the challenges to it. The foreigners article takes the second book from 1946 to the consolidation of 2025, including the tribunals, the burden of proof and the detention framework. The passports article takes the third book, from Satwant Singh Sawhney and Maneka Gandhi to the refusal and impounding grounds. A reader who begins with this guide and then follows the cross-links in that order will hold the field as its statutes hold it: a constitutional settlement that ended in 1950, a citizenship code that has been tightened at the door of birth and widened at the door of the diaspora, a foreigners code that has been consolidated without being softened, a travel code, and, at the centre where a protection code would stand, a silence that executive practice and Article 21 litigation fill without closing. This guide is a work of legal information; it describes the law of citizenship and does not advise on the status of any individual, which depends on facts and dates that only the deciding authority can determine.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which laws govern citizenship in India?

Citizenship in India is governed by Part II of the Constitution, Articles 5 to 11, which settled who was a citizen at the commencement of the Constitution on 26 January 1950 and gave Parliament the power over everything after that date, and by the Citizenship Act, 1955, Act 57 of 1955, which Parliament made under that power. The 1955 Act states the modes of acquisition and loss, the overseas cardholder scheme and the register provision, and it operates through the Citizenship Rules, 2009. Two neighbouring statutes complete the field: the Immigration and Foreigners Act, 2025 governs the person who is not a citizen, and the Passports Act, 1967 governs travel documents.

### Q: What do the constitutional provisions on citizenship provide?

Articles 5 to 11 of Part II determine citizenship at one moment only, the commencement of the Constitution. Article 5 covers the domiciled person born in India, or whose parent was born in India, or who had resided in India for five years. Article 6 covers migration from Pakistan, with a registration route for those arriving on or after 19 July 1948, and Article 7 covers migration to Pakistan, subject to an exception for return under a resettlement permit. Article 8 covers persons of Indian origin abroad registered at an Indian consulate, Article 9 excludes anyone who voluntarily took a foreign citizenship, Article 10 continues the status subject to parliamentary law, and Article 11 hands the whole subject to Parliament.

### Q: Can a person hold Indian and foreign citizenship together?

No. Indian law does not permit dual citizenship. Article 9 of the Constitution excluded a person who had voluntarily acquired a foreign citizenship from the founding citizenship, and Section 9 of the Citizenship Act, 1955 carries the rule forward: a citizen who voluntarily acquires the citizenship of another country ceases by that act to be a citizen of India. The question whether a foreign citizenship was in fact acquired voluntarily is decided by the Central Government under Section 9(2). It is because concurrent citizenship is barred that the Overseas Citizen of India cardholder scheme exists, giving former citizens and their descendants a bundle of statutory privileges while they remain foreign citizens in law.

### Q: What is an Overseas Citizen of India cardholder?

An Overseas Citizen of India cardholder is a foreign citizen registered under Section 7A of the Citizenship Act, 1955, a provision inserted in 2003 and in force from 3 December 2004. Eligibility reaches a person who was eligible to be a citizen at the commencement of the Constitution, who was a citizen on or after 26 January 1950, who belonged to a territory that became part of India after 15 August 1947, and the children, grandchildren and spouses of such persons, with citizens of Pakistan and Bangladesh among those excluded. The cardholder holds a lifelong multiple-entry visa, exemption from foreigners registration, and parity with non-resident Indians in economic, financial and educational fields except for agricultural and plantation property. The cardholder is not a citizen, has no vote, and the registration can be cancelled under Section 7D.

### Q: Does India have a statute governing refugees?

No. India is not a party to the Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees of 1951 or to its Protocol of 1967, and Parliament has enacted no statute defining a refugee, conferring a protection status or setting up a determination procedure. A person seeking protection in India is dealt with under the general law governing foreigners, together with executive decisions taken for whole groups or case by case. Groups received over the decades, including Tibetans, Sri Lankan Tamils and Chakmas, were sheltered under such executive arrangements, which differ group by group. The office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees conducts status determination in India for some asylum seekers under its own mandate, without statutory recognition. Protection is therefore administrative rather than legal.

### Q: What is the National Register of Citizens?

The National Register of Citizens is the register of Indian citizens authorised by Section 14A of the Citizenship Act, 1955, inserted by the amendment of 2003, which empowers the Central Government to maintain a National Register of Indian Citizens and to issue national identity cards to registered citizens. The Citizenship (Registration of Citizens and Issue of National Identity Cards) Rules, 2003 prescribe how such a register is prepared, through local registers rising to the national register, with a claims and objections stage. A register has been prepared under this framework for one State, Assam, under the supervision of the Supreme Court and in connection with Section 6A, and its final version was published on 31 August 2019. No register for the whole country has been prepared under the provision.

### Q: Which law governs foreigners in India now?

The Immigration and Foreigners Act, 2025, Act 13 of 2025, which received assent on 4 April 2025 and came into force on 1 September 2025. It repealed and replaced four older enactments, the Passport (Entry into India) Act, 1920, the Registration of Foreigners Act, 1939, the Foreigners Act, 1946 and the Immigration (Carriers' Liability) Act, 2000, and consolidated their subject matter in one statute covering entry, visas, registration, presence and removal, administered through a Bureau of Immigration. The Foreigners Act, 1946 remains essential reading for the case law and administrative practice built under it over eight decades, and the Foreigners Tribunals in Assam descend from an order made under it, but the operative law of foreigners is the Act of 2025.

### Q: How does a person prove Indian citizenship?

By establishing the statutory basis on which the citizenship was acquired, because no single document is conclusive proof of the status. A person shows citizenship by birth under Section 3 by proving the birth in India and, for the periods after 1987 and 2004, the citizenship and status of the parents that the section requires; by descent under Section 4 by proving the parent's citizenship and, for births abroad after 3 December 2004, the consular registration; or by producing the certificate of registration or naturalisation granted under Sections 5 or 6. Birth certificates, passports, electoral rolls, land and school records are evidence of those underlying facts, and their weight is assessed document by document by the authority deciding the question.

### Q: What is single citizenship in India?

Single citizenship means that India has one citizenship only, the citizenship of the Union, and no citizenship of any State. A person is a citizen of India or is not a citizen at all; no State can confer, vary or withdraw the status, and citizenship, naturalisation and aliens form Entry 17 of the Union List, within Parliament's exclusive competence. The design departs from federations such as the United States, where state citizenship exists beside national citizenship. Its consequences run through the field: movement between States never raises a citizenship question, changes of State residence affect domicile-based entitlements and documentary records rather than the status itself, and the citizenship consequences of any redrawing of State boundaries are nil, which is why reorganisation legislation is silent on status.

### Q: Which constitutional rights are reserved for citizens alone?

The Constitution gives some rights to every person and reserves others to citizens. Every person, citizen or foreigner, holds Article 14 on equality before the law, Articles 20 and 21 on criminal safeguards and on life and personal liberty, and Article 25 on religious freedom. Citizens alone hold Article 15 on discrimination by the State, Article 16 on equality of opportunity in public employment, the six freedoms of Article 19 including movement, residence and settlement and the practice of professions, and the cultural and educational rights of Articles 29 and 30. The political structure is also citizen-only: the franchise under Article 326, membership of Parliament and the State legislatures, and eligibility for offices such as President, Governor and judge of the Supreme Court or a High Court.

### Q: Does the Census decide who is a citizen of India?

No. The Census, conducted under the Census Act, 1948, counts the population and records usual residents; it determines nobody's citizenship and its returns are confidential. It must be distinguished from two instruments made under the Citizenship (Registration of Citizens and Issue of National Identity Cards) Rules, 2003: the National Population Register, a register of usual residents prepared door to door alongside the house-listing of the Census of 2011, and the National Register of Indian Citizens, which alone is a register of citizens and which requires a verification and claims process beyond enumeration. Counting who lives in a place, recording who usually resides there, and adjudging who is a citizen are three different operations, and only the third decides status.

### Q: What is the difference between a citizen and a foreigner under Indian law?

A citizen is a person who holds Indian citizenship under Part II of the Constitution as continued by, or acquired under, the Citizenship Act, 1955. A foreigner is defined by inversion, as a person who is not a citizen of India, the definition carried in the foreigners legislation from the Act of 1946 into the Immigration and Foreigners Act, 2025. The division controls which statute book governs the person: the citizen is governed by the citizenship code, holds the citizen-only rights of Articles 15, 16 and 19, and cannot be deported; the foreigner is governed by the foreigners code of entry, registration, presence and removal, and holds the person-wide rights, principally Articles 14 and 21. Every other status in the field, including the overseas cardholder, is a foreign citizen holding statutory privileges.

### Q: What is the difference between citizenship and domicile in India?

Citizenship is a person's membership of the Indian State, governed by Part II of the Constitution and the Citizenship Act, 1955, and it is single and national. Domicile is a private-law concept connecting a person to a legal territory for purposes such as succession, marriage and jurisdiction, acquired by birth and changeable by choice under the rules codified in part in the Indian Succession Act, 1925. India has one citizenship but a person's domicile may be located in a particular State, and domicile within a State carries consequences for matters such as educational and employment preferences that States administer. Article 5 used domicile as one element of the founding citizenship in 1950, which is the historical reason the two ideas are so often confused, but after commencement they answer different questions in different bodies of law.

### Q: What power does Article 11 give Parliament over citizenship?

Article 11 provides that nothing in Part II of the Constitution derogates from the power of Parliament to make any provision with respect to the acquisition and termination of citizenship and all other matters relating to citizenship. The power is plenary within its subject: under it Parliament enacted the Citizenship Act, 1955, narrowed citizenship by birth in 1986 and 2003, created the overseas cardholder scheme and the register provision in 2003, legislated for Assam alone through Section 6A, and amended the definition of illegal migrant in 2019. Because Part II is transitional, almost every question of citizenship after 26 January 1950 is answered by legislation made under Article 11, tested against the rights provisions of the Constitution rather than against any continuing constitutional code of citizenship.

### Q: How does the incorporation of new territory affect citizenship?

Section 7 of the Citizenship Act, 1955 answers the question: where any territory becomes part of India, the Central Government may by order published in the Official Gazette specify the persons who shall be citizens of India by reason of their connection with that territory, and they become citizens from the date the order specifies. The power has been exercised for each enlargement of the Union, in the citizenship orders for Goa, Daman and Diu in 1962, for Pondicherry following the treaty of cession with France, and for Sikkim in 1975, each dealing class by class with the population whose nationality the territory carried, in some cases with an option to retain the former nationality. The enclave exchange with Bangladesh in 2015 used the same logic, giving residents of the exchanged enclaves a choice of nationality.

### Q: Which authority decides a doubtful claim to Indian citizenship?

It depends on the question in doubt. Whether a person voluntarily acquired a foreign citizenship, which terminates Indian citizenship under Section 9, is reserved by Section 9(2) to the Central Government, whose determination the courts review rather than re-make. A doubt about citizenship generally can be resolved by the Central Government itself under Section 13, which empowers it to grant a certificate of citizenship in case of doubt, after which the person is deemed a citizen from the date specified unless the certificate was obtained by fraud. In Assam, whether a person is a foreigner is decided in the first instance by a Foreigners Tribunal, subject to the writ jurisdiction of the Gauhati High Court. Courts deciding citizenship incidentally, in election or service matters, apply the statute to the facts proved before them.

### Q: What role does the UNHCR play in India?

The office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees in New Delhi conducts refugee status determination under its international mandate for asylum seekers in India, principally those from countries that do not share a border with India, and issues documentation to persons it recognises. The Indian State tolerates and in practice gives weight to that documentation, but the UNHCR has no statutory recognition in Indian law, because India is not a party to the Refugee Convention of 1951 or its Protocol of 1967 and no Indian statute assigns the agency a role. Its certificates are therefore not a status under Indian law, and the protection of the much larger populations who arrive across India's land borders is handled by the Government of India directly, through group-wise executive arrangements rather than through the agency.

### Q: How are citizenship and the right to vote connected in India?

Article 326 of the Constitution founds elections to the Lok Sabha and the State assemblies on adult suffrage: every person who is a citizen of India and who is not less than eighteen years of age, and who is not otherwise disqualified under the Constitution or a law on grounds such as non-residence, unsoundness of mind or corruption, is entitled to be registered as a voter. The Representation of the People Act, 1950 works the connection in practice, since enrolment on an electoral roll requires citizenship, which is why an entry on a roll is evidence supporting a citizenship claim without being the status itself. Citizens resident abroad may enrol as overseas electors in the constituency of their Indian address. A foreigner, including an overseas cardholder, has no vote, however long the residence in India.

### Q: Which rules govern applications for Indian citizenship?

The Citizenship Rules, 2009, made under the Citizenship Act, 1955 and replacing the Citizenship Rules, 1956, govern applications for registration under Section 5 and naturalisation under Section 6, and for the other grants and declarations under the Act. They prescribe the forms of application, the authorities before whom applications are filed, ordinarily the district-level collector or equivalent authority of the applicant's place of residence, the verification to be carried out, the channel through the State government to the Central Government which decides, the fees, and the oath of allegiance taken from the Second Schedule. A separate instrument, the Citizenship (Registration of Citizens and Issue of National Identity Cards) Rules, 2003, governs the population register and the register of citizens, and the Citizenship (Amendment) Rules, 2024 govern applications under the amendment of 2019.

### Q: Which ministry administers citizenship law in India?

The Ministry of Home Affairs administers citizenship law. Within it, the Foreigners Division handles citizenship, the overseas cardholder scheme, the register of citizens and the law governing foreigners, including the Bureau of Immigration, which functions under the Ministry's oversight. The allocation is a survival of the subject's history: citizenship, naturalisation and aliens stand together in Entry 17 of the Union List, and the ministry responsible for internal security and the States also holds the status questions that arise at the border between citizen and foreigner. Two neighbouring subjects sit elsewhere, which the map of the field must keep distinct: passports are administered by the Ministry of External Affairs under the Passports Act, 1967, and the Census and the population register are conducted by the Registrar General and Census Commissioner within the Home Ministry itself.
