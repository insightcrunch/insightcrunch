The Deve Gowda and Gujral governments of 1996 to 1998 are usually filed under failure. H.D. Deve Gowda, a Karnataka politician elevated from the chief ministership of his state to the prime ministership of India, held office from 1 June 1996 to 21 April 1997. I.K. Gujral, a diplomat turned politician who had served as external affairs minister in the same cabinet, held office from 21 April 1997 to 19 March 1998. Neither man led a party with a majority. Neither government survived a full year under its own leader. Both depended on the Indian National Congress for their parliamentary lives, and both died when the Congress withdrew the support it had lent from outside the government. The standard treatment of the period stops at that record of brevity and dependence, and moves on to the Bharatiya Janata Party government of March 1998 as the point where the decade's politics became serious again. This article defends a different claim. The two interregnums were the first full test of an institutional device, the outside-support coalition, and the device outlasted the governments that tested it. The Common Programme as a written constraint on governance, the supporting party's reserved right to withdraw on stated issues, and the rhythm of crisis followed by renegotiation were all worked out between June 1996 and March 1998. When the United Progressive Alliance took office in May 2004 with the Left Front supporting from outside, the arrangement that governed India for the following four years was a rebuilt version of the machine first assembled, and twice broken, in the Deve Gowda and Gujral months.

## The Dismissive Reading: Twenty-One Months Treated as a Footnote

The dismissive reading has a clean narrative shape, and its cleanliness is the first reason to distrust it. In that reading, the general election of April and May 1996 produced a hung Lok Sabha, the Bharatiya Janata Party formed a government that collapsed in thirteen days, and the United Front governments that followed were improvisation stretched across twenty-one months until the electorate could be asked again. Deve Gowda appears in the story as an accident of arithmetic, a regional leader raised to an office he had not sought by a coalition that could agree on no one else. Gujral appears as the accident's sequel, a caretaker of foreign policy distinction and domestic weightlessness. The February and March 1998 election then closes the parenthesis, and the institutional history of Indian coalition government is written from the National Democratic Alliance of 1998 and 1999, with its common agenda and its full term, as though coalition practice began when it first succeeded.

Each element of that narrative is factually anchored, which is what gives the framing its hold. Deve Gowda did take office on 1 June 1996 at the head of a coalition whose largest constituent, his own Janata Dal, held 46 seats. Gujral did succeed him on 21 April 1997 only because the Congress made a change of leader the price of continued support. The Congress did withdraw support twice, on 30 March 1997 and again on 28 November 1997, and both withdrawals were terminal. The Lok Sabha elected in 1996 was dissolved on 4 December 1997, barely eighteen months into its five-year term. A reader who knows only these dates knows real things. The argument of this article is that the dates describe the fate of two governments and say almost nothing about the significance of the arrangement under which those governments worked.

The distinction matters because Indian governments are usually judged by the durability of the ministry rather than the durability of the method. A method can be transmitted even when every ministry that pioneers it falls. The outside-support coalition was a method: a defined relationship between a governing alliance that lacked a majority and a supporting party that supplied the missing votes without taking office, bound to a published programme and entitled, by the terms of the arrangement itself, to withdraw on issues it named. Before June 1996, outside support in Indian national politics had been an episode, extended to V.P. Singh in 1989 by two opposed parties acting separately and to Chandra Shekhar in 1990 by a Congress leadership managing a caretaker. Between 1996 and 1998 it became a system with a text, a procedure and a jurisprudence of its own. The Deve Gowda government fell over the supporting party's demand for a different prime minister. The Gujral government fell over the supporting party's demand for the expulsion of a constituent. Both demands were pressed through the arrangement's own logic: support was conditional, the conditions were arguable in public, and withdrawal was the sanctioned remedy. That the remedy was used twice in twenty-one months shows the system's instability. That the system had conditions, texts and remedies at all shows its novelty, and its teachability.

The policy record complicates the footnote treatment further. The government of Deve Gowda presented the budget of 28 February 1997, in which Finance Minister P. Chidambaram cut the peak rate of personal income tax to 30 percent and reduced corporate tax rates in the most praised fiscal exercise of the decade. The same government carried the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India Act of 1997, creating a statutory regulator for a sector the state had run as a department. The Gujral government gave its name to a doctrine of non-reciprocal generosity toward India's smaller neighbours that structured the country's regional diplomacy for years afterwards, and signed the Ganges water treaty with Bangladesh on 12 December 1996 while Gujral held the external affairs portfolio under Deve Gowda. Governments that produce a landmark budget, a regulatory statute and a named foreign policy doctrine inside twenty-one months are many things, but a blank interval between real governments is not one of them.

What follows reconstructs the interregnums in the order the institutional pattern reveals itself. The electoral verdict of 1996 created the conditions. The United Front's construction supplied the governing alliance. The outside-support arrangement supplied the method. The two falls supplied the stress tests, and the arrangement of 2004 supplied the proof of transmission. The failed-interludes reading and the institutional-pattern reading agree on the chronology. They disagree on what the chronology is evidence of, and that disagreement is adjudicated on the primary record: the United Front Common Programme of June 1996, the Lok Sabha debates of 1996 to 1998, and the report of the Justice M.C. Jain Commission, whose findings closed the period.

Even the vocabulary of the period betrays the framing under examination. An interregnum is, by the word's construction, the interval between reigns, a gap in which nothing of constitutional substance occurs because the substance resides in the reigns on either side. The metaphor smuggles the Congress and BJP centuries into the analysis: the years in which neither party governed become, by definition, the years in which governing did not happen. The metaphor fails on the period's own facts. The state's business did not pause between June 1996 and March 1998. Two budgets were framed and enacted, one of them after its author's fall. A statutory regulator was created. A water treaty with a neighbour was negotiated and signed. A commission of inquiry reported and, in reporting, altered the composition of the government. State governments were not dismissed. An election was administered by a caretaker ministry that observed the conventions it was simultaneously inventing. If this is an interregnum, the word has lost its meaning, and its survival in the literature is a measure of how completely the two national parties have owned the period's narration. The reference treatments arranged around the singular ministries and the Front's organisation tell the story as biography and arithmetic. The institutional story, of a method devised, tested, broken and transmitted, is legible only when the period is read as a unit, and reading it as a unit is the first move this article makes.

A second assumption inside the dismissive reading also fails inspection. The reading treats brevity as a property of the method: governments built this way fall quickly, therefore the way is defective. The period's own sequence refutes the inference in its crude form. The Deve Gowda government fell when its backer's leadership changed. The Gujral government fell when a judicial inquiry's interim findings made one constituent radioactive to the backer. Neither fall was caused by the programme's collapse, by a constituent's defection over policy, or by a lost vote on supply; the money bills passed, the constituents held, and the programme's text was never repudiated by any party to it. The causes of death were exogenous to the method's machinery, located in a party's internal succession and in the long tail of an assassination. A method is not refuted by the accidents of its first operators, any more than parliamentary government was refuted by the falls of the 1979 and 1990 experiments that preceded the coalition era. It is refuted only if its machinery cannot be operated by steadier hands, and 2004 answered that question in the negative.

## A Verdict Without a Government: India After the Poll of 1996

The eleventh general election, held across April and May 1996, returned a Lok Sabha in which no party approached the majority mark of 272. The Election Commission of India's statistical report on the 1996 general election records the Bharatiya Janata Party as the largest party with 161 seats on 20.29 percent of the vote, and the Indian National Congress second with 140 seats on 28.80 percent. The inversion mattered. The Congress had won the larger share of votes and the smaller share of seats, and its 140 seats were the lowest total in its history to that point, a collapse recorded state by state across its northern strongholds. The Janata Dal, the largest single element of the alliance that would govern, held 46 seats. Behind those three numbers stood a chamber built out of regional blocs: the Communist Party of India (Marxist) with 32 seats, the Tamil Maanila Congress with 20, the Samajwadi Party and the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam with 17 each, the Telugu Desam Party with 16, the Shiv Sena with 15, the Communist Party of India with 12 and the Bahujan Samaj Party with 11.

The geography of the result mattered as much as its arithmetic, because the verdict was assembled state by state and each state told a different story about the old order. In Tamil Nadu, the AIADMK of J. Jayalalithaa, which had governed the state and contested in alliance with the Congress, was wiped out entirely, returning no member to the Lok Sabha, while the DMK and the Tamil Maanila Congress, the latter a formation born of a split in the state Congress over that very alliance, divided the state's seats between them. In Uttar Pradesh, the Congress fell to a remnant in the state that had supplied most of its prime ministers, outpolled by the Samajwadi Party, the Bahujan Samaj Party and the BJP alike. In Bihar, the Janata Dal of Laloo Prasad Yadav held its ground against all comers. In West Bengal and Kerala the Left Front's organisations delivered their customary blocs. A national government formed from this chamber would not be a Delhi government with regional decoration. It would be a federation of these state verdicts, and its prime minister would hold office as their compromise. The election had not merely failed to produce a majority. It had produced a map on which the majority had to be negotiated among governments, and the negotiation, once begun in May 1996, never really adjourned for the twenty-one months that followed.

President Shankar Dayal Sharma followed the convention of inviting the leader of the largest party. Atal Bihari Vajpayee took office on 16 May 1996 and resigned on 28 May 1996, before the confidence vote he knew he would lose, in the episode examined in [the 1996 thirteen days as immediate antecedent](/2019/05/31/1996-vajpayee-thirteen-days-explained/). The thirteen days established the negative fact on which everything after it rested: the largest party in the House could not convert plurality into a governing majority, because the rest of the chamber, for incompatible reasons of its own, preferred almost any other arrangement. The non-Congress and non-BJP parties had spent the election campaigning against both national parties in different registers. The verdict they produced was a mandate for a third formation, delivered without giving that formation the seats to govern alone.

The structural meaning of the result reaches back to [the post-1989 verdict's structural rupture](/2019/05/20/vp-singh-1989-verdict-india/). The election of 1989 had ended the era in which a single party could expect to command the Lok Sabha; the elections of 1991 had restored the Congress to office as a minority that grew into stability; the election of 1996 removed even that fallback. Three successive verdicts had fragmented the party system along regional lines that Pradeep Chhibber would analyse in Democracy without Associations, published in 1999, as a durable reconfiguration rather than a passing disorder. Yogendra Yadav, writing in Economic and Political Weekly in January 1996 on the eve of the poll, described a party system being rebuilt from the states upward. The 1996 result was that analysis rendered in seats. Any government formed from such a chamber would be a coalition. The only open questions were the coalition's composition and the terms on which its missing votes would be supplied.

### How many seats did the Bharatiya Janata Party win in 1996?

The Bharatiya Janata Party won 161 seats in the 1996 general election, the largest tally of any party, on 20.29 percent of the national vote, as recorded in the Election Commission of India's statistical report on the poll. The Congress won 140 seats on a larger vote share of 28.80 percent, and the Janata Dal won 46.

Those questions were answered in the last days of May 1996. The parties of the United Front, a formation whose core had contested the election together and whose ranks the result enlarged, settled their claim to govern with the support of the Congress and the Left. The Congress, meeting under P.V. Narasimha Rao in the aftermath of its worst defeat, chose support without office: the party would sustain a United Front government from outside, keeping the BJP out of power and keeping its own depleted organisation out of a cabinet it did not control. The Communist Party of India (Marxist) made a parallel choice, supporting from outside while its ally the Communist Party of India entered the government. The arrangement is the hinge of this article, and its framework is the one set out in [the coalition-era renegotiation framework](/2019/05/15/coalition-era-1989-2014-explained/): a coalition era government is best read not as a majority that failed to form but as a standing negotiation among parties that retained separate existences, separate electorates and separate exit options. The United Front government was that negotiation given ministerial form.

## The Congress Decision: Backing Without Office, May 1996

The outside-support arrangement began as a Congress decision, and the terms the Congress set in May 1996 governed everything that followed. The party's leadership met in the week after Vajpayee's resignation with three options before it. It could attempt a Congress government, although its 140 seats left it 132 short of a majority and dependent on the same regional parties it had spent the campaign fighting in the states. It could force a second election within the year, although its organisation had been routed in the first and its finances and morale were in no condition for another. Or it could lend its votes to a United Front government, exacting a programme and a posture of consultation as the price, and use the interval to rebuild. The Congress Working Committee, under P.V. Narasimha Rao, settled on the third option in the last days of May 1996. The decision was announced as a commitment to keep the Bharatiya Janata Party out of power and to give the country a government, and both announcements were true. The decision was also an acknowledgement, which no Congress leader made aloud, that the party of the freedom movement had become one bloc among several in a chamber it no longer organised.

The terms on which the backing was given reveal the arrangement's original asymmetry. The Congress did not publish a charter of conditions comparable to the Common Programme it expected the Front to honour. Its undertaking was conveyed politically rather than contractually: the party would vote for the government on confidence and on supply, its leadership would be consulted on major questions, and the government would pursue the secular and federal commitments the two sides shared. No joint committee was created to manage the relationship. No schedule of consultation was fixed. The Congress kept its freedom of manoeuvre entire, and the Front accepted the freedom as the cost of office. Inder Malhotra's account of the period stresses how personal the channel consequently became: the arrangement ran through conversations between the prime minister and the Congress president, and it therefore inherited the health of that single relationship. Under Rao, who had designed the formula and whose authority inside his party remained formidable despite the defeat, the channel held without strain through the government's first months. The design flaw was invisible in the summer of 1996. It became the government's central fact when the Congress presidency changed hands in the autumn.

The Congress decision also fixed the ideological perimeter within which the United Front could govern. The Front's constituents spanned the socialist left and the regional centre, and several of them had built their state careers on anti-Congress platforms; the Samajwadi Party in Uttar Pradesh and the Left in West Bengal and Kerala defined themselves against Congress rule in their states. A government resting on Congress votes could not pursue the anti-Congress edge of those identities at the Centre. The Common Programme's emphases, federalism, secularism, social justice and a reformed continuation of the economic opening of 1991, were chosen partly because they were the propositions on which a Janata Dal minister and a Congress backbencher could vote together. The perimeter explains the government's curious ideological shape, more ambitious on the structure of the federation than on the redistribution of wealth, because the federation was the question its own composition forced it to answer and redistribution was the question its backer's sensitivities fenced off. Students of the period who treat the programme as a diluted compromise miss the mechanism: the text reads the way the arithmetic required it to read, and its coherence is the coherence of a negotiated instrument rather than of a manifesto.

One further consequence of the May 1996 decision deserves record, because the standard narrative assigns it to the wrong year. The Congress's choice of backing without office established, for the first time in the party's history, that a Congress leadership could rationally prefer influence over a government to the possession of one. The preference horrified the party's older instinct, and the horror is the emotional background of Sitaram Kesri's conduct in 1997. But the preference itself, once taken, entered the party's repertoire permanently. In May 2004 the Congress would construct a government on the mirror image of the 1996 formula, accepting the premiership while leaving its Left backers outside the cabinet, and it would do so with an ease that suggests the lesson of 1996 had been filed where the party keeps its usable knowledge. The interregnums taught both of their successor generations: the Front's parties learned how to govern in coalition, and the Congress learned how to be a coalition's outside pillar and then its inside core.

## Thirteen Parties and a Compromise Candidate: Building the United Front

The United Front that took office on 1 June 1996 was an alliance of thirteen parties whose unity was procedural before it was ideological. Its constituents included the Janata Dal, the Samajwadi Party of Mulayam Singh Yadav, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam of M. Karunanidhi, the Tamil Maanila Congress of G.K. Moopanar, the Asom Gana Parishad of Prafulla Kumar Mahanta, the Telugu Desam Party of N. Chandrababu Naidu, the Communist Party of India, the Revolutionary Socialist Party and the All India Forward Bloc, with the Communist Party of India (Marxist) aligned to the alliance while staying outside the cabinet. Several of these parties were rivals in their home states. The Samajwadi Party and the Janata Dal's Bihar leadership contested overlapping electorates. The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam and the Tamil Maanila Congress had parted from the Congress in Tamil Nadu on different grievances. What held the alliance together was a steering committee, a negotiated common programme and a shared refusal to let either the Bharatiya Janata Party or the Congress hold office.

The steering committee was the Front's institutional invention, and its workings deserve the attention usually reserved for cabinets. N. Chandrababu Naidu, the Andhra Pradesh chief minister whose Telugu Desam Party held 16 seats, served as the Front's convenor, a role that made a regional leader the broker of a national government. The committee met to allocate portfolios, to arbitrate disputes among constituents and to choose, twice, the prime minister. Its existence conceded a fact that single-party governments conceal: the authority to govern India in 1996 was distributed among party leaderships, most of them seated in state capitals, and the prime minister held office as the committee's nominee rather than as the proprietor of a parliamentary majority. The Front's common programme, adopted in June 1996 as the United Front Common Programme, converted that distribution into a text. The programme committed the government to federal restraint, including a pledge against the misuse of Article 356 for the dismissal of state governments, to the continuation of economic reform with an emphasis on employment and agriculture, to social justice measures in the Mandal Commission's line of descent, and to a foreign policy of improved relations with India's neighbours.

The choice of prime minister exposed both the system's constraint and its odd flexibility. The Front's first preference among many of its constituents was Jyoti Basu, the West Bengal chief minister and the senior figure of the Indian Left. The Central Committee of the Communist Party of India (Marxist), meeting in May 1996, voted against permitting Basu to take the office, holding to the party's position of support without participation at the head of a government it could not control. Basu later described that refusal, in interviews given after his retirement, as a historic blunder, and the phrase entered the period's vocabulary. The steering committee then turned to H.D. Deve Gowda, the Janata Dal chief minister of Karnataka, a leader whose base was regional, whose party was the Front's largest, and whose candidacy threatened no other constituent. Deve Gowda's elevation was read at the time as evidence of the Front's weakness. Read institutionally, it demonstrates the arrangement's defining property: the prime ministership had become an assignable office within a coalition system, filled by negotiation among parties and revocable by the same process. That property, unremarkable in the parliamentary systems of continental Europe, was new at the head of an Indian government, and both of the period's crises would turn on it.

The search had not begun with Deve Gowda. The Front's soundings first ran to V.P. Singh, the former prime minister whose National Front government of 1989 supplied the coalition era with its founding memory and whose standing across the non-Congress parties remained unmatched. Singh declined, citing the state of his health, which required regular dialysis, and a settled reluctance to hold the office again. His refusal removed the one candidate whose authority might have stood above the steering committee rather than inside it. The committee next weighed G.K. Moopanar, the Tamil Maanila Congress leader whose twenty seats and unaligned reputation attracted support, but Moopanar lacked a base beyond his state party and the northern constituents would not converge on him. The Basu candidacy then absorbed the committee's hopes until the CPI(M) Central Committee's vote in May 1996 closed it. Each refusal narrowed the field toward the same profile: a Janata Dal leader, because the Janata Dal was the Front's largest party; a leader seated in a state rather than in Delhi, because the Front's centre of gravity was federal; and a leader whose elevation no constituent experienced as a defeat. Deve Gowda satisfied every term of the profile, and the committee's choice, taken in the final week of May 1996, was ratified by the Congress leadership's signal that it would vote for the man the Front had chosen.

Deve Gowda's own presentation of himself sharpened the experiment's meaning. He was a farmer's son from Hassan district who had risen through Karnataka's cooperative and irrigation politics, and the Kannada press's name for him, mannina maga, son of the soil, travelled with him to Delhi as both credential and caricature. He made no claim to a national mandate and said so. In his first addresses as prime minister he described himself as a man elevated by circumstances to a responsibility he had not campaigned for, and he governed in a style the Delhi establishment read as provincial: long consultations, decisions deferred to committee, and a suspicion of the capital's senior bureaucracy that his critics called inadequacy and his defenders called federal instinct. For this article's argument the style matters less than the structure it revealed. A prime minister who owed his office entirely to a committee's arithmetic had no independent source of authority to set against the committee or against the party outside the cabinet whose votes sustained him. The office had been separated from the person for the first time in the republic's history, and the separation, visible in every account of the government's workings, is the institutional fact the dismissive reading mistakes for a biographical accident.

Deve Gowda took the oath on 1 June 1996 and won his confidence vote in the Lok Sabha on 12 June 1996, with the Congress and the Left voting in support and the Bharatiya Janata Party in opposition. His cabinet distributed portfolios across the Front's constituents: P. Chidambaram of the Tamil Maanila Congress at Finance, Indrajit Gupta of the Communist Party of India at Home, Murasoli Maran of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam at Industry, Mulayam Singh Yadav of the Samajwadi Party at Defence, and I.K. Gujral of the Janata Dal at External Affairs. The cabinet was the coalition rendered as a list of names, each appointment a clause in the negotiation. The government's survival depended on two external facts: the Congress Parliamentary Party's continued willingness to vote for it, and the steering committee's continued ability to hold thirteen parties inside a single programme.

## The Architecture of Outside Support: Four Features That Outlasted the Governments

Outside support is easily described and rarely defined. In the arrangement constructed in June 1996, a party or bloc that was not in the government undertook to vote with it on confidence and supply, in exchange for a published programme and a consultative relationship, while reserving the right to withdraw when it judged the terms broken. Four features gave the 1996 design its specific character, and each of the four can be traced forward to the arrangement of 2004. The four features are set out in the four-feature institutional-pattern table below, which states each feature, records how the Deve Gowda and Gujral governments tested it between 1996 and 1998, and records the form in which the United Progressive Alliance and Left arrangement of 2004 operated it.

The first feature was a governing coalition composed of neither national party. This was more than a curiosity of arithmetic. Because the United Front excluded both the Congress and the Bharatiya Janata Party, the government's programme could not be dismissed as either party's platform in disguise, and the government's existence proved that the Lok Sabha contained a governing majority that bypassed the two organisations that had monopolised office since 1947. The second feature was the published programme as a governance constraint. The Common Programme of June 1996 was negotiated before the government formed, and constituents and supporters alike cited it in subsequent disputes as the standard against which ministerial conduct was measured. The third feature was outside support without office: the Congress supplied votes and received consultation, but no Congress member sat in the cabinet, no Congress minister answered for the government's failures, and the party's support remained a revocable political act rather than a share in power. The fourth feature was the withdrawal right, exercised issue by issue. The Congress did not withdraw in 1997 over the budget it disliked in parts, or over the foreign policy it had not written. It withdrew over named grievances, the leadership question in March 1997 and the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam question in November 1997, and it stated those grievances as the arrangement's terms being violated.

The four-feature institutional-pattern table:

| Feature | Test of 1996 to 1998 | Form in the 2004 UPA-Left arrangement |
|---|---|---|
| Non-Congress, non-BJP coalition government | United Front of thirteen parties governed from 1 June 1996 to 19 March 1998 under two prime ministers | Congress-led UPA governed with the Left outside; the coalition principle generalised to a Congress-led variant |
| Published common programme as constraint | United Front Common Programme, June 1996, cited in coalition disputes and support negotiations | National Common Minimum Programme, May 2004, adopted as the UPA government's stated policy boundary |
| Outside support without office | Congress voted confidence and supply from 1996 to 1997 and again for Gujral, holding no cabinet seat | Left Front parties supported from outside with 61 Lok Sabha seats, holding no cabinet seat |
| Withdrawal right on named issues | Congress withdrew on 30 March 1997 over leadership and on 28 November 1997 over the DMK question | Left withdrew on 8 July 2008 over the nuclear agreement, after issue-based crises and renegotiations |

### Who supported the United Front government from outside?

The Indian National Congress and the Communist Party of India (Marxist) supported the United Front government from outside. The Congress, with 140 seats, sustained both the Deve Gowda and Gujral ministries without taking cabinet office, and the CPI(M), with 32 seats, voted with the government while its Left ally the Communist Party of India sat in the cabinet.

The arrangement's bookkeeping can be followed in the Lok Sabha's divisions, and the ledger is more orderly than the period's reputation suggests. Across the twenty-one months, the House voted confidence in the government on three occasions: for Deve Gowda in June 1996, against Deve Gowda in April 1997, and for Gujral in April 1997. Between those votes, the ordinary business of supply, the demands for grants, the finance bills and the appropriation bills of two financial years, passed with the backing the arrangement promised. No money bill was lost. No session was prorogued to escape a vote. The government's defeats, when they came, arrived through the front door of the confidence procedure, moved and debated in the chamber, rather than through the side entrances of gubernatorial arithmetic or midnight defections that had closed other Indian governments. The point is not bookkeeping pedantry. A coalition method exists to convert a fragmented chamber's preferences into a governable sequence of decisions, and the conversion is measurable precisely in the votes the method wins and the manner of the votes it loses. By that measure the 1996 design functioned until the days its backer withdrew, and the withdrawal itself was executed through the design's own clause. Systems are known by their failure modes. This one's failure mode was a letter, a debate and a division, conducted in daylight, and the normalisation of that failure mode in Indian practice is among the interregnums' least celebrated bequests.

One feature the 1996 design conspicuously lacked deserves separate notice, because its absence structured both crises and its correction in 2004 confirmed the diagnosis. The arrangement created no standing body in which the government and its outside backer could process disputes. The United Front had a steering committee for its own constituents; the Congress had its Working Committee and its parliamentary leadership; between the two there was only the personal channel joining the prime minister to the Congress president. Grievances therefore accumulated without a forum in which they could be priced cheaply. A complaint about consultation, raised inside a joint committee in the government's second month, is an agenda item. The same complaint, stored for eight months and then published as a reason for withdrawal, is a casus belli. Kesri's March 1997 letter reads, in its enumeration of unconsulted decisions and unreturned courtesies, as the minutes of meetings that never happened. The 2004 arrangement's coordination committee, in which the governing alliance and the Left processed their disputes on a schedule, was the institutional answer to precisely this defect, and its invention is unintelligible except as a correction drafted from the 1996 record.

The architecture had a federal logic that the Front's composition made unavoidable, and the constitutional background is the one traced in [federalism in the Indian constitution](/2019/01/03/federalism-indian-constitution-explained/). A coalition of state-based parties, led by a steering committee convened by a chief minister, could not plausibly run a centralising government. The Common Programme's Article 356 pledge was the architecture written as policy: the Centre promised restraint toward state governments because the Centre was, in composition, a congress of state leaderships. Outside support completed the design by keeping the largest opposition party inside the majority and outside the ministry, a position that gave the Congress influence without responsibility and gave the government votes without a partner it could not discipline. The design's weakness is visible in the same sentence as its strength. A supporter with influence and no responsibility holds a permanent option to renegotiate by threat, and the history of the two governments is the history of that option being priced, twice, at the government's life.

## Governing Anyway: The Policy Record of the Deve Gowda Months

The strongest evidence against the failed-interludes reading is the Deve Gowda government's own output, which was neither caretaker in scope nor trivial in consequence. The precedent for a non-majority government legislating seriously had been set by [the 1991 minority-government precedent](/2019/05/26/1991-rao-government-silent-reforms/), in which P.V. Narasimha Rao's Congress ministry converted a minority into a full term and an economic transformation. The United Front worked under a harder version of the same condition: Rao had led a single party that could grow toward a majority, while Deve Gowda led thirteen parties that could only hold together. The government's strategy, visible across its ten months, was to move fastest where the Common Programme had already settled the argument, and to convert the coalition's breadth from a survival problem into a ratifying mechanism for decisions a single-party government might have lacked the reach to carry.

The centrepiece was the budget of 28 February 1997. Finance Minister P. Chidambaram presented a budget that cut the peak personal income tax rate to 30 percent, reduced the corporate tax rate for domestic companies to 35 percent, lowered customs duties toward East Asian levels, and paired the rate cuts with a widening of the tax base and a minimum alternate tax on companies that reported profits and paid nothing. The Indian press named it the dream budget within days, and the name held because the exercise reconciled constituencies the coalition was supposed to make irreconcilable: industry received lower rates, the Left received the alternate tax and expanded social allocations, and the fiscal deficit target was trimmed to 5 percent of gross domestic product. The budget's passage is the cleanest refutation of the claim that outside support paralysed governance. The Congress, which would destroy the government five weeks later, voted the budget through, because the arrangement's programme bound the supporter as well as the government on the questions the programme had settled.

### What was the dream budget of February 1997?

The dream budget was the Union budget presented by Finance Minister P. Chidambaram on 28 February 1997 under the Deve Gowda government. It cut the peak personal income tax rate to 30 percent, reduced corporate tax rates, lowered customs duties and introduced a minimum alternate tax, earning its press nickname from the breadth of its reception.

The same budget launched the Voluntary Disclosure of Income Scheme, which ran to 31 December 1997 and drew declarations from more than 350,000 declarants, yielding tax collections estimated between Rs 7,800 crore and Rs 10,000 crore according to the Finance Ministry's reporting of the scheme's outcome. A disclosure scheme is an administrative instrument rather than a statute, and its inclusion here matters for a narrower reason: it was a politically exposed decision, an amnesty offered by a government dependent on Left support and Congress sufferance, and it was carried without a coalition crisis. The regulatory record shows the same pattern. The Telecom Regulatory Authority of India was created by ordinance in January 1997 and placed on a statutory footing by the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India Act of 1997, separating the regulation of telecommunications from the department that operated the network. Work on replacing the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act with a management-oriented statute, the drafting that produced the Foreign Exchange Management Act later in the decade, proceeded inside the Finance Ministry during the same months. Each measure used the Common Programme as its warrant. Students of the period who reproduce the budget speech and the programme side by side for comparison do so most legibly in a [PowerPoint viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) that preserves the original documents' layout, and the comparison shows how much of the government's legislative energy was spent executing a text agreed before the government existed.

The federal commitments were executed with the same literalism. No state government was dismissed under Article 356 during the United Front's tenure, a restraint without precedent in the preceding decades of Congress and Janata practice alike, and the government convened chief ministers on the devolution questions the Common Programme had flagged. Deve Gowda toured the north-eastern states in October 1996 and announced an economic package for the region, an early instance of a prime minister treating the periphery's development backlog as a first-order national question. None of this required a majority. All of it required the arrangement: a programme negotiated in advance, a backer bound to the programme rather than to the ministry, and constituents whose state-level interests the federal pledges directly served.

The government's prime ministerial office itself registered the change of regime. Deve Gowda staffed his secretariat from Karnataka, bringing T.R. Satish Chandran, a former chief secretary of that state, to Delhi as his principal secretary, and he ran the office with a chief minister's habits transplanted to the capital: petitioners received in person, files pursued through the state governments that had sent the petitioners, and a suspicion of the Delhi secretariat's instinct to centralise every question in its own committees. The capital's commentary treated the style as a deficiency of polish. Its structural meaning was plainer. The prime ministership had been occupied, for the first time, by a man whose political formation was entirely post-Mandal and entirely federal, whose party existed as a federation of state units, and whose instincts on the questions the Common Programme had flagged, river waters, devolution, the treatment of state governments, were the instincts of the state side of the table. When the Cauvery waters dispute between Karnataka and Tamil Nadu pressed upon his government, the prime minister was a former Karnataka irrigation minister negotiating with a Tamil Nadu governed by his own coalition's second-largest constituent, and the dispute was managed inside the coalition's forums rather than by central fiat. The interregnum's defenders rarely cite this texture. It is, however, the texture of the federal promise being kept in the only way it could be kept: by a government whose composition made centralisation personally and politically unavailable to it.

## The Winter of Strain: A Government Learning the Price of Its Backing

The government's difficulties through the winter of 1996 to 1997 were not a single crisis but an education, delivered in instalments, in what the outside arrangement actually cost each side. The first instalment was economic. The Common Programme had promised reform with a human face, a phrase the period used constantly, and the Finance Ministry's disinvestment programme tested the phrase's elasticity. When the government moved to sell minority stakes in public sector undertakings and to grant the strongest of them greater managerial autonomy as navratna enterprises, the Left parties, inside and outside the cabinet, objected that the programme's employment commitments were being traded away. The disputes were settled inside the steering committee by slowing the sales and restating the criteria, a small rehearsal of crisis and renegotiation conducted at ministerial level. The rehearsal succeeded because the stakes were divisible. It taught the participants a habit, settle by adjustment, that the arrangement's later crises would find unusable, because the stakes in those crises were a prime minister's continuation and a constituent's honour, which do not divide.

The second instalment was the relationship between the prime minister and the Congress president, and it deteriorated on a schedule set by Congress's internal calendar rather than by the government's conduct. Kesri's election to the Congress presidency in the autumn of 1996, and his assumption of the Congress Parliamentary Party's leadership on 3 January 1997, converted a relationship between offices into a rivalry between men. Kesri had not designed the outside arrangement and owed it nothing. His party organisation, which he was attempting to rebuild after the 1996 rout, experienced the arrangement as a daily humiliation: Congress workers in the states confronted United Front constituents as rivals in every local contest while their national leadership voted to keep those rivals in office in Delhi. The grievance was structural before it was personal, and Kesri's public complaints through the winter, that the government took decisions on disinvestment, on appointments and on the handling of communal organisations without the consultation the Congress had been promised, gave the structural grievance a personal voice. Deve Gowda's responses compounded the difficulty. He insisted in interviews and in the Front's internal meetings that he was the prime minister of the country and not the nominee of any party's convenience, a correct constitutional statement that the Congress leadership heard as ingratitude from a man its votes sustained.

The third instalment arrived from Uttar Pradesh, where the state election of October 1996 had produced a hung assembly and a continuation of President's Rule, and where the Bahujan Samaj Party and the Bharatiya Janata Party constructed in March 1997 a rotational arrangement that made Mayawati chief minister with BJP backing on 21 March 1997. The episode is peripheral to the government's survival and central to its meaning. A Front committed against the misuse of Article 356 watched a state government form through floor arithmetic it deplored and did not intervene, because intervention was the practice its programme had forsworn and because several of its constituents preferred any Uttar Pradesh outcome that kept the Congress from recovering the state. The Congress, which had governed Uttar Pradesh for most of the republic's life and had been reduced there to a remnant, read the Front's restraint as collusion with its enemies. By the time the Congress Working Committee met in the spring of 1997, the party's internal argument had simplified itself: the arrangement was costing the Congress its identity in the states and returning nothing it could show its workers. Kesri's letter of 30 March 1997 was written inside that simplification. It was less a verdict on the Deve Gowda government's record than a decision by the backing party that the price of the arrangement, reckoned in its own organisational currency, had exceeded the value of the government it bought.

Deve Gowda's conduct in the eleven days between the letter and the vote supplies the period's most quoted sentence and its most revealing procedural fact. He declined the option, pressed on him by intermediaries from both camps, of resigning on the announcement of withdrawal and negotiating his succession from outside office. He required the Congress to vote his government down in the Lok Sabha, in the open, on a confidence motion he himself moved. His speech in the debate of 11 April 1997, recorded in the Lok Sabha debates of the 1997 budget session, contained the sentence the Irish and Indian press carried the following morning: he had never, ever aspired to the office. The sentence is usually quoted as humility. In context it was an accusation and a constitutional argument in one breath: the office had been given by an arrangement, the arrangement's terms were being rewritten by one party to it, and the rewriting would be done publicly or not at all. The government fell by 292 votes to 158 that evening. The procedural fact is that the arrangement's death was executed through the arrangement's own forms, a confidence motion debated and divided upon, and that orderliness, in a decade when governments had also fallen by midnight letters and gubernatorial reports, is part of what the interregnums normalised.

## The First Withdrawal: Sitaram Kesri and the Fall of April 1997

The arrangement's first stress test came from the supporting party's internal politics, and the sequence is unintelligible without the Congress leadership transition of 1996. P.V. Narasimha Rao, defeated in the general election and facing prosecution in corruption cases arising from his tenure, resigned the Congress presidency in September 1996. Sitaram Kesri, the party's treasurer of seventeen years and a Rajya Sabha member from Bihar, succeeded him, taking the presidency in the autumn of 1996 and the leadership of the Congress Parliamentary Party on 3 January 1997. Kesri inherited a party that had sustained the United Front as Rao's strategy. It was not Kesri's strategy. His calculation, as Inder Malhotra's chronicle of the period records and as Congress insiders of the time described it, was that propping up a Janata Dal prime minister from Karnataka consolidated nothing for the Congress, that Deve Gowda's independence of manner toward the Congress leadership advertised the supporter's subordination, and that a change of United Front leader would restore the Congress to the centre of the arrangement on which its votes conferred life.

Kesri's own formation made him the arrangement's unlikely executioner. He was a Congressman of the old school in the literal sense: drawn into the freedom movement as a boy in Bihar, elected to the Lok Sabha from Katihar in 1967, a minister in the governments of Indira Gandhi, Rajiv Gandhi and Narasimha Rao, and the party's treasurer for seventeen years, an office that made him the custodian of its funds and much of its organisational memory. Men formed in that Congress understood power as something the party held or lent at its pleasure, and the outside arrangement of 1996 offended the understanding at its root: the Congress was lending, indefinitely, to a formation of parties it regarded as its subordinates in the natural order, several of them led by men who had begun their careers in Congress ranks. Kesri's statements through the winter return to the theme of the government's ingratitude with a consistency that suggests doctrine rather than pique. In his reading, the Front owed the Congress not merely consultation but deference, the tribute a tenant pays a landlord. Deve Gowda's refusal to pay it, his references to his mandate from the Front's constituents and his public indifference to the Congress president's displeasure, converted a negotiable grievance about procedure into a question of the Congress's dignity, on which its president could not be seen to settle cheaply. The arrangement died of many causes in April 1997. The incompatibility of the two men's theories of what the Congress's votes had purchased is the cause that made the others unmanageable.

The grievances were assembled through the winter. Kesri complained publicly that the government did not consult the Congress on decisions the party was expected to ratify, that Deve Gowda had failed to act against communal organisations with the vigour the Congress demanded, and that prices and unemployment were rising under a government the Congress sustained without sharing credit. On 30 March 1997, Kesri wrote to President Shankar Dayal Sharma withdrawing Congress backing from the Deve Gowda government. The letter is the fourth feature of the architecture operating exactly as designed and exactly as feared: the supporter named its grievances, invoked its reserved right, and converted a conditional arrangement into a terminated one by a single communication. Deve Gowda, refusing the Congress suggestion that he resign in advance of a vote, sought a confidence motion in the Lok Sabha. On 11 April 1997 the motion was defeated by 292 votes to 158, and the first United Front government fell, ten months and ten days after taking office, with its budget passed and its programme largely unexecuted beyond the first instalments.

One transaction of the interregnum between the fall and the succession states the article's thesis in miniature. The budget of 28 February 1997, the fallen government's principal achievement, had been presented but not enacted when the confidence vote was lost; the Finance Bill and the appropriation measures had to be passed by 14 May 1997 or the tax proposals would lapse and the state's finances with them. The Gujral government, formed on 21 April 1997, carried its predecessor's budget through Parliament substantially unaltered, and the Congress, which had destroyed the budget's author, voted for the budget. Industry associations had appealed publicly during the April crisis for exactly this outcome, and the political system delivered it without treating the outcome as remarkable. It was remarkable. A finance bill is the most partisan instrument a government possesses, and in April 1997 it passed from a dead ministry to a successor ministry of the same arrangement without revision, because the text belonged to the Common Programme's logic rather than to Deve Gowda's person. The episode is the cleanest available demonstration that the arrangement, not the ministry, was the period's durable political object: ministries fell in April 1997, and the programme's fiscal chapter did not notice.

The aftermath is more instructive than the fall. The United Front did not dissolve. The steering committee met, consulted the Congress, and concluded the only bargain the arithmetic allowed: the Front would continue in office under a new leader, and the Congress would resume its backing. Deve Gowda was replaced, not the arrangement. The contrast with an earlier model of prime ministerial authority is sharp enough to state plainly. In the period of [Indira Gandhi as personality and institution](/2019/03/21/indira-gandhi-personality-institution/), the leader and the government were a single fact, and removing the leader meant removing the regime. In April 1997 the regime survived its leader's removal by a fortnight of negotiation, because the government's foundation was a text and a tally of seats rather than a person. I.K. Gujral, the external affairs minister, acceptable to the Congress leadership and unencumbered by a state machine of his own, was chosen by the steering committee and endorsed by the Congress. He took office on 21 April 1997. The system had performed its first leader replacement, an operation the Indian republic had previously managed only through elections, deaths and party splits.

## The Gujral Doctrine: The Interregnum's Foreign Policy

I.K. Gujral came to the prime ministership with a formed foreign policy, and the doctrine that carries his name was the United Front period's most durable single export. Gujral had been external affairs minister under Deve Gowda from June 1996, and in that office he set out the principles that journalists and scholars codified as the Gujral Doctrine: that India, as the largest power in South Asia, should give generously to its smaller neighbours without demanding reciprocity; that no South Asian country should allow its territory to be used against another's interests; that disputes should be settled bilaterally and peacefully; and that the states of the region should respect each other's sovereignty and refrain from interference in internal affairs. The doctrine's core move was the first principle, non-reciprocity, which inverted the bargaining posture Indian diplomacy had inherited. Where earlier governments had traded concession for concession with Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka and the Maldives, Gujral proposed that India pay the premium for regional stability out of its own asymmetry, on the reasoning that a subcontinent at ease with Indian predominance served Indian interests better than a subcontinent organised against it.

The doctrine was practice before it was theory. The Ganges Water Sharing Treaty between India and Bangladesh, signed in New Delhi on 12 December 1996 by Deve Gowda and Sheikh Hasina, settled the Farakka dispute that had poisoned relations between the two countries for two decades, on terms that gave Bangladesh guaranteed flows in the lean season without extracting a reciprocal Bangladeshi concession of equivalent weight. Gujral negotiated the treaty as external affairs minister, and its signature six months into the United Front's tenure demonstrated that a coalition government dependent on outside support could still conduct a foreign policy of initiative. The Mahakali Treaty with Nepal, ratified in the same period, applied the doctrine's mutual-respect principles to the other open file of the northern rivers.

### When did I.K. Gujral become Prime Minister?

I.K. Gujral became Prime Minister of India on 21 April 1997, succeeding H.D. Deve Gowda after the United Front steering committee chose him as its new leader and the Congress agreed to resume the outside support it had withdrawn on 30 March 1997. Gujral had served as external affairs minister in the Deve Gowda cabinet.

The doctrine's hardest application was Pakistan, where non-reciprocity had to survive contact with the adversary the doctrine's critics considered its refutation. Gujral met Pakistan's prime minister Nawaz Sharif at the SAARC summit at Male in May 1997, weeks after taking office, and the two governments agreed to resume the structured dialogue between their foreign secretaries that had lapsed for three years; the talks reopened in 1997 and produced the framework of a composite dialogue addressing all outstanding questions in parallel baskets. The gains were procedural rather than substantive, and the doctrine's Indian critics, writing in the strategic journals of the period, objected that generosity offered without reciprocity to a state whose army sponsored militancy in Kashmir was sentiment misread as strategy. Gujral's answer, given in Parliament and in his public defences of the doctrine, was that the doctrine was addressed in the first instance to the smaller neighbours, where India's predominance was the fact to be managed, and that even toward Pakistan the alternative to structured engagement was a permanent crisis conducted at higher cost. The disagreement is recorded here because it defines the doctrine's actual claim: not that generosity dissolves conflict, but that a predominant power buys more security by underwriting its region's stability than by invoicing each neighbour for it. That claim, and the criticism it attracted, both survived the government that authored them, which is more than the dismissive reading of the interregnums can explain.

As prime minister, Gujral kept the external affairs portfolio in his own hands, the only major ministry he retained, and the doctrine's later elaboration belongs to his premiership as much as to his ministership. The doctrine matters to this article's argument for a structural reason. Foreign policy was the one domain in which the United Front government's freedom of action was nearly complete, because the Common Programme's commitments were permissive and the supporting party's sensitivities were not engaged. In that domain the government produced the period's most coherent achievement. The correlation is the point: the outside-support arrangement constrained the government where the programme and the supporter had positions, and freed it where they did not. Later treatments of the Gujral Doctrine in the foreign policy pillar of this series take up the doctrine's afterlife; for the coalition question, the doctrine stands as evidence that the interregnum governments were policy-producing entities whose failures were specific rather than general.

## The Second Withdrawal: The Jain Commission and November 1997

The second fall was set in motion by a document. The assassination of Rajiv Gandhi at Sriperumbudur on 21 May 1991 had been investigated by a commission of inquiry under Justice M.C. Jain, appointed in 1991 to establish the conspiracy behind the killing and the failures that permitted it. The Commission's interim report, laid before Parliament in November 1997, examined the political environment in Tamil Nadu in the years before the assassination and recorded findings on the relationship between the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam and the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam, concluding that the DMK's leadership had provided an atmosphere of political encouragement and shelter in which the LTTE operated in Tamil Nadu during the period when the DMK held office in the state, from 1989 to 1991. The chapters of the interim report dealing with the DMK and the LTTE are the under-read primary source of the entire episode: the standard accounts of the Gujral government's fall cite the report's political effect exhaustively and engage its evidentiary chapters hardly at all.

The Commission's own history explains both its authority and its timing. Appointed in 1991 by the government of P.V. Narasimha Rao and chaired by Justice M.C. Jain, a former Chief Justice of the Delhi High Court, it worked for six years through the documentary residue of the assassination: the security arrangements at Sriperumbudur, the intelligence warnings that preceded the rally, the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam's networks in Tamil Nadu, and the political shelter those networks had found in the state's competitive Dravidian politics of the late 1980s, when the DMK government of M. Karunanidhi held office in the state from 1989 until its dismissal in January 1991. The interim report of 1997 addressed the conspiracy's wider environment rather than its mechanics, and its chapters on the DMK recorded the judgment that the party's leadership had tolerated and encouraged an atmosphere in which the LTTE moved freely, a judgment the DMK contested as a conflation of political sympathy with operational complicity. The final report followed in 1998. For the Gujral government's fate, the distinction between the interim and the final text was academic. The interim report was the document in the field in November 1997, its findings were the ones the Congress could quote, and its status as an interim product, partial and open to revision, was the ground on which Gujral stood when he refused to treat it as a verdict. Both parties to the quarrel were therefore reading the same document correctly for their purposes: the Congress read its findings, the prime minister read its status, and the arrangement had no procedure for deciding which reading governed.

The Congress read the interim report as a test the arrangement could not pass. The DMK was a constituent of the United Front government, holding cabinet office through ministers including Murasoli Maran. The Congress leadership under Sitaram Kesri demanded that Gujral dismiss the DMK ministers and expel the party from the Front, presenting the demand as the minimum response a Congress that had lost its own leader to the assassination could make to a report connecting a governing party to the assassins' political cover. Gujral refused. His stated ground, given in his communications with the Congress leadership and defended in the Lok Sabha debates of November 1997, was that an interim report's findings against a constituent could not be executed as a sentence by the prime minister without destroying the coalition's federal premise: the DMK denied the characterisation of its record, the findings were interim and contested, and a prime minister who expelled constituents on a supporter's instruction would convert outside support into outside government. The refusal was the architecture defending itself. It was also fatal to the government.

The Congress withdrew its backing on 28 November 1997. Gujral resigned the same day rather than face a confidence vote the arithmetic had already decided. The dissolution that followed was not instantaneous, and the interval is instructive. The United Front and its Left backers asked President K.R. Narayanan for time to explore whether an alternative arrangement could be assembled inside the existing Lok Sabha, and the President, following the constitutional courtesies the period consistently observed, allowed the exploration its days. No alternative existed: the BJP would not sustain a Front government, the Front would not join a Congress government on the terms the Congress could offer after the Jain report, and the Congress itself shrank from claiming an office its numbers could not hold. Narayanan dissolved the Lok Sabha on 4 December 1997, and Gujral's ministry continued in a caretaker capacity through the election. The caretaker months were conducted with a restraint that the period's critics rarely credit. A government that had lost its majority made no irreversible appointments spree, rushed no ordinances through the President's office, and contented itself with the administration of the election it had lost the right to postpone. The conventions of caretaker government in India, which later decades would cite as settled practice, were being written in those months by a ministry with every incentive of wounded pride to break them.

The election campaign of January and February 1998 supplied the period's epilogue in advance. The United Front, which had governed as a unit, campaigned as a scattered convention of its parts: the Janata Dal's fragments contested against each other in Bihar, the Front's constituents cut local arrangements that contradicted the national posture, and no common manifesto bound them. The Congress, by contrast, acquired in the campaign's course the instrument of its recovery, when Sonia Gandhi entered active campaigning in January 1998 and addressed her first great rally at Sriperumbudur, the site of her husband's assassination, converting the election for her party into a referendum on restoration. The contrast completes the interregnums' double lesson. As a government, the Front had demonstrated that thirteen parties could administer a programme and a budget under outside backing. As an electoral machine, it demonstrated the opposite limit: a coalition assembled for the Lok Sabha's arithmetic could not automatically become a coalition assembled for the electorate's verdict, and the parties of 2004 would correct for that limit too, stitching their alliances before the poll rather than after it. Gujral continued as caretaker prime minister through the general election of February and March 1998, in which the Bharatiya Janata Party won 182 seats and the Congress 141, and he handed office to Atal Bihari Vajpayee on 19 March 1998. The interim report's authors had not designed it as an instrument of coalition politics. It functioned as one because the outside-support arrangement had made the supporting party the arbiter of the government's composition, and because the grievance it supplied, the memory of a murdered Congress leader, was the one grievance on which the Congress could not be asked to compromise and remain itself.

### Why did President K.R. Narayanan return the Uttar Pradesh advice in February 1998?

President K.R. Narayanan returned the Gujral cabinet's advice to impose President's Rule in Uttar Pradesh on 21 February 1998, after the state government's contested floor test, asking the cabinet to reconsider. The cabinet let the matter drop. The episode, in the caretaker weeks of the United Front's end, marked a presidential check on Article 356.

The Uttar Pradesh episode belongs in this record because it completes the period's federal account. A caretaker coalition cabinet, in the last month of its existence, recommended the dismissal of a state government of the opposing party, and a President who had himself been elected with the Front's support returned the advice for reconsideration, an assertion of presidential discretion without precedent since the Constitution's framing. The Common Programme's Article 356 pledge had been framed against the Congress practice of earlier decades. Its hardest test came from the Front's own cabinet, and the pledge survived because the President enforced the spirit the programme had announced. Institutional patterns are visible in such survivals. The restraint of 1996 to 1998 on Article 356, maintained first by commitment and finally by presidential return, fed directly into the jurisprudence and the political convention that governed the provision afterwards.

## The Front Consumes Itself: Bihar, the Fodder Scam and the Janata Dal Split

The Gujral government's second vulnerability was not the Congress at all. It was the disintegration, in office, of the coalition's own largest constituent. The Janata Dal held the prime ministership twice over, through Deve Gowda and then through Gujral himself, and across the same months the party tore itself apart in Bihar. The animal husbandry scandal known as the fodder scam, in which public funds drawn against fictitious fodder supplies had been looted in Bihar across a decade, reached its political detonation in 1997. The Central Bureau of Investigation, pursuing the case under the supervision of the Patna High Court, moved against the Bihar chief minister Laloo Prasad Yadav, the Janata Dal's most powerful state leader and the president of the party. In July 1997, with a chargesheet filed and arrest imminent, Laloo Prasad Yadav broke the Janata Dal, carrying his supporters into a formation of his own, the Rashtriya Janata Dal, and resigned the Bihar chief ministership on 25 July 1997, installing his wife Rabri Devi in the office the same day.

The institutional consequences ran in several directions at once. The prime minister of India belonged to a party whose presidency had been seized by his rival: Sharad Yadav, aligned with the faction opposed to Laloo Prasad Yadav, took the Janata Dal presidency in the party elections of 1997, so that Gujral governed as the leader of a parliamentary coalition whose core party he did not control and whose largest state unit had seceded to a rival formation. The United Front's steering committee absorbed the shock because the Front's architecture did not require its constituents to be internally whole; it required only their parliamentary votes, and Laloo Prasad Yadav's Rashtriya Janata Dal continued to vote with the Front in the Lok Sabha even after the split. The episode therefore refines the article's central distinction. A single-party government whose core party splits in office usually dies of the split, as the Congress governments of the late 1960s and the Janata government of 1979 attest in their different ways. The United Front government survived the split of its largest party for a further eight months, because its constitution was a tally of blocs and a text, and the blocs' internal arrangements were their own affair. The arrangement's resilience and its hollowness were the same property viewed from two sides: nothing inside a constituent could break the government, and nothing inside a constituent could save it either.

The fodder scam crisis also disciplined the government's conduct in a way the standard narrative undervalues. Gujral declined to use the Centre's instruments against the Bihar government through the crisis of July 1997, although the state was governed by a breakaway faction of his own party and although the governor's reports from Patna supplied material on which a more interventionist Centre could have acted. The restraint was partly conviction and partly arithmetic. The Common Programme's federal pledges bound him, the Front's other state parties were watching the precedent for application to themselves, and a prime minister installed by a steering committee of state leaders could not dismiss a state government and survive the committee's next meeting. The episode belongs in the balance sheet of the interregnums beside the budget and the doctrine: a Centre that refrained, on stated principle and visible interest, from the interventionist habits of the preceding half-century, at the moment of its own maximum provocation.

## Two Falls and One Pattern: Adjudicating the Failed-Interludes Reading

The case for the failed-interludes reading deserves its strongest form before it is answered. That form runs as follows. A governmental method that cannot keep a government alive for a year is not a method but a symptom. Both United Front ministries were destroyed by the same structural defect, a supporter with the power to kill and no stake in survival, and a technique whose first two applications end in collapse within twenty-one months has demonstrated instability, not transferability. The comparison that the dismissive reading invites is with [the 1977 Janata coalition's institutional fragility](/2019/04/28/1977-janata-verdict-explained/): the Janata Party of 1977 also assembled a broad anti-Congress formation, also quarrelled over leadership, and also fell inside three years, and the lesson drawn from 1977 was that such formations carry their dissolution inside them. On this reading, 1996 to 1998 is 1977 with the fragmentation deepened, and the interesting political science begins in 1998, when the Bharatiya Janata Party learned to pre-build its coalition and hold it for a full term.

The evidence assembled in this article supports a different adjudication, and the distinction on which it turns is between the instability of a model and the instability of the features composing it. The specific coalitions of 1996 to 1998 were unstable for reasons internal to their membership: a supporting party in leadership transition in 1997, and a constituent compromised by a commission of inquiry in the same year. Neither reason indicts the four features. The features are separable from the membership, and the test of a feature is whether a different membership can operate it for longer. Pradeep Chhibber's Democracy without Associations, published in 1999, supplies the analytical ground: the party system that emerged after 1989 was not a temporary splintering but a re-foundation, in which state parties with durable social bases had become permanent suppliers of national majorities. In a re-founded system, the relevant institutional question is not how to restore single-party rule but how to write the operating rules of coalition. Suhas Palshikar's essays in Economic and Political Weekly on the post-1996 party system, and Yogendra Yadav's analyses of the same years, treated the United Front experiment in exactly this register, as a draft of rules composed under pressure. Drafts are judged by what the final text retains.

Yadav's formulation deserves its precision. In his analyses of the 1996 verdict and its sequels, he described Indian politics as having entered a third electoral system, succeeding the Congress system of the first decades and the transitional disorder of 1989 to 1991, in which the states had become the effective units of political competition and national governments were aggregations of state verdicts. A third electoral system so defined cannot be governed by the instruments of the first. It requires exactly the technologies the interregnums improvised: negotiated programmes in place of manifestos, steering committees in place of high commands, and prime ministers who are brokers of a coalition rather than proprietors of a majority. Chhibber's companion argument, that India's parties had shed the associational linkages that once connected them to organised society and therefore competed as electoral machines around state-level leaderships, explains why the brokerage had to be continuous: a coalition of machines holds together only while the terms are being actively serviced. Read through these two lenses, the falls of 1997 are not anomalies interrupting a stable norm. They are the tuition fees of a system learning its own operating costs, paid by the first governments required to operate it.

Inder Malhotra, whose biographical and journalistic work on the period remains the standard narrative source, recorded the interregnums' failures in unsparing detail and still resisted the conclusion that the period was negligible, because the men and parties of 2004 conducted themselves as students of 1996. The Congress that sustained the United Front had learned what outside support cost the supporter: influence without credit, and responsibility without office, resented by the party's own organisation. The Left parties that sustained the United Front from outside had learned the companion lesson, that support given without a written programme and a consultative mechanism degenerates into a series of ambushes. The United Front's constituents learned the government's lesson, that a common programme is the only currency in which a supporting party can be paid and the only shield a prime minister holds. Each of these lessons was available in 2004 because 1996 to 1998 had taught it at the price of two governments.

The comparison with the arrangement's one true predecessor sharpens the measure of the advance. In November 1990, the Congress had sustained Chandra Shekhar's breakaway ministry from outside, in an arrangement without a negotiated programme, without a consultative mechanism and without any public statement of the terms on which the backing would continue. That backing was withdrawn in March 1991 over the surveillance of Rajiv Gandhi's residence by two Haryana policemen, a grievance whose triviality against the scale of the consequence advertised the arrangement's lawlessness: where no terms are published, any incident can be made a term retrospectively. The 1996 design reads, clause by clause, as the correction of 1990. A programme was published so that breaches could be identified against a text. The grievances of 1997, a leader's acceptability and a constituent's implication in an assassination's prehistory, were grave in a way the grievance of 1991 was not, and they were argued in the vocabulary of the arrangement itself. The interregnums did not invent backing from outside. They civilised it, by giving it documents, procedures and a jurisprudence of precedent, and the civilisation held: no subsequent Indian arrangement of the kind has been constructed without its programme, its forum and its stated exit.

The Janata comparison, pressed to its conclusion, cuts against the dismissive reading. The Janata formation of 1977 merged its constituents into a single party and thereby destroyed the constituents' separate accountability; when the merged party quarrelled, there was no architecture left to negotiate within, and the experiment vanished so completely that its successor formation of 1989 had to be built from parts. The United Front never merged. Its parties kept their names, their state organisations and their exit options, and its method therefore survived its governments: the Front as an organisation faded after 1998, but the operating rules it drafted, programme first, support from outside, withdrawal on named issues, passed into the common practice of Indian coalition politics. A formation whose rules outlive it has a different historical status from a formation that merely falls, and the status is the one this article claims for the interregnums.

## The Inheritance: The Model Rebuilt in 2004

The general election of April and May 2004 returned the Congress and its allies to the largest position in the Lok Sabha, and the government formed that month was the United Front arrangement rebuilt by its former supporter. The United Progressive Alliance, led by the Congress under Manmohan Singh, took office on 22 May 2004. The Left Front, whose principal party had sustained the United Front from outside in 1996, sustained the UPA from outside with 61 Lok Sabha seats. The correspondences with 1996 are exact enough to enumerate. A National Common Minimum Programme was adopted on 27 May 2004, negotiated between the governing alliance and the outside supporter before the government's policy course was set, and cited thereafter by both sides as the boundary of legitimate action. A coordination mechanism between the UPA and the Left met through the government's life, performing the consultative function whose absence the Congress had cited among its grievances against Deve Gowda.

The inheritance extended to the choice of the prime minister himself, the most sensitive operation the interregnums had normalised. When Sonia Gandhi, having led the Congress to its plurality, declined the premiership in May 2004 and named Manmohan Singh in her place, the operation performed was the steering-committee manoeuvre of 1996 and 1997 translated into a single party's internal process: the leader with the electoral claim assigned the office to the figure the coalition's arithmetic and the backers' vetoes could sustain. Manmohan Singh, an economist without a state machine or a parliamentary faction, resembled Gujral in the structural properties that mattered, seniority, acceptability across the arrangement, and an authority derived from the office's assignment rather than from an independent base. The UPA's coordination committee then institutionalised the consultative channel whose absence had killed Deve Gowda. Its meetings brought the government's senior ministers together with the Left's leadership, Prakash Karat and Sitaram Yechury of the CPI(M), A.B. Bardhan and D. Raja of the Communist Party of India among them, on a recurring schedule, and the committee's existence converted the Left's grievances from withdrawal threats into agenda items for most of four years. The committee did not abolish the exit option. It priced it, by giving the backer a cheaper instrument to use first, and the four-year life of the arrangement, against the twenty-one months of its prototype, is in large part the biography of that single design correction. The supporter reserved, and was understood to reserve, the right to withdraw on issues it named in advance, of which the civilian nuclear agreement with the United States was the largest.

The rebuilt model ran for more than four years, and its crises followed the 1996 grammar with the volume lowered. Disputes over disinvestment, over foreign investment in insurance and retail, and over the pace of the programme's social commitments produced the period's characteristic sequence: a Left note of dissent, a negotiation, a governmental adjustment or a deferral, and a resumption. Crisis and renegotiation, which in 1997 had been the prelude to two funerals, became in 2005 and 2006 a routine instrument of coalition management, because both parties had priced the exit option and preferred the adjustment. When the end came, it came on the feature the model had always contained. The Left withdrew its support on 8 July 2008 over the government's decision to proceed with the nuclear agreement at the International Atomic Energy Agency. The government sought a confidence vote on 22 July 2008 and won it by 275 votes to 256, surviving on votes rearranged from other parties, and the arrangement's eight-year line of descent from the Common Programme of June 1996 closed with the withdrawal right exercised, honoured and survived.

The inheritance did not lapse when the arithmetic that required it changed. The UPA's second term, formed in 2009 with a larger Congress contingent, no longer needed an outside pillar, yet the government continued to govern through a published programme negotiated with its allies, through coordination with partners whose ministries it could not command, and through the management of named-issue disputes by adjustment and deferral, including the long negotiation over the nuclear liability legislation that the 2008 crisis had left behind. Habits of this kind are the residue institutions leave when their originating conditions pass. A political class that had learned coalition as an emergency procedure in 1996 had, by the end of the following decade, absorbed it as the default grammar of national government, available even to majorities. The grammar's persistence through the years of single-party majority after 2014, when it lay dormant for want of a fragmented chamber, is a question for the pillar that treats those years. For the period under examination, the transmission is complete and documented at every joint: text, forum, exit clause and the leader-assignment operation all travelled from the broken governments of 1996 to 1998 to the durable government of 2004 to 2008, and each travelled in the custody of politicians who had operated, opposed or observed the prototype at close quarters.

Feature by feature, the inheritance is traceable. The 2004 programme was written tighter than the programme of 1996 because the drafters of 2004 had watched a loose programme fail to protect a government. The 2004 coordination committee existed because 1996 had shown what a supporter without a forum becomes. The 2004 withdrawal, when it came, was announced on a named issue with months of notice, because the ambush withdrawals of 1997 had taught the supporter the reputational cost of the alternative. None of these refinements required the United Front governments to have succeeded. They required the United Front governments to have happened, in public, with their texts published and their crises argued out in the Lok Sabha debates of 1996 to 1998. Institutional learning of this kind is the strongest form of significance a short-lived government can have, and it is invisible to any historiography that measures governments by lifespan alone.

## The Verdict on the Interregnums

The namable claim of this article can be restated against the full record. The Deve Gowda and Gujral interregnums tested the outside-support model that the 2004 UPA-Left arrangement would later operationalise; the standard failed-interludes framing misses the institutional pattern. The record supporting the claim is documentary: a Common Programme of June 1996 that bound a government and its supporter to a negotiated text; a budget and a regulatory statute carried under that text; a doctrine of regional diplomacy produced inside the arrangement's zone of freedom; two withdrawals executed through the arrangement's own reserved right, on grievances stated in its own vocabulary; and a successor arrangement in 2004 that reproduced the features with refinements explicable only as lessons of the first test. The record against the claim is also documentary, and it has been given its weight in these pages: two governments dead inside twenty-one months, a prime minister replaced at the supporter's demand, a second destroyed by an inquiry's interim findings, and a Lok Sabha dissolved before half its term. The adjudication between the two records turns on the question each is asked to answer. Asked whether the United Front governments were durable, the record answers no, and the failed-interludes reading is correct and uninteresting. Asked what the governments established, the record answers that they established the operating system of the coalition era's most durable arrangement, and tested it to destruction so that its successors would not have to.

The claim is falsifiable, and stating its test is the honest close. If the arrangements of 1998, 1999 and 2004 had been constructed without published programmes, without consultative forums joining governments to their backers, and without the convention that backing is withdrawn on named issues after notice, the interregnums would stand as a dead end and the failed-interludes reading would hold. The record shows the opposite at every joint. Each successor arrangement reproduced the features, refined the ones whose absence had killed in 1997, and was built by men and women who had served in, sustained or opposed the original experiment. Transmission of that completeness is not resemblance. It is descent.

Deve Gowda returned to Karnataka politics and to Parliament. Gujral returned to the writing of his memoirs and the diplomacy of the lecture circuit. The United Front dissolved into its constituents, several of which anchored the alliances of the following decade. What remained in service was the pattern: programme, outside backing, named-issue withdrawal, renegotiation. Indian governments formed without a single-party majority after 1998 were all, in varying degrees, operators of that pattern, and the two men whose names the interregnums carry were its first, unwilling and indispensable instructors.

The pattern's migration across the aisle is the final proof of its institutional rather than partisan character. The Bharatiya Janata Party, which had spent the interregnums denouncing the arrangement as a conspiracy of the defeated, constructed its own governments of 1998 and 1999 on the Front's technologies: a pre-negotiated common agenda published as the coalition's governing text, a coordination practice among constituents, and a prime minister, in Vajpayee, whose authority within his alliance was understood by every constituent to be conditional and negotiated. The BJP's version proved more durable because the party at its core was larger and because its allies were bound before the election rather than after it, corrections that stand to 1996 as improvements stand to a prototype. Between the Front's steering committee of 1996 and the alliance managements of the following two decades there runs a single line of institutional descent, and it begins in the twenty-one months that the standard narrative files as an interlude between real governments. The interregnums were not the coalition era's embarrassment. They were its founding laboratory, and the two governments that the era consumed in the experiment are entitled to be remembered as the experiment's authors.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was H.D. Deve Gowda?

H.D. Deve Gowda was a Janata Dal politician from Karnataka who became Prime Minister of India on 1 June 1996 and held the office until 21 April 1997. Born in 1933 at Haradanahalli in Hassan district, he built his career in Karnataka state politics, served as a minister in the Ramakrishna Hegde government and became chief minister of Karnataka in December 1994 after leading the Janata Dal to victory in the state election. The United Front steering committee chose him as its prime ministerial candidate in May 1996 after the CPI(M) declined to let Jyoti Basu take the office. He returned to parliamentary politics after 1997 and remained a figure in Karnataka's regional politics.

### Q: Who was I.K. Gujral?

I.K. Gujral was an Indian politician and diplomat who became Prime Minister of India on 21 April 1997 and held the office until 19 March 1998. Born in 1919 at Jhelum, he joined the freedom movement, served as a Union minister under Indira Gandhi, and was India's ambassador to the Soviet Union from 1976 to 1980. As external affairs minister in the Deve Gowda government from 1996, he authored the Gujral Doctrine of non-reciprocal generosity toward India's smaller neighbours. The United Front steering committee selected him to succeed Deve Gowda in April 1997 with the Congress's agreement, and he continued as caretaker prime minister after the Lok Sabha's dissolution in December 1997.

### Q: What was the United Front government?

The United Front government was the coalition government of India from 1 June 1996 to 19 March 1998, formed by an alliance of thirteen parties including the Janata Dal, the Samajwadi Party, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, the Tamil Maanila Congress, the Asom Gana Parishad, the Telugu Desam Party and the Communist Party of India. It governed under a negotiated Common Programme adopted in June 1996, with the Indian National Congress and the Communist Party of India (Marxist) supporting it from outside the cabinet. It produced two prime ministers, H.D. Deve Gowda and I.K. Gujral, and fell in stages as the Congress withdrew its outside support in March and November 1997.

### Q: Why did the Deve Gowda government fall?

The Deve Gowda government fell because the Indian National Congress withdrew its outside support on 30 March 1997. Congress president Sitaram Kesri, who had succeeded P.V. Narasimha Rao in the autumn of 1996, cited the government's failure to consult the Congress on decisions, its handling of communal organisations and rising prices among the party's grievances, and calculated that a change of United Front leader would restore Congress influence over the arrangement its votes sustained. Deve Gowda refused to resign in advance and sought a confidence vote in the Lok Sabha, which he lost on 11 April 1997 by 292 votes to 158. He continued as caretaker until I.K. Gujral succeeded him on 21 April 1997.

### Q: What is the Gujral Doctrine?

The Gujral Doctrine is the foreign policy framework set out by I.K. Gujral as India's external affairs minister in 1996. Its central principle is non-reciprocity: India, as South Asia's largest power, should give generously to its smaller neighbours, Bangladesh, Bhutan, the Maldives, Nepal and Sri Lanka, without demanding equivalent concessions in return. Its remaining principles hold that no South Asian state should permit its territory to be used against another state of the region, that disputes should be settled bilaterally and peacefully, and that the states of the region should respect each other's sovereignty and territorial integrity. The Ganges Water Sharing Treaty with Bangladesh of 12 December 1996 was its first major application.

### Q: What is the outside-support coalition model?

The outside-support coalition model is an arrangement in which a party or bloc sustains a government in parliament without joining it, voting for it on confidence and supply in exchange for a published common programme and consultation, while reserving the right to withdraw support on named issues. India tested the model at national level between 1996 and 1998, when the Congress and the CPI(M) sustained the United Front governments of Deve Gowda and Gujral from outside the cabinet. The United Progressive Alliance government of 2004 operated the same model with the Left Front in the supporting role, and the arrangement endured for more than four years before the Left exercised the withdrawal right in July 2008.

### Q: How did the Justice Jain Commission affect the Gujral government?

The Justice M.C. Jain Commission, appointed in 1991 to investigate the conspiracy behind Rajiv Gandhi's assassination, destroyed the Gujral government through its interim report, laid before Parliament in November 1997. The report's chapters on Tamil Nadu recorded findings about the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam's political relationship with the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam in the years before the assassination. The Congress demanded that Gujral expel the DMK, a constituent of the United Front holding cabinet office, from the government. Gujral refused to execute an interim and contested finding against a coalition partner, and the Congress withdrew its outside support on 28 November 1997. Gujral resigned the same day, and the Lok Sabha was dissolved on 4 December 1997.

### Q: When did H.D. Deve Gowda become Prime Minister of India?

H.D. Deve Gowda became Prime Minister of India on 1 June 1996, when President Shankar Dayal Sharma swore him in at the head of the United Front government. He had been chief minister of Karnataka until the United Front steering committee selected him in the last week of May 1996, after Atal Bihari Vajpayee's thirteen-day government resigned and after the CPI(M) Central Committee declined to permit Jyoti Basu to accept the office. Deve Gowda won the Lok Sabha's confidence on 12 June 1996 with the votes of the Congress and the Left, and his government fell on 11 April 1997 when a second confidence vote went against him by 292 votes to 158.

### Q: What was the United Front Common Programme of June 1996?

The United Front Common Programme was the policy document negotiated among the Front's thirteen constituent parties and adopted in June 1996, before the Deve Gowda government took office. It committed the government to federal restraint, including a pledge against the misuse of Article 356 to dismiss state governments, to the continuation of economic reform with emphasis on agriculture, employment and irrigation, to social justice measures following the Mandal Commission's line, and to improved relations with India's neighbours. The programme functioned as the coalition's constitution: constituents cited it in internal disputes, and the Congress, as outside supporter, treated adherence to it as the condition of its continued backing for the government.

### Q: Who was Sitaram Kesri, and what did he demand in March 1997?

Sitaram Kesri was the president of the Indian National Congress from the autumn of 1996 to 1998, a Rajya Sabha member from Bihar who had served as the party's treasurer for seventeen years before succeeding P.V. Narasimha Rao. In March 1997 Kesri demanded, in substance, the replacement of H.D. Deve Gowda as prime minister, presenting grievances that the United Front government did not consult the Congress on decisions its votes sustained, had not acted firmly against communal organisations, and had failed on prices and employment. When the Front would not concede the change on his timetable, Kesri wrote to President Shankar Dayal Sharma on 30 March 1997 withdrawing Congress support, and the government fell on 11 April 1997.

### Q: Why did the Congress support the United Front from outside in 1996?

The Congress supported the United Front from outside in 1996 because the alternatives were worse for a party that had just recorded its lowest seat tally in its history. With 140 seats, the Congress could not form a government. Joining a United Front cabinet would have subordinated the party to a thirteen-party steering committee and implicated it in a government it did not lead. Supporting from outside kept the Bharatiya Janata Party, the largest party with 161 seats, out of office, preserved the Congress's freedom to withdraw, and gave the party influence over the government's survival without responsibility for its record. P.V. Narasimha Rao's leadership settled on the formula in May 1996, and Sitaram Kesri inherited it that autumn.

### Q: What role did N. Chandrababu Naidu play in the United Front?

N. Chandrababu Naidu, the Telugu Desam Party leader and chief minister of Andhra Pradesh, served as convenor of the United Front, the role that made him the coalition's chief broker between 1996 and 1998. From that position he chaired the work of the Front's steering committee, mediated among constituents whose state interests conflicted, and coordinated the alliance's parliamentary strategy with the Congress and the Left partiesers. Naidu's convenorship marked a structural change in Indian coalition practice: the pivotal figure of a national government was a state chief minister whose own party held 16 Lok Sabha seats, and whose authority rested on the Front's collective mandate rather than on a national office.

### Q: Did the CPI(M) join the United Front government?

The Communist Party of India (Marxist) did not join the United Front government. The party supported the Deve Gowda and Gujral ministries from outside the cabinet, voting with the government on confidence while holding no ministerial office, in line with its Central Committee's assessment that it should influence the government without administering it. The CPI(M)'s Central Committee also declined, in May 1996, to permit West Bengal chief minister Jyoti Basu to become prime minister, a refusal Basu later called a historic blunder. The CPI(M)'s smaller Left ally, the Communist Party of India, took the opposite course and joined the cabinet, with Indrajit Gupta serving as home minister.

### Q: What was the Voluntary Disclosure of Income Scheme of 1997?

The Voluntary Disclosure of Income Scheme was an income declaration scheme announced in Finance Minister P. Chidambaram's budget of 28 February 1997 and open until 31 December 1997. It allowed holders of undisclosed income and assets to declare them on payment of tax at 30 percent for individuals and 35 percent for companies, with immunity from penalty and prosecution under the direct tax laws. The scheme drew declarations from more than 350,000 declarants, and the Finance Ministry's reporting placed the resulting tax collections between Rs 7,800 crore and Rs 10,000 crore. It was the largest such disclosure exercise conducted in India to that date and formed part of the dream budget's strategy of lower rates exchanged for wider compliance.

### Q: What did the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India Act of 1997 establish?

The Telecom Regulatory Authority of India Act of 1997 established the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India, a statutory regulator for telecommunications, separating the regulation of the sector from the Department of Telecommunications, which had operated the network as a government monopoly service. The Deve Gowda government first created the authority by ordinance in January 1997, and Parliament placed it on a statutory footing in the same year. The Act empowered the authority to recommend licensing terms, regulate tariffs and adjudicate disputes between service providers. It was the United Front period's principal piece of regulatory state-building, and it began the institutional separation of policymaker, operator and referee in Indian telecommunications.

### Q: How long did the Gujral government last in office?

The Gujral government lasted eleven months, from I.K. Gujral's swearing-in on 21 April 1997 to the swearing-in of the Vajpayee government on 19 March 1998. Gujral resigned as prime minister on 28 November 1997, the day the Congress withdrew its outside support over the Jain Commission's interim report and the DMK question, having concluded that a confidence vote was already lost. He nevertheless remained in office as caretaker prime minister for nearly four further months, through the dissolution of the Lok Sabha on 4 December 1997 and the general election of February and March 1998, because the Constitution required a government in place until a successor could be formed.

### Q: Why did I.K. Gujral refuse to remove the DMK ministers in November 1997?

I.K. Gujral refused to remove the DMK ministers in November 1997 because he held that an interim report's contested findings could not be executed as a sentence against a coalition constituent by prime ministerial order. The Jain Commission's interim report had recorded findings on the DMK's political relationship with the LTTE before Rajiv Gandhi's assassination, and the Congress demanded the DMK's expulsion from the government as the price of continued support. Gujral answered that the findings were interim, that the DMK denied the characterisation of its record, and that a prime minister who dismissed partners on a supporter's instruction would convert outside support into outside government and destroy the coalition's federal premise.

### Q: What was the United Front Steering Committee and how did it work?

The United Front Steering Committee was the coalition's governing body, composed of the leaders of its thirteen constituent parties, which allocated portfolios, arbitrated disputes among constituents and selected the prime minister. It functioned as a collective executive above the cabinet: the decisions that mattered to the government's survival, including the choice of H.D. Deve Gowda in May 1996 and his replacement by I.K. Gujral in April 1997, were taken in the committee and ratified by the constituents. N. Chandrababu Naidu, as the Front's convenor, coordinated its work. The committee's existence formalised a fact of the coalition era, that authority over a national government could be held by a body in which state party leaderships sat as principals.

### Q: Who won the general election of February and March 1998?

The Bharatiya Janata Party won the general election of February and March 1998, taking 182 Lok Sabha seats and forming the National Democratic Alliance government under Atal Bihari Vajpayee, who took office on 19 March 1998. The Congress won 141 seats, one more than in 1996, and the Janata Dal collapsed to 6 seats as the United Front's constituents fought the election separately and on divergent platforms. The election closed the United Front period: the alliance that had governed India for twenty-one months could not reproduce itself as an electoral alliance, although the coalition method it had tested passed to the BJP-led and Congress-led alliances that succeeded it.

### Q: How did the United Front choose its prime ministers in 1996 and 1997?

The United Front chose its prime ministers by negotiation in its steering committee, subject to the consent of the Congress as outside supporter. In May 1996 the committee's preferred candidate, Jyoti Basu, was vetoed by the CPI(M) Central Committee, and the committee settled on the Janata Dal's H.D. Deve Gowda, a candidate acceptable to every constituent and to the Congress leadership. In April 1997, after the Congress made a change of leader the condition of renewed support, the committee chose I.K. Gujral, the external affairs minister, whose seniority and lack of an independent state machine made him acceptable across the Front and to Sitaram Kesri's Congress. Both choices treated the prime ministership as an assignable coalition office.
