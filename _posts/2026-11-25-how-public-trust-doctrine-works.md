## The Shore Held in Common

The public trust doctrine works by treating certain lands under water as held by a state in trust for the public, rather than as ordinary state property that officials may sell or fence at will. Under Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois, 146 U.S. 387 (1892), a state holds tidelands and the beds of navigable waters for navigation, commerce and fishing, and a transfer that surrenders those uses to private control without serving them fails. The doctrine works, in short, less as a public ownership slogan than as a limit on the trustee. A state holds tidelands for navigation, commerce, fishing and recreation, and a grant or permit that destroys those uses without serving them fails, whatever the paperwork supporting the transfer says.

That limit explains why disputes over a beach path, a marina lease or a filled marsh reach courts as questions about power rather than questions about preference. A legislature may manage trust land, and a state agency may issue permits for piers, ports and restoration work, but neither acts as an unrestricted owner. In National Audubon Society v. Superior Court, 33 Cal.3d 419 (1983), the Supreme Court of California described the trust as an affirmative duty to protect public uses whenever feasible. The claimant in a trust case therefore asks a distinctive question. The question is not whether a private owner behaved badly, and not whether a project seems useful in general terms. The question is whether the state, in granting, leasing, blocking or permitting, stayed inside the purposes for which title was taken at statehood.

The doctrine also explains why a deed alone rarely settles a shore dispute. A deed records a transfer between parties, but trust analysis asks whether the transferor had power to convey free of the trust in the first place. In Arnold v. Mundy, 6 N.J.L. 1 (1821), the Supreme Court of New Jersey treated a legislative grant of oyster beds as ineffective against the common right of fishery, even though the grant carried formal legislative authority. Form mattered less than capacity. A legislature that holds land as trustee cannot enlarge its own power by reciting ownership in a statute, just as a private trustee cannot defeat beneficiaries by drafting a generous deed to a purchaser. Courts therefore read old grants narrowly, ask what public use the grant served, and measure the remaining public interest after the transfer.

Geography gives the doctrine its boundaries, and history gives the doctrine its force. The trust attaches first to land beneath tidal and navigable water, to the foreshore between high and low water marks, and to the waters themselves, following the account in Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Mississippi, 484 U.S. 469 (1988). The trust does not swallow every parcel near a coast. Upland held in ordinary private title stays private unless a separate rule, such as the dry sand doctrine in Matthews v. Bay Head Improvement Association, 95 N.J. 306 (1984), extends a right of access across it. Precision about the covered land matters because the remedy follows the classification. On trust land, the public right is original and superior. On private upland, the public right, where one exists at all, is narrower and fact specific.

## What Property the Trust Covers

The trust covers three related categories of property, and confusion among them accounts for much disagreement in shore cases. The first category is submerged land, meaning the bed beneath navigable rivers, lakes and coastal waters. The second category is tideland, meaning land alternately covered and exposed by the tide between the mean high water mark and the mean low water mark. The third category is the water itself, together with the rights of passage and use that the water carries. In Martin v. Waddell's Lessee, 41 U.S. 367 (1842), the Supreme Court of the United States treated the soil under navigable water and the fisheries in that water as a single subject of sovereign holding. A dispute over a bed, a shore or a fishery therefore raises the same underlying question about the character of sovereign title.

Navigability once did most of the classificatory work, and the older cases reflect that emphasis. Under Pollard v. Hagan, 44 U.S. 212 (1845), title to land beneath navigable waters passed to a new state on admission to the Union. Waters that were navigable in fact, or tidal in character, marked the boundary between state sovereign land and land the federal government could grant to private parties before statehood. In The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. 557 (1871), the Supreme Court defined navigable waters as waters used, or susceptible of use, as highways for commerce. A waterway that carried trade in the ordinary modes of travel on water qualified, whether the traffic consisted of steamboats, barges or smaller commercial craft. 

Tidal character later supplied an independent route into the trust, and Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Mississippi, 484 U.S. 469 (1988), settled the point. In that case, Mississippi claimed land beneath waters that were subject to the ebb and flow of the tide but were not navigable in fact. The Supreme Court held that the equal footing doctrine gave the state title to all land beneath tidal waters, navigable or not, because English law had treated tidal land as sovereign land and the original states had succeeded to that title. The holding in Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Mississippi, 484 U.S. 469 (1988), extended state trust title to thousands of acres of marsh and shore along the Gulf Coast and the Atlantic Coast. Land under a tidal creek too shallow for commerce therefore falls inside the trust in a tidal jurisdiction, even though a deed in the chain of title may describe the parcel in ordinary private terms.

The mean high water mark fixes the seaward boundary of most private upland and the landward boundary of the foreshore in many states. Surveyors locate the mark by averaging high water levels over a tidal cycle of eighteen point six years, the period used by federal surveying practice and recognised in Borax Consolidated, Ltd. v. City of Los Angeles, 296 U.S. 10 (1935). Land seaward of the line is foreshore or submerged land, presumptively trust land. Land landward of the line is upland, presumptively private where a valid grant or patent supports private title.

Several kinds of land sit outside the core categories, and naming them prevents overclaiming in both directions. Fast land above the mean high water mark, filled land created under a valid grant for wharves or port facilities, and land beneath wholly private artificial waterways may all stand outside the trust or carry a weakened form of it. In Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois, 146 U.S. 387 (1892), the Supreme Court accepted that a state may grant parcels of submerged land for wharves, docks and piers in aid of navigation. A grant of that limited kind does not defeat the trust, because the use granted serves the purposes of the trust itself.

## The Rights the Public Keeps

The classical statement of public rights names three uses, and courts still begin there. Navigation comes first, because sovereign title to submerged land grew out of the need to keep highways of water open. Commerce follows navigation, covering the wharves, ports, channels and loading places that allow waterborne trade to function. Fishing completes the triad, covering both commercial fisheries and the common right of individual fishers to take fish in tidal and navigable waters. In Arnold v. Mundy, 6 N.J.L. 1 (1821), the protected interest was a common fishery in oyster beds. In Martin v. Waddell's Lessee, 41 U.S. 367 (1842), Chief Justice Taney described the dominion over navigable waters and the soil under them as held for the common use and benefit of the whole community.

Recreation entered the protected list later, as patterns of shore use changed and courts confronted exclusion from beaches rather than exclusion from fisheries. In Matthews v. Bay Head Improvement Association, 95 N.J. 306 (1984), the Supreme Court of New Jersey held that public trust rights extend to recreational uses including swimming and sunbathing, and that access to the ocean may require reasonable use of privately owned dry sand where no alternative exists. The reasoning in Matthews v. Bay Head Improvement Association, 95 N.J. 306 (1984), treated recreation not as a lesser indulgence but as a modern form of the older common uses of the shore. Sunbathing, bathing and walking along the water serve the same structural purpose as fishing once did.

Ecological values entered through a related door, most prominently in California. In National Audubon Society v. Superior Court, 33 Cal.3d 419 (1983), the Supreme Court of California recognised ecological preservation and scenic values as trust uses affected by diversions of water away from Mono Lake. The protected interests in that litigation included the lake ecosystem, its bird colonies and the recreational and scientific value of an intact saline lake. Earlier, in Marks v. Whitney, 6 Cal.3d 251 (1971), the same court had listed ecological preservation, habitat protection and scientific study among the purposes of the trust. The expansion in Marks v. Whitney, 6 Cal.3d 251 (1971), matters because ecological harm rarely presents as a blocked channel or a closed fishery. A trust confined to the classical triad would miss the destruction of a marsh that filters water, shelters young fish and buffers a coast against storm surge, even though destruction of that marsh injures every classical use in time.

Joseph L. Sax supplied the scholarly architecture for the broader reading in Joseph L. Sax, The Public Trust Doctrine in Natural Resource Law: Effective Judicial Intervention, Michigan Law Review (1970). Sax argued that courts should treat certain resources as subject to heightened judicial scrutiny when a legislature or agency transferred them to private benefit, because the diffuse public interest in such resources faces systematic disadvantage in ordinary politics.

Public rights under the trust are rights of use, not rights of ownership in the ordinary possessory sense, and the distinction limits what an individual member of the public may claim. A fisher may fish in tidal waters, but acquires no title to the bed by fishing there. A swimmer protected by Matthews v. Bay Head Improvement Association, 95 N.J. 306 (1984), may cross and use dry sand reasonably, but acquires no right to build, camp indefinitely or exclude the fee owner from the same sand. The trust therefore protects a defined set of activities against exclusion and destruction. The trust does not convert every shore parcel into a public park, and a claim framed as general public ownership of all coastal land overstates the doctrine and invites rejection of the narrower claim that would have succeeded.

## The State as Trustee and What a Trustee May Not Do

Trust law supplies the working analogy, and courts use the analogy with care rather than as a literal transplant of private trust codes. In a private trust, a settlor transfers property to a trustee, who holds legal title for beneficiaries, owes duties of loyalty and prudence, and must administer the corpus for the purposes the trust instrument defines. In the public trust, the corpus consists of tidelands, submerged lands and associated waters. The beneficiaries consist of the public, including generations still unborn, a feature courts noted in Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois, 146 U.S. 387 (1892), when describing title held in trust for the people of the state. The trustee is the state, acting through legislatures, agencies and courts. No single written instrument creates the arrangement. Title at statehood, constitutional structure and the nature of the resource together impose the obligation, which is why a legislature cannot repeal the trust by ordinary statute in the way a legislature repeals a regulatory programme.

The duty of loyalty does the heaviest work in the analogy. A private trustee commits self-dealing by selling trust property to the trustee personally, or by managing the corpus for the benefit of a favoured third party at the expense of beneficiaries. A state commits the public analogue of self-dealing when officials transfer trust land to a private party for private advantage while reciting a public purpose that the transaction does not serve. In Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois, 146 U.S. 387 (1892), the grant of the Chicago lakefront to a railroad failed that test in substance, because the state surrendered continuing control over a great harbour to a single corporation.

A trustee also labours under a duty against abdication, which is distinct from a duty against any delegation at all. Abdication occurs when the state gives away the power of continuing supervision itself, so that no public body retains authority to protect the beneficiary uses. Justice Field captured the distinction in Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois, 146 U.S. 387 (1892), by accepting grants that promote public interests or that do not impair the public interest in what remains, while denying a power to abdicate general control of a harbour. The line in that decision runs between management and surrender. A state may not place the resource beyond public correction for the indefinite future.

English law expressed the same structure through a pair of Latin terms that American courts inherited. In De Jure Maris, written in the seventeenth century and published in 1787, Matthew Hale distinguished jus publicum, the public right of navigation and fishery held by the Crown for subjects in common, from jus privatum, an interest in the soil that a subject might hold subject to the public right. The distinction in De Jure Maris explains a recurring pattern in American title disputes. A private party may hold a genuine proprietary interest in foreshore soil, derived from a colonial grant or a state patent, and still hold that interest burdened by public rights that the grant never extinguished. Title and use therefore travel on separate tracks. Litigation that asks only who owns the soil misses half the analysis, because ownership of the soil answers nothing about whether the owner may exclude the public from the uses the jus publicum protects.

The trustee analogy has limits, and responsible courts state them plainly. In Appleby v. City of New York, 271 U.S. 364 (1926), the Supreme Court gave substantial effect to legislative grants of land under water around New York City, illustrating judicial reluctance to unsettle long standing titles granted in good faith.

## Roman Beginnings and the English Inheritance

Roman law placed the seashore outside ordinary private ownership at the foundation of the Western legal tradition. In the Institutes of Justinian, compiled in 533, the jurists classified air, running water, the sea and consequently the shores of the sea as common to all by the law of nature, using the category later writers rendered as res communes. The classification in the Institutes of Justinian in 533 carried a practical consequence. Use of the shore for drying nets, landing boats and temporary shelter belonged to everyone, and no owner of land behind the shore could treat the shore itself as an extension of a private estate.

The Roman category rested on a physical argument that later systems repeated. Running water and the sea resist stable exclusive possession because the resource moves, renews and serves many users in sequence without division into parcels. In England, however, the idea met a feudal land system in which all land title traced to the Crown. The meeting produced a compromise. The Crown held prima facie title to the foreshore and the beds of tidal waters, but held that title burdened by public rights, rather than as beneficial private property of the monarch personally.

Magna Carta contributed a different strand, concerned less with title than with obstruction and monopoly in rivers. The charter of 1215 ordered the removal of fish weirs from the Thames, the Medway and other rivers, because fixed weirs blocked navigation and captured fisheries for private benefit. Later confirmations and statutes repeated restrictions on weirs and on exclusive fisheries created by royal grant. The strand matters for trust history because the grievance behind Magna Carta was structural. A king who granted away a fishery or licensed an obstruction converted a common resource into private revenue, and the barons who forced the charter treated that conversion as an abuse of sovereign power rather than as ordinary royal generosity. American courts citing the charter in trust cases, including the historical discussion in Martin v. Waddell's Lessee, 41 U.S. 367 (1842), drew on that structural lesson. Sovereign power over common resources carries limits that exist precisely because the sovereign holds the resource for others.

English prima facie Crown title became the doctrinal bridge to America. Under the rule described by Matthew Hale in De Jure Maris, the Crown was presumed to own the soil of the seashore and of tidal rivers, a presumption a subject could rebut by showing a specific grant or prescription. Even where a subject rebutted the presumption and established private title to soil, the jus publicum survived. The public retained rights of navigation and fishery over private soil under tidal water. The arrangement in De Jure Maris therefore produced outcomes that puzzle readers trained on unitary ownership. Two parties could hold legally significant interests in the same strip of shore, the subject holding the soil and the public holding enforceable rights across that soil, with priority between them resolved by the character of the use in conflict. American courts inherited both the presumption and the split, then adapted each to a republic in which sovereignty passed to state governments and to the people of each state.

Fisheries litigation in England sharpened the inheritance further. English courts resisted claims that an exclusive fishery in tidal waters could arise from a modern royal grant, because such a grant contradicted the common right. In Malcolmson v. O'Dea, decided by the House of Lords in 1863, an exclusive fishery claim in tidal waters failed for want of proof reaching back before legal memory to a lawful origin.

## Arnold v. Mundy and the Oyster Beds (1821)

Arnold v. Mundy, 6 N.J.L. 1 (1821), is the first great American decision on the public trust, and the facts were humble enough to fit in a boat. Benajah Mundy planted oysters in beds under the Raritan Bay, in waters off New Jersey, under colour of a legislative arrangement favouring private planters who enclosed portions of the bay bottom for cultivation. Robert Arnold took oysters from the beds, asserting the common right of fishery in tidal waters. Mundy sued in trespass, treating the planted bed as private ground from which the public could be excluded. The dispute in Arnold v. Mundy, 6 N.J.L. 1 (1821), therefore presented the recurring trust problem in miniature. A legislature had encouraged private investment in a common resource, an investor had relied on the encouragement, and a member of the public insisted that the resource had never been the legislature's to enclose in the first place.

### Can a legislature give away the oyster beds under a bay?

A legislature holds bay beds in trust for the common fishery and cannot grant exclusive rights that destroy that common use. In Arnold v. Mundy, 6 N.J.L. 1 (1821), the Supreme Court of New Jersey rejected a planter claim founded on legislative authority, because sovereign title to tidal soil carried public rights from the start.

Chief Justice Kirkpatrick wrote the opinion, and the reasoning moved from English sources to American sovereignty in a deliberate sequence. Kirkpatrick traced title to the soil under Raritan Bay through the Crown, the colonial proprietors and the state of New Jersey after independence. At each transfer, the soil passed subject to the common right of fishery. The state therefore held the bay bottom in a sovereign capacity, for the benefit of the people in common, and the legislature acted as a trustee with limited powers rather than as an owner free to dispose of the corpus. The opinion in Arnold v. Mundy, 6 N.J.L. 1 (1821), treated the legislative grant relied on by Mundy as incapable of supporting an exclusive right against Arnold, because a grantor cannot convey an interest the grantor does not beneficially own.

The decision protected a working fishery, and the economic stakes explain the care of the analysis. Oyster beds in Raritan Bay supported a substantial regional trade in the early nineteenth century, employing dredgers, planters, shuckers and merchants along the New Jersey and Staten Island shores. The court answered at the level of title rather than policy.

Kirkpatrick also addressed the colonial history that Mundy invoked, because the chain of title ran through the grant by Charles the Second to the Duke of York and the subsequent conveyances to the proprietors of East Jersey. Mundy argued that the proprietors had received full ownership of soil and fishery alike, capable of passing into private hands without public burden. The court read the instruments against the background rule of English law. A charter granting government and soil did not silently extinguish the common rights of subjects in tidal waters, and ambiguous words in a proprietary grant received a construction favouring the public. The interpretive method in Arnold v. Mundy, 6 N.J.L. 1 (1821), became a lasting contribution independent of the oyster dispute. Grants of sovereign resources are construed strictly, exclusivity must appear unmistakably, and silence preserves the public right.

The limits of the holding deserve equal precision. Arnold v. Mundy, 6 N.J.L. 1 (1821), did not forbid regulation of the oyster fishery, licensing of planters, leasing of beds under statutes that preserved the trust, or state management of seasons and methods. A lease that remains revocable, supervised and directed to a public fishery differs from a grant that removes the bed from public authority.

## Martin v. Waddell and Title at Statehood (1842)

Martin v. Waddell's Lessee, 41 U.S. 367 (1842), carried the oyster dispute of New Jersey to the Supreme Court of the United States and converted a state doctrine into a national rule of title. William Waddell claimed an exclusive oyster fishery in the Raritan River and surrounding waters under a chain of title that began with the colonial proprietors. The lessee of Martin sued, and the case required the Supreme Court to decide whether the original grant of New Jersey had conveyed the fishery free of public rights, and whether the state after independence held the waters as sovereign trustee or as proprietor able to recognise exclusive claims. The decision in Martin v. Waddell's Lessee, 41 U.S. 367 (1842), answered both questions against exclusivity.

Chief Justice Taney wrote for the Court, and the opinion is a work of historical reconstruction. Taney began with the charter granted by Charles the Second to the Duke of York in 1664, then followed the conveyance by the Duke of York to Lord Berkeley and Sir George Carteret, the proprietors of New Jersey. Taney nevertheless held that the charter and the Berkeley and Carteret grant passed the soil under navigable waters subject to the public rights recognised by English law. The reasoning in Martin v. Waddell's Lessee, 41 U.S. 367 (1842), followed the rule that a sovereign grant is read against the background of the common law, and the common law treated the jus publicum in navigable waters as an attribute of sovereignty held for the community.

The succession holding gave the decision its lasting importance. When the colonies declared independence, Taney reasoned, the people of each state succeeded to all the rights of the Crown and of Parliament within their territory, including dominion over navigable waters and the soil beneath them. That dominion passed for the common use and benefit of the whole community, to be held, managed and regulated by the state as representative of the people. The formulation in Martin v. Waddell's Lessee, 41 U.S. 367 (1842), located the trust in the transfer of sovereignty itself. Title and trust arrived together, because the title received was the title the Crown had held, and the Crown had held tidal soil burdened by public rights since the period described by Matthew Hale in De Jure Maris.

Taney distinguished between the proprietary grants a proprietor could make of ordinary land and the sovereign rights no proprietor could sever from the community. The proprietors of New Jersey could convey farms, lots and manors, and such conveyances passed full private title. Navigable waters stood on a different footing, because the charter itself was understood in England to leave public rights intact. The distinction in Martin v. Waddell's Lessee, 41 U.S. 367 (1842), protected settled expectations in both directions. A contrary holding would have unsettled the working waters of every original state, converting common fisheries into private monopolies wherever a colonial instrument used broad granting language, which was nearly everywhere along the Atlantic coast.

The decision also fixed the relationship between state power and federal authority over navigable waters in the early republic. The states held title to the beds and primary authority over fisheries, subject to the federal power over commerce and navigation granted by the Constitution of the United States. In Martin v. Waddell's Lessee, 41 U.S. 367 (1842), that federal power appeared as a qualification rather than as the source of the public right. The path from Taney in 1842 to Justice Field in 1892 is direct, because Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois, 146 U.S. 387 (1892), drew the inference that a burdened title restricts what a legislature may grant away.

## Illinois Central and the Grant a Legislature Could Not Make (1892)

Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois, 146 U.S. 387 (1892), is the central American decision on the alienation of trust land, and the scale of the grant explains the scale of the holding. In 1869, the Illinois legislature granted the Illinois Central Railroad a vast area of submerged land along the Chicago lakefront, extending a mile into Lake Michigan and covering the harbour on which the commerce of Chicago depended. The grant in Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois, 146 U.S. 387 (1892), conveyed rights in more than one thousand acres of lake bed, together with authority over wharves and facilities in the harbour. In 1873, a later legislature revoked the grant. The railroad sued, arguing that the 1869 act was a contract the state could not impair and a conveyance of property the state could not reclaim. The Supreme Court upheld the revocation, holding that the original grant had exceeded legislative power over trust land in the first place.

Justice Field wrote the opinion, and the reasoning began with the character of state title to the lake bed. Illinois had acquired title to the land under Lake Michigan on admission to the Union in 1818, under the equal footing principle associated with Pollard v. Hagan, 44 U.S. 212 (1845). Field held that the title was held in trust for the people, for navigation, commerce and fishing, and that the state could no more abdicate control of the harbour of Chicago than a trustee could surrender a trust estate to a stranger.

Field was careful to preserve ordinary grants, and the qualifications in the opinion matter as much as the prohibition. A state may grant parcels of trust land for wharves, docks and piers that aid navigation and commerce. A state may also grant parcels where the grant does not impair the public interest in the lands and waters that remain. Grants of those kinds promote the purposes of the trust or leave the purposes unharmed, and courts sustain them routinely. The grant in Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois, 146 U.S. 387 (1892), failed both qualifications at once. The grant covered the whole harbour rather than a parcel within the harbour, displaced public control over the central commercial asset of the state, and served the private advantage of a single railroad rather than the navigation and commerce of the public. Wholesale abdication, not alienation in itself, was the defect. The test that emerges asks about proportion, retained control and effect on the remaining public interest.

The contract argument received a direct answer that shaped later public law. The railroad contended that the Contract Clause of the Constitution of the United States protected the 1869 grant from revocation. Field answered that a legislature lacks power to make an irrevocable surrender of trust property beyond the limits of the trust, so a grant beyond those limits creates no contract right immune from correction.

Later courts narrowed the practical reach of the decision without disowning the principle, and an accurate account must hold both facts together. In Appleby v. City of New York, 271 U.S. 364 (1926), the Supreme Court sustained waterfront grants in New York and emphasised respect for legislative judgment and settled titles. Richard J. Lazarus, in Changing Conceptions of Property and Sovereignty in Natural Resources, Iowa Law Review (1986), situated the decision within shifting conceptions of sovereignty and warned against reading the case as a general judicial veto over resource policy.

## Equal Footing and the Title Every New State Takes

The equal footing doctrine answers the question of how states admitted after the original thirteen acquired the same trust title the original states received from the Crown. The Constitution of the United States provides for admission of new states, and the Supreme Court read that provision against a background promise, expressed in instruments including the Northwest Ordinance of 1787, that new states would enter on an equal footing with the original states in sovereignty and dignity. In Pollard v. Hagan, 44 U.S. 212 (1845), the Supreme Court held that Alabama, admitted in 1819, acquired title to the soil beneath navigable waters within the state on the same terms as the original states. The holding in Pollard v. Hagan, 44 U.S. 212 (1845), made trust title a uniform feature of American statehood rather than a peculiarity of colonial history.

The facts of Pollard v. Hagan, 44 U.S. 212 (1845), show the doctrine at work on a modest parcel with large consequences. The dispute concerned land in Mobile, Alabama, lying between high water mark and the navigable channel, claimed under a federal grant made after Alabama had become a state. The Supreme Court held the grant ineffective against the state title, because dominion over shores and submerged land passed to Alabama at statehood as an incident of sovereignty. The reasoning in Pollard v. Hagan, 44 U.S. 212 (1845), treated shores of navigable waters as sovereign land by nature, not as ordinary public land in a federal inventory awaiting disposal.

Equal footing also disciplines the federal government during the territorial period, and later cases enforced that discipline against grants made before statehood. In Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1 (1894), the Supreme Court recognised a narrow federal power to grant submerged land before statehood for purposes consistent with future state interests, such as aids to navigation under international obligations, while affirming that the general title passed to the state on admission. Courts therefore scrutinise territorial period grants of trust land with the same strict construction applied to colonial charters in Martin v. Waddell's Lessee, 41 U.S. 367 (1842). Ambiguity resolves in favour of the incoming state and the public rights the state will hold.

Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Mississippi, 484 U.S. 469 (1988), completed the title story by defining what passed at statehood in tidal areas. Mississippi entered the Union in 1817, and the question in 1988 concerned land beneath tidal but non-navigable waters along the Gulf Coast, claimed by the state and leased by private parties to oil companies. The Supreme Court held that equal footing title includes all land beneath tidal waters, whether or not the waters are navigable, because the original states had succeeded to Crown title over tidal land as such. The decision in Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Mississippi, 484 U.S. 469 (1988), rejected the argument that navigability alone measured state title.

The federal Submerged Lands Act of 1953 then confirmed state title against a different federal claim. Congress granted and confirmed to the states title to lands beneath navigable waters within state boundaries, generally three geographical miles from the coastline, under the Act codified at 43 U.S.C. sections 1301 to 1356. In United States v. California, 332 U.S. 19 (1947), the Supreme Court had held that the federal government, rather than California, held paramount rights in the marginal sea off the coast, a holding extended to other coastal states in companion litigation. The Act did not create the public trust.

## A Boundary That Moves With the Water

Every dispute over tidelands begins with a line that refuses to stay still. The public trust attaches to land covered and uncovered by the tide, and the legal boundary between public foreshore and private upland is ordinarily the mean high water line. That line is not the mark left by a single storm, a single season, or the highest reach of an exceptional tide. Federal surveyors measure it as an average taken over a full tidal epoch of 18.6 years, a period long enough to capture the complete cycle of lunar influences that raise and lower the sea. The United States Supreme Court adopted that long averaging method in Borax Consolidated, Ltd. v. City of Los Angeles (1935), reasoning that a boundary meant to govern title for generations could not turn on the accident of one wet winter or one unusually calm summer.

The zone between mean high water and mean low water carries its own name and its own legal character. Surveyors and courts call it the foreshore, and beachgoers know it as the wet sand, the firm band packed down by each receding tide. Landward of the line lies the dry sand and the upland, where private title ordinarily begins. Seaward of the line lies the submerged land beneath navigable water. The foreshore sits between those two worlds, and that position explains much of its contested status. At low tide a walker crosses it freely on foot. At high tide the sea covers it completely. Ownership follows that rhythm in most coastal states, with the state holding the foreshore in trust and the upland owner holding the ground above the line, subject to whatever public rights a particular state recognizes in the dry sand beyond it.

No single national rule fixes the line in the same place everywhere, because the trust itself is a matter of state law after statehood. Most states follow the federal method and place the boundary at mean high water, computed over the tidal epoch. A smaller group of states inherited a different colonial rule and allow private title to extend to the mean low water line, leaving a narrower public strip. Oregon and Texas illustrate a third approach built around vegetation and custom rather than tide gauges alone. In Oregon, the line of vegetation became central to the analysis of dry sand rights in State v. Thornton (1969), where growth marked the practical edge between beach and upland. Texas law under the Open Beaches Act of 1959 likewise uses the vegetation line as a reference point for the public easement that runs along Gulf Coast beaches. A survey that would settle title in New Jersey may therefore answer a different question in Texas.

The choice of method carries practical consequences that reach far beyond surveying. A boundary drawn at mean high water leaves the entire wet sand zone in public hands throughout the day. A boundary drawn by vegetation can shift the contested strip landward or seaward by many feet, deciding whether a fence, a dune, or a row of beach chairs stands on trust land or on private ground. Because the line is an average, a single survey is evidence rather than destiny. Courts admit tidal records, historical charts, and expert testimony, and they treat the 18.6 year epoch as the discipline that keeps each side from selecting the season that favors its claim. The moving character of the line is the doctrine working as designed, tying title to the water itself rather than to a fixed mark that the sea would soon make false.

## Accretion, Erosion and Avulsion

If the boundary moves with the water, then the slow movement of water changes ownership itself. Accretion is the gradual addition of soil to a shoreline, grain by grain and season by season, through the action of waves, currents, and wind. Erosion is its mirror image, the gradual loss of soil from the bank. Under the common law rule applied across the United States, gradual change moves title. The upland owner gains land added by accretion and loses land taken by erosion, and the public trust boundary travels with the new mean high water line. The rule rewards and burdens the same owner under the same principle. A riparian owner whose access to the water defines the value of the parcel keeps that access when the shore advances, and accepts the loss when the shore retreats, without constant resort to courts and resurveys.

Sudden change follows the opposite rule. Avulsion is a sudden and perceptible shift, such as a flood that tears a recognizable piece of land from one bank and deposits it downstream, or a storm that opens a new channel overnight. Avulsion does not move title. The original boundary remains where it stood, and the owner of the severed land retains title even though water now separates the parcel from the rest of the holding. The distinction turns on perceptibility. Change that an observer can watch happening belongs to avulsion. Change that reveals itself only through comparison across seasons belongs to accretion and erosion. Courts have repeated that test for more than a century because it protects settled expectations against a single violent event while allowing title to follow the ordinary work of the river and the sea.

### What happens to ownership when a shoreline slowly moves?

When a shoreline moves slowly through accretion or erosion, title moves with it. The upland owner gains ground added gradually by water and loses ground taken gradually away, and the public trust boundary shifts to the new mean high water line without any new deed or court order.

The United States Supreme Court applied these principles to river boundaries in two decisions that still govern interstate and private disputes. In Nebraska v. Iowa (1892), the Court held that the boundary between the two states along the Missouri River followed gradual accretion but remained fixed after avulsive changes caused by the river cutting a new channel. In County of St. Clair v. Livingston (1899), the Court confirmed that land formed by gradual accretion along the Mississippi River belonged to the riparian owner, even against a competing claim grounded in an earlier survey. Together the decisions show the doctrine operating at two scales. Between states, the rule preserves a stable sovereign boundary against sudden floods. Between private parties, the same rule assigns new land to the owner whose frontage created the opportunity for its formation.

Artificial change complicates the analysis without displacing it. When accretion results from works built by strangers, such as a jetty or harbor improvement constructed by a government or a distant landowner, the riparian owner ordinarily still receives the benefit of the gradual addition. When the owner personally causes the accretion by filling, dredging, or building structures designed to trap sediment, many states deny the gain or treat the resulting land as remaining subject to public rights, on the ground that no owner should manufacture title out of trust land by deliberate engineering. Courts examine who built the work, for what purpose, and whether the addition was a natural consequence or a planned capture of the foreshore. Gradual natural change belongs to the shore owner, sudden change changes nothing, and self help that manufactures land out of the trust earns no reward.

## Private Title on Trust Land

The trust has never meant that every foot of tideland remains forever in state hands. From the colonial period forward, sovereigns granted parcels of foreshore and submerged land to private parties for wharves, docks, warehouses, and mills, and those grants carried a private interest that lawyers call the jus privatum, the private right held alongside the public jus publicum. A grant of that kind could convey a genuine and defensible title. What it could not do, without unmistakable legislative authority, was extinguish the public rights of navigation, commerce, and fishing that the trust protects. Courts construe ambiguous grants narrowly, resolving doubt in favor of the public, because a legislature that intends to surrender a trust asset is expected to say so in language that leaves no room for misunderstanding.

Wharves illustrate the balance in its oldest form. A wharf built under a valid grant into navigable water serves commerce itself, one of the very purposes of the trust, and the wharf owner may charge for its use and exclude strangers from its deck. Filled land presents a harder question. Once tideland is filled and built upon, the water that defined its trust character is gone, and later generations inherit streets, lots, and buildings where the tide once ran. States have answered that problem in different ways. Some treat a lawful grant followed by authorized filling as a complete transfer of title, free of the trust. Others treat the trust as surviving in the filled land unless the legislature clearly released it, leaving owners of long settled parcels to defend titles that their predecessors believed secure. The age of a grant, the clarity of its language, and the public purpose it served all weigh in the outcome.

The leading American treatment of vested rights under such a grant is Appleby v. City of New York (1926), where the United States Supreme Court examined a series of New York waterfront grants reaching back into the nineteenth century. The Court sustained private rights acquired under grants that the state had authority to make, and it refused to allow a later public claim to sweep away interests that purchasers had taken in reliance on the sovereign grant itself. Appleby stands for a limiting principle that runs through trust law. The doctrine restrains a legislature from giving the trust away, but it also restrains a state from reclaiming, without compensation, private rights that a lawful grant genuinely conveyed. Trust litigation therefore often turns less on grand principle than on the close reading of an old deed, its statutory authority, and the consideration paid for it.

New Jersey gives the problem an administrative form that other states watch closely. The state asserts ownership claims over tidelands across its coast and riverfronts through the Tidelands Resource Council, the body charged with managing those claims, selling or leasing riparian grants, and issuing licenses for the use of trust land. Owners of filled tidelands and of structures built on former tidelands may face a state claim requiring a grant or license, with fees measured by the value and extent of the occupation. For a homeowner whose lot was filled generations earlier, the encounter can arrive as a title question raised during a sale or refinancing, long after the filling itself. The New Jersey system reflects a deliberate choice. Rather than litigate the trust parcel by parcel in court alone, the state maps its claims, publishes them, and regularizes private occupation through payment and paperwork, converting an ancient doctrine into a working land records system.

## Matthews and the Four Factors for Beach Access (1984)

For most of the twentieth century, the wet sand foreshore was the practical limit of the public beach in New Jersey. The upland owner held the dry sand, posted it, and excluded strangers from it, and the public right stopped where the dry sand began. That arrangement contained an obvious defect. A right to use the foreshore means little when the tide covers the foreshore for hours each day, and a bather confined to a narrow wet strip has no place to sit, place belongings, or remain when the water rises. The New Jersey Supreme Court confronted that defect in Matthews v. Bay Head Improvement Association, 95 N.J. 306 (1984), where a private association controlled the dry sand beaches and limited access to members and guests.

The court held that the public trust doctrine reaches beyond the foreshore where access to the dry sand is reasonably necessary to full enjoyment of the foreshore and the water it borders. The holding did not open every private beach in full. It announced a balancing inquiry, known as the Matthews factors, that weighs the circumstances of each beach rather than applying a single statewide rule. As the opinion frames the inquiry, a court considers the location and extent of public demand for the beach, the nature and extent of the public need for access, the availability of alternative access to nearby beaches and foreshore, and the extent of the private owner foreshore rights and the interference that public use would cause with those rights. Each factor directs attention to evidence. Demand is proved by population and use patterns. Need is measured against the purposes of the trust itself, including swimming, sunbathing, and recreation. Alternatives matter because a public already served by a nearby open beach presents a weaker claim than a public with no other route to the water. Interference matters because the owner retains genuine property rights that the doctrine respects even while it limits them.

### Can a private beach owner keep the public off the dry sand?

Not always. Under Matthews v. Bay Head Improvement Association (1984), a private owner may be required to allow public use of the dry sand where that use is reasonably necessary to enjoy the foreshore, after a court weighs public demand, public need, alternative access, and interference with the owner rights.

Applied to Bay Head itself, the balance favored the public. Public demand was substantial, alternative access was limited, and regulated public use of portions of the dry sand could proceed without destroying the owners enjoyment of their property. The remedy was measured rather than absolute. Hours, areas, and conditions could be set so that the public gained the access the trust required while the association retained use and management of its land for much of the day. Matthews treats beach access as a question of degree, answered beach by beach, rather than as a slogan in favor of either side. A wide municipal beach presents one balance. A narrow strip behind a single home presents another.

The decision also changed how lawyers plead a beach case in New Jersey. A claimant who recites the trust in general terms has not met the Matthews standard. The record needs testimony and documents on each factor, from counts of visitors turned away to maps showing the distance to the nearest public beach. An owner resisting access needs a record of the same kind, showing burden, interference, and the adequacy of alternatives. By converting a constitutional sounding doctrine into an evidence driven test, Matthews gave the public a right with practical content and gave owners a framework in which their interests count instead of being dismissed at the threshold.

## How Sister States Draw the Line

No national beach rule exists, and the map of American shore access is a map of separate state doctrines built from separate histories. Five states show the range of answers a court or legislature can give. California built its system on a constitutional commitment to access and on statute. The California Coastal Act of 1976 created a permitting regime for coastal development administered by the California Coastal Commission, and new development that burdens access to the shore can be conditioned on providing access in return. California courts and the commission treat the foreshore as public and press landward through easements, dedications, and permit conditions, so public use along much of the California coast extends well into the dry sand.

Texas took a statutory path of a different kind. The Texas Open Beaches Act of 1959 declares a public easement along Gulf Coast beaches from the mean low tide line to the line of vegetation, and the easement is described as rolling, moving landward as erosion pushes the vegetation line back. That rolling quality produced the leading modern limit in Severance v. Patterson (2012), where the Supreme Court of Texas held that a sudden avulsive change caused by a hurricane did not roll the easement onto previously unencumbered private land in the same automatic way that gradual erosion could. Texas law after Severance distinguishes land already burdened by a proven easement from land the state seeks to burden after a storm, and requires the state to establish the easement by dedication, prescription, or custom for particular parcels.

Oregon reached broad access through the doctrine of custom. In State v. Thornton (1969), the Supreme Court of Oregon held that the public had acquired rights in the dry sand beaches of the state through customary use reaching back through statehood and beyond, to the practices of Native peoples and early settlers. Custom requires proof of use that is ancient, continuous, peaceable, and exercised by right, and the Oregon court found that proof for the dry sand area generally. The legislature reinforced the result through the Beach Bill of 1967, and Oregon beaches stand as the strongest example in the United States of dry sand access resting on history rather than on a balancing test alone.

Florida presents custom under legislative revision. For decades, Florida courts recognized customary use of dry sand beaches where the public proved the elements of the doctrine, a framework the Supreme Court of Florida stated in City of Daytona Beach v. Tona-Rama, Inc. (1974). In 2018 the Florida legislature enacted a statute that changed the procedure for establishing customary use, requiring local governments to follow a judicial process with notice to affected owners before adopting a customary use ordinance. The statute did not abolish custom, but it moved the contest into courtrooms and raised the cost of proving the public right county by county.

Hawaii extends the trust furthest in subject matter. Hawaiian law treats the shoreline as public to the highest wash of the waves, a boundary rooted in Native Hawaiian practice and carried into state law, and public access along the shore is protected by statute and constitution alike. In In re Water Use Permit Applications, known as the Waiahole Ditch decision (2000), the Supreme Court of Hawaii held that the public trust extends to water resources themselves, including groundwater connected to surface streams, and it imposed on state agencies a duty to protect trust purposes when allocating water. Read together, the five states mark the spectrum. Oregon and Hawaii ground broad rights in custom and history, California and Texas work through statutes of opposite temper, and Florida shows a legislature narrowing the path by which a common law right is proved.

## When a Legislature Writes the Doctrine Into a Statute

A doctrine developed by courts case by case carries strengths that codification can preserve and weaknesses that codification can cure. A statute can state the trust purposes in public language, direct an agency to consider access in every permit, and give owners and applicants a text to plan around instead of a shelf of opinions to reconcile. Codification also carries losses. A legislature that writes a general test into numbered sections may freeze a doctrine that grew by adapting to new facts, and a statute that lists its own criteria invites the argument that the listed criteria displace the older judicial test wherever the statute applies. That tension between continuity and displacement sits at the center of modern trust law, because most shore disputes now arrive through a permit file rather than through a trespass action between neighbors.

New Jersey undertook that codification in the Public Access Law of 2019, codified at N.J.S.A. 13:1D-150 to -156. The statute opens with legislative findings that restate the public trust doctrine in statutory form, declaring that tidal waterways and their shores are public resources held for navigation, commerce, fishing, and recreation, and that the state bears responsibility for protecting those uses. By placing the doctrine in findings rather than leaving it solely in case law, the legislature gave agencies and courts a shared starting text. The findings do not decide individual cases by themselves, but they establish the purpose against which the operative sections are read, and they signal that the statute means to implement the trust rather than to replace its substance with a lesser administrative standard.

The operative machinery of the statute runs through the New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection. Section 153(a) directs the department, when it reviews permits and approvals for development and other regulated activity, to consider and protect public access to tidal waterways and their shores, applying the criteria the statute itself supplies. That direction matters because permit review is where the doctrine meets ordinary land use. A marina expansion, a bulkhead, a residential development, or an industrial project on the shore passes through department review, and the statute makes access a required element of that review rather than a favor the applicant may volunteer. The department evaluates the access a project provides or burdens, the availability of access in the surrounding area, and the conditions that can preserve or improve the public ability to reach and use the water.

The statute also works through municipal public access plans. A municipality may prepare a plan, subject to department approval, that maps its waterfront, inventories existing access points, and states how access will be maintained and enhanced across the town as a whole. An approved plan gives the municipality a framework for evaluating individual projects consistently, and it gives applicants advance notice of what the town and the department expect. For the public, a plan converts scattered access points, street ends, and easements into a documented network that can be defended when a single project threatens one link in it. The combination is characteristic of codification at its best. Findings state the principle, permit review applies it project by project, and municipal planning applies it across a waterfront over time, while the older case law remains in force for disputes that arise outside the statutory scheme altogether.

## Permit Conditions and the Sea Point Decision

Permit conditions are the point where the trust stops being a doctrine recited in opinions and becomes a set of obligations attached to a particular piece of ground. A coastal permit does more than allow construction. It can require the owner to build or maintain a walkway along the water, keep a fishing pier open during stated hours, provide parking or a path from the street to the shore, or contribute money toward access improvements elsewhere when physical access on the site itself is impractical or unsafe. The logic follows from the trusteeship. Development on or near trust land consumes a public resource, through bulkheads that harden the shore, structures that occupy the foreshore, or buildings that block the routes by which the public reaches the water. A condition that restores or replaces the access the project displaces ties the permission to the trust purpose it affects, and agencies defend such conditions on exactly that ground.

Administrative practice supplies recurring examples of the mechanism at work. A permit to replace a bulkhead along a bayfront lot may carry a condition preserving a lateral access strip along the waterline, because the new wall fixes the shoreline and removes the natural migration of the public foreshore. An after the fact approval for a pier built without authorization may require the pier to remain open for fishing during daylight hours, converting an unlawful private structure into a regulated public benefit as the price of allowing it to stand. A large project may satisfy its access obligation through a combination of an on site waterfront promenade and a monetary contribution to a nearby access project, where the site cannot safely accommodate the full measure of access the standards require. Each example shares the same structure. The agency identifies the trust interest affected, measures the burden the project places on it, and drafts a condition proportioned to that burden rather than using the permit as leverage for unrelated public gains.

On November 24, 2026 the Supreme Court of New Jersey decided Kevin Moran, on behalf of Sea Point Condominium Association, v. New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection, a decision that clarified which test governs that permit process. The court held that N.J.S.A. 13:1D-153(a) does not incorporate the Matthews four factor test, because the statute sets its own criteria for department review of public access in permit decisions. At the same time, the court confirmed that Matthews remains the governing test in common law beach access cases that arise outside the permit framework. The distinction is one of source rather than of value. In a permit case, the legislature has spoken and its criteria control. In a common law case between the public and a beach owner, the judicial doctrine developed in Matthews continues to supply the rule of decision.

The Sea Point decision therefore illustrates [the way a precedent survives without being overruled](/2026/11/08/how-supreme-court-overrules-precedent/) even as a statute takes over part of its field. Matthews was not discarded or treated as an error to be corrected. Its domain was located. Lawyers reading the decision take a practical instruction from it. The first question in a New Jersey access dispute is classificatory. If the dispute arises from a department permit decision under the Public Access Law of 2019, the statutory criteria frame the argument and the record must be built around them. If the dispute arises as a direct common law claim over beach access, the Matthews factors frame the argument instead. Confusing the two frameworks wastes the record, because evidence that persuades under one test may answer a question the other test never asks.

## Takings Objections and the Background Principles Answer

Every expansion of public shore rights meets the same objection from the owner whose land bears the new burden. The objection sounds in the takings clauses of the United States Constitution and of state constitutions, and it argues that a public right to cross or occupy private dry sand, or a permit condition that exacts access as the price of development, takes private property for public use without just compensation. The objection is serious and sometimes successful. Property owners hold rights that the trust never erased, and a state that uses the doctrine as a label for acquiring new public assets without payment will lose. The contest is therefore over the content of the title the owner acquired in the first place, because a decision cannot take a right that the deed never conveyed.

The framework for that contest comes from Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council (1992), where the United States Supreme Court held that a regulation depriving an owner of all economically beneficial use of land effects a taking unless the prohibited use was already barred by background principles of state property and nuisance law. The background principles exception in Lucas is the doorway through which the public trust enters takings analysis. If state law, as it stood when the owner took title, already limited the parcel by the rights of the public in the foreshore and the adjacent dry sand, then enforcing that limit takes nothing, because the owner never held the right to exclude the public in the first place. The age of the doctrine strengthens the argument. A limit announced in the nineteenth century and applied continuously since then is difficult to describe as a surprise imposed on a purchaser who bought with constructive notice of state law.

Cedar Point Nursery v. Hassid (2021) sharpened the opposing argument. In Cedar Point, the Supreme Court held that a California regulation granting union organizers a right of access to agricultural property effected a per se physical taking, because a government granted right to invade private land appropriates the owner right to exclude, a fundamental element of ownership. Beach owners invoke Cedar Point for the proposition that a public right to enter dry sand is a physical appropriation requiring compensation, however worthy its purpose. The trust answer distinguishes the cases at the level of title. The organizers in Cedar Point held a new right created by regulation against owners whose titles had never contained such a limit. A trust right, where established, predates the deed. The public does not receive a new easement from the court deciding the case. The court declares an old limit that ran with the land from the start.

That distinction decides outcomes only when the historical record supports it, and owners win where the record fails. A state that cannot show a customary right, a trust limitation in its own case law, or a statutory easement that attached before purchase faces a genuine takings problem, and several beach access programs have been restructured on exactly that ground. Permit conditions face a related discipline under the exactions cases, which require an essential nexus and rough proportionality between a condition and the burden of the proposed development. The trust strengthens the nexus showing for shore projects, because the connection between building on trust affected land and providing access is direct rather than manufactured. Even so, the condition must be measured. A small bulkhead repair cannot carry the cost of a regional beach complex. The doctrine survives takings review not by overriding property rights but by defining, with evidence, what the property right contained from the beginning.

## The Trust Beyond the Shore

A doctrine that began with tidelands did not remain confined to them, because its reasoning reaches any resource that a community holds in common and that private capture would destroy. Wildlife supplied the first great extension. In Geer v. Connecticut (1896), the United States Supreme Court upheld state regulation of game birds on the theory that the state held wildlife in trust for its people, a common ownership that justified restrictions no ordinary police power analysis had fully explained. Its ownership fiction did not survive intact. In Hughes v. Oklahoma (1979), the Supreme Court overruled Geer in part, rejecting state ownership of wildlife as a basis for discriminating against interstate commerce while leaving state regulatory power over wildlife conservation standing on other grounds. The sequence teaches a recurring lesson. Trust conclusions often endure after the original reasoning is replaced, because the need for collective management of a common resource remains even when a court discards the metaphor that first justified it.

Parkland drew the same logic inland. Land dedicated as a public park is held for the public, and a government that seeks to divert it to unrelated construction faces a trust style inquiry into authority and purpose. In Paepcke v. Public Building Commission (1970), the Supreme Court of Illinois applied public trust reasoning to parkland in Chicago, requiring clear legislative authorization before park property could be transferred for a school building and examining whether the new use served the purposes to which the land was dedicated. The Illinois Central Railroad v. Illinois (1892) logic travels well to parks because the structure of the problem is identical. A legislature holds a resource for a defined public use, a later proposal would convert it, and the court asks whether the conversion was authorized in unmistakable terms and whether it serves the trust purpose or betrays it. Many states require explicit legislative approval for alienation of dedicated parkland, a rule owing its force to trust reasoning even where opinions avoid the label.

State constitutions give the extension a textual home in a growing number of jurisdictions. The Pennsylvania Environmental Rights Amendment of 1971, Article I, Section 27 of the Pennsylvania Constitution, declares the people right to clean air and pure water and names the Commonwealth as trustee of public natural resources, with a duty to conserve and maintain them for all the people, including generations yet to come. For decades Pennsylvania courts treated the amendment as largely aspirational. In Robinson Township v. Commonwealth (2013), the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania revived it as an enforceable trust standard and struck down provisions of a state oil and gas statute that conflicted with the trustee duty. The Hawaii Constitution, Article XI, goes further, directing the state to conserve and protect natural beauty and natural resources and affirming that public lands, waters, and resources are held in trust for the benefit of the people. Hawaii courts read that text together with the common law trust, producing the strongest combined doctrine in the United States.

These extensions share a promise and a risk. The promise is coherence. Groundwater drawn from an aquifer, a park built over filled tideland, and a beach behind a private fence present the same question of whether a present majority may permanently surrender a resource held for everyone. The risk is boundlessness. A trust without defined subjects and purposes becomes a general judicial veto over land and resource policy, the core of the scholarly critique that has accompanied the doctrine since the 1970s. The durable extensions have therefore been the disciplined ones. Each names the resource, identifies the trust purpose in law or constitutional text, and asks whether the challenged action serves the trust or surrenders it without authority.

## Mono Lake and the Duty to Keep Reconsidering (1983)

Mono Lake sits in the eastern Sierra, fed by freshwater tributary streams and holding no outlet, so its salinity rises as evaporation draws water away and falls only when inflow restores the balance. In 1940 the California State Water Resources Control Board granted the City of Los Angeles permission to divert water from four of the lake's tributary streams into the aqueduct system that supplied the growing city. The decision treated the streams as appropriative water available for municipal use and gave little weight to the condition of the lake itself. Over the decades that followed, diversion reduced inflow, the surface dropped, salinity increased, and exposed alkali flats produced dust while a land bridge formed toward an island where birds nested, opening the nesting ground to predators. The harm was not a sudden event. It accumulated through lawful diversions carried out under permits that had seemed settled when issued.

In National Audubon Society v. Superior Court, 33 Cal.3d 419 (1983), the California Supreme Court confronted the relationship between two systems that had developed largely apart. The appropriative rights system allocated water by priority and permit, rewarding the party that put water to beneficial use and recorded the claim. The public trust doctrine protected navigable waters and their tributaries for navigation, commerce, fishing, and ecological values held for the public. The plaintiffs argued that the trust limited diversions harming the lake. The city argued that its permits, granted decades earlier, had fixed its entitlement and that the trust had been displaced by the statutory water rights scheme.

### Does the public trust doctrine apply to a lake with no navigation?

Yes, within the reasoning of the 1983 decision. The court treated Mono Lake as a navigable water held in trust, emphasized ecological and recreational uses alongside navigation, and held that diversions from non-navigable tributaries fell within trust review when those diversions impaired the navigable lake they fed.

The court held that the two systems had to be integrated rather than treated as rivals in which one erased the other. The state held a continuing supervisory power over trust resources, and that power did not end when a permit issued. No person acquired a vested right to divert water in a manner harmful to the trust, because the entitlement had always been subject to the trust that burdened the resource from the start. Reconsideration therefore did not take away a right that had been absolute. It recognized a limit that had been present in the right all along, even when administrators had failed to account for it.

Integration also carried a forward looking discipline. Before approving diversions, the state had a duty to consider trust uses and to protect them whenever feasible, balancing municipal need against harm to the lake through findings rather than assertion. The court did not order diversions to stop, and it did not set a lake level by judicial decree. It remanded the matter so the water board could reconsider allocations in light of the trust, a remedy that respected administrative competence while denying that an old allocation was beyond review. That restraint matters to the meaning of the case. Mono Lake expanded the doctrine's reach to tributary diversions and to ecological values, but its method was supervisory and procedural as much as substantive. The state had to keep looking, keep weighing, and justify harm rather than presume that a permit closed the question forever.

## The Elements a Claimant Must Prove

A public trust claim begins with trust property, and the claimant must identify the corpus with precision rather than gesture toward nature in general. The property may be tidelands, submerged lands beneath navigable waters, the waters themselves, or, in states following the reasoning of the Mono Lake decision of 1983, tributary flows whose diversion impairs a navigable water. Deeds, surveys, navigability determinations, and the history of title from statehood all bear on that first element, because a court cannot protect a trust it cannot locate. A claim that skips the corpus and moves straight to environmental harm asks for a conclusion without the premise that gives the doctrine its legal force.

The second element is a public use within the trust that stands impaired, or threatened with impairment, by the challenged action. Navigation, commerce, and fishing form the historic core, and recreation, bathing, and ecological preservation appear in many state formulations as uses consistent with the waters in their natural state. Impairment must be shown in concrete terms: acreage filled, frontage fenced, depth lost to a channel, flow reduced below a level that sustains a fishery. Aesthetic objection alone, untethered to a recognized use, carries little weight, while documented loss of access or function carries a great deal. The measure is substantiality, and courts compare the use destroyed against the use remaining rather than counting any disturbance as a breach.

The third element is a state act or omission connecting the impairment to the trustee. The act may be a legislative grant of tidelands, an agency permit authorizing fill or diversion, a lease on terms that exclude the public, or prolonged neglect in which an agency permits encroachment it had a duty to prevent. Purely private conduct on genuinely private land ordinarily falls outside the doctrine, which constrains the sovereign as trustee rather than regulating every owner near water. The omission branch requires care, since courts are slower to find breach in inaction than in an affirmative grant, and a claimant must show that the state knew, or plainly should have known, that trust property faced impairment it had power to address.

The fourth element is negative in form. Once corpus, use, impairment, and state action stand established, the justification offered for the action must serve the trust rather than merely outweigh it in general public benefit. A highway, a port improvement, or a public marina may satisfy that demand because each advances navigation or access. A private condominium, however valuable its tax revenue, does not become trust serving by being useful. The absence of a trust serving justification completes the claim, and its presence defeats the claim even where impairment is real, provided the impairment itself remains limited.

Standing and review frame how those elements reach judgment. Joseph L. Sax, writing in 1970, defended broad citizen standing because trust beneficiaries are the public at large, and a doctrine enforceable only by the trustee against itself would rarely be enforced at all. Many states permit citizen suits, organizational plaintiffs whose members use the affected water, and municipalities asserting access interests, while still requiring a concrete connection to the resource rather than an abstract grievance. Courts then review the legal question of trust scope for themselves, weigh agency fact finding with deference where a record supports it, and treat the ultimate judgment, whether a grant alienates the corpus or substantially impairs remaining uses, as a question of law that no agency conclusion can settle by assertion alone.

## How Courts Review a Grant or a Permit

Review begins with characterization, because the standard a court applies depends on what kind of action stands challenged. A legislative grant of tidelands receives the closest scrutiny, since alienation by statute can remove land from the trust for generations and the grant language itself becomes the central evidence. An agency permit receives scrutiny of a different shape, directed at the record the agency built, the findings it made, and whether it considered trust uses at all before authorizing fill, diversion, or exclusion. A lease falls between the two, examined both for its statutory authority and for the practical degree of public exclusion its terms produce over its full duration. Courts also ask whether the government acted as regulator of private conduct or in a special sovereign capacity as trustee and grantor, a distinction that parallels the analysis used in [the government speech doctrine](/2026/11/18/how-government-speech-doctrine-works/), where the character of government action determines which limits govern it.

The substantive inquiry follows the structure set by Illinois Central Railroad v. Illinois in 1892, organized as two questions that must both be answered in the state's favor. The first asks whether the grant or permit serves a purpose within the trust, such as improving navigation, expanding public access, or supporting commerce on the water. The second asks whether the disposition substantially impairs the public interest in the lands and waters that remain. A project can pass the first question and fail the second, as a navigation improvement might do if its scale consumed the harbor it claimed to improve. A project can also fail the first question so completely that the second never matters, since a purely private benefit has no trust purpose to weigh. Keeping the questions separate prevents a court from trading away the corpus on the strength of general economic benefit dressed in trust language.

Evidence disciplines both questions. Acreage, frontage, water depth, flow records, patterns of public use, and the availability of alternative access all enter the record, and conclusory findings receive little respect. Courts examine whether conditions attached to a permit genuinely protect trust uses or merely recite them, whether mitigation replaces the function lost or substitutes an unrelated benefit, and whether the disposition is revocable or effectively permanent. Duration matters because a short, conditional, revocable permission burdens the trust far less than a transfer that a grantee can mortgage, assign, and defend as an investment backed expectation. Reversibility, in practice, often decides how suspicious a court becomes.

Deference has a defined boundary in that analysis. Agencies receive deference on contested facts within their expertise, such as hydrological modeling or engineering feasibility, provided the record discloses the basis for the finding. Agencies receive no deference on the meaning of the trust itself, on whether a use falls within it, or on whether the two part inquiry has been satisfied, because those questions state the law of the trustee's obligation. A reviewing court may therefore accept an agency's flow figures while rejecting its conclusion that a private marina serves the trust. That division preserves administrative competence without allowing the trustee to define the limits of its own duty, a point a careful reader can test against any well kept [reference shelf](https://vaultbook.net) of the governing cases. The result is review that is neither a rubber stamp nor a fresh trial, but a structured insistence that purpose, impairment, and record align before trust property changes hands or changes character.

## Remedies When the Trust Is Breached

The characteristic remedy for a breached trust is voidness, and its logic explains much of the doctrine's distinctive force. A grant that exceeds the trustee's power does not merely create a claim for payment. It fails to convey what the state had no authority to convey free of the trust, so a court may declare the grant void, or voidable at the instance of the public, and restore the property to trust status. In Illinois Central Railroad v. Illinois in 1892, the legislature itself had repealed the grant at issue, and the court sustained that repeal against claims of vested right and contract impairment, reasoning that a legislature cannot bargain away the trust in a manner binding its successors. Voidness follows from the structure of title rather than from judicial disapproval of the bargain's wisdom.

Revocation and quiet title actions give that principle practical form. A state may revoke a permit issued in disregard of trust limits, reclaim tidelands conveyed under an invalid grant, and sue to quiet title so that records reflect the public interest the deed could not extinguish. Private parties in possession under color of such a grant face ejectment or negotiated surrender, although courts attend to reliance interests in shaping timing, removal obligations, and the treatment of improvements placed on the land in good faith. Reliance shapes the remedy's administration without converting an invalid grant into a valid one, a distinction courts preserve even when unwinding a transaction proves expensive and slow.

Injunctions address impairment in progress. A court may enjoin diversion that draws down a navigable lake, halt fill that would convert tidelands to upland, or bar fencing that excludes the public from trust frontage, and it may do so before the harm completes because filled tideland and drained marsh are difficult to restore. Permit denial and permit conditioning operate earlier in the sequence, preventing breach rather than remedying it. An agency faithful to the trust refuses authorization for a project that fails the governing test, or conditions approval on public access, reduced footprint, maintained navigation, and monitoring, with the conditions enforceable against successors so they cannot be shed in a later transfer.

Damages occupy a limited place in the remedial scheme, and the limit is instructive. Trust property is not a commodity whose loss money naturally repairs, since the beneficiary is the public across generations and the asset is a functioning shore rather than a sum. Some jurisdictions permit damages for past harm, restoration costs, or profits derived from wrongful use, yet no payment by a grantee purchases the right to continue a breach, and no payment to the state retroactively validates a grant the trustee lacked power to make. Compensation answers a takings question where private property has been taken. Voidness answers a trust question where public property was given away without authority. Confusing the two remedies confuses the two kinds of title, and the doctrine's remedial history consistently returns to restoration of the corpus and its uses as the outcome that fits the wrong.

## Federal Water Law Beside the State Trust

State trust law operates within a federal structure, and the boundary between the two systems determines which sovereign answers which question in a shore dispute. The United States holds a navigational servitude over navigable waters, a dominant federal interest rooted in the commerce power that permits the federal government to build navigation works, alter channels, and regulate obstructions without compensating riparian owners for losses within the scope of that servitude. The servitude is an instrument of federal navigation authority, not a general federal ownership of tidelands. Title to lands beneath navigable waters passed to the states at statehood under the equal footing doctrine, and the trust that burdens that title remains, in the main, a matter of state law that each state defines for its own shores.

Federal statutes add regulatory layers without displacing that title structure. The Rivers and Harbors Act requires federal authorization for structures and work in navigable waters, directed chiefly at obstruction to navigation. The Clean Water Act, enacted in 1972, requires permits for the discharge of dredged or fill material and for pollutant discharges, administered through federal and delegated state programs whose standards address water quality and wetland function. A developer may therefore need a federal dredge and fill permit, a state water quality certification, and state authorization to use tidelands held in trust, and success under one regime establishes nothing under the others. The analysis in [the Clean Water Act framework](/2012/02/15/clean-water-act-1972-complete-guide/) shows how the federal permit inquiry proceeds on its own statutory criteria, concerned with discharges and waters of the United States rather than with whether a state, as trustee, may alienate the bed at all.

That separation produces outcomes that puzzle observers who treat a permit as a general approval. A federal agency may issue a fill permit after finding environmental criteria satisfied, while a state court later holds the underlying grant of tidelands invalid under the trust, leaving the permit holder with federal permission to fill land it has no state right to occupy. The reverse sequence also occurs, where a state approves a use that federal law forbids for water quality reasons. Neither result signals conflict in the constitutional sense. Each sovereign applies its own law to its own interest, navigation and water quality at the federal level, title and trust obligation at the state level, and a prudent project must satisfy both.

Federal law does control one question that sits beneath the whole doctrine, namely which waters were navigable at statehood and therefore carried title to the state in the first place. That title question is federal, answered under federal standards, and it frames the state trust without dictating the trust's content. Beyond that threshold, federal courts generally leave the scope of protected uses, the validity of grants, and the remedies for breach to state courts applying state law. The layered arrangement is untidy in operation and coherent in principle. The federal government guards navigation and national water quality. The states hold the shore, and the trust disciplines what a state may do with an inheritance it received as sovereign rather than as proprietor free to sell.

## The Strongest Case Against an Expansive Trust

The strongest case against an expansive public trust doctrine begins with democracy. Richard J. Lazarus, in Changing Conceptions of Property and Sovereignty in Natural Resources, published in the Iowa Law Review in 1986, examined the doctrine's revival and questioned the legitimacy of courts displacing legislative and administrative judgments about resource use through a doctrine whose historical core was far narrower than its modern claims. Legislatures answer to voters and balance employment, housing, tax base, and environment in statutes that can be amended. When the doctrine expands from policing alienation of tidelands into supervising resource management generally, the countermajoritarian difficulty that shadows all judicial review sharpens, because the text being enforced is neither a constitution nor a statute but a judicially elaborated tradition whose limits the judges themselves announce.

Title uncertainty forms the second strand, and it is a practical objection rather than an ideological one. Waterfront economies run on recorded title. Municipalities bonded harbor works, families bought lots platted under old grants, and ports financed terminals on tidelands conveyed generations earlier, all in reliance on the apparent finality of state action. If every historic grant remains perpetually reopenable under a trust whose scope grows with judicial understanding, reliance loses its protection and the cost of title insurance, financing, and development rises along every shore. The critique asks whether voidness, applied without repose, punishes good faith successors for the sins of a legislature long dissolved.

James Huffman pressed a third objection, aimed at the doctrine's reasoning rather than its results. In his critique, the trust too often functions as a slogan that announces a conclusion while deciding nothing, because calling land trust property does not specify which uses count, how much impairment is too much, or how a court should weigh navigation against ecology against access when those uses conflict among themselves. Without a theory of which uses the trust protects and why, the label licenses judges to reach outcomes on intuition and attribute them to Justinian. That charge lands hardest on opinions that recite the doctrine's pedigree and then balance interests without identifying the element that proved decisive.

The cumulative risk is a general environmental veto that no legislature enacted. If any citizen may challenge any permit affecting any water, lake, park, or habitat under an unbounded trust, then the doctrine swallows the statutory schemes legislatures built to govern those resources, schemes with standards, exemptions, and compromises that represent democratic settlement. Environmental protection achieved by veto also protects badly, since it blocks harmful and beneficial projects alike whenever a court finds impairment, and it channels policy into litigation where outcomes turn on the composition of a panel rather than on a program the public chose.

The thesis survives that critique in narrowed form, and the narrowing is a strength rather than a retreat. As a limit on alienation, the doctrine rests on firm historical ground: the state received tidelands as sovereign, grants that destroy trust uses without serving them were doubted from the nineteenth century forward, and legislatures themselves have repeatedly ratified the limit. As a guarantee of access to waters the public owns in that sovereign sense, it has determinate content that courts can administer through the purpose and impairment inquiry. Where the critique prevails is against the free floating version, a judicial commission to manage resources wherever water appears. Confined to alienation, access, and the supervisory duty recognized at Mono Lake in 1983, the doctrine answers the democratic objection by enforcing a limit on government rather than governing in government's place.

## What the Doctrine Cannot Do

A doctrine defined by its limits invites misunderstanding when its rhetoric outruns its holdings, and the public trust has accumulated claims that courts have not accepted. The first limit concerns ownership. The trust does not make the public the owner of all natural resources, and it does not convert private uplands, private groundwater in most formulations, forests, minerals, or wildlife into trust corpus by the force of their public importance. States regulate those resources extensively under police power and statute, and game and fish are managed under distinct doctrines of state ownership and conservation law. Each body of law has its own source and tests. Folding all of them into the trust would dissolve the distinctions that tell a court which test applies, and most courts decline to do so even when sympathetic to the underlying protective goal.

The second limit concerns statutes. The trust constrains how a legislature disposes of trust property, but it does not displace the statutory schemes that govern water allocation, environmental review, coastal management, and permitting on trust and non trust land alike. A codifying statute can define procedures, specify access standards, and channel the doctrine's application, as access legislation in several states demonstrates, and a claimant must ordinarily work through those statutory standards rather than around them. Nor does the trust supply a private right to every remedy a statute withholds, or excuse compliance with standing, exhaustion, and limitations rules that govern litigation generally. The doctrine operates inside the legal system, not above it, and its antiquity confers no exemption from the ordinary disciplines of adjudication.

The third limit concerns takings and genuinely private title. Where land was never trust property, or where a valid grant conveyed tidelands free of the trust under the standards governing such conveyances, the owner holds private property protected by the takings clauses of the federal and state constitutions. Regulation that goes too far, or a judicial redefinition that eliminates established property rights, can require compensation, and the trust cannot be invoked as a label that dissolves those protections by declaration. Courts therefore examine the history of title with care before applying trust limits, and background principles of state property law matter precisely because they determine what the owner ever acquired. A deed to land the state held in trust carries the trust within it. A deed to land outside the trust does not acquire a trust burden because a later court finds the location attractive for public use.

The fourth limit is geographic and evidentiary, stated with precision in PPL Montana v. Montana in 2012. The Supreme Court of the United States held that navigability for title is determined segment by segment, so that a river navigable in one reach may be non navigable in another, and the state takes title only to the beds of segments that were navigable in fact at statehood under the federal test of use for travel and commerce in the customary modes of the period. Portages around falls or rapids could defeat navigability for the segment in question rather than being averaged away across the whole river. PPL Montana, decided in 2012, rejected a claim built on the river as an undifferentiated whole and insisted on evidence addressed to each segment. The decision narrows the corpus at its foundation. Purely private waters, non navigable segments, and waters over private beds remain outside the trust in most states, however valued they may be, and any expansion beyond that foundation must come from state law stated and defended as such, not from the federal title doctrine itself.

## Reading a Shore Dispute Through the Trust

A dispute of this shape recurs along developed coasts. A marina operator proposes to fill two acres of tidelands, extend a basin for additional slips, and fence a tidal creek that small boats and anglers have used for generations, offering a parking area and a viewing platform on the upland as public benefits in exchange. Neighbors object, a conservation organization objects, and the permitting agency holds a file containing an engineering report, a lease application for the submerged land, and letters divided between jobs and access. An analyst reading the dispute through the trust begins not with the balance of letters but with the corpus, asking what the fill would occupy. Surveys, the mean high water line, and the history of the creek's navigability determine whether the two acres are tidelands held in trust or private upland misdescribed, and that determination, more than any argument about policy, fixes the legal terrain on which everything else stands.

The second step identifies the uses at stake and the uses proposed. Navigation on the creek, fishing along its banks, and passage by small craft belong to the historic core of protected uses, and recreation and ecological function may join them under the governing state's formulation. The marina's slips serve navigation in one sense, since moored boats use the water, yet the fence across the creek destroys navigation in another, and the analysis must distinguish between a use that serves the trust on the filled parcel and a use that excludes the trust from the water behind it. The viewing platform counts for less than it first appears, because access to look at water is not access to the water itself, and courts comparing substitution ask whether the public receives the same kind of use it loses, in the same place, with the same practical freedom.

The third step examines the instrument. A lease of submerged land for a term of years, revocable for trust inconsistency and conditioned on maintained passage, burdens the trust differently from a permit that authorizes permanent fill and a deed that removes the parcel from public title altogether. Fill deserves special attention because it converts water to land and is, for practical purposes, irreversible, so the instrument that authorizes fill is read as a partial alienation whatever its label.The fourth step applies the governing test to the assembled facts. Under the two part inquiry associated with Illinois Central Railroad v. Illinois in 1892, the question is whether the disposition serves a trust purpose and whether it substantially impairs the public interest in what remains. The marina expansion may satisfy the first branch in part, while the fencing of the creek fails it entirely, since no trust purpose is served by excluding the public from a navigable channel for the convenience of slip holders. Substantial impairment is measured against the creek and frontage as a whole, not against the coastline of the entire state, because a test scaled to the largest geography would find no single loss substantial and would license the corpus away parcel by parcel.

The final step follows the remedy from the analysis. A court persuaded that the fence and fill breach the trust may enjoin the fencing, void or condition the lease, and require restoration of filled area where restoration remains feasible, while leaving the lawful portion of the marina in operation. The dispute resolves by returning to the same sequence: corpus, uses, instrument, test, remedy. That sequence is the doctrine in working form, and it explains why disputes that sound in grievance become tractable when each claim is assigned to the step where it must be proved.

## Why a Roman Idea Still Decides American Shorelines

An idea traced through the Institutes of Justinian in the sixth century, carried through English writing on Crown title, received into American law at statehood, and tested in cases from Arnold v. Mundy in 1821 to the Mono Lake decision of 1983 might be expected to survive as ceremony, quoted for dignity and decided on other grounds. The public trust doctrine survives instead as working law because it answers a question that no other doctrine in the system answers as directly. Deeds, permits, and statutes all presume that the government granting them had the power to grant, and ordinary property law rarely examines the capacity of the sovereign as grantor. The trust examines exactly that capacity. It treats the state not as an owner disposing of surplus inventory but as a fiduciary holding a defined inheritance for beneficiaries who include generations that cannot appear in any hearing room, and it measures each grant against the terms of that holding.

Restated without the vocabulary of the opening, the doctrine is a rule about what kind of owner the state is. Sovereignty delivered the shore into public hands on conditions that private ownership never carries, and those conditions travel with the title into every later transaction. A legislature may manage the inheritance, improve it, open it, and in narrow circumstances convey parcels that serve the purposes for which the inheritance exists. A legislature may not treat the inheritance as a treasury asset to be spent on unrelated ends, and it may not extinguish the public's remaining interest for private benefit and call the transaction final. The limit binds the trustee rather than enlarging the beneficiary's estate, which is why the doctrine coexists with extensive private waterfront ownership and why its strongest applications concern alienation and access rather than the management of every resource a state touches.

The single point worth carrying away from the doctrine's long record is that paperwork does not settle a shore dispute by itself. A deed, a lease, a permit, and a statute each require a further question about the authority behind them when trust land is involved, and that question has a structured answer built from corpus, use, purpose, and impairment. Generations of litigants have discovered that a flawless instrument fails when the grant it records was beyond the trustee's power, and generations of agencies have learned to build records that answer the trust question before a court asks it. The doctrine rewards that discipline and exposes its absence, quietly, in case after case where the decisive paragraph concerns not what the parties agreed but what the state was entitled to agree to.

The table of authorities set out after the equal footing discussion tells the same story in compressed form: every generation argued about the powers of the trustee, and no generation succeeded in arguing the trust itself away. American property law is usually described as a system of private rights recorded against the world, and the description is accurate for the great majority of land. The shore is the systematic exception, and the exception is structural rather than sentimental. Title to tidelands and navigable waters reached the states through sovereignty, as an incident of independence and equal footing, not through purchase, grant from a prior private owner, or market transaction. Because the title arrived by that route, it arrived burdened, and every subsequent deed in the chain carries the burden forward unless a lawful conveyance under the trust's own demanding standards removes it. No private instrument, however formally perfect, can extinguish that permanent public remainder, because no grantor in the chain ever held the unburdened title that such an extinguishment would require. The shore therefore remains, in American law, the place where the public never entirely leaves the title, and a Roman classification of running water and the seashore still decides who may fence a creek, fill a flat, or close a beach.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the public trust doctrine in simple terms?

The public trust doctrine is a rule of property law under which a state holds certain lands and waters not as an ordinary owner but as a trustee for the public. The submerged land beneath navigable waters, and the shore between the high and low water marks, belong to the state in that special capacity. Because the state holds title for the benefit of everyone, its power to dispose of the land is limited. A legislature may manage trust land, lease it, or improve it for public purposes, but a grant that hands the resource to private control and destroys public use exceeds what a trustee may do. The protected uses begin with navigation, commerce, and fishing, and in many states extend to bathing, swimming, and other recreation. The doctrine therefore operates less as a slogan about public ownership and more as a restraint on government itself: the trustee cannot give away the trust.

### Q: Which lands and waters does the public trust doctrine cover?

The core of the trust is the bed of navigable waters and the tidelands along the seacoast, meaning the land exposed and covered by the ordinary tides between the mean high water line and the mean low water line. Navigable rivers and lakes are included because their beds passed to the states at statehood, and tidally influenced waters are included even where a small boat could not travel, as the Supreme Court confirmed for Mississippi tidelands in 1988. Beyond that core, coverage varies by state. Some states extend the trust to non-navigable tributaries, to groundwater connected with a navigable stream, to parkland dedicated to public use, and to wildlife. Others confine it strictly to submerged land and the foreshore. Dry upland above the high water line is ordinarily private, although access across it may be required where the public cannot otherwise reach or use the trust land below.

### Q: Who owns the beach below the high tide line?

Below the mean high water line, the beach is generally owned by the state and held in trust for the public, a position the English common law took and American courts adopted for the original states and every state admitted afterward. The strip between high and low water, called the foreshore, is therefore public in its legal character even where it looks like part of a private resort. Two qualifications matter. First, in a few states, most notably Massachusetts and Maine, colonial ordinances granted the foreshore to the adjoining upland owner, subject to a reserved public easement for fishing, fowling, and navigation. Second, a state may have conveyed particular parcels by an early grant that a court later sustained. Subject to those exceptions, a private deed describing land to the water does not carry the foreshore free of the trust, and an owner cannot fence the wet sand against the public whose trustee holds it.

### Q: Why does the public trust doctrine come from Roman law?

The doctrine traces to the Institutes of Justinian, compiled in the sixth century, which classified the air, running water, the sea, and the seashore as things common to all by the law of nature, incapable of exclusive private ownership in the ordinary way. Roman jurists treated the shore as open to everyone for fishing and navigation, while allowing temporary structures that had to yield to the common use. English law absorbed the idea through writers such as Bracton and, later, Lord Hale, whose treatise on the sea argued that the Crown held the foreshore with a public right embedded in it. American courts received that combined inheritance after independence, reasoning that the people succeeded to the Crown position in each state. The Roman origin explains the doctrine shape: the resources are common because of what they are, not because a legislature chose to make them public property by statute.

### Q: What did Illinois Central Railroad v. Illinois decide in 1892?

In 1869 the Illinois legislature granted the railroad roughly a thousand acres of submerged land along the Chicago lakefront, including much of the harbor. A later legislature revoked the grant, the railroad sued, and in 1892 the Supreme Court sustained the revocation. The Court held that a state holds title to the beds of navigable waters in trust for the people, so that they may enjoy navigation, commerce, and fishing, and that one legislature cannot abdicate that trust by transferring the whole resource to private control. Grants of trust land are not void in every case: a conveyance that serves trust purposes, or that disposes of a small parcel without substantial impairment of the public interest in what remains, can stand. A grant of an entire harbor to a single corporation failed that measure. The decision became the foundation of American public trust law and the standard citation for the limit on legislative power over tidelands.

### Q: Can a state sell its tidelands to a private developer?

A state may sell or lease some trust land, but the power is narrower than its power over ordinary state property. Two kinds of conveyance survive scrutiny. The first serves a trust purpose directly, such as a wharf, marina, port facility, or filled land dedicated to navigation and commerce. The second disposes of a parcel so small or so removed from public use that the sale does not substantially impair the public interest in the lands and waters that remain. A sale that transfers a working waterfront, a beach, or a harbor to purely private use, cutting off navigation, fishing, or access, falls outside the trustee power described in Illinois Central and may be revoked or declared void. Many states add procedural protection through tidelands statutes, licensing systems, and constitutional provisions. The paperwork of a deed therefore does not settle the question; a court asks what the grant does to the trust uses, not only whether the legislature authorized it.

### Q: What is the difference between jus publicum and jus privatum?

The paired Latin terms separate the two interests bundled in Crown and later state title to the foreshore. Jus publicum is the public right: the interest in navigation, commerce, and fishing that the sovereign holds for everyone and cannot surrender. Jus privatum is the proprietary interest: the ordinary ownership value of the soil itself, which the sovereign could grant to a private party much like any other land. English writers, Hale among them, used the distinction to explain how a private person might own the shore and yet take it burdened by public rights, so that the grantee could exclude others from the soil for some purposes but could not block passage or fishing. American courts borrowed the vocabulary in early cases such as Arnold v. Mundy in 1821. The distinction still does practical work: a valid conveyance of the jus privatum never by itself extinguishes the jus publicum, which is why a tidelands deed and continuing public rights can coexist in the same strip of sand.

### Q: How does the equal footing doctrine relate to the public trust?

The equal footing doctrine holds that every state enters the Union on the same terms as the original thirteen, with the same sovereignty and the same title to the beds of navigable waters within its borders. When independence transferred the Crown trust title to the original states, admission on equal footing gave the same title to later states at the moment of statehood, without any federal conveyance. The Supreme Court applied the principle in cases such as Pollard v. Hagan in 1845 and used it as the platform for Illinois Central in 1892. Equal footing supplies the source of title; the public trust supplies the limit on what the new owner may do with it. Before statehood, the federal government holds future trust beds in a preparatory capacity and generally cannot defeat the coming state title by private grants, apart from narrow exceptions for international boundaries and federal purposes. The two doctrines together explain why a landlocked date of admission, rather than any purchase, fixes ownership of a riverbed.

### Q: What is the Matthews test for beach access in New Jersey?

The Matthews test comes from Matthews v. Bay Head Improvement Association, decided by the New Jersey Supreme Court in 1984. The association controlled much of the beach in a shore borough and restricted use of the dry sand above the high water line. The court held that public trust rights in the foreshore could be meaningless without some use of the dry sand, and it weighed four factors: the location of the dry sand area in relation to the foreshore, the extent and availability of publicly owned upland sand, the nature and extent of the demanded use, and the presence of quasi-public or public dedication. No single factor controls; the inquiry is practical, asking how much dry sand the public reasonably needs to enjoy the wet sand it already owns in trust. The doctrine produced outcomes ranging from access across a narrow strip to broader recreational use, depending on how the four factors combined on a given beach.

### Q: Does the public trust doctrine protect recreation or only navigation and fishing?

The oldest formulation protects navigation, commerce, and fishing, the uses named in Illinois Central and in the English sources behind it. Recreation entered through state courts rather than through that original federal statement. New Jersey extended the trust to bathing, swimming, and sunbathing in cases culminating in Matthews in 1984, reasoning that the foreshore cannot be enjoyed for the older uses while the public is excluded from the activities that bring people to a shore. California, Oregon, Hawaii, and several other states recognize recreational and ecological values in varying degrees, and the Mono Lake decision treated scenic and ecological interests as trust values. Other states keep the list close to the historic three and handle recreation through separate doctrines such as custom, dedication, or prescription. The answer therefore depends on the state whose law governs the shoreline, and a recreational claim that succeeds in one jurisdiction may need a different legal vehicle in the next.

### Q: How is the mean high water line determined?

The mean high water line is the average of all high tides at a place over a full tidal epoch of 18.6 years, the cycle across which tidal patterns repeat. Surveyors establish it from tide gauge records maintained by the National Ocean Service, converting the averaged tidal datum into an elevation and then locating the line where that elevation meets the shore. Where gauge data are unavailable, shorter observation periods are adjusted by comparison with a nearby long-term station, and physical indicators such as vegetation lines or debris marks serve only as supporting evidence. The line is ambulatory: it moves as the shore erodes or accretes, so a boundary fixed by the tide rather than by a survey monument migrates over time. Sudden changes caused by a storm, called avulsion, are treated differently from gradual change and generally do not move the legal boundary. Because so many rights turn on the line, its determination is usually expert work contested through surveys rather than a measurement taken from a single tide.

### Q: What happens to public trust rights when land is filled?

Filling submerged land creates dry ground where water stood, and developers have used fill to convert trust land into building sites in harbors and bays across the country. The fill does not automatically erase the trust. In many states, filled tidelands remain subject to the doctrine unless the legislature has validly authorized the fill and the resulting use serves trust purposes or leaves the public interest in the remaining resource unimpaired, the same measure applied to outright grants. States such as Massachusetts regulate filled tidelands through a licensing system that has run in various forms since the nineteenth century, attaching conditions that preserve navigation and access. Other states treat some early fills as settled by statute or by long legislative acquiescence, particularly where port facilities were built on the fill. A purchaser of waterfront land therefore cannot assume that dry ground is free of the trust; the history of the parcel, including wharfing-out licenses and tidelands grants, forms part of its title.

### Q: Does the public trust doctrine apply in every state?

Every coastal and Great Lakes state, and every state with navigable rivers, recognizes some version of the doctrine, because title to the beds of navigable waters passed to each state at statehood. Beyond that common floor, the doctrine is state law, and its content differs considerably. States divide on the boundary between public and private shore, on whether recreation and ecological protection count as trust uses, on whether the trust reaches non-navigable waters and groundwater, and on how freely a legislature may convey trust land. Hawaii applies an expansive trust grounded in Native Hawaiian tradition as well as English law. Massachusetts retains the colonial rule of private foreshore ownership burdened by a public easement. Federal law supplies the title framework and the navigational servitude, but the working rules in a beach or lake dispute come from the constitution, statutes, and case law of the state where the land lies. A national statement of the doctrine is therefore a summary, never a substitute for the local rule.

### Q: How did the Mono Lake decision change the public trust doctrine?

In National Audubon Society v. Superior Court, decided in 1983, the California Supreme Court confronted diversions from tributary streams that were lowering Mono Lake, a navigable saline lake, and damaging its ecological and scenic values. Los Angeles held appropriative water rights granted under state permits decades earlier, and the question was whether those rights were immune from the trust. The court held they were not. The public trust applies to navigable waters and to diversions from non-navigable tributaries that harm them, and it operates alongside the appropriative rights system rather than being displaced by it. The state, as trustee, has a continuing duty to reconsider allocations when trust values are harmed, and no one acquires a vested right to use water in a way that injures the trust. The decision expanded the doctrine in two directions at once: from land to water allocation, and from a limit on conveyances to a continuing supervisory obligation over permits already granted.

### Q: Can the public trust doctrine stop a water diversion?

Yes, within limits that the Mono Lake decision defined in 1983. Where a diversion from a stream or tributary lowers a navigable lake, degrades a fishery, or destroys trust values in navigable water, a court may require the responsible agency to reconsider the diversion under its trust duty, reduce it, condition it, or in an extreme case end it. The doctrine does not create an automatic veto. Courts weigh the feasibility of protecting trust values against the purposes served by the diversion, including municipal water supply, and they generally direct agencies to study alternatives and to protect the trust where feasible. The distinctive force of the doctrine is that a permit is not the end of the matter: an allocation granted without regard to trust values remains open to revision, and reliance on an old permit does not ripen into a right to continue a harmful diversion. Diversions with no effect on navigable waters or trust resources fall outside the doctrine and are governed by ordinary water law.

### Q: Is a public trust claim a taking of private property?

Ordinarily no, because of the order in which the rights arose. Takings doctrine protects property a person actually owns, and land within the trust was never held free of the public rights attached to it. The Supreme Court expressed the underlying idea in Illinois Central by treating the trust as a limit inherent in the state title itself, and later courts describe the doctrine as a background principle of state property law of the kind recognized in Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council in 1992: a restriction that inheres in the title takes nothing when it is enforced. Disputes arise at the margins, where an owner claims that a court has expanded the trust onto land previously understood to be private. In Stop the Beach Renourishment v. Florida Department of Environmental Protection in 2010, the Court divided over whether a judicial redefinition could itself be a taking and produced no majority rule. Established trust limits are safe ground for the state; novel extensions onto settled private titles are where takings challenges gain traction.

### Q: What is the navigational servitude and how does it differ from the public trust?

The navigational servitude is a federal doctrine arising from the commerce power. Under it, the United States may control, improve, or obstruct navigable waters for navigation without compensating riparian owners for the loss of access, wharfage, or water power within the scope of the servitude, as decisions from Gibson v. United States in 1897 onward establish. The public trust, by contrast, is state law governing the state in its capacity as trustee of the beds and shores it owns. The differences run in several directions. The servitude belongs to the federal government; the trust constrains state government. The servitude serves navigation and commerce; the trust protects fishing and, in many states, recreation and ecology as well. The servitude is a dominant power that overrides private riparian rights without payment; the trust is a limit on disposition that can invalidate a state grant or permit. A single harbor project can implicate both, with federal action measured by the servitude and the state tidelands grant measured by the trust.

### Q: Who can bring a public trust lawsuit?

Standing follows ordinary principles with a public character added. A person who uses the affected water or shore and suffers a concrete injury from a grant, fill, or diversion, such as a fisher excluded from a fishery or a resident cut off from a beach, can sue. Organizations may sue on behalf of members who hold that kind of stake, which is how the National Audubon Society reached the California Supreme Court in 1983. Some states also permit citizen suits under environmental statutes, and attorneys general may sue to defend trust resources as part of the office itself. Courts are less receptive to a plaintiff whose only complaint is that the law was broken in the abstract, with no use or injury tied to the resource. Defendants are usually the agency that issued the permit or made the grant, joined with the private party relying on it. Because the trust belongs to the public, settlements and decrees in these cases are often supervised with an eye to interests beyond the named parties.

### Q: What remedies can a court order for a breach of the public trust?

The characteristic remedy is invalidation: a grant or conveyance that exceeds the trustee power may be declared void or revoked, as the revocation in Illinois Central was sustained in 1892, and a permit issued without regard to trust duties may be vacated and sent back to the agency for reconsideration. Courts also enjoin threatened harm, ordering a fill stopped, a fence removed, or a diversion reduced while the trust analysis is performed. Where access is the injury, the decree may define the public right in practical terms, specifying the strip of dry sand open to the public, the hours, and the routes across private upland, in the manner of the New Jersey beach cases. Monetary relief plays a smaller role, although rents and damages for past occupation of trust land are available in some states. Courts frequently retain a supervisory posture after judgment in water cases, since the trust duty continues and conditions on a lake or stream change with the water itself.

### Q: How does a statute like the New Jersey Public Access Law change the doctrine?

The New Jersey Public Access Law, enacted in 2019, moved part of the doctrine from case law into a statutory code, codified at N.J.S.A. 13:1D-150 and the sections that follow. The statute directs the state environmental agency to require public access to tidal waters as a condition of certain coastal permits, and it sets its own criteria for when access is required, including provisions on the location and adequacy of access points. Codification changes the working method of the doctrine. Under the common law, access questions were resolved through the four Matthews factors applied case by case; under the statute, the agency applies the enacted criteria, and a court reviewing the agency asks whether the statute was followed rather than reweighing the common-law factors. The doctrine itself is not abolished by such a statute: the common-law trust continues to govern questions the statute does not reach, including beach access disputes outside the permit setting. Statute and doctrine thus divide the field, with the enacted text controlling inside its stated scope.
