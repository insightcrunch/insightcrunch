Two relationships between an Egyptian queen and two Roman commanders are the most retold story in ancient history, and the retelling has almost entirely obscured what was actually at stake. The standard version presents a woman who seduced the two most powerful men in the world and destroyed them both, which is a story about character. What the evidence describes is a sequence of political transactions in which each party wanted something specific, negotiated for it, got some of it, and eventually lost everything to a third party who understood the propaganda value of the first version.

The argument this article makes is the alliances-not-just-romances thesis. Cleopatra's partnerships with Julius Caesar and then Mark Antony were strategic bids to secure Egyptian independence through the personal position of whichever Roman held real power, because no other route to independence existed. Each partnership was entered for reasons that can be stated in territorial and fiscal terms, each produced measurable gains for Egypt, and each was structured the way client-king relationships across the Roman east were structured. That personal bonds also existed, particularly in the second case, is not in doubt and is not the point. The point is that treating these as love affairs that happened to have political consequences reverses the causation. They were political arrangements that happened to involve intimacy, and the arrangements are what changed the map.

This article is the event-and-turning-point treatment of those alliances, and it keeps to that scope. The queen herself, her ancestry, her administration, her Egyptian religious policy, and her death are covered in the profile of [Cleopatra VII, the last pharaoh of Egypt](/2007/11/01/cleopatra-last-pharaoh-of-egypt/). The beauty legend and its two-thousand-year afterlife belong to the article on [the real Cleopatra versus the legend](/2008/03/01/real-cleopatra-vs-the-legend/). The annexation itself, the conversion of a kingdom into an imperial possession, is the subject of the article on [how Egypt fell to the Roman Empire](/2008/04/01/how-egypt-fell-to-rome/). What is answered here is how Egypt became a piece on the board of a Roman civil war, and why the game ended the way it did.

## What Egypt Needed and What Rome Wanted

The transaction only makes sense if both sides of the ledger are set out first, and both are concrete.

Egypt needed three things. It needed protection from annexation, which meant a Roman patron powerful enough to block the faction at Rome that wanted the province and its revenues seized outright. It needed the return of lost territory, above all Cyprus, which Rome had annexed in 58 BCE and which supplied timber, copper, and naval bases that Egypt could not produce for itself. And it needed relief from the debts contracted by her father, obligations to Roman financiers that were large enough to give private creditors leverage over Egyptian fiscal policy.

Rome, or rather individual Roman commanders, wanted two things from Egypt. The first was money. Roman civil wars were fought by armies that expected pay, donatives, and land on discharge, and the accumulated treasure of the Ptolemaic house was the largest liquid fortune in the Mediterranean. The second was grain and logistical support. An eastern campaign needed a secure supply base with a fleet, shipyards, and agricultural surplus, and Egypt was the only power in the eastern Mediterranean that could supply all three.

### Why did Roman commanders want Egyptian support so badly?

Egypt held the largest treasure in the Mediterranean, a grain surplus that could feed an army, and shipbuilding capacity. Roman civil wars were won by whoever could pay and supply troops longest, which made an Egyptian alliance a decisive strategic asset rather than a diplomatic nicety.

That symmetry is the whole basis of both alliances. A Roman commander in the east needed Egyptian resources; an Egyptian ruler needed Roman protection. The arrangement was not unusual in kind. Rome managed its eastern frontier through a network of client kingdoms, in Judaea, Cappadocia, Galatia, Pontus, Commagene, and elsewhere, whose rulers held their thrones by Roman favor and paid for it in troops, money, and loyalty. The Ptolemaic kingdom was simply the richest and most prestigious member of that network, and its ruler was in a position to negotiate rather than merely obey.

What made these particular arrangements exceptional was scale and personalization. A client king of Cappadocia dealt with whichever Roman governor was posted nearby. The Egyptian queen dealt directly with the men who were deciding who would rule Rome itself, and she did so at moments when that question was open. Her leverage was correspondingly greater, and so was her exposure.

## Why Pompey Died on an Egyptian Beach

The first alliance was made possible by a murder, and the murder was a calculation by people other than her.

Pompey the Great was defeated by Caesar at Pharsalus in Thessaly in August 48 BCE, and fled east with a small following. He chose Egypt because his relationship with the Ptolemaic house was long standing: the Senate had entrusted him with Egyptian affairs, he had supported her father's restoration, and the young king was formally his ward under the terms of the will. He expected sanctuary.

The court faction around Ptolemy XIII, examined in the profile article, made a different calculation. Sheltering a defeated general would earn Caesar's hostility; killing him would earn Caesar's gratitude and cost nothing. The rhetorician Theodotus is credited in the tradition with the argument that a dead man does not bite. Pompey was killed in a small boat as he came ashore near Pelusium in late September 48 BCE, in sight of his wife and son on the ship behind him, and his head was preserved for presentation.

### Who killed Pompey, and was Cleopatra involved?

The court faction around her brother Ptolemy XIII ordered it, executed by the general Achillas and a Roman officer serving in Egypt. She had been driven from Alexandria and was with her own army on the eastern frontier at the time, and no source places her in the decision.

Caesar arrived days later with a small force, two under-strength legions amounting to perhaps four thousand men, which is a detail that matters for everything that follows. He was not in a position to impose terms by force on a large city with its own army. He was in a position to be trapped, which is exactly what happened. The sources report that he was displeased rather than gratified by the presentation of Pompey's head, and whether that reaction was genuine or performed, it removed the diplomatic benefit the faction had expected to buy.

Caesar then did the thing that transformed a stopover into a decisive intervention. He took up residence in the palace, asserted the right to arbitrate between the joint rulers on the basis of her father's will, which had named Rome as guarantor, and simultaneously demanded repayment of the outstanding Ptolemaic debt, reduced from a claimed figure to a still enormous sum. He was, in effect, announcing that Egypt's internal succession was a Roman matter and that Egypt's treasury was a Roman asset.

## The Meeting and the Alexandrian War

She was outside the city and outside the negotiation, which meant her brother's faction would settle the dispute with Caesar in her absence and to her cost. Her response was to get into the palace.

The famous account, in Plutarch, has her carried in concealed inside a bedding sack, a strōmatodesmon, by a Sicilian retainer named Apollodorus, and delivered to Caesar's quarters. The detail that has entered popular memory, a rolled carpet, is a later embellishment; the ancient word describes bedding or a sleeping roll. The essential point is not the fabric but the operation: she penetrated a guarded palace held by her enemies to reach the arbitrator before the arbitration concluded. That is a covert infiltration under threat of death, planned and executed at about twenty-one, and it is routinely retold as a seduction stunt rather than as the act of nerve it was.

### How did Cleopatra get to Caesar?

She had herself smuggled into the palace inside a bedding roll, carried by a retainer, to reach Caesar before her brother's faction could settle the succession dispute without her. The rolled carpet of popular retelling is a later embellishment of the ancient account.

What she said to him is unrecorded and everything written about it is invention. What is recorded is the outcome: Caesar ruled that the joint rule specified in the will should be restored, which reinstated her, and the court faction responded with war. Achillas brought the Egyptian army, several times the size of Caesar's force, into Alexandria, and the Alexandrian War began.

The fighting ran through the winter of 48 and 47 BCE and was genuinely dangerous for Caesar. He held the palace quarter and the adjacent harbor front and was besieged in them. To prevent the Egyptian fleet from cutting his sea communications he ordered ships in the harbor burned, and the fire spread into the dockside district, an episode that became the origin of the story that he destroyed the Library, which the article on [the Library and Lighthouse of Alexandria](/2007/10/01/library-lighthouse-of-alexandria/) examines and finds to have burned warehouse stock rather than the collection itself.

The war also produced a rival claimant. Arsinoe IV, her younger sister, escaped the palace, joined the Egyptian forces, and was proclaimed queen. Her tutor Ganymedes took command and had Achillas killed, which tells us something about the cohesion of the Egyptian side. Fighting extended to the causeway and Pharos island, and at one point Caesar was forced to swim from a position that had been overrun.

Relief arrived overland in early 47 BCE, a force raised in Syria and Cilicia and led by Mithridates of Pergamum, which took Pelusium and advanced on the capital. In the engagement conventionally called the Battle of the Nile, the Egyptian army was defeated and Ptolemy XIII drowned attempting to escape across the river. She was restored to the throne, formally married to her remaining younger brother as Ptolemy XIV, and the first alliance was consummated in the only sense that mattered politically: her rival was dead, her sister was a prisoner, and a Roman army guaranteed her position.

## What Caesar Gave and What He Took

Setting out the terms of the first arrangement in ledger form shows how much of it was transactional.

Egypt received the throne for her, the elimination of her principal rival, and, decisively, the return of Cyprus to Ptolemaic administration. That restoration reversed the annexation of 58 BCE, restored a revenue stream, and returned naval facilities and timber access to a kingdom that had neither. It is the single most concrete gain of her entire foreign policy and it is almost never mentioned in popular accounts, which prefer to discuss the barge and the bedding roll.

Egypt also received the continued formal independence of the kingdom at a moment when annexation was entirely feasible. Caesar had an army in Alexandria, a compliant queen, and a Roman political faction that had wanted the province for decades. He did not take it. Whether that reflected the alliance, a judgment that a client kingdom served him better than a province administered by a potential rival, or both, the outcome for Egypt was another two decades of formal sovereignty.

### What did Cleopatra actually gain from the alliance with Caesar?

Her throne, the death of her rival brother, and the return of Cyprus to Egyptian control, reversing a Roman annexation of 58 BCE. She also secured Egypt's continued formal independence at a moment when a Roman commander with an army in Alexandria could simply have annexed it.

What Caesar took was money and legitimacy. The Ptolemaic debt was serviced, Egyptian resources supported his subsequent campaigns, and he acquired the standing that came from having settled the affairs of the wealthiest kingdom in the east. He also left three legions in Egypt, garrisoning the country under a commander of his own choosing, which is the standard mechanism by which a client kingdom is kept client. Those legions were as much a guarantee against her as for her.

A tradition preserved in later Roman writers describes a Nile voyage taken together after the war, with a great fleet of ships travelling upriver. The story is attractive and is treated skeptically by many historians, largely because Caesar's known movements leave a narrow window and because he had urgent business in Asia Minor, where he defeated Pharnaces at Zela in August 47 BCE. Whether or not the cruise happened at the reported scale, it would have made political sense: a joint royal progress through Egypt, with a Roman commander visibly endorsing the restored queen before the Egyptian population, is exactly the demonstration a newly reinstated ruler needed.

## What Caesar Wanted in the East

Understanding the first alliance requires knowing what Caesar was planning when he died, because the alliance was an early move in a project that never happened.

By 44 BCE he was preparing a campaign against Parthia on a very large scale, intended to avenge the Roman disaster at Carrhae in 53 BCE and to settle the eastern frontier permanently. That campaign would have required exactly what Antony's later attempt required: money, grain, ships, and a secure base in the southeastern Mediterranean. Egypt was the obvious supplier, and a friendly Egypt with a ruler who owed her throne to him was the ideal arrangement.

Read against that plan, the settlement of 47 BCE looks less like indulgence and more like preparation. He installed a client he had personally restored, garrisoned the country with three legions under a commander of his choosing, secured a treaty relationship in Roman law, and returned Cyprus, which gave the client the naval capacity a supporting fleet would need. Every element is what a commander planning an eastern war would want in place beforehand.

### Why did Caesar restore Cleopatra instead of annexing Egypt?

A client kingdom served his purposes better than a province. Annexation would have required a governor with an army in a wealthy country, creating a potential rival, whereas a restored queen who owed him her throne supplied money, grain, and ships without the political risk.

That reading also explains the restraint that puzzles readers who expect Roman commanders to annex whatever they can reach. Annexation would have created a rich province requiring a senior governor with legions, and in the late Republic that combination was how rivals were made. Pompey had built his position on eastern commands; Caesar had built his on Gaul. A man about to take an army to Parthia had no interest in leaving a Roman rival behind him with Egypt's revenues. A queen dependent on his personal favor was strictly safer, and this calculation is the same one that kept Egypt a special case even after annexation, when it was governed by an equestrian prefect rather than a senator.

The assassination cancelled the eastern campaign and left the arrangement without its purpose. She held a treaty with a dead man, a client relationship with a state now at war with itself, and a son whose claim threatened the man the will had made heir.

## Caesarion and the Paternity Problem

A son was born in 47 BCE, named Ptolemy Caesar and known to the Alexandrians by the diminutive Caesarion. He is the single most politically consequential product of the first alliance, and the question of his paternity is genuinely unresolvable and genuinely important.

The case for Caesar's paternity is circumstantial but not weak. The timing fits the period Caesar spent in Egypt. The name was given openly and was not challenged in Egypt. Mark Antony later stated in the Roman Senate that Caesar had acknowledged the child, and Antony was in a position to know what had been said in Caesar's circle. The queen's own government treated the boy as Caesar's son consistently and made that claim the basis of his standing.

The case against, or rather the case for doubt, is equally real. Caesar never named the boy in his will, which is the decisive Roman instrument for acknowledging a child, and instead adopted his great-nephew Octavian. He did not bring the child formally into his household at Rome. Caesar had no other surviving children despite three marriages and a long career, which some ancient and modern writers have read as evidence of infertility, though a single legitimate daughter had been born early in his life. A pamphlet by Caesar's associate Oppius argued specifically against the paternity, which shows the claim was being contested within his own circle during or shortly after his life.

### Was Caesarion really Caesar's son?

It cannot be determined. The timing fits, Antony told the Senate that Caesar had acknowledged him, and Egypt treated the claim as established. Against that, Caesar never named him in his will and adopted Octavian instead, and one of Caesar's own associates published a denial.

What matters more than the biological answer is the political function of the claim, and here the record is clear. A living son of Caesar was a standing threat to Octavian, whose entire position rested on being Caesar's heir by adoption. Adoption was a strong claim in Roman law and practice, but a claim of blood carried emotional force with the veterans and the urban population that adoption did not. That is why the boy's existence became a weapon in the propaganda war of the 30s BCE, why Antony pressed the acknowledgment publicly, and why Octavian had him killed in 30 BCE rather than raising him alongside the other children, as he did with his half siblings.

Read as a piece of policy, the child was the alliance made permanent. A queen with Caesar's son had a claim on Caesar's political heirs that no treaty could supply, and she pressed it for the rest of her life. That is also why the claim had to be destroyed, and the destruction was thorough: Octavian's regime denied the paternity, killed the claimant, and left the historical record with a question that cannot now be closed.

## Rome, 46 to 44 BCE

She spent a substantial part of the years between 46 and 44 BCE at Rome, staying in Caesar's villa across the Tiber, accompanied by her nominal co-ruler brother and her son. The visit is often described as a scandalous royal progress; it was a diplomatic residence, and it was normal for client rulers to spend time at Rome cultivating patrons.

The political content is visible in two things. First, a formal treaty relationship was established, with the Egyptian rulers recognized as friends and allies of the Roman people, which is the standard legal formula defining a client kingdom's status and which gave her a defensible position in Roman law. Second, Caesar placed a golden statue of her in the temple of Venus Genetrix, the goddess from whom his family claimed descent. That gesture is remarkable and has been argued over ever since. Placing a foreign queen's image in a temple associated with the founder's own divine ancestry made a public visual statement linking her to his line, and it is the strongest single piece of evidence that he intended some formal recognition of the connection.

### What was Cleopatra doing in Rome?

Conducting diplomacy. She secured formal recognition as a friend and ally of the Roman people, the legal status that defined a protected client kingdom, and cultivated the political class. Caesar also placed a golden statue of her in the temple of Venus Genetrix, publicly linking her to his family's divine ancestry.

The Roman elite reaction was hostile and is preserved most vividly in the letters of Cicero, who found her arrogant and disliked dealing with her, and whose complaints have the useful quality of being private correspondence rather than published propaganda. That hostility is informative in two ways. It shows that she was engaging directly with senior Roman politicians rather than remaining a decorative presence, and it shows the reflexive Roman discomfort with a foreign monarch, and particularly a female one, operating inside their political world. That discomfort was the raw material Octavian would later refine into a war.

She left Rome after Caesar's assassination on the Ides of March 44 BCE. The murder destroyed her entire strategic position in a single morning. Her patron was dead, his will named a nineteen-year-old great-nephew rather than her son, and Rome dissolved into a fresh round of civil war in which every faction would want Egyptian money.

## The Years of Hedging

The period between Caesar's death and the emergence of Antony as master of the east is the least discussed part of the story and the most revealing about how she operated, because it shows her behaving as a state rather than as a lover.

Her first act on returning to Egypt was dynastic housekeeping. Ptolemy XIV died in mid-44 BCE at the age of about fifteen, and ancient and modern opinion has generally suspected poison, since the death was extremely convenient and immediately preceded the elevation of her son as co-ruler. Whatever the cause, the constitutional effect was that the male name beside hers on official documents became Caesarion, which converted the paternity claim into the formal basis of Egyptian government.

Her second problem was that Egypt was in crisis. Nile floods failed in the early 40s BCE, famine followed, and plague is reported in the same period. A kingdom in that condition could not fight a war and could barely feed its capital.

### What did the Ides of March cost her?

It destroyed her strategic position. Her patron was dead, his will passed over her son in favor of Octavian, and Rome fragmented into competing factions each demanding Egyptian money. She spent the following years avoiding commitment while famine and plague weakened the kingdom.

Her third problem was that both sides in the Roman conflict demanded support. Cassius, one of Caesar's assassins, operating in Syria, required money and troops. The Caesarian side required the same. The record of her conduct is a study in deliberate ambiguity. The Roman legions Caesar had left garrisoning Egypt were handed over to a Caesarian commander, which was the choice she wanted made, though the sources differ on how much agency she had in it. She declined Cassius's demands while avoiding open defiance. Her governor in Cyprus, Serapion, supplied ships to Cassius, and she later disavowed him and had him executed, which is either evidence of a subordinate acting independently or evidence of a ruler hedging through deniable agents and then disposing of the agent.

She eventually committed. A fleet was prepared and sailed westward to support the Caesarian side, and it was damaged by storms and turned back without engaging. The commitment was therefore real in intent and null in effect, which may have been the ideal outcome from her position: demonstrable loyalty to the winning side without expended resources.

Philippi in October 42 BCE settled the question. Antony and Octavian destroyed the armies of Brutus and Cassius, the Caesarian faction won, and the Roman world was divided between the victors, with Antony taking the east. That division placed the man who controlled Syria, Asia Minor, and the eastern frontier directly on Egypt's border, and it made a second alliance not merely desirable but unavoidable.

## Tarsus, 41 BCE

The second alliance began with a summons, not an invitation, and the distinction matters because it establishes the power relationship at the outset.

Antony required money for a campaign against Parthia, which was the strategic priority of the Roman east and the project on which his reputation would rest. He also required an accounting from client rulers whose conduct during the civil war had been ambiguous, and hers had been ambiguous by design. She was ordered to Cilicia to explain herself.

What she did with that summons converted a subordinate's appearance into a diplomatic event on her own terms. She arrived at Tarsus by river, on a ship fitted out at enormous expense, with gilded stern, purple sails, silvered oars, music, perfume, and attendants costumed as sea nymphs and cupids, and she presented herself in the dress and attributes of Aphrodite coming to meet Dionysus. Plutarch's description of the scene is the most famous passage written about her and has furnished every artistic treatment since.

### What was the point of the barge at Tarsus?

It was a staged theophany, not a seduction. Antony was already publicly identifying himself with Dionysus in the Greek east, and she arrived as Aphrodite meeting the god, framing a summons to account for herself as a divine encounter between equals before an audience of Greek cities.

The theatrical reading misses what the theater was doing. Antony had been received in the Greek cities of Asia Minor as the New Dionysus, an identification the eastern Greek world understood and expected of a great ruler. By arriving as Aphrodite, she matched his divine claim with her own, established parity rather than subordination, and did it in front of an audience of Greek cities whose opinion shaped how Roman commanders were perceived in the east. She also refused to attend him on his terms, instead entertaining him aboard her own ship, which reversed the protocol of the summons entirely.

The expense is a point in itself. A ruler of a famine-stricken kingdom spent lavishly on a single appearance, which is irrational as consumption and rational as investment. She was demonstrating that Egypt remained wealthy, that its wealth was accessible, and that the Roman commander who needed Parthian-campaign funding should treat with her rather than dictate to her. That message was received.

The immediate political outcomes of the meeting were practical. Arsinoe IV, her surviving sister and a standing rival claimant who had been spared after being displayed in Caesar's triumph and was living under sanctuary at Ephesus, was executed at her request. Serapion, the Cypriot governor who had aided Cassius, was handed over and killed. The alliance began with her enemies being removed by Roman authority, which is precisely what the first alliance had also delivered.

## The Man the Second Alliance Involved

Antony is usually presented either as a drunken soldier ruined by a woman or as a romantic hero, and neither portrait survives contact with his record.

He was, before Egypt, one of the most effective Roman commanders of his generation. He had served under Caesar in Gaul, commanded the left wing at Pharsalus, and won the decisive engagement at Philippi in 42 BCE while Octavian was militarily irrelevant to the outcome. He was popular with soldiers to an unusual degree, which in the late Republic was the fundamental political asset. His speech at Caesar's funeral turned the Roman crowd against the assassins and drove them out of Italy, which is one of the most consequential pieces of political oratory in ancient history.

His weaknesses were also real and predated the alliance. He was inconsistent in administration, generous to the point of fiscal carelessness, and dependent on subordinates of variable quality. He had a demonstrated tendency to secure a strong position and then fail to consolidate it, which describes his conduct after Caesar's death as accurately as it describes the Parthian campaign.

### Was Antony a competent commander before Egypt?

Yes. He commanded the left wing at Pharsalus, won the decisive fighting at Philippi in 42 BCE, and held the loyalty of Caesar's veterans. His weaknesses were administrative inconsistency and a failure to consolidate advantages, and both predate the Egyptian alliance entirely.

The Roman assessment of him was shaped after his defeat by writers with obvious interests, and Plutarch's biography, which is the fullest surviving account, is built on a moral architecture in which a naturally great man is corrupted by an eastern queen. That structure is a literary convention rather than a finding, and it required the author to treat the Egyptian phase as a departure from his character rather than a continuation of it. A more sober reading is that he behaved in the east as ambitious Roman commanders in the east generally behaved, adopting local royal idiom because it worked, and that his real failures were operational.

The eastern identification with Dionysus is a case in point. It is regularly cited as evidence of megalomania or oriental corruption. In fact it followed a well-established pattern by which Roman commanders in the Greek east accepted the divine honors that Greek cities customarily offered to great benefactors, because refusing them was diplomatically clumsy. The identification made him legible to Greek subjects. It became damning only when Octavian pointed at it and told a Roman audience what it meant.

## What Each Side Was Buying

The Antony alliance ran for a decade and was renegotiated at least twice, so its terms are best understood as a standing arrangement rather than a single deal.

Antony needed Egyptian money and logistics for the Parthian campaign, which required an army of unprecedented size for an eastern expedition, with a supply train, a fleet, and the capacity to sustain a multi-year operation far from Roman bases. Egypt was the only power that could underwrite that. He also needed a secure southeastern flank while he was campaigning in Armenia and Media, and a hostile or unstable Egypt behind him was intolerable.

She needed three things. She needed the territorial restoration of the Ptolemaic maritime empire, the coastal possessions in Cyprus, Cilicia, Phoenicia, and Cyrenaica that the dynasty had held in its strength and lost across two centuries. She needed timber, which Egypt does not produce and which a fleet requires, and which meant control of or access to the Lebanese and Cilician coasts. And she needed her son's position secured, which meant a Roman patron willing to affirm his paternity publicly against Octavian's interest.

### What did each side actually need from the other?

He needed Egyptian money, ships, and grain to fund a Parthian campaign and a secure flank while he fought it. She needed the coastal territories the Ptolemies had lost, access to timber for a fleet, and a Roman patron willing to affirm her son's claim to be Caesar's.

Each side delivered substantially. That is the test that separates an alliance from an infatuation, and it is passed. He wintered in Alexandria in 41 and 40 BCE, and the twins Alexander Helios and Cleopatra Selene were born in 40 BCE. Then he left, and the alliance entered a long suspension that is the strongest single piece of evidence against the purely romantic reading.

## The Three-Year Gap

Between 40 and 37 BCE they did not meet, and Antony's conduct during those years is incompatible with the story of a man in the grip of an overwhelming passion.

A Parthian invasion of Syria and a political crisis in Italy pulled him west. The settlement he reached with Octavian, the Treaty of Brundisium in the autumn of 40 BCE, divided the Roman world between them and was sealed in the standard Roman manner, by marriage. Antony married Octavia, Octavian's sister, a woman of considerable ability and, by all accounts including hostile ones, real integrity. Two daughters were born of that marriage. He lived with her in Athens. He did not see the Egyptian queen for roughly three and a half years, during which she governed Egypt, raised his children, and made no recorded attempt to recall him.

### Why did the alliance go dormant for three years?

To seal the political settlement with Octavian at Brundisium in 40 BCE. Marriage was the standard Roman instrument for binding rival leaders, and the alliance with Octavian was more urgent at that moment than the Egyptian one, which he suspended entirely for over three years.

That gap is the fact the romantic tradition cannot accommodate and generally omits. It shows both parties treating the relationship as an instrument that could be shelved when other instruments served better. It also shows her behaving exactly as a head of state behaves when a treaty partner becomes temporarily unavailable: she waited, kept the kingdom running, and preserved the assets, meaning his children, that gave her a claim when circumstances changed.

Circumstances changed in 37 BCE. The renewed agreement with Octavian at Tarentum secured Antony's eastern command for a further term but delivered fewer legions than promised, which left him needing resources from somewhere else. He returned east, summoned her to Antioch, and the alliance resumed on substantially better terms for Egypt than before. The renegotiation is precisely what one would expect from a partner whose bargaining position had improved, and the timing tracks his political needs rather than any personal development.

## Antioch, 37 BCE: The Territorial Settlement

The Antioch arrangement is the concrete heart of the second alliance and deserves to be set out in detail, because it is the part that popular accounts skip and it is the part that made Egypt briefly a serious power again.

Antony transferred to Egyptian control or Egyptian revenue a substantial band of eastern territory. The grants included parts of Cyprus, portions of Cilicia on the southern Anatolian coast, coastal Phoenician territory, the region of Cyrenaica to the west of Egypt, and revenue rights over the balsam and bitumen production around Jericho and the Dead Sea, which were among the most valuable commodity monopolies in the ancient world. Nabataean territory was also affected.

The strategic logic is unmistakable once the map is considered. Cilicia and Phoenicia are timber country; both supply the cedar and pine that Egypt lacked and that a war fleet consumes. Cyprus adds copper, timber, and naval bases. Cyrenaica secures the western approach and adds grain land. Jericho's balsam is pure revenue. In a single settlement, the kingdom recovered much of the maritime and resource base that had made the early Ptolemaic state the dominant naval power of the eastern Mediterranean, the position described in the [complete guide to Ptolemaic Egypt](/2007/07/01/ptolemaic-egypt-complete-guide/).

### What did Egypt gain from the alliance with Antony?

Territory and resources on a substantial scale: parts of Cyprus and Cilicia, coastal Phoenician territory, the grain land of Cyrenaica, and revenue rights over the balsam production around Jericho. Those grants restored much of the timber, copper, naval bases, and agricultural base that had underpinned early Ptolemaic sea power.

The Roman objection, then and since, was that Antony was giving away Roman provincial territory to a foreign monarch. That objection is legally serious and was the basis of much of the propaganda that followed. It is also worth noting what Antony received: the resources to build and crew a fleet, finance an army, and mount the campaign that would have made him the conqueror of Parthia and, on any realistic assessment, the master of the Roman world. He was not making a gift. He was buying a war.

The question of whether the two were married at this point has been argued for two thousand years and has a clear answer that satisfies nobody. Under Roman law no valid marriage existed, since a Roman citizen could not contract legal marriage with a foreigner lacking the right of intermarriage, and Antony remained legally married to Octavia until 32 BCE. Under Egyptian and Hellenistic royal practice, a union may well have been solemnized and treated as real, and coins and inscriptions present the pair as a royal couple. Both statements are true in their own legal systems, and the disagreement between them was itself exploited as a scandal at Rome.

## Where This Fits: The Client-King System

The alliances read differently once they are set beside the other arrangements Rome maintained on the same frontier, because the pattern is unmistakable and it removes the aura of exceptionalism.

Rome governed its eastern periphery through client kings, rulers who kept their thrones by Roman recognition, supplied troops and money on demand, and were removed when they became inconvenient. Judaea under Herod, Cappadocia, Galatia, Pontus, Commagene, and Nabataea all operated on this basis. Client rulers travelled to Rome to cultivate patrons, paid heavily for favorable settlements, denounced rivals to their Roman superiors, and sought territorial grants at their neighbors' expense.

Herod of Judaea is the closest comparison and the most instructive. He was installed with Roman backing, he attached himself personally to Antony, he supplied money and troops, he lost territory to the Egyptian grants of 37 BCE, including the Jericho balsam revenues that he then had to lease back, and he competed continuously for Antony's favor. After Actium he did what a client king does: he presented himself to the victor, argued that his loyalty to a patron was evidence of reliability rather than treason, and kept his throne for another quarter century.

### How unusual was Cleopatra's arrangement with Rome?

Not unusual in kind. Rome ran its eastern frontier through client kings who paid for protection with money and troops and competed for the favor of Roman commanders. Egypt was the richest and most prestigious member of that system, which gave its ruler more leverage and more exposure.

That comparison sharpens the question of why the outcomes diverged. Herod survived because he had backed Antony as a subordinate and could plausibly transfer allegiance; his kingdom was not rich enough to be worth annexing outright and its internal politics were difficult enough to make direct rule unattractive. Egypt was too rich to leave alone, and its queen had made herself the personal enemy of the victor through the Donations, the paternity claim, and the propaganda war. There was no version of the settlement in which Octavian left Caesarion alive on an Egyptian throne.

The comparison also disposes of the idea that a woman ruling and negotiating with Roman commanders was inherently scandalous in eastern terms. Client rulers across the region conducted exactly this kind of politics. What was scandalous, at Rome, was that this particular client was female, wealthy, and successful enough to be treated as a principal rather than a supplicant.

## The Parthian Disaster

The campaign the alliance was built to fund failed, and the failure is the hinge on which everything after it turns.

Antony invaded in 36 BCE through Armenia into Media Atropatene, taking a very large army by the standards of eastern campaigning. The operation went wrong in the way eastern campaigns characteristically went wrong for Roman armies: the siege train, which was essential for taking fortified cities, was separated from the main column and destroyed with its escort, the Armenian ally withdrew, and the army was left besieging a fortified capital without the equipment to take it. The retreat through the mountains in deteriorating weather, harassed continuously by Parthian cavalry, cost enormous casualties. Estimates in the sources suggest losses approaching a third of the force, much of it to cold, hunger, and disease rather than to battle.

The consequences were political rather than merely military. Antony's reputation had rested on being the soldier of the triumvirate, the man who had won at Philippi while Octavian was ill in his tent. Parthia removed that advantage. Meanwhile Octavian, through his admiral Agrippa, was eliminating Sextus Pompeius, securing the western seas, and building a naval capability that would prove decisive. The balance of the partnership inverted between 36 and 33 BCE, and Antony's dependence on Egyptian resources deepened at exactly the moment when that dependence became politically damaging at Rome.

### Why did Antony's Parthian campaign matter so much?

It was the project the Egyptian alliance was funding, and its failure in 36 BCE cost him his military reputation and a third of his army. From that point he depended more heavily on Egyptian resources while Octavian consolidated the west, and the partnership's balance never recovered.

She met him after the retreat with supplies and money for the surviving troops, which is what an ally does and which the hostile tradition records as further evidence of his dependence on her. The third child of the partnership, Ptolemy Philadelphus, was born in 36 BCE. In 34 BCE an Armenian campaign produced a real if modest success, the capture of the Armenian king, and it was this success that Antony chose to celebrate in a way that handed his enemy the weapon that destroyed him.

## The Donations of Alexandria

In 34 BCE, in Alexandria, Antony staged a procession celebrating the Armenian victory and followed it with a ceremony in which eastern territories were distributed among the queen and her children. The event is usually called the Donations of Alexandria, and it is the single most damaging political act of his career.

The procession came first and was itself provocative. A victory parade with a captive king, staged in a Greek city rather than at Rome, read to Roman eyes as a triumph celebrated in the wrong place, before the wrong people, by a man who was arrogating to himself in Egypt the honors that belonged to the Roman state. Whether Antony intended that reading is disputed; that it was available to his enemies is not.

The ceremony that followed was worse. Seated on golden thrones on a raised platform, with the children on lower thrones before them, Antony proclaimed a settlement of the east. She was named Queen of Kings, ruling jointly with Caesarion, who was named King of Kings and, decisively, publicly affirmed as the true son of Julius Caesar. Alexander Helios received Armenia, Media, and the Parthian territories not yet conquered. Cleopatra Selene received Cyrenaica and Crete. Ptolemy Philadelphus, aged two, received Syria and Cilicia.

### What exactly was proclaimed in 34 BCE?

A ceremony in 34 BCE at which Antony distributed eastern territories among Cleopatra and her children, naming her Queen of Kings and publicly affirming Caesarion as Julius Caesar's true son. It handed Octavian the propaganda material that turned a Roman civil war into a foreign one.

Read as eastern policy, the settlement is defensible. Rome had always governed its eastern frontier through client kings, and installing a friendly dynasty across the region under a single royal house was an administrative solution to a genuine problem. The territories granted to the younger children were largely notional, including regions not under Roman or Egyptian control at all, which is characteristic of Hellenistic royal proclamations and would have been understood as such by a Greek audience.

Read at Rome, it was catastrophic. It appeared to give away Roman territory to a foreign queen, to establish a rival dynastic center in Alexandria, and above all to elevate a boy claiming to be Caesar's blood son directly against Octavian's adopted claim. That last element made the ceremony a personal attack on the legitimacy of Octavian's entire position, and it guaranteed a war.

The strategic error was one of audience. The ceremony was designed for the Greek east, where royal display, divine titulature, and grand territorial proclamations were the expected language of power. It was consumed at Rome, where those same elements read as monarchy, oriental despotism, and treason. Both Antony and the queen had spent years managing eastern opinion successfully; on this occasion they forgot that the audience that mattered was two thousand kilometers to the west.

## The Propaganda War

Between 33 and 32 BCE the conflict was fought with words before it was fought with ships, and Octavian won that phase decisively.

His central achievement was framing. A war between two Roman commanders was a civil war, and Italy was exhausted by civil wars and would not enthusiastically fund another. A war against a foreign queen who had corrupted a Roman general was a patriotic war of defense. Octavian therefore made her the enemy and Antony the victim, which had the further advantage of leaving a path open for Antony's supporters to change sides without appearing to betray a Roman cause.

The material he used was partly supplied by his opponents and partly manufactured. The Donations were real. The eastern territorial grants were real. Antony's divorce of Octavia in 32 BCE, dismissing the sister of his colleague from his house, was real and was a gross insult in Roman terms. To these were added the standard repertoire of accusations: drunkenness, subjection to a woman, adoption of eastern dress and manners, and the charge that the war's object was the transfer of the capital to Alexandria.

### Why did Rome declare war on Cleopatra and not Antony?

Because declaring war on Antony would have made it another civil war, which an exhausted Italy would not support. Naming a foreign queen as the enemy made it a patriotic defensive war and let Antony's supporters change sides without appearing to betray Rome.

The most striking move was the will. Octavian obtained Antony's testament from the Vestal Virgins, with whom Roman wills were customarily deposited, and read portions of it publicly. This was a serious violation of religious and legal convention and Octavian took a real risk in doing it. The contents, as reported, included recognition of Caesarion as Caesar's son, legacies to the Egyptian children, and a request to be buried in Alexandria beside the queen rather than at Rome.

Whether the document was genuine, selectively quoted, or partly fabricated cannot be determined, and modern historians divide on it. Its effect was not in doubt. The burial clause in particular converted the abstract charge of eastern corruption into something an ordinary Roman could feel: a Roman commander who did not wish to be buried in Roman soil had, in the terms of that culture, ceased to be Roman. The Senate stripped Antony of his offices, and war was declared on the queen of Egypt in 32 BCE.

## The Numbers Problem

Ancient accounts of this conflict supply figures for fleets, armies, and treasure, and every one of them needs handling with the same caution the rest of this series applies to ancient statistics.

The forces at Actium are reported at figures that would make it one of the largest naval engagements ever fought, with several hundred warships on each side and land armies in six figures. Those numbers come from literary sources written well after the event, several of them working within a tradition that had an interest in making the victory look enormous. Modern estimates generally reduce them, and the more important qualification is that raw ship counts are misleading here for a specific reason: Antony's fleet was undermanned. He burned a portion of his own ships before the battle because he lacked crews for them, which means a nominal count of hulls overstates his fighting strength substantially.

The type of ship matters more than the count in any case. Antony's fleet included very large vessels, heavy, high-sided, and powerful in a boarding action but slow and dependent on full crews. Octavian's fleet, built and trained under Agrippa in the campaigns against Sextus Pompeius, was lighter and more maneuverable. A fleet of heavy ships with sick and depleted rowing crews facing lighter ships with healthy ones is not an even match regardless of what the totals say.

### How reliable are the ancient figures for Actium?

Poorly. Ship and troop counts come from literary sources written long afterward by writers with an interest in magnifying the victory, and Antony's nominal fleet size is misleading because he lacked crews and burned ships before the battle. Ship types and crew health mattered more than totals.

The treasure figures raise the same problem in a different form. The Ptolemaic reserve seized at Alexandria is described in the sources as enormous without a securely reported total, and the most useful evidence is indirect and economic. Ancient testimony indicates that the influx of Egyptian bullion into the Roman economy after 30 BCE depressed interest rates sharply and drove up land prices, which is a measurable macroeconomic consequence and a better indication of scale than any stated figure. A single conquest that visibly moves the cost of credit in the capital was a very large transfer of wealth.

The lesson generalizes. Where an ancient source gives a round number for an army, a fleet, or a fortune, treat it as an order of magnitude at best, and look instead for indirect evidence with a physical or economic footprint. Coins, price movements, and construction programs are harder to exaggerate than casualty lists.

## Actium

The campaign of 31 BCE was decided by supply and disease before it was decided by fighting, which is the least dramatic and most accurate account of it.

Antony and the queen wintered with a large fleet and army on the western coast of Greece, positioned to defend the approaches to the east. Agrippa, commanding Octavian's fleet, conducted an aggressive campaign against their supply bases and sea communications, taking positions to the south and progressively cutting the routes by which the fleet and army were provisioned. Octavian landed forces to the north and established a fortified position.

The consequences of the blockade were severe. Supplies ran short, and the army camp on marshy ground near the Ambracian Gulf suffered heavily from malaria and dysentery, which reduced fighting strength and, critically, reduced the trained rowers needed to work the ships. Desertions to Octavian began among senior officers, most notably Domitius Ahenobarbus, and a Roman commander whose lieutenants are changing sides is a commander whose position has already failed.

### What actually decided the Actium campaign?

Supply and disease. Agrippa cut the sea routes feeding Antony's fleet and army, the camp on marshy ground was ravaged by malaria and dysentery, rowers died or deserted, and senior officers began changing sides. The naval battle confirmed a position already lost.

The battle itself, fought on the second of September 31 BCE at the mouth of the Ambracian Gulf, was accordingly less a trial of strength than an attempt to escape one. Antony's fleet was undermanned, and before engaging he burned a portion of his own ships because he lacked crews for them, which tells the story on its own. The fighting was inconclusive along much of the line. Then the Egyptian squadron of some sixty ships, which had been held in reserve and which carried the treasury, hoisted sail, broke through the center, and ran south for Egypt. Antony transferred to a lighter vessel and followed. The remainder of the fleet surrendered within days, and the army, left without its commander, capitulated about a week later.

## Did She Betray Him at Actium?

This is the most consequential interpretive dispute in the whole story, and it deserves a defended verdict rather than a shrug.

The hostile reading, which is the ancient Roman one, holds that she fled in panic and that Antony, enslaved by passion, abandoned his fleet and his army to follow her, thereby throwing away an empire. This account appears in Plutarch and Dio and became the moral centerpiece of the whole tradition. It is the version that makes the story a tragedy of character.

The alternative reading holds that the breakout was planned. Several details support it and they are cumulative rather than individually decisive. The presence of the treasury aboard the Egyptian ships is difficult to explain except as preparation for withdrawal, since a fleet expecting to fight a decisive battle does not carry its war chest into the line. Sails were taken aboard, which warships did not normally carry into battle, since they fought under oar; Antony reportedly explained this as preparation for pursuit, which is unconvincing and looks like cover. The squadron was positioned to exploit a gap and did so at the moment one opened. And the strategic situation made escape rational: with supplies cut, rowers dying, and officers deserting, preserving the fleet's core, the treasure, and the commander for a continued war from Egypt was a better outcome than annihilation.

### Was the breakout planned or a panic?

The evidence favors a planned breakout rather than a betrayal. Her ships carried the treasury and had sails aboard, neither of which belongs in a battle line, and the strategic position was already lost. Preserving the fleet's core and the war chest was the rational move.

The verdict this article defends is that the flight was a prearranged escape that failed in its larger purpose. It succeeded narrowly, in that both commanders and the treasure reached Egypt. It failed completely in that the abandoned army and fleet surrendered rather than fighting on, which removed the forces that any continued resistance required. Whether the surrender was foreseeable is arguable; troops whose commander has visibly sailed away tend not to hold, and that consideration should have been part of the plan.

The Roman version survived because it was more useful and more satisfying. It explained a Roman defeat by Roman standards, converting a logistical collapse into a moral failure, and it preserved Antony's reputation as a soldier by blaming a woman. That the version served everyone's interests except hers is the reason to distrust it.

## Love or Politics?

Having argued the strategic reading throughout, honesty requires addressing the emotional dimension directly rather than pretending it was absent.

The case for genuine attachment in the second partnership is substantial. Antony spent winters in Alexandria when Roman interests would have been better served elsewhere. He acknowledged three children publicly at real political cost. He divorced Octavia, which was politically suicidal and served no strategic purpose that a continued formal marriage would not have served better. He requested burial in Alexandria, if the will is genuine on that point. And after Actium, when a purely calculating commander would have negotiated separately, he did not.

Her side is harder to read, because almost nothing survives that expresses her view of anything. The strongest indicators are behavioral. She committed the Egyptian fleet and treasury to his war rather than hedging as she had after Caesar's death, which was the riskier choice and the one that destroyed her. She did not detach in 33 or 32 BCE when the balance of power had visibly shifted and when an approach to Octavian was still conceivable. A ruler optimizing purely for Egypt's survival would have explored that door; she did not.

### Where does the evidence for genuine attachment lie?

The evidence for genuine attachment is far stronger in the Antony partnership, where both parties made choices that damaged their political interests. With Caesar the relationship looks transactional on both sides. Her own view is unrecoverable, since nothing survives in her voice.

The verdict this article defends is that the alliances were political in origin, structure, and content, and that the second one became something more, which is visible precisely in the decisions that cannot be explained politically. That is not a romantic conclusion. It is an observation that both parties, in the last phase, stopped optimizing, and the failure to hedge is the evidence.

It is worth adding what the romantic tradition obscures by focusing on two relationships. She was in her partnerships with Caesar and Antony for a combined total of perhaps six years out of a twenty-one-year reign. The remaining fifteen years were spent governing: coinage, grain, temples, priesthoods, and famine, which the profile article covers. Reducing the reign to the relationships is a distortion of proportion before it is a distortion of interpretation.

## What Changed Because of These Alliances

The event-and-turning-point question is what the world looked like afterward that it had not looked like before, and the answer has four parts.

The first is that Egypt's independence ended. The kingdom that Ptolemy I had built by manufacturing legitimacy in four currencies, as the profile of [Ptolemy I and the founding of a dynasty](/2007/09/01/ptolemy-i-founding-of-dynasty/) describes, became the personal possession of a Roman ruler, administered by a prefect of equestrian rank rather than a senatorial governor precisely because its wealth made it too dangerous to entrust to a senator with ambitions. That transition and its mechanics are covered in the article on [how Egypt fell to the Roman Empire](/2008/04/01/how-egypt-fell-to-rome/) and its consequences in the [complete guide to Roman Egypt](/2008/05/01/roman-egypt-complete-guide/).

The second is that the Roman Republic ended. This is the larger consequence and it is easy to miss because the Egyptian frame obscures it. Actium was the last battle of a century of civil war, and its outcome delivered sole power to one man, which is the definition of the transition from republic to principate. Egyptian money paid for a substantial part of the losing side, and Egyptian money, seized after the conquest, paid for the winning settlement. The treasure that Octavian took from Alexandria funded the veteran discharges and land purchases that stabilized his regime, and ancient testimony indicates it depressed interest rates at Rome and raised property values, which is a measurable macroeconomic effect from a single conquest.

### What was the long-term consequence of these alliances?

Egypt lost its independence and became an imperial possession supplying grain to Rome, and the treasure seized at Alexandria funded the settlement that converted the Roman Republic into the principate. Both outcomes were decided by which Roman commander the alliance backed.

The third is that Egypt became Rome's granary. The country's agricultural surplus was redirected to feed the capital, an arrangement examined in the article on [Egypt as the breadbasket of Rome](/2008/06/01/egypt-breadbasket-of-rome/), and that role defined the province for centuries. A kingdom that had used its grain as a diplomatic instrument became a territory whose grain was simply collected.

The fourth is cultural and is the one with the longest life. The Augustan propaganda campaign that made a foreign queen the enemy created a portrait that outlived the regime that manufactured it, entered Roman literature as a fixed type, and passed from there into the whole European tradition. The most successful piece of political messaging in ancient history is a set of poems about a woman who died in 30 BCE, and its success is the reason the article on the real Cleopatra versus the legend is needed at all.

## Five Claims Tested

Because this story attracts embellishment more than any other in ancient history, five common claims are worth grading directly.

The first claim is that she was smuggled to Caesar rolled in a carpet. This survives in substance and fails in detail. The ancient account describes a bedding sack or sleeping roll, not a carpet, and the carpet is a later refinement. The smuggling itself is well attested and is the significant part.

The second claim is that Caesar acknowledged Caesarion as his son. This is undetermined. Antony asserted it publicly in the Senate and Egypt treated it as established, but Caesar's will named Octavian and not the boy, and one of Caesar's own associates published a denial. The claim's political function is clear; its truth is not recoverable.

The third claim is that Antony and Cleopatra were married. This depends on the legal system. Under Roman law no valid marriage existed, since a citizen could not contract legal marriage with a foreigner lacking the necessary right, and Antony remained married to Octavia until 32 BCE. Under Egyptian and Hellenistic royal practice a union may have been solemnized and was presented as real on coins and inscriptions.

The fourth claim is that she fled Actium in panic and Antony followed out of infatuation. This fails as stated. The presence of the treasury and of sails aboard her squadron indicates a prepared breakout, and the strategic position had already collapsed through blockade, disease, and desertion. The panic reading is Roman moralizing applied to a logistical defeat.

The fifth claim is that Antony intended to move the capital of the Roman world to Alexandria. This fails on evidence. It appears as an accusation in Octavian's propaganda and in poets writing under his patronage, and no independent source supports it. It was an effective charge precisely because it was unfalsifiable.

### Which parts of this story are securely documented?

The main sequence is firm: Pompey's murder in 48 BCE, the Alexandrian War, Cyprus returned, Caesarion born in 47 BCE, Tarsus in 41 BCE, the Antioch grants of 37 BCE, the Donations of 34 BCE, the declaration of war in 32 BCE, and Actium in 31 BCE. Motives and private conduct are not.

## The Last Year

Between Actium in September 31 BCE and the fall of Alexandria in August 30 BCE lay eleven months in which both partners attempted, separately and together, to find a way out. The attempts failed, but they are evidence about how each behaved under terminal pressure, and they contradict the tradition of a couple who spent the interval drinking.

Her efforts were the more inventive. The best documented is an attempt to move ships overland from the Mediterranean to the Red Sea across the desert, with the intention of escaping eastward with a fleet and the treasury toward the trade routes of Arabia and India, where Roman power did not reach. The operation was reportedly wrecked by Nabataean raiders who burned the vessels, an act with an obvious motive given the territory transferred away from Nabataea in 37 BCE. Whether the plan was as large as reported, its underlying logic is sound: get out of the trap, keep the money, and re-establish somewhere beyond reach.

She also negotiated. Approaches were made to Octavian offering her abdication in exchange for the throne passing to her children, and Caesarion was sent south toward the Red Sea coast with money and an escort, evidently with the intention of getting him out of Egypt entirely. Both efforts were rational attempts to preserve the dynasty at the cost of her own position, and both failed, the second because the boy was persuaded or tricked into returning and was killed.

### What did Cleopatra do after Actium?

She attempted to drag ships overland to the Red Sea to escape eastward with the treasury, sent her son Caesarion south with money and an escort, and negotiated with Octavian to secure the throne for her children in exchange for her own abdication. All three attempts failed.

Antony's conduct in the same period is harder to defend and is the basis of the tradition of collapse. He withdrew for a time to an isolated dwelling on the coast, and the sources report a period of despondency before he returned to the palace. He also attempted negotiation and was ignored. When Octavian's forces arrived, first from Syria through Pelusium and then from Cyrene in the west, Antony's remaining troops and ships defected rather than fought, which was the predictable consequence of Actium.

The final sequence occupied the first days of August 30 BCE. Alexandria fell, Antony died by his own hand after receiving a false report of her death, and she was taken alive in the mausoleum where she had gathered the treasure. She died on or about the tenth or twelfth of August. The details of her death, the competing accounts of the method, and the question of the tomb are handled in the profile of [Cleopatra VII, the last pharaoh of Egypt](/2007/11/01/cleopatra-last-pharaoh-of-egypt/).

Two political facts complete the story. Caesarion was killed, ending the Ptolemaic line and, more importantly for the victor, ending the rival claim to Caesar's blood. The three younger children were taken to Rome, displayed in the triumph, and raised in the household of Octavia, the woman Antony had divorced, which is one of the stranger arrangements in Roman history and a demonstration of how thoroughly the personal and the political were fused in this whole affair.

## A Chronology of the Alliances

The sequence matters for anyone reproducing it under examination conditions, and the dates in this period are unusually firm because the Roman side is well documented.

Caesar defeated Pompey at Pharsalus in August 48 BCE. Pompey was murdered on the Egyptian coast near Pelusium in late September 48 BCE, and Caesar arrived days later with a small force. The queen was smuggled into the palace, Caesar ruled for the restoration of joint rule, and the Alexandrian War followed through the winter of 48 and 47 BCE. Relief arrived early in 47 BCE, Ptolemy XIII drowned in the fighting near the Nile, and she was restored with Ptolemy XIV as nominal co-ruler. Caesarion was born later in 47 BCE. Cyprus returned to Egyptian control under the settlement.

She resided at Rome between 46 and 44 BCE, securing recognition as a friend and ally of the Roman people. Caesar was assassinated on the Ides of March 44 BCE. She returned to Egypt, Ptolemy XIV died later that year, and Caesarion became co-ruler. Famine and plague afflicted Egypt in the following years while she avoided firm commitment in the war between the triumvirs and the assassins. Antony and Octavian won at Philippi in October 42 BCE, and Antony took the east.

The Tarsus meeting followed in 41 BCE, with the executions of Arsinoe IV and Serapion, and Antony wintered in Alexandria. The twins were born in 40 BCE, the year Antony returned west, agreed the Treaty of Brundisium, and married Octavia. The partnership was suspended until 37 BCE, when the Tarentum agreement sent him east again and the Antioch settlement transferred Cyprus, Cilician and Phoenician coast, Cyrenaica, and Jericho revenues to Egyptian control.

The Parthian campaign failed in 36 BCE, the year Ptolemy Philadelphus was born. The Armenian campaign of 34 BCE produced the Alexandrian procession and the Donations. Antony divorced Octavia in 32 BCE, Octavian published his will, and war was declared on the queen. Actium was fought on the second of September 31 BCE. Alexandria fell on the first of August 30 BCE, Antony died as it fell, and she died on or about the tenth or twelfth of August. Caesarion was killed shortly afterward and Egypt passed to Roman control.

## How the Alliances Have Been Judged

The judgment history divides into three phases and each phase reveals more about its own period than about the events.

The Augustan phase produced the framework everything else inherited. Horace's ode on the queen's defeat, Propertius, and Virgil supply the images of the foreign menace, the drunken general, and the deliverance of Rome, and they were writing under the patronage of the victor. That is not a neutral tradition and should never be quoted as though it were.

The moralizing phase runs from Plutarch through the medieval and early modern reception. Plutarch's Life of Antony gives the story its enduring shape as a tragedy of a great man undone by passion, which is a moral architecture imposed on a political failure. Later writers found the shape irresistible because it teaches a lesson, and the dramatic tradition built on it. That reception history belongs to the article on [the real Cleopatra versus the legend](/2008/03/01/real-cleopatra-vs-the-legend/).

### How has modern scholarship reassessed these alliances?

By reading them as client-king politics rather than as a moral drama. Documentary evidence and comparison with other eastern client rulers show standard transactional arrangements, and Actium is now explained primarily by blockade, disease, and desertion rather than by infatuation.

The modern phase has moved substantially and in a consistent direction. Work on the Roman client system placed her arrangements in a context where they look ordinary. Work on the Actium campaign, particularly on the logistics and the disease environment, established that the position had collapsed before the battle. Work on the Augustan cultural program identified the propaganda as propaganda and dated it. The cumulative effect is that the events now read as a conventional if very high-stakes struggle over the eastern Mediterranean, decided by supply, ships, and money.

A countercurrent deserves noting. Some popular treatments have overcorrected into a version in which she is a farsighted anti-imperial statesman resisting Roman aggression on behalf of the east. That is as anachronistic as the seductress. She was a Hellenistic dynast pursuing her house's territorial and fiscal interests, entirely willing to receive other kingdoms' territory when a Roman patron granted it, and her quarrel with Rome was about who held Egypt rather than about whether empires should exist.

## The Alliances Seen From Alexandria

Almost every account of these events is written from the Roman side, which makes it easy to forget that the alliances had a domestic dimension inside Egypt, and that dimension was not entirely comfortable.

Three Roman legions garrisoned the country after 47 BCE. A population that had spent the previous century watching Roman commanders arrive, extract payments, and install or remove rulers now had Roman soldiers permanently quartered among them. The Alexandrian crowd, which had a long record of deposing kings, had watched a Roman general fight a street war through their city, burn part of their harbor district, and leave a queen in place. Whatever the diplomatic gains, the visible fact on the ground was foreign troops.

The fiscal dimension was equally visible. The obligations to Roman creditors inherited from her father were serviced out of Egyptian revenue, the Roman commanders who came required funding, and the enormous expenditure on displays such as the Tarsus appearance came from the same treasury. All of it was raised, ultimately, from the grain and the taxes of a countryside that had suffered consecutive failed Nile floods and the famine and plague that followed. The two-tier social order examined in the article on [Greek and Egyptian life in Alexandria](/2008/01/01/greek-egyptian-life-alexandria/) meant those costs fell most heavily on the Egyptian cultivators who had the least say in the policy.

### What did the Roman alliances mean for ordinary Egyptians?

Foreign troops garrisoned in the country, heavy taxation to service Roman debts and fund Roman campaigns, and famine years in which the grain that remained was directed to Alexandria. The territorial gains benefited the crown and the elites rather than the countryside.

Against that, the domestic religious policy continued and even intensified through the same period, with temple building and priestly patronage running steadily, as the article on [temples as power centers in late Egypt](/2006/07/01/temples-as-power-centers-egypt/) describes for the wider Late Period. The crown was buying internal legitimacy with one hand while spending Egyptian wealth on Roman politics with the other, and the two policies were connected: a ruler whose foreign policy imposed costs needed internal support more, not less.

There is a further point about how the alliances were presented domestically. In Egyptian religious terms a queen partnered with a Roman commander could be framed within existing categories, since the identification with Isis carried a consort role built into the theology. The Tarsus staging as Aphrodite meeting Dionysus works in Greek terms; the same relationship read in Egyptian terms as Isis and her divine partner. The presentation was pitched separately to each constituency, which is the same dual-track technique the dynasty had used since its founder.

## What the Sources Do Not Tell Us

A short inventory of what cannot be known is worth stating explicitly, because confident narration tends to paper over the gaps.

Nothing survives in her voice. There is no letter, no memoir, no recorded speech, and no Egyptian document that expresses a view rather than issuing an instruction. Every statement about what she intended, felt, or calculated is inference, and most of it is inference by writers who disliked her.

The private conduct of both relationships is entirely unrecoverable. The scenes that fill the tradition, the conversations, the banquets, the reported wagers and gestures, come from a genre of anecdote that ancient biography collected precisely because it was vivid rather than because it was verified. They may preserve something real; they cannot be used as evidence for anything.

### What is genuinely unknowable about Cleopatra's alliances?

Her own view of them. No document survives in her voice, so every statement about her motives is inference, usually by hostile writers. The private conduct of both relationships and the content of the conversations that shaped them are equally beyond recovery.

The Egyptian internal debate is also missing. Ptolemaic court politics is visible only in outline, and whether her advisers argued for or against the Antony commitment, whether there was an Egyptian faction favoring accommodation with Octavian, and how the priesthoods viewed the Roman entanglement are all questions the surviving material cannot answer. Given that the court had removed her once already, the existence of internal opposition is likely and its shape is invisible.

That inventory is not a counsel of despair. The territorial grants, the coinage, the treaties, the dates, and the outcomes are solid, and they support the argument this article makes. What they do not support is the confident psychological narration that dominates popular accounts, and the honest course is to build the analysis on the ledger and to mark the rest as unknown.

## How to Argue This Topic Well

For a student or teacher handling these events, the topic has two standard failure modes and naming them is the fastest route to a strong treatment.

The first failure is narrating the Roman moral story: passion, corruption, and the fall of a great man, with the queen as the agent of ruin. That is not a traditional account requiring balance; it is the losing side's obituary written by the winner's clients, and reproducing it without attribution is a source-handling error rather than an interpretive choice.

The second failure is treating the relationships as merely romantic and therefore historically trivial, skipping to Actium as if the decade between meant nothing. The Antioch settlement of 37 BCE moved more territory than most wars of the period and is the reason Egypt could field a fleet at all in 31 BCE. An account that omits it cannot explain the campaign it is building toward.

### What is the strongest way to write about these alliances?

Treat them as client-king politics with named terms. State what each side supplied and received, date the territorial grants, explain Actium by supply and disease before invoking character, and attribute the moral narrative to the Augustan writers who produced it.

The general method transfers, which is why this series keeps returning to it. When a famous historical relationship is described almost entirely in terms of character and desire, check who wrote the description and what they gained by it, then look for the ledger underneath: what changed hands, when, and in which direction. In this case the ledger is unusually legible, since territories, coins, and treaties leave records that poems do not.

## The Honest Verdict

The two alliances were the correct policy, executed with skill, and they failed because the variable that decided them was never under Egyptian control.

The strategic logic is not seriously contestable. Egypt could not resist Rome militarily; that had been demonstrated repeatedly and no Ptolemaic army of the first century BCE could face consular legions. The kingdom's independence therefore depended on being valuable to a Roman patron powerful enough to protect it, and on that patron staying in power. Both alliances pursued that logic, and both produced concrete returns: Cyprus in the first case, an entire coastal empire in the second, plus the elimination of rival claimants in both.

The execution shows real ability. Getting into the palace at Alexandria in 48 BCE to reach the arbitrator was a considerable risk taken decisively. The staging at Tarsus in 41 BCE converted a summons into a negotiation between equals. The hedging between 44 and 42 BCE kept Egypt out of a war whose winner was unknown, at the cost of some credibility and no resources. These are the moves of a competent operator.

Two failures belong to her rather than to circumstance. The first is the Donations of Alexandria, which was designed for an eastern audience and read by a western one, and which handed Octavian the framing that turned a civil war into a patriotic crusade. The second is the failure to hedge after 33 BCE, when Antony's decline was visible and an approach to the other side was still conceivable. She had hedged expertly once before; she did not do it again, and the most plausible explanation is that the second alliance had stopped being purely an alliance.

One further comparison sharpens the verdict, because it isolates the variable that actually decided the outcome. Herod of Judaea backed Antony as loyally as she did, supplied money and troops, lost territory to the Egyptian grants of 37 BCE, and was on the losing side at Actium. He kept his throne for another quarter of a century. The difference was not competence, loyalty, or judgment; on every one of those measures the two client rulers behaved similarly. The difference was that Judaea was not worth annexing and Egypt was, and that Herod had never made himself the personal enemy of the victor while she had, through the paternity claim, the Donations, and a decade of propaganda exchange. A client kingdom survives a change of Roman patron if it is too troublesome to govern directly and if its ruler has not become the symbol the new regime needs to destroy. Egypt failed both tests, and it failed the second one partly through her own choices.

### Could a different policy have saved the kingdom?

Probably not the kingdom, but possibly the dynasty. Egypt was too rich to leave outside direct control once one man held Rome. A ruler who had hedged after 33 BCE and avoided becoming the enemy's chosen symbol might have kept a throne for her children under Roman supervision, as Herod did in Judaea.

What changed because of all this is larger than Egypt. A three-thousand-year sequence of independent Egyptian kingship ended, the Roman Republic ended, the treasure of the Ptolemies financed the settlement that made an emperor, and the grain of the Nile began feeding a city on the Tiber. A last observation belongs here because it is the one most often lost. She did not lose to Antony's weakness or to her own. She lost to Agrippa, a naval commander of the first rank who spent the years between 38 and 36 BCE building and training a fleet in Italy while Antony was losing an army in the mountains of Media. The decisive asset in the final contest was seapower competently developed over half a decade, and the side that developed it won. Every dramatic element of the traditional story, the barge, the banquets, the asp, sits on top of that unglamorous fact, and any account that cannot accommodate it is telling a story rather than explaining an outcome.

The two most retold love stories of antiquity were, at the level that decided outcomes, negotiations over timber, silver, and ships. That is a less charming account than the one everyone knows, and it is the one the evidence supports.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was Cleopatra's relationship with Julius Caesar?

It was primarily a political alliance and secondarily a personal one. Caesar arrived in Egypt in 48 BCE pursuing Pompey, asserted the right to arbitrate the Ptolemaic succession under her father's will, and demanded repayment of Egyptian debts. She reached him by having herself smuggled into the palace her enemies controlled, and he ruled in favor of restoring the joint rule that reinstated her. The Alexandrian War followed, ending with her brother dead and her on the throne. What she gained was concrete: the elimination of her rival, the return of Cyprus to Egyptian control reversing a Roman annexation, treaty status as a friend and ally of the Roman people, and two more decades of formal independence. What Caesar gained was money, grain, a secure base for a planned Parthian campaign, and a client ruler who owed him everything.

### Q: What was Cleopatra's relationship with Mark Antony?

A decade-long alliance, renegotiated at least twice, that became genuinely personal in its later stages. It began in 41 BCE with a summons to Tarsus, where Antony required money for a Parthian campaign and an accounting for her conduct during the preceding civil war. The partnership delivered substantially to both sides. Antony received Egyptian funding, ships, and grain, plus a secure southeastern flank. Egypt received, in the Antioch settlement of 37 BCE, parts of Cyprus and Cilicia, coastal Phoenicia, Cyrenaica, and the balsam revenues around Jericho, restoring much of the timber, naval, and territorial base the Ptolemies had lost. Three children were born of it. Its later phase involved choices on both sides that damaged their political interests, which is the strongest evidence that it had ceased to be purely transactional.

### Q: Did Cleopatra love Caesar or Antony?

Her own view is unrecoverable, since nothing survives in her voice, but the behavioral evidence points clearly toward Antony. With Caesar the arrangement looks transactional on both sides: she gained a throne and a territory, he gained money and a client, and after his death she spent years carefully avoiding commitment to any Roman faction. With Antony both parties made decisions that cannot be explained by interest. He divorced Octavia in 32 BCE, which was politically ruinous and served no strategic purpose. He acknowledged three children publicly at real cost. She committed the Egyptian fleet and treasury to his war rather than hedging as she had before, and she did not open a channel to Octavian in 33 or 32 BCE when a calculating ruler would have. The failure to hedge is the evidence.

### Q: How did Cleopatra meet Julius Caesar?

By infiltrating a palace held by her enemies. In 48 BCE she had been driven from Alexandria by a court faction around her brother and was with her own army on the eastern frontier when Caesar arrived and began arbitrating the succession dispute in her absence. Rather than let the settlement be reached without her, she had herself smuggled into the palace concealed inside a bedding roll, carried by a retainer named Apollodorus, and delivered to Caesar's quarters. The rolled carpet familiar from paintings and films is a later embellishment; the ancient word describes bedding or a sleeping sack. What is significant is not the fabric but the operation, which was a covert entry into a guarded building held by people who wanted her dead, undertaken at about twenty-one.

### Q: Did Cleopatra and Caesar have a son?

She had a son born in 47 BCE, named Ptolemy Caesar and known as Caesarion, whom she presented as Caesar's child. Whether Caesar was the father cannot be determined. The timing fits the period he spent in Egypt, Mark Antony later told the Roman Senate that Caesar had acknowledged the boy, and the Egyptian government treated the claim as established, placing him on temple reliefs with full pharaonic titles and elevating him as co-ruler in 44 BCE. Against that, Caesar never named him in his will, which was the decisive Roman instrument for acknowledging a child, and instead adopted his great-nephew Octavian. One of Caesar's own associates published a pamphlet denying the paternity. The claim's political function is far clearer than its truth.

### Q: Why did Antony and Cleopatra ally?

Because each held exactly what the other needed. Antony had taken the Roman east after Philippi in 42 BCE and was planning a campaign against Parthia that required an army, a fleet, a supply base, and funding on a scale only Egypt could underwrite. He also needed his southeastern flank secure while he campaigned in Armenia and Media. She needed the coastal territories the Ptolemies had lost across two centuries, particularly the Cilician and Phoenician timber country that a war fleet requires and Egypt cannot produce, along with Cyprus and Cyrenaica. She also needed a Roman patron willing to affirm publicly that her son was Julius Caesar's, a claim that directly threatened Octavian and that no neutral party would support. Both sides delivered, which is what distinguishes an alliance from an infatuation.

### Q: How did Caesar's death affect Cleopatra?

It destroyed her position overnight. Her patron was dead, his will passed over her son entirely in favor of Octavian, and the Roman world fragmented into competing factions that each demanded Egyptian money. She left Rome, returned to Egypt, and within months her nominal co-ruler Ptolemy XIV was dead, probably poisoned, which allowed her to elevate Caesarion as co-ruler and make the paternity claim the formal basis of Egyptian government. The following years were the most precarious of her reign. Egypt suffered failed Nile floods, famine, and plague while both Roman sides demanded support, and she conducted a careful hedging operation, avoiding firm commitment until Philippi in 42 BCE settled the question and left Antony in control of the east.

### Q: What happened to Antony and Cleopatra?

They lost a war and died within days of each other in 30 BCE. After the defeat at Actium in September 31 BCE they spent eleven months attempting to escape or negotiate. She tried to move ships overland to the Red Sea to flee eastward with the treasury, an effort reportedly wrecked by Nabataean raiders, and sent Caesarion south with money and an escort. Both approached Octavian; he ignored Antony and strung her along, wanting the treasure intact and a captive for his triumph. When his forces reached Alexandria from Syria and Cyrene, Antony's remaining troops and ships defected. The city fell on the first of August 30 BCE, Antony died by his own hand after a false report of her death, and she died on or about the tenth or twelfth of August.

### Q: What was the Alexandrian War?

The siege fought in Alexandria through the winter of 48 and 47 BCE between Caesar, holding the palace quarter with a small force of about four thousand men, and the Egyptian army under the court faction that had expelled Cleopatra. Caesar had arrived pursuing Pompey and found himself trapped in a hostile city with far too few troops. He burned ships in the harbor to prevent his sea communications being cut, and the fire spread into the dockside district, which is the origin of the story that he destroyed the Library. Her sister Arsinoe IV escaped and was proclaimed queen by the Egyptian forces. Relief arrived overland from Syria in early 47 BCE under Mithridates of Pergamum, the Egyptian army was defeated near the Nile, and Ptolemy XIII drowned attempting to escape.

### Q: Why did Caesar support Cleopatra against her brother?

Partly law, partly calculation. Ptolemy XII's will had specified joint rule with Rome as guarantor, so restoring the joint arrangement was the legally defensible ruling and gave Caesar the standing of arbitrator rather than conqueror. The calculation mattered more. The court faction controlling her brother had murdered Pompey expecting gratitude, which showed they would kill a Roman for advantage, and they resisted Caesar's demands for debt repayment. She, by contrast, would owe her restoration entirely to him. A client who depends on a patron's continued power is a more reliable client than a faction that governs through a child and calculates its own interests. Annexation was the alternative, and Caesar avoided it because a rich province needing a governor with legions was how Roman rivals were created.

### Q: Did Cleopatra visit Rome?

Yes, she spent a substantial part of the period between 46 and 44 BCE at Rome, staying in Caesar's villa across the Tiber, accompanied by her nominal co-ruler brother and her infant son. The visit was diplomatic rather than scandalous in intent; client rulers routinely spent time at Rome cultivating patrons and securing legal status. She obtained recognition as a friend and ally of the Roman people, the formula defining a protected client kingdom. Caesar also placed a golden statue of her in the temple of Venus Genetrix, the goddess from whom his family claimed descent, which was a striking public gesture linking her to his line. The Roman elite reaction was hostile, and Cicero's private letters record his personal dislike of dealing with her. She left after the Ides of March 44 BCE.

### Q: What were the Donations of Alexandria?

A ceremony staged in Alexandria in 34 BCE, after Antony's Armenian campaign, at which eastern territories were distributed among Cleopatra and her children. Seated on golden thrones with the children before them, Antony named her Queen of Kings ruling jointly with Caesarion, who was proclaimed King of Kings and publicly affirmed as the true son of Julius Caesar. Alexander Helios received Armenia, Media, and the unconquered Parthian lands; Cleopatra Selene received Cyrenaica and Crete; Ptolemy Philadelphus, aged two, received Syria and Cilicia. As eastern policy the settlement was defensible, since Rome had always governed the region through client kings and several grants were notional. At Rome it read as giving away Roman territory to a foreign queen and as a direct attack on Octavian's claim to Caesar's inheritance.

### Q: Why did Antony marry Octavia?

To seal a political settlement. In 40 BCE, with a Parthian invasion in Syria and a crisis in Italy, Antony and Octavian negotiated the Treaty of Brundisium dividing the Roman world between them, and marriage was the standard Roman instrument for binding rival leaders. Octavia was Octavian's sister and, by the account of even hostile sources, a woman of real ability and integrity. Two daughters were born of the marriage, and Antony lived with her in Athens. The significant point for this story is what the marriage demonstrates: he suspended the Egyptian alliance entirely for over three years, from 40 to 37 BCE, without seeing Cleopatra at all. That gap is difficult to reconcile with the tradition of a man overwhelmed by passion and is usually omitted from popular retellings.

### Q: What caused the war between Antony and Octavian?

An accumulation of grievances that Octavian converted into a patriotic cause. The Donations of Alexandria in 34 BCE appeared to hand Roman territory to a foreign dynasty and elevated a rival claimant to Caesar's blood. Antony's divorce of Octavia in 32 BCE was a gross personal insult to Octavian. Octavian then obtained Antony's will from the Vestal Virgins and read portions publicly, a serious violation of convention, reporting that it recognized Caesarion, left legacies to the Egyptian children, and requested burial in Alexandria. That last clause converted an abstract charge of eastern corruption into something ordinary Romans could feel. War was then declared not on Antony but on the queen of Egypt, which turned another round of civil war into a foreign one that Italy would support.

### Q: What happened at the Battle of Actium?

The campaign was decided before the battle. Through 31 BCE Agrippa, commanding Octavian's fleet, cut the sea routes supplying Antony's fleet and army on the western coast of Greece. The camp on marshy ground near the Ambracian Gulf was ravaged by malaria and dysentery, rowers died, supplies ran short, and senior officers began deserting. On the second of September 31 BCE Antony brought out an undermanned fleet, having already burned ships he could not crew. The fighting was inconclusive along much of the line until the Egyptian squadron of some sixty ships, held in reserve and carrying the treasury, broke through the center and sailed for Egypt, with Antony following in a lighter vessel. The remaining fleet surrendered within days and the army capitulated about a week later.

### Q: Did Cleopatra betray Antony at Actium?

The evidence favors a planned breakout rather than a betrayal or a panic. Two details are difficult to explain otherwise. Her squadron carried the treasury, which no fleet expecting to fight a decisive battle would bring into the line, and the ships had sails aboard, which warships did not normally carry into combat since they fought under oar. The strategic position had already collapsed through blockade, disease, and desertion, so preserving the fleet's core, the war chest, and the commander for continued resistance from Egypt was the rational choice available. The Roman version, in which she fled in terror and an infatuated Antony abandoned an empire to follow, converted a logistical defeat into a moral failure and preserved his reputation as a soldier at her expense, which is why it should be distrusted.

### Q: Why was Caesarion politically dangerous to Octavian?

Because he embodied a rival claim to the same inheritance. Octavian's entire position rested on being Julius Caesar's heir by adoption, which was a strong claim in Roman law but carried less emotional force with Caesar's veterans and the urban population than a claim of blood would. A living boy presented as Caesar's biological son, publicly affirmed as such by Antony at the Donations of Alexandria in 34 BCE, was therefore a permanent threat to the legitimacy on which Octavian's power was built. That is why the paternity was denied by Octavian's circle, why it became a central issue in the propaganda war, and why Caesarion was killed in 30 BCE at about seventeen while his three younger half siblings were spared and raised in Rome.

### Q: Did Antony and Cleopatra get married?

The answer depends on which legal system is applied, which is precisely why the question was exploited as a scandal. Under Roman law no valid marriage existed, because a Roman citizen could not contract legal marriage with a foreigner who lacked the right of intermarriage, and Antony remained legally married to Octavia until he divorced her in 32 BCE. Under Egyptian and Hellenistic royal practice, a union may well have been solemnized and treated as real, and coins and inscriptions present the two as a royal couple in the conventional manner of Hellenistic dynastic pairs. Both statements are true within their own frameworks. Octavian's propaganda made effective use of the Roman reading, presenting the relationship as a Roman general abandoning a Roman wife for a foreign queen.
