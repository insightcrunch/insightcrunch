## Vows, Oaths, and Promises in the Play: A Verdict on the Force of Sworn Words

Romeo and Juliet turns on sworn words. The lovers are not united by a shared glance, a shared sonnet, or even a shared grave; they are united by a vow, spoken in an orchard, examined, refused, corrected, and then accepted on terms Juliet herself sets. The tragedy that follows is not the consequence of a single oath broken. It is the consequence of a city in which every kind of sworn word is in circulation at once: feasts open with oaths of revenge, fathers tender their daughters with the language of contract, servants cheapen every formula they touch, a friar lends the sacrament to a secret marriage, and two fathers close the play by vowing statues in pure gold to children they could not keep alive. The verdict of the play is precise and unsparing. A vow binds only where it is examined before it is spoken, and the lovers survive longest in the very hours when their vow is under Juliet's interrogation. Where a sworn word escapes examination, in Tybalt's mouth, in Capulet's house, in the Friar's cell, in Paris at the tomb, it turns into a trap, a weapon, or a monument. Shakespeare does not celebrate swearing. He tests it, and the test is a ledger: which vows are kept, which are broken, and at what price each outcome arrives.

![Vows and oaths in Romeo and Juliet - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The ledger begins with a distinction the play itself insists upon. A vow in the Verona of the text is not the same thing as an oath, and neither is the same thing as a promise. An oath calls a witness, something sacred or powerful, and puts the speaker's credit on the line before that witness. A vow binds the speaker's future conduct, usually before heaven, and carries a sanction the speaker accepts in advance. A promise binds one person to another and relies on the relationship, not on the witness. The play uses all three, and its catastrophes follow from the confusion between them. Romeo offers an oath when a vow is needed. Tybalt offers an oath when a promise of restraint would save him. Capulet offers a promise and then discovers, too late, that a father who tenders a daughter's love has made something closer to a vow than he intended. The Friar offers a sacrament and treats it, for one crucial afternoon, as a piece of diplomacy. Each miscalculation is examined in the sections below, against the primary text in a named edition, the Arden Shakespeare third series edition edited by Rene Weis, and against the critics who have taken the sworn word of this play most seriously. The verdict is stated at the outset and defended throughout: the lovers' vows are admirable precisely because Juliet doubts them, and dangerous precisely because everyone around the lovers swears without doubt.

## How Does the Language of Swearing Actually Work in Verona?

The play opens not with a vow but with a street full of men who swear the way other men breathe. Sampson announces that he will take the wall of any man or maid of Montague's, Gregory answers with swagger of his own, and the two servants build a quarrel out of gesture, bravado, and a thumb bitten in derision. No formal oath is spoken in the first scene in the sense of a formula with a named witness, and that absence is part of the point. The servants' speech is full of undertakings that cost nothing: I will do this, I will do that, I will show myself a tyrant. The words are cheap because nothing is staked on them except vanity. When Benvolio and Tybalt arrive, the language changes register at once. Tybalt's sword is drawn with a phrase that functions as a challenge to the whole world, and the Prince's entrance brings the first sworn instrument of the play into view, an edict rather than an oath but a speech act of the same family: on pain of death, the citizens shall not break the peace again. The Prince does not swear; he pronounces, and his pronouncement carries the forfeit. Already, in the first scene, Shakespeare has laid out the full spectrum that the rest of the play will sort through. At one end, the servants' costless bravado. At the other, the Prince's proclamation, a word that binds because power stands behind it. Between those poles lie the lovers' vows, which bind because the speakers mean them, and Tybalt's oath, which binds because hatred stands behind it.

The distinction matters because the play's world treats sworn words as currency, and currency has an exchange rate. When everyone swears casually, the value of any single oath falls, and the lovers' vow must then do extra work to prove itself real. This is the economic metaphor that runs through the play's language of swearing, and it is not imposed on the text from outside. The play itself reaches for it again and again. Juliet speaks of the exchange of vows, of love's faithful vow traded for her own, of a contract too rash and too sudden. Capulet speaks of a tender, the word of the marketplace and the law, for his child's love. Montague closes the play by pricing devotion in gold. The language of bargain is not decorative; it is the play's way of asking what a sworn word is worth when the speaker's circumstances change. A vow made in the orchard at night must survive the morning, the marriage, the banishment, the tomb. A promise made at a feast must survive a daughter's refusal. An oath sworn at a dance must survive a death. The play's ledger measures each of these against the same question: did the speaker pay, or did someone else pay for the speaker's words?

To answer that question fairly, the play's swearing must be understood in its own period terms, not in the language of greeting cards. In the England of the play's first audiences, an oath was a serious legal and religious instrument. Swearing by the moon, by heaven, by a saint, or by the rood invoked a witness who could not be subpoenaed but could, in the theology of the day, punish. A vow of chastity, like the one Rosaline is reported to have made, was a recognized form of religious self-binding, and breaking it was a spiritual matter, not merely a social embarrassment. A betrothal promise, the exchange of consent before witnesses, could constitute a binding marriage in canon law even without a church ceremony, which is why the question of whether the lovers are married in law matters to the Friar's defenders and accusers alike. A father could not, in the law of the day, simply command a daughter's marriage, but his consent was a powerful social and legal instrument, and a daughter's refusal carried real consequences. The play knows all of this, and it uses the knowledge to make its sworn words carry weight. When Capulet says his will to her consent is but a part, he is stating a legal and moral truth of his world, and when he later reverses himself and tenders Juliet's love to Paris without asking her, the reversal is not a plot convenience. It is a breach, a father breaking his own stated principle, and the play treats it as the breaking of a promise, with Juliet paying the price.

Against that background, the minor oaths of the play take on their proper significance. The Nurse swears by her maidenhead at twelve year old when she recalls Juliet's infancy, a homely, comic oath that tells the audience two things at once: the Nurse is a woman who has lived her life among oaths and gossip, and her swearing is the swearing of the household, the intimate register where small promises are kept or not as the day allows. Capulet, in the fury of Act 3 Scene 5, swears by God's bread, an oath that invokes the Eucharist in a domestic tantrum, and the blasphemy is the point. A father who swears by the host to enforce his daughter's obedience has reached the bottom of his authority, and the oath measures his desperation rather than his power. Mercutio's curse, a plague on both your houses, is not an oath at all in form, and that is why it belongs in the ledger as a caution. A curse calls no witness and promises nothing; it wishes. Yet the plot obeys it as though it were the best-kept vow in the play, because the plague that quarantines Friar John is the very mechanism that kills the lovers. The play's irony is exact here. The one sworn utterance that the plot honours without exception is the one that no one intended as a vow, spoken by a man who dies laughing at his own wound. The rest of the play's vows, spoken with full ceremony, are honoured only in part, or honoured by the wrong person, or honoured too late.

In the orchard, Romeo and Juliet do not simply swear. They negotiate the conditions under which swearing can be trusted, and the negotiation is the vow. That is the claim this article will defend, and the next section tests it against the scene itself, line by line, in the Arden text. Before turning there, one further distinction should be fixed. The play distinguishes, without ever stating the rule, between vows that bind the speaker and vows that bind someone else. The lovers' vows bind the speakers. Tybalt's oath binds Tybalt. Capulet's promise binds Juliet, and that asymmetry is the play's deepest moral observation about swearing. A vow that binds the speaker is a form of freedom; it is the speaker choosing a future. A vow that binds another person is a form of power, and in this play it is the form of power that fathers, princes, and priests exercise most dangerously. The Friar's plan binds Juliet to a potion and a tomb. The Prince's edict binds Romeo to banishment. Capulet's promise binds Juliet to Paris. Against these, the orchard vows stand out as the one moment in the play when two people bind themselves, and only themselves, by their own words, and every other sworn word in the text is measured in this article against the standard the orchard sets.

One sworn word stands outside the city altogether, and it is the first one the audience hears. The Prologue, speaking for the play itself, ends with a promise to the audience: what the summary has missed, the performance will strive to mend, if the audience will attend with patient ears. The promise is a small contract, and the play keeps it, which is more than most of its characters manage. The frame matters to the ledger because it sets the standard of the telling. The play promises its audience a complete account and delivers one, bodies, letters, confessions and all, so that the audience closes the play in possession of the whole record, the one position no character in Verona ever occupies. The characters swear in fragments, each holding a part of the truth. The play swears whole, and its wholeness is the condition of the audience's judgment. Every verdict this article has reached depends on that kept promise: the ledger can be compiled only because the teller kept faith.

## What Happens When Romeo Swears by the Moon in the Orchard?

The balcony scene, Act 2 Scene 2 in the Arden numbering, is the play's great laboratory for the sworn word, and it earns that description because it stages a failure of swearing before it stages a success. Romeo, discovering Juliet above him, begins in the language of worship and then moves, as the scene's famous exchange unfolds, to the language of oath. He has already declared that he will call her love and be new baptized, that henceforth he will never be Romeo again, and Juliet, who has been listening to his soliloquy and now answers it, takes the measure of what he has offered. The exchange that follows deserves quotation in full, because every later vow in the play is a variation on it. Romeo says, in the Arden text, "Lady, by yonder blessed moon I vow, / That tips with silver all these fruit-tree tops." Juliet answers, "O, swear not by the moon, th' inconstant moon, / That monthly changes in her circled orb, / Lest that thy love prove likewise variable." Romeo, corrected, asks what he should swear by, and Juliet answers with the sentence that defines the play's whole theory of vows: "Do not swear at all; / Or, if thou wilt, swear by thy gracious self, / Which is the god of my idolatry, / And I'll believe thee."

Four features of that exchange deserve attention, and each of them governs a later section of this article. First, Juliet refuses the witness before she accepts the vow. The moon is inconstant, and an oath sworn by an inconstant witness makes the love it guarantees variable by association. The objection is not romantic delicacy; it is logic. A vow is only as steady as the thing it is sworn by, and Juliet will not let Romeo mortgage his love to a body that changes monthly. Second, Juliet replaces the witness with the speaker himself. Swear by thy gracious self, she says, which is the god of her idolatry. The move is startling, and it has been read as blasphemy, as flattery, and as theology. Read in the context of the play's ledger, it is something more precise: it is the conversion of an oath into a vow. An oath needs an outside witness. A vow needs only the speaker's own future conduct. By telling Romeo to swear by himself, Juliet is telling him to stake himself, not the moon, and a man who stakes himself cannot be released by the moon's change. Third, Juliet makes her belief conditional on the form of the vow, not on its fervour. She will believe him, she says, if he swears by himself, and the conditional is the whole point. Belief in this play is not a feeling; it is a judgment about form, and Juliet is the play's most exacting judge of form. Fourth, the exchange is conducted as a dialogue of questions and answers, not as a pair of declarations. Romeo asks what he shall swear by. Juliet answers. The vow is constructed between them, in the interrogative, and that construction is what makes it trustworthy. A vow declared alone, like Tybalt's, can be a weapon. A vow constructed in dialogue, like this one, is a contract, and contracts can be enforced.

The next step is Juliet's famous doubt, the speech that this article treats as the scene's moral centre. Juliet says, in the Arden text, "It is too rash, too unadvised, too sudden; / Too like the lightning, which doth cease to be / Ere one can say, 'It lightens.'" The speech is usually quoted as evidence that Juliet is wise beyond her years, and she is, but the wisdom is specific. She is not doubting love; she is doubting the instrument. A vow made too quickly is like lightning because it cannot be examined before it vanishes, and a vow that cannot be examined cannot be trusted. The image that follows is the play's most famous defence of slow vowing: "This bud of love, by summer's ripening breath, / May prove a beauteous flower when next we meet." The bud is not a denial of the vow; it is a timetable for it. Let the vow grow, Juliet says, and it will be a flower. Spoken at once, it is only lightning. The distinction between lightning and flower is the distinction the whole play draws between rash swearing and examined vowing, and it is worth noting that Juliet, not Romeo, draws it. The play gives the theory of vows to its heroine, and the heroine's theory is the one the plot vindicates.

What follows the doubt is the exchange that the ledger records as the play's founding vow, and it is phrased, tellingly, as a bargain. Juliet proposes, "The exchange of thy love's faithful vow for mine," and the sentence does double work. It is a love speech, and it is a contract term. The word exchange belongs to the market and the law, and Juliet uses it deliberately. She is not asking Romeo to feel more; she is asking him to trade, vow for vow, so that each speaker holds the other's word as collateral. The symmetry matters. In every other vow in the play, the collateral is held by someone else: a father holds a daughter's future, a priest holds a sacrament, a prince holds a life. Here, and only here, each speaker holds the other's vow, and the holding is mutual. That mutuality is why the orchard vow survives, in the play's own terms, when so much else fails. It is not that the lovers never break faith; it is that they never break the form of the exchange. Romeo's banishment, Juliet's apparent death, the Friar's failed plan, none of these breaks the vow, because the vow was never conditioned on circumstances. It was conditioned on the speakers, and the speakers hold to the end.

The scene's final movement confirms the reading. Juliet sets a practical test, a message at nine the next morning, a messenger, a time and place for the wedding, and she refuses to accept a promise in place of an arrangement. The test is often read as mere plot machinery, the device that gets the lovers to the Friar's cell, but in the ledger's terms it is the scene's most important moment. A vow that cannot produce an appointment is a feeling. A vow that produces an appointment is a contract, and Juliet, having negotiated the contract, now insists on its performance. The appointment is kept, the wedding follows, and the play's tragedy is therefore not the failure of the orchard vow. It is the failure of everything built on top of it by people who swore less carefully. The next section lays out that failure in tabular form, so that the reader can see, at a glance, the whole economy of sworn words that the play sets in motion from the orchard outward.

### What Does Swearing by the Moon Get Wrong About Love?

Swearing by the moon gets two things wrong, and both errors are logical rather than sentimental. The first error is the choice of witness. The moon changes, and a love guaranteed by a changing witness inherits the change. Juliet's objection is not that the moon is unromantic; it is that the moon is unreliable.

An oath is a reliability instrument. The second error is the direction of the guarantee. An oath sworn by an outside witness lets the speaker off the hook: if the witness changes, the speaker can claim the guarantee changed with it. Juliet's correction removes that escape. Swear by yourself, and there is no one else to blame when the love changes. The moon passage therefore teaches the play's first lesson about vows, which is that the witness must be as constant as the love it guarantees, and the only constant witness in the play is the speaker's own self.

### Why Does the Play's Truest Vow Arrive Without Ceremony?

The orchard vow arrives without priest, without parent, without witness, and without any of the apparatus that the play elsewhere associates with binding speech, and that absence is precisely what makes it binding. Ceremony in this play is a technology of power, and the orchard has none of it.

The Friar's ceremony binds the lovers to a plan, Capulet's ceremony binds Juliet to Paris, the Prince's ceremony binds Romeo to exile. The orchard has no power except the speakers' own. The vow is true because nothing compels it except the two people who make it, and the play's later ceremonies, for all their robes and Latin, never produce a vow as trustworthy as the one made in the dark, in questions and answers, between two people who have examined the instrument before using it.

The table also makes visible the play's most painful asymmetry. The speakers who swear the most carefully, the lovers, pay the most. The speakers who swear the most carelessly, the fathers and the Friar, pay the least in their own persons, and the difference between those two payments is the tragedy.

Two rows deserve emphasis before the argument moves on, because they are the rows most often misread. The first is Rosaline's vow. It is the only vow in the play that is never spoken onstage, and it is the vow that sets the whole action in motion, because Romeo's reported despair over it drives him to the feast where he meets Juliet. A vow that no one hears, reported by a lover who has an interest in exaggerating it, governs the first act more thoroughly than any oath spoken in full view. A vow's power, the row teaches, does not depend on its performance but on its reputation, and Rosaline's reputation as a woman who has forsworn love makes her the play's first unmoved mover. The second row is Benvolio's undertaking, the quietest entry in the ledger and the most instructive. Benvolio promises Montague that he will learn the cause of Romeo's sorrow, and he does, reporting it faithfully in Act 1 Scene 1. No one remembers the promise, because nothing goes wrong with it, and that is exactly the point. A kept promise that costs nothing and harms no one is invisible in a tragedy, because tragedy is the record of promises that cost everything. Benvolio's row is the ledger's control case: proof that the play knows what a sound promise looks like, and chooses, everywhere else, to show what happens when promises are unsound.

## By What Does Tybalt Swear, and Why Is His Oath the Feud's Truest Word?

Against the orchard vow stands Tybalt's oath at the feast, and the contrast between the two is the play's clearest demonstration that a vow's danger lies not in swearing but in swearing without examination. Tybalt, recognizing Romeo's voice beneath the mask in Act 1 Scene 5, rages against the intrusion, and Capulet restrains him for the evening. Tybalt's parting words are the feud's finest oath, in the Arden text: "Now by the stock and honour of my kin, / To strike him dead I hold it not a sin." Every element of the orchard exchange is present here in inverted form. Juliet refused a witness that changes; Tybalt chooses a witness that cannot change, because the stock and honour of his kin are fixed by birth and cannot be renegotiated. Juliet converted an oath into a vow by making the speaker the collateral; Tybalt converts a vow into an oath by making his ancestry the collateral, so that his hatred is guaranteed by something larger than himself. Juliet constructed her vow in dialogue, in questions and answers; Tybalt speaks alone, to himself, in a couplet that closes like a door. And where Juliet's vow binds only the speaker, Tybalt's oath binds the speaker to an act against another person. It is the play's purest example of a sworn word that binds its speaker to someone else's death.

The oath's language repays the closest reading. Tybalt does not say that he will strike Romeo dead; he says that he holds it not a sin to do so. The oath's work is therefore not motivational but legal: it pre-acquits the speaker. Tybalt is, in effect, granting himself a pardon in advance, by the authority of his kin's stock and honour, and the pardon is the oath's true content. The feud's sworn words all work this way. They are not promises of future conduct so much as licences for present feeling, dressed in the grammar of obligation. When Tybalt later encounters Romeo in the street in Act 3 Scene 1, the oath is executed almost as a matter of paperwork, and Tybalt proceeds to the serving as a man proceeds to a debt appointment. Romeo's refusal to fight, grounded in a marriage vow Tybalt cannot know, interrupts the appointment, Mercutio steps into the gap, and the oath's price is paid by the wrong man first and by the right man second. The intrusion that seemed sweet at the feast has converted, as Tybalt vowed it would, to bitter gall. The ledger's verdict on Tybalt's oath is that it is kept, and that its keeping is the catastrophe. A vow that is kept and kills is worse, in the play's arithmetic, than a vow that is broken and saves, and the arithmetic is what distinguishes Shakespeare's treatment from a simpler moral scheme in which keeping one's word is always the good.

### What Makes the Feud's Sworn Words Different in Kind from the Lovers'?

The feud's sworn words differ from the lovers' in three respects, and each difference is a matter of kind, not degree. First, they are sworn by things, by stock and honour, by God's bread, by the name of a house, where the lovers swear by the speaker's own self.

Second, they are spoken to be heard by others, or to license acts against others, where the lovers' vow is spoken to be heard by one person and binds only the two who speak. Third, and most decisively, they are never examined. No character in the feud's world asks whether an oath should be sworn, or by what, or on what terms, until the question is asked too late, over the bodies. The lovers' world exists inside the feud's world, but its sworn words follow a different grammar, and the play's tragedy is the collision of the two grammars in a single street, a single tomb, and a single morning.

The feud's swearing extends upward to the Prince, whose edict in Act 1 Scene 1 is the law's version of a vow. The Prince does not swear by anything; he pronounces a forfeit, and the forfeit is a sworn instrument of the state. When the forfeit falls due in Act 3 Scene 1, the Prince commutes it, banishing Romeo instead of executing him, and the commutation, merciful as it is, teaches the city a lesson the city cannot afford: the law's sworn words are negotiable. Against that negotiable edict stands Tybalt's non-negotiable oath, and the contrast explains the feud's persistence. The private oath binds without exception; the public pronouncement bends. A city in which private oaths hold without exception and public law is flexible will be governed by its hatreds, and Verona is. The play's final irony is that the Prince's closing words impose no new oath and exact no new forfeit. They sum up the losses and let the fathers' gold do the swearing.

One more feud oath deserves a place here, because it is the oath the lovers' story is most often measured against and because its speaker is dead before the play's midpoint. Mercutio swears by small things, by his heel, with the casual profanity of a man for whom swearing is a style rather than an instrument. His curses and oaths are indistinguishable in his mouth, and the play exploits the indistinction. When he is wounded, the curse he pronounces is spoken in the same register as his jokes, and the audience, like the characters, is slow to hear that this time the words will be honoured. The play's point is that the man who swears most lightly utters the most binding words, because the plot, indifferent to intention, takes the curse at face value. Intention, the ledger suggests, is not what makes a sworn word dangerous. Reception is. Tybalt intends his oath and it kills. Mercutio does not intend his curse and it kills. The lovers intend their vow and it saves nothing except the vow itself. Three cases, one conclusion: in Verona, words bind according to the system that receives them, and the system is rigged toward violence.

## Forswearing: The Terror That Stands Behind Every Vow in the Play

Every vow implies its opposite, and the play's word for the opposite is forswearing. The term enters the text early and quietly, inside Romeo's report of Rosaline in Act 1 Scene 1: "She hath forsworn to love, and in that vow / Do I live dead that live to tell it now." Rosaline's act is described with the vocabulary of renunciation, because in the play's world a vow of chastity is a forswearing as much as a swearing: to vow the cloister is to renounce the world, and the word carries both motions at once. The ambiguity is the play in miniature. Every sworn word in the text forswears something else. Romeo's orchard vow forswears his name and his house. Juliet's acceptance forswears her obedience. The marriage forswears the feud's claim on both speakers. Tybalt's oath forswears mercy. Capulet's tender forswears his own Act 1 condition. The Friar's plan forswears the truth, temporarily and catastrophically. To swear in this play is always to renounce, and the tragedy can be retold as the sum of its renunciations, each one exacted by a vow that seemed, at the moment of speaking, to cost nothing but words.

The fear of forswearing, the terror that a sworn word will be reversed, is the play's most persistent anxiety, and it belongs, characteristically, to Juliet. Her orchard corrections are usually read as tests of Romeo's sincerity, but their deeper function is prophylactic: she is designing a vow that cannot easily be forsworn. The moon is rejected because a changing witness invites a changing love. The self is installed as collateral because a man who stakes himself has nowhere to retreat to. The appointment is set because a vow with a deadline can be checked. Each device is an anti-forswearing device, and the need for them tells the audience what kind of city this is: a city where words are reversed so routinely that a girl of not quite fourteen builds her first vow like a fortification. Her later anguish in Act 3 Scene 2, when the news of Tybalt's death arrives entangled with Romeo's banishment, is the terror surfacing. "O serpent heart, hid with a flowering face," she cries in the Arden text, imagining for one terrible passage that the orchard words were a beautiful binding on a vile book, that the vow she fortified was forsworn from the start. The passage resolves, she chooses the vow over the news, but the resolution is a decision, not a certainty. Juliet believes Romeo because she decides to, having weighed the alternative, and the weighing is the price of a vow in a world where forswearing is the norm.

The period weight of forswearing should not be underestimated, because the play's first audiences brought it into the theatre with them. Perjury was a sin before it was a crime, a matter for the conscience and the confessional, and the oath culture of the period rested on the belief that a false oath endangered the soul of the swearer. A vow broken was therefore not a social embarrassment but a spiritual emergency, and the characters' behaviour makes sense only against that background. The Friar's horror at the prospect of a bigamous marriage for Juliet, his insistence that the remedy must avoid the sin even as it courts the appearance of death, is the behaviour of a man who will risk a tomb before he will risk a forsworn sacrament. Juliet's own readiness to die rather than marry Paris is the same calculus in a younger soul: the marriage vow, once sworn, cannot be forsworn, and death is preferable to perjury. Modern productions sometimes play her refusal as romantic desperation, and it is, but it is also doctrine. She has sworn, and the sworn word outranks her life because in her world it outranks her soul's safety too, and no other character in the play states the priority so clearly by acting on it so completely.

### Why Is Forswearing the Play's Quietest Terror?

Forswearing is the play's quietest terror because it is the one catastrophe that needs no dagger, no poison, and no feud: only a speaker who changes. The play's loud terrors arrive with swords drawn; its quiet terror arrives in a sentence, in a father reversing a condition, a nurse reversing her counsel, a friar reversing his timetable.

Juliet's whole orchard method, the refused moon, the self as collateral, the appointment at nine, is engineered against this terror, and her engineering is the play's acknowledgement that vows fail not when the world opposes them but when their speakers are permitted to drift. The terror is quiet because forswearing wears the dress of ordinary life: a change of mind, a practical adjustment, a grief, a pressure. The play insists that the audience learn to hear it anyway, in every gentle reversal, as the sound of a vow coming apart.

## Capulet's Tender: The Promise That Binds the Wrong Person

If Tybalt's oath is the play's most dangerous sworn word, Capulet's promise to Paris is its most consequential, because it is the vow that binds someone other than its speaker, and it does so twice, in two different forms, with two different outcomes. The first form, in Act 1 Scene 2, is a condition, and it is worth quoting in the Arden text for its precision: "My child is yet a stranger in the world; / Let two more summers wither in their pride / Ere we may think her ripe to be a bride." Paris presses, and Capulet adds the condition that the whole later play violates: "But woo her, gentle Paris, get her heart; / My will to her consent is but a part." The sentence is a father's vow in all but name. It promises Paris a fair chance, it promises Juliet a voice in her own marriage, and it promises the audience a world in which a daughter's consent is a part, however small, of the machinery. The promise is kept for exactly as long as circumstances allow, which is to say until the moment the play needs it broken, and its breaking is the tragedy's hinge.

The second form, in Act 3 Scene 4, is a tender. Capulet, grieving for Tybalt and anxious for the household's recovery, says to Paris, in the Arden text, "I will make a desperate tender / Of my child's love: I think she will be ruled / In all respects by me; nay more, I doubt it not." The word tender belongs to the law and the market: it is an offer of payment, a formal proposal, and Capulet's use of it for his daughter's love is the play's bluntest exposure of the household economy in which Juliet is an asset. The tender reverses the Act 1 condition in every particular. The two summers vanish. The consent clause vanishes. Juliet's heart, which Paris was told to woo, is now tendered by her father as a thing already ruled. And Capulet, who in Act 1 made his will a part of her consent, now makes her consent a part of his will, without noticing, or without caring, that the sentence has been turned inside out. The parallel with the play's larger system of marriage and family duty is exact, and readers pursuing that system will find it laid out in full in the companion study of [the play's marriage and family duty as an institution](/2024/05/10/marriage-and-family-duty-analysis/), where the Capulet household's obligations are traced scene by scene.

### When Does a Promise Become a Threat in Verona?

A promise becomes a threat when its speaker gains the power to enforce it against the person it concerns, and Capulet's tender crosses that line in the scene of Juliet's refusal. The promise to Paris was made about Juliet, in Juliet's absence, and enforcement was always its destination.

When Juliet declines to perform it, the promise is enforced against her with the full vocabulary of paternal power: disowning, dragging, begging, starving, the threat to turn her out to die in the streets. The tender's gentleness lasted only while it was a word. The moment it is tested, it becomes a sentence. The play's judgment on such promises is embedded in their structure: a promise made about a person who is not present to examine it will be enforced as a threat when that person examines it at last. Juliet examines it, refuses it, and the promise's transformation is complete. What was tendered as love is executed as expulsion.

Capulet's broken Act 1 promise also deserves its place in the ledger because it is the play's clearest case of a promise broken without malice. Capulet does not lie to Paris in Act 1; he means the two summers, means the consent clause, means the whole condition, and then grief and social pressure change his circumstances, and the promise changes with them. The play does not present him as a villain for this. It presents him as an ordinary father in an extraordinary week, and the ordinariness is the indictment. Promises that depend on circumstances are not promises at all, in the play's severe arithmetic; they are intentions wearing the costume of obligation. Juliet's vow, made in the same week under greater pressure, does not change with her circumstances, and the contrast between father and daughter is the play's verdict on the generations. The father promises Paris a daughter whose consent matters, and delivers a daughter whose consent is commanded. The daughter promises a husband her faithful vow, and delivers it in a tomb. One of these is the play's model of swearing, and the audience has never had difficulty telling which.

The consequences of the tender are worth tracing to the end, because the promise binds even after its maker repents. Capulet, having tendered Juliet's love on a Wednesday for a Thursday wedding, cannot untender it when Juliet appears to submit. The feigned obedience of Act 4 Scene 2 is possible only because the promise stands: Juliet performs agreement with a marriage she has no intention of completing, and her performance works because her father believes his own tender has been accepted. The promise thus becomes the instrument of its own defeat. It forces Juliet into the Friar's desperate remedy, and the remedy's failure finishes what the tender began. When Capulet finds his daughter apparently dead on the morning of the wedding, his grief is expressed in the language of the promise's ruin, a flow of metaphors about death marrying his daughter in his place. The tender's author has become its mourner, and the play lets the transformation speak for itself. A father who tenders a child's love will, if the tender is refused, be left tendering her corpse to the family vault. The image is the ledger's final entry for the Capulet household, and it is entered against the promise, not against the father who meant, at first, to keep a better one.

## Lady Capulet's Revenge Vow: The Household's Other Sworn Word

The Capulet household contains a second vow-maker, quieter than the father and in her way more frightening, and the ledger is incomplete without her entry. Lady Capulet, comforting Juliet in Act 3 Scene 5 for a grief she misreads as mourning for Tybalt, promises a revenge of her own devising: she will send to a man in Mantua who will give the banished Romeo a dram that will make him keep Tybalt company. The promise is a vow in everything but formula. It is specific, it is actionable, it names its instrument and its outcome, and it is spoken, like Tybalt's oath, as a settled intention rather than a passing rage. It is also never performed. No messenger is shown going to Mantua on her errand; the play drops the thread entirely, and Romeo dies by his own purchased poison in a tomb, not by her hired dram in exile. The dropped thread is usually treated as one of the play's loose ends, a mother's threat the drama outgrows. The ledger reads it differently. Lady Capulet's revenge vow is the household's truest mirror of Tybalt's oath: the same blood-logic, the same pre-acquittal, the same conversion of grief into a death appointment. That it comes to nothing changes nothing about its nature. It comes to nothing because the plot outruns it, because Juliet's crisis consumes the household before the errand can be sent, and the audience is left with the uncomfortable knowledge that the Capulet parents, between them, tender their daughter to one man and vow poison to another in the same movement of the play. The marriage promise and the revenge vow are the household's twin instruments in Act 3 and Act 4, and both are aimed, with perfect parental confidence, at people who are not consulted. Juliet is not consulted about Paris. Romeo is not consulted about the dram. The household swears about its young people the way the feud swears about its enemies, and the ledger's symmetry is exact: in Verona, to be young is to be the object of other people's vows.

Her vow also completes the play's account of grief as a swearing engine. Tybalt's death sets every surviving Capulet to promising: the father promises a wedding, the mother promises a poisoning, and the daughter, the only one of the three who grieves for Tybalt and Romeo in the same tears, promises nothing except, later and falsely, obedience. The contrast is instructive. Grief in the older generation converts at once into instruments, marriages and murders, vows that dispose of other people's futures. Grief in Juliet waits, weeps, and finally acts on her own person and no one else's. The play's moral preference is visible in the distribution. The vows grief makes against others fail or curdle; the fidelity grief keeps toward the beloved, at ruinous personal cost, is the one the ending mourns. Lady Capulet's unperformed vow therefore earns its row in the table of the play's sworn words even in its failure, as the proof that an unkept vow can still be a kept intention, and that Verona's houses are dangerous not only for the promises they perform but for the ones they are prevented, by mere accident of plot, from performing.

## The Friar's Seal: A Promise Made to the City, Not to the Couple

The Friar's role in the play's economy of vows is the most complicated in the ledger, because his sworn words are of two kinds at once. He is the priest who administers the marriage vow, and he is the adviser who makes a promise of his own, and the two kinds interfere with each other in ways that the play never quite lets him escape. His consent in Act 2 Scene 3 is the foundation of everything: Romeo comes to the cell at dawn, asks to be married that day, and the Friar, after rebuking the speed of the transfer from Rosaline, agrees. His reason, in the Arden text, is not love but policy: "I'll thy assistant be; / For this alliance may so happy prove / To turn your households' rancour to pure love." The consent is a promise made to the city, not to the couple. The Friar is not vowing to serve Romeo and Juliet; he is wagering that their marriage will serve Verona, and the wager is the first step toward disaster, because a promise made for a third party's benefit will be managed for that party's benefit when the crisis comes. When Romeo is banished, the Friar manages the marriage as an investment to be protected, devising a proclamation at a future time, a reconciliation to be staged when the political weather clears. The vow he administered becomes, in his hands, a diplomatic instrument, awaiting its moment, and the moment never arrives.

The wedding scene itself, Act 2 Scene 6, is where the Friar's divided role is most visible, because he seals the marriage not with a blessing but with a warning. His great speech on the way to the rite is the play's other famous meditation on haste, and it shares Juliet's logic while missing her conclusion. "These violent delights have violent ends," he says in the Arden text, "And in their triumph die, like fire and powder, / Which as they kiss consume." The image is of a vow that destroys itself by its own intensity, fire and powder kissing and consuming each other, and it is usually quoted as the play's wisdom about passion. In the ledger's terms, the warning does something else: it transfers responsibility. The priest who warns the couple that their delights are violent has insured himself against the outcome, and the insurance is audible in the scene. He counsels moderation, "Therefore love moderately: long love doth so; / Too swift arrives as tardy as too slow," and then performs the ceremony anyway, at the speed the couple demands. A vow administered with a disclaimer is a new kind of sworn word in the play, and it is the Friar's invention: the sacrament with a warning label. Romeo's answer in the same scene shows why the disclaimer fails. "Amen, amen! but come what sorrow can, / It cannot countervail the exchange of joy," he says, and the word exchange reaches back to the orchard, to Juliet's exchange of faithful vows, and forward to the tomb. Romeo hears the wedding as the orchard vow fulfilled, a joy traded for a vow, and no disclaimer can reach him inside that certainty. The Friar warns; the couple exchanges; the ceremony proceeds. The ledger records the marriage vow as kept without exception, and the Friar's part in it as the play's saddest case of a promise kept in the letter and failed in the outcome.

The Friar's later promises compound the pattern. In Act 3 Scene 3 he promises the banished Romeo a sequence of consolations, a visitation of Juliet, a proclamation of the marriage, a pardon to be begged from the Prince, each step plausible and each step dependent on the next man's cooperation. In Act 4 Scene 1 he promises Juliet a remedy, and the remedy is a potion and a feigned death, a promise that requires her to break faith with every living person who loves her in order to keep faith with the one person who is absent. The promises are not lies. The Friar believes each of them as he speaks, and the play's judgment is therefore more interesting than simple blame. His promises fail because they are promises about other people's conduct, letters and friars and princes and watchmen, and a promise about other people's conduct is a vow with its collateral held by strangers. The orchard vow held its collateral in the speakers. The Friar's promises hold their collateral in the postal arrangements of Mantua and the alertness of a sleeping watch. The difference is not sincerity. It is structure, and the play's ledger is a study in structure. The Friar's final confession in Act 5 Scene 3, that he stands both to impeach and purge himself, condemned and excused in the same breath, is the language of a man who has discovered that his promises were sound in intention and ruinous in design, and the Prince's pardon, neither a conviction nor an acquittal, closes the ledger on him with the same ambiguity.

### Which Vows Does the Plot of the Play Actually Enforce?

The plot enforces the vows that bind their speakers to a single, simple action, and it declines to enforce every vow that depends on a sequence. Tybalt's oath, to strike Romeo dead, is one action, and the plot delivers the occasion for it within two days.

The lovers' vow, to be faithful, is one disposition, and the plot tests it continuously but never breaks it. Against these, the Friar's promises depend on sequences: a letter delivered, a watchman awake, a timing held, a prince persuaded, and the plot breaks the sequences at their weakest link with mechanical indifference. Capulet's tender depends on a daughter's compliance, a sequence of one, and it breaks there. The pattern is the play's law of sworn words. Simplicity is enforceable; complexity is a promise the plot will not keep, and the characters who swear simply, however terribly, are the characters whose words come true.

## Rash or Faithful? Juliet's Doubt and the Speed of the Vows

The complication at the heart of the brief must now be faced directly, because it is the complication on which every classroom argument about the play turns. Are the lovers' vows admirable, the one honest speech in a city of false ones, or are they dangerous, a pair of children swearing eternal faith within hours of meeting, with consequences the whole city pays for? The play's answer, on the evidence of the ledger, is that the vows are admirable in form and dangerous in context, and that the danger comes not from the vows but from the system that receives them. The form is Juliet's work. She refuses the inconstant witness, converts the oath into a self-staked vow, imposes the exchange, and sets the appointment. No other vow in the play is examined at this standard. The context is Verona's work. The vow is received by a feud, a father, and a friar, each of whom converts it into something else: Tybalt into a death warrant, Capulet into a rival tender, the Friar into a diplomatic instrument. The lovers swear well and are sworn around badly, and the tragedy is the difference between those two facts.

Juliet's doubt deserves the central place in this argument, because it is the only doubt in the play that improves the thing doubted. Her objection to the moon does not weaken the vow; it strengthens it, by forcing the vow onto better collateral. Her description of the contract as too rash, too unadvised, too sudden does not retract the exchange; it schedules it, giving the bud a summer in which to ripen. Her insistence on the appointment, the messenger at nine, the time and place, does not commercialize the love; it verifies it, converting a night of speeches into a morning of action. At every stage, Juliet's doubt functions as quality control on the vow, and the vow that emerges from her control is the play's only sworn word that is never broken, never regretted, and never renegotiated. The point can be put as a paradox, and the paradox is the play's thesis in miniature. The vow survives because it was doubted. The vows that destroy Verona are the ones that were never doubted at all. Tybalt does not doubt. Capulet does not doubt until doubt is useless. The Friar doubts privately and proceeds publicly. Only Juliet doubts in the open, before the vow is sealed, and only her vow holds.

The speed objection, which the doubt is often mistaken for, should be separated from it. The vow is certainly fast: spoken on the night of the feast, sealed the next afternoon, tested within the week. The play itself raises the objection, in the Friar's rebuke, in Juliet's lightning image, and in the Chorus's account of a love that moves from Rosaline to Juliet in a day. Readers who want the play's own treatment of pace will find it argued in full in the study of [whether the romance moves too fast](/2024/09/27/is-romeo-and-juliet-love-too-fast/), where the calendar of the courtship is laid out day by day. The ledger's contribution to that argument is narrower and, for the study of vows, decisive. Speed is not the variable that predicts a vow's fate in this play. Tybalt's oath is as old as the feud and as slow as inheritance, and it kills. Benvolio's undertaking is made and kept within a scene, and it harms no one. Rosaline's vow is presumably the fruit of long consideration, and it governs the first act like a tyrant. Juliet's vow is the fastest in the play and the soundest. What predicts a vow's fate is not its speed but its structure: who it binds, by what it is sworn, and whether it was examined before it was spoken. The play's judgement on haste is therefore a judgement on unexamined haste, and the lovers, examined by Juliet at every step, are acquitted by the very standard that condemns the feud.

## The Messenger at the Hour of Nine: A Promise Tested by Its Performance

Between the vow and the wedding stands a test the play stages at unusual length, and the length is itself an argument. Juliet, in the orchard, set the performance condition for the exchange: a messenger, sent at the hour of nine, to learn the time and place of the marriage. Act 2 Scene 5, the scene of the Nurse's return, is the audit of that promise, and Shakespeare stretches the audit across one of the longest teasing sequences in the play. The Nurse comes home with the answer in her head and withholds it, complaining of aching bones, demanding a moment's leave, digressing into Romeo's face and figure, while Juliet circles her with questions. A vow is not complete when it is spoken. It is complete when its performance arrives, and the scene makes the audience sit inside the gap between word and performance for as long as Juliet must. Every delay in the Nurse's report is a small rehearsal for the play's great delay, the undelivered letter of Act 5, and the parallel is structural. The first messenger succeeds, barely, through comedy. The second never arrives, through plague. The play teaches the difference between the two scenes so that the audience will feel, in the tomb, the full weight of a promise that depended on delivery.

Juliet's impatience in the scene is often played for charm, and the charm is earned, but the impatience has a contractual edge that productions sometimes soften. She has performed her side of the exchange: the messenger was sent at the hour named, the conditions were stated plainly, the vow's machinery was built to her own design. What she is waiting for is not news of Romeo's feelings, which she does not doubt in this scene, but evidence of Romeo's arrangements, the proof that the word has become a plan. The distinction matters to the ledger because it shows what kind of believer Juliet is. She does not ask the Nurse whether Romeo loves her. She asks where and when, the questions of a contracting party auditing performance. When the answer finally comes, it converts the orchard exchange into a wedding within the hour, and the conversion vindicates her method. The vow that was examined, collateralized, and scheduled produces its performance on the day, and the play's later promises, the Friar's letter, Capulet's tender, the Prince's pardon, fail exactly where this one succeeds: at the point of delivery, in the hands of intermediaries, under the pressure of a city that treats sworn words as negotiable everywhere except in the orchard.

The Nurse's part in the audit deserves its own sentence in the ledger, because she is the play's first intermediary and its first warning about intermediaries. Her teasing is affectionate, her loyalty at this point is real, and her report, when it comes, is accurate. Yet the scene establishes the pattern that will ruin everything: the lovers' vow, sound in itself, must pass through other people's hands to reach its performance, and other people's hands have their own aches, humours, and delays. The Nurse delays for sport. Friar John will be delayed by quarantine. Balthasar will arrive promptly and fatally early, with true news wrongly understood. The vow's enemies in this play are rarely villains. They are couriers, counsellors, and circumstances, the ordinary friction of a world in which a promise is only as good as its delivery. Juliet's design in the orchard anticipated even this, in its way: she kept the chain short, one messenger, one hour, one answer. The tragedy begins when the chain lengthens, and every link added after the wedding is a link the play breaks.

## Kept, Broken, and Paid in Blood: What the Ledger Proves

The ledger's kept column is short and expensive. Benvolio keeps his undertaking and is forgotten by the play. Rosaline keeps her vow and never appears. Tybalt keeps his oath and dies of it. Paris keeps his tomb vow for a single night and dies keeping it. The lovers keep their vow and die of the keeping. The pattern is not subtle, and it is not accidental. In the world of the play, keeping a vow is the most expensive act a person can perform, because the vow holds the speaker to a course while the world changes around the course, and the world of Verona changes violently and fast. The speakers who survive the play are, almost without exception, the speakers whose vows were flexible, negotiable, or broken: the Prince, whose edict bends; Capulet, whose condition reverses; the Friar, whose promises fail and who is pardoned anyway. It draws the colder conclusion that Verona rewards the flexible and buries the faithful, and that the audience's love for the faithful is the play's protest against its own city.

The broken column is longer and cheaper, and its entries repay a different kind of attention. Capulet's Act 1 condition is broken, and no one in the play ever mentions it again; the father himself does not seem to remember making it. The Nurse's faith is broken in a single speech of counsel, and Juliet's response, the feigned amen and the private dismissal, records the break without any scene of confrontation. The Friar's promises are broken by events, and he is forgiven by the Prince in the play's closing lines, more talked of than punished. The broken vows of the play are forgiven, forgotten, or absorbed, and their costs are invoiced to others: to Juliet, who loses her father's protection and her nurse's faith in the same week; to the lovers, who lose the letter and the timing; to the city, which loses three young men and two young women to other people's broken words. The play's deepest observation about swearing may be this asymmetry of invoicing. A kept vow sends its bill to the keeper. A broken vow sends its bill to whoever is weakest in the room when it breaks. The ledger is therefore not only a record of fidelity and failure. It is a record of who pays, and the payer is never, in the broken cases, the person who swore.

The tomb scene gathers the ledger's threads into a single room, and its treatment of vows is the play's final word on the subject. Paris arrives performing a vow, strewing flowers, vowing nightly obsequies, and dies at the threshold of the vault he came to decorate. Romeo arrives carrying out a vow of a different order, the grim service he promises to Tybalt's corpse, the favour of sundering, with the hand that cut Tybalt's youth in twain, the man who was Tybalt's enemy, which is to say himself. The vow is kept within the hour, and Romeo's death is thereby framed not as despair alone but as a payment: a debt owed to the cousin he killed, discharged in the only currency the feud recognizes. Juliet wakes to find both vows paid around her, and her own death completes the orchard exchange; the faithful vow traded in the dark is redeemed in the dark, in the same mutual form, each speaker having held the other's word to the end. The fathers' closing vows, the statues in pure gold, are sworn words of the purest Verona type: binding no one, examined by no one, priced in metal, addressed to the public gaze. The play ends with the cheapest vow in its ledger spoken over the bodies of the two people whose vow was the dearest, and the juxtaposition is the verdict. Around the lovers' story runs the larger pattern traced in the companion study of [loyalty and betrayal in Verona](/2024/09/06/loyalty-betrayal-romeo-and-juliet/), where the households' shifting allegiances are mapped in full; the vow ledger is that pattern's sharpest instrument, because a vow is loyalty with a signature on it, and the signatures in this play are honoured in inverse proportion to the rank of the people who sign.

One written vow survives the tomb, and it is the play's final irony about sworn words. Romeo's letter to his father, carried by Balthasar and produced before the Prince, explains the marriage, the purchase of the poison, and the deaths, and it is accepted by everyone as true. The only sworn document in the play that is believed without question is a suicide letter. Spoken vows were doubted, corrected, broken, and enforced by the wrong people; the written vow arrives after every deadline and is honoured at once, because it asks for nothing except belief. The play's last lesson about swearing is therefore about timing. A vow that arrives on time is tested; a vow that arrives too late is simply entered into the record. The fathers' statues belong to the record too, promises to build rather than promises to act, and the Prince's closing speech, which imposes no oath and exacts no forfeit, leaves the record open. The ledger closes without a balance being struck, because the play knows that the balance cannot be struck in words. It can only be paid, and it has been.

## The Oaths That Are Missing: Defiance, Purchase, and the Unsworn Peace

A ledger should record absences as faithfully as entries, and the play's most eloquent sworn words are the ones nobody speaks. The first absence is heaven's. Prayers, curses, invocations, and oaths rise from Verona in every act, and no answering voice descends. The heavens are addressed as witness, as judge, as god of idolatry, and they remain, throughout, the silent party to every contract in the play. The silence is structural. A vow system whose supreme witness never speaks must generate its own enforcement, and Verona's enforcement mechanisms are the feud, the family, and the law, each of which the play shows to be corrupt, flexible, or slow. The lovers' self-staked vow is, among other things, an adaptation to the silence: if heaven will not witness, the speaker must. Juliet's correction of the moon oath can be read as theology in action, the design of a vow for a world in which the witness chair is empty.

The second absence is an oath of peace. The play opens with a city under edict and closes with a city under grief, and at no point does any character swear peace, or exact an oath of it, or propose the instrument that the period used for exactly such feuds. The Prince, who has the authority, pronounces penalties and pardons but never administers a reconciliation oath to the two houses. The fathers, who have the motive, exchange statues instead. The Friar, who has the sacrament, spends it on the marriage and has none left for the feud. The missing oath is the play's quietest accusation: the one sworn word that might have bound the future was never attempted, because every authority in Verona preferred instruments that bound other people, edicts, tenders, plans, monuments, to the one instrument that would have bound the authorities themselves. The lovers, alone, swear in the self-binding form, and they swear it to each other rather than to the city, because the city's form was never offered to them.

Against the missing oaths stand two spoken utterances that complete the taxonomy by inversion. The first is Romeo's defiance in Mantua, "Then I defy you, stars," an anti-vow: a sworn word addressed not to a witness but against one, pledging the speaker to war with the order of things. It binds like a vow and performs like one, since Romeo holds to it without deviation through the purchase of the poison, the journey, and the tomb, and its fidelity is the mirror image of the orchard's. The orchard vow bound Romeo to Juliet; the defiance binds him against the stars, and the two oaths together map the whole of his arc, from a love examined into being to a despair that needs no examination because it has nothing left to lose. The second inversion is the bargain with the apothecary, where swearing is replaced by purchase. Romeo does not persuade the man; he prices him, observing that the world affords no law to make him rich and inviting him to break the law that makes him poor, and the apothecary's answer is the anti-vow in its purest form: "My poverty, but not my will, consents." A consent extracted by gold is the opposite of a vow, a word that binds the hand while freeing the will, and the play places it at the exact point where the vow system collapses. The lovers swore with will and no gold. The fathers will close with gold and no will. The apothecary's consent, gold moving a hand whose will dissents, is the hinge between those two economies, and the poison it buys is the interest on every broken promise in the play.

## Critics on the Sworn Word: A Disagreement Adjudicated

Three readers of the play have taken its sworn words most seriously, and they do not agree, and the disagreement is the most useful one in the play's criticism for the purposes of this article. M. M. Mahood, in Shakespeare's Wordplay, argues that the play's verbal surface, its puns, quibbles, and formulas, is never ornamental in Shakespeare, and that the sworn formulas of the text carry the same semantic weight as its grandest images. On Mahood's account, an oath in this play is a small contract in the language itself: the formula binds because the words are load-bearing, and a reader who skips the oaths as decoration misses the structure they hold up. Applied to the orchard, Mahood's principle dignifies Juliet's pedantry. Her fussing over what Romeo swears by is not girlish caution; it is the play's own verbal intelligence at work, testing the formula because the formula is where the meaning lives.

Catherine Belsey, in The Subject of Tragedy, reads the balcony scene from a different direction and reaches a stronger conclusion about Juliet. For Belsey, Juliet's speech in the orchard is the moment a woman in this play takes the position of speaking subject, authoring her own desire in a world that has authored her as a daughter, a bride-price, a tender. The interrogative form of the exchange matters to Belsey's reading: Juliet asks the questions, sets the terms, and converts Romeo's declarations into a dialogue she conducts. The vow, on this account, is an emancipation. It is the instrument by which Juliet stops being spoken about and starts speaking, and its binding force is the force of a self being constituted in language. The reading aligns with the ledger's finding that Juliet's vow is the play's soundest, and gives that finding a politics: the vow is sound because it is hers, negotiated in her voice, on her terms.

The disagreement arrives with the textual evidence, and it is carried by the play's editor. Rene Weis, editing the Arden third series text, prints a balcony scene whose fullness is a function of the Second Quarto of 1599, the good quarto, since the First Quarto of 1597 gives a shorter, rougher version of the orchard exchange. The caution this creates for Belsey's reading is real. If the celebrated vow exchange, with Juliet's interrogations and corrections at full length, is a feature of the fuller, later text, then the emancipation reading rests on wording whose authority is editorial as much as authorial, and the critic who builds a politics of female self-authorship on the exchange must reckon with the possibility that the exchange grew in the printing. The caution can be pressed further into a genuine counter-reading: that the vow scene's authority is a textual accident, celebrated because one quarto happened to preserve more of it, and that the play's actual first audiences heard a brisker, less interrogative Juliet.

The adjudication this article reaches is as follows. The textual caution is sound as a warning and unsound as a refutation. It is sound as a warning because editors and critics alike should know which quarto carries which wording, and the orchard scene's fame is indeed inseparable from the Second Quarto's amplitude. It is unsound as a refutation because the shorter First Quarto text retains the exchange's essential structure: the refusal of the moon, the correction, the exchange of vows, and the appointment are present in embryo even where the wording is compressed, so the scene's argument about swearing is not a printing accident but a feature of the scene in both its surviving forms. Belsey's reading therefore survives, with a qualification she would perhaps accept: Juliet's self-authorship is a property of the scene's design, its questions and answers, rather than of any single celebrated line, and design is exactly what textual revision tends to clarify rather than invent. Mahood's principle supplies the method that reconciles the two positions. The formulas are load-bearing in both quartos; the editor's work shows the loads being adjusted; the critic's work shows who carries them. The verdict of this article accordingly stands with Belsey on the substance, with Weis on the evidence, and with Mahood on the method: the orchard vow is the play's one emancipation, it is documented in both early texts in differing degrees of fullness, and its mechanism is the formula itself, the sworn words examined until they can be trusted.

## The Name Beneath the Vow: Why Renaming Is the Vow's First Instrument

One element of the orchard scene has been treated in this article as background, and it deserves a paragraph of its own before the argument closes, because it is the vow's precondition. Before Juliet will discuss what Romeo swears by, she has already asked what he is called, and the scene's most famous question, what's in a name, is the vow's foundation stone. Romeo's promise to be new baptized, to take a new name at her direction, is the first vow of the scene, earlier than the moon oath, and everything else in the exchange is built on it. A man who has surrendered his name has surrendered the feud's claim on him, and only such a man can swear by his gracious self, because the self he swears by is no longer Montague property. The naming system of the play, the rose and the surname and the whole grammar of identity that the feud trades in, is another article's ground, and it is treated at length in the companion study of [the balcony's big question about names](/2023/08/04/whats-in-a-name-romeo-and-juliet/). For the ledger, the point is brief. The renaming is the vow's instrument: it is how Romeo becomes a person who can make the exchange Juliet demands, and its presence at the head of the orchard scene shows that Shakespeare understood a vow to require not only the right witness and the right form but the right speaker, a speaker who has first been freed from the names that would swear in his place.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch vow ledger, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch vow ledger is the table at the centre of this article, recording the play's vows, oaths, promises, and one curse that behaves like a vow, with each entry's speaker, collateral, outcome, and price. The pattern it exposes is a law of invoicing. Vows that bind only their speakers tend to be kept, and the bill for keeping them arrives at the speaker's own door: Tybalt dies of his oath, Paris dies of his tomb rite, the lovers die of their exchange. Vows that bind someone else tend to be broken, and the bill arrives elsewhere: Capulet's reversed condition is paid by Juliet, the Friar's failed sequence is paid by the lovers, the Nurse's broken faith is paid by the girl she raised. The ledger's value is that it converts an impression every audience carries out of the theatre, that the faithful suffer and the flexible survive, into evidence that can be checked row by row against the text.

### Q: What is the difference between a vow and an oath in the world of the play?

An oath calls a witness and stakes the speaker's credit before that witness, while a vow binds the speaker's future conduct and carries its sanction within the speaker's own resolution. The distinction governs the play's central scene. Romeo offers an oath, swearing by the moon, and Juliet refuses it because the witness is inconstant and because an oath lets the speaker shelter behind the witness. She demands the vow form instead: swear by thy gracious self, stake the person, not the planet. A promise, the third instrument, binds one person to another without a sacred witness, and the play treats promises as the weakest of the three, because they depend on circumstances, as Capulet's condition to Paris demonstrates when grief reverses it in a single scene. The tragedy can be mapped onto the three forms: oaths kill their speakers, vows save their speakers' honour at the cost of their lives, and promises fail quietly and bill the bystanders.

### Q: What does Juliet put in place of an oath when she tells Romeo to swear by his gracious self?

Juliet puts the speaker himself in place of the witness, and with him she puts the whole burden of proof. An oath by the moon distributes responsibility: the moon guarantees, the speaker recites, and if the love changes, the changing moon can share the blame. Juliet's correction abolishes the distribution. Swear by thy gracious self, which is the god of my idolatry, she tells him in the Arden text, and the formula makes Romeo his own collateral, his own witness, and his own sanction. Nothing outside him can release him, because nothing outside him was invoked. The move also converts swearing from a performance into a constitution: Romeo is not reciting a formula but becoming the kind of person whose word is the guarantee. That is why the exchange that follows can be so brief. Once the collateral is the self, the vow needs only to be traded, faithfully, for hers, and the trade is the scene's great sentence about the exchange of love's faithful vow.

### Q: Why is Rosaline's vow the most powerful oath in the play that nobody ever hears spoken?

Rosaline's vow is the most powerful unheard oath because it governs the entire first act while existing only as a report in the mouth of the man it wounds. Romeo tells Benvolio that she has forsworn to love, and in that vow he lives dead; the sentence is the play's first account of a sworn word, and it establishes the pattern that a vow's force lies in its reputation rather than its performance. No audience ever hears Rosaline swear. No scene tests her resolve. Yet the reported vow drives Romeo to the Capulet feast, delivers him to Juliet, and sets the tragedy in motion as surely as any oath spoken in full view. The play is making a point about how sworn words circulate: they travel as other people's sentences, gather authority in the travelling, and end up binding people who never heard them spoken. Rosaline's vow binds Romeo, then Benvolio's curiosity, then the plot itself, and its speaker, the one person it actually obliges, never appears to be bound by it onstage at all.

### Q: By what does Tybalt swear at the feast, and why does the object of his oath guarantee it will be kept?

Tybalt swears by the stock and honour of his kin, in the Arden text: to strike Romeo dead he holds it not a sin. The object guarantees the keeping because ancestry cannot be renegotiated. A witness like the moon changes; a witness like the self can waver; but a man's stock and honour are fixed at birth and policed by the whole household, so an oath staked on them borrows the feud's entire enforcement machinery. Tybalt is also, by choosing that collateral, defining himself as the feud's instrument rather than its agent: the honour of the kin demands the act, and he is merely its executor. The guarantee has a second, darker layer. Honour in this play is a debt instrument, and a slight unpaid is a dishonour compounded, so the oath accrues interest from the moment it is sworn. By Act 3 Scene 1 the debt is called in, Mercutio pays it first by accident of proximity, and Tybalt pays it second, with his life, in the same street. The oath was kept, and the keeping is the catastrophe the orchard scene's whole method was designed to prevent.

### Q: Why does Capulet's promise to Paris bind his daughter while leaving Capulet himself free to change its terms?

Capulet's promise binds Juliet rather than Capulet because it was never, in structure, a vow at all: it was a disposition of property, spoken by the property's owner, about a person who was not present to examine it. In Act 1 Scene 2 the terms are gentle, two summers of delay and Juliet's consent as part of the arrangement, but gentleness does not change the structure. The promise runs from father to suitor; Juliet is its object, not its party, and objects cannot enforce terms. When grief and shame change Capulet's circumstances in Act 3, nothing in the promise's form resists the change, because the only party who might have held him to the original terms is the daughter the promise disposes of. He therefore revises freely, tendering her love for a Thursday, and enforces the revision against her when she refuses. A vow binds its speaker because the speaker staked the self. Capulet staked his daughter, and the ledger records the result: the person staked pays, the person who staked walks away to mourn her.

### Q: What service does Romeo promise Tybalt's corpse in the tomb, and why is it framed as the payment of a debt?

Romeo promises Tybalt's corpse the service of killing the man who killed him, who is Romeo himself, and the promise is framed as a payment because Romeo understands his death in the tomb as the settlement of a feud account. In the Arden text he asks what more favour he can do Tybalt than to sunder, with the hand that cut Tybalt's youth in twain, the man who was Tybalt's enemy, and he begs his cousin's forgiveness in the same breath. The language is the ledger's language: favour, debt, discharge. Romeo has carried the unpaid account since Act 3 Scene 1, when his refusal to fight cost Mercutio's life and his revenge cost Tybalt's, and the tomb is where the account closes. The framing matters because it distinguishes his suicide from mere despair. Despair is present, but the vow form gives the death a direction: it is owed, it is addressed to a named creditor, and it is performed at the creditor's side. The feud taught Romeo that deaths are payments. His last act accepts the lesson and turns it, with terrible consistency, on himself.

### Q: Is any character in the play punished for perjury, and what does the absence of such a punishment reveal?

No character in the play is punished for perjury as such, and the absence reveals that Verona's enforcement of sworn words has been entirely privatized. Heaven, the witness who in the period's theology punished false swearing, never speaks. The Prince, the earthly enforcer, punishes violence with banishment and fines but never addresses the broken promises that caused the violence: Capulet's reversed condition, the Nurse's reversed counsel, the Friar's failed sequence all pass without legal notice. The only sanctions that operate are the feud's own, honour demanding satisfaction, and the lovers' own, conscience refusing a second marriage. A city that will not punish perjury will be governed by whoever enforces promises privately, and Verona is governed accordingly, by Tybalt's honour code and by Juliet's conscience, the two most absolute enforcers in the play, one of whom dies for an oath and the other for a vow. The absence at the centre, no court, no heaven, no priest with authority over false words, is the tragedy's enabling condition, the vacuum into which the young people's sworn words fall and are lost.

### Q: Why does the Friar seal the marriage with a warning instead of with a blessing on the vows themselves?

The Friar seals the marriage with a warning because his consent to the match was never a blessing in the first place: it was a wager on the feud's cure, and the warning is the wager's hedge. These violent delights have violent ends, he tells the couple in Act 2 Scene 6, and counsels moderation on the threshold of the rite, and the speech functions as a disclaimer, transferring the risk of the enterprise from the priest who enables it to the couple who demand it. A blessing would have committed him; a warning insures him. The distinction is not a quibble, because the play returns to it in his final confession, where he stands to impeach and purge himself in one breath, condemned and excused together, the exact posture of a man who warned everyone and committed to nothing. The lovers, by contrast, accept their vow without hedge or disclaimer, which is why the play's sympathy never leaves them even when its judgment of their haste is severe. The Friar's warning is wise, and the play honours its wisdom. It simply notices that wisdom, spoken as insurance, is not the same thing as faithfulness.

### Q: What does Juliet's word "contract" claim about the orchard exchange that the word "love" could not?

Juliet's word "contract" claims that the orchard exchange creates obligations that survive the feelings of the people who made it, and the word "love" alone could not claim that. Love, in the play's opening acts, is a weather: it moves Romeo from Rosaline to Juliet in an evening, it bows, it sighs, it writes sonnets. A contract is a structure. When Juliet calls the exchange too rash, too unadvised, too sudden, and then proposes the exchange of faithful vows, she is moving the night's feelings into the night's structure, giving them terms, parties, and performance conditions. The claim embedded in the word is that the vow will still be standing when the feelings change, under grief, banishment, and false news, and the play tests the claim to destruction and finds it holds. The contract outlives the lightning. That is also why her word scandalizes the romantic reading of the scene: the heroine of the great love scene speaks like a lawyer at the decisive moment, and the play vindicates the lawyer over the sonneteer at every point where the two approaches are compared.

### Q: Which kept vow costs its keeper the most, and what does the play prove by the price?

The lovers' vow costs its keepers the most, since they pay for its keeping with their lives within four days of speaking it, and the play proves by that price that cost is its measure of a vow's reality. Every other kept vow in the ledger is expensive in proportion to its genuineness: Tybalt's oath, sincerely meant, costs his life; Paris's tomb vow, sincerely meant, costs his; Benvolio's undertaking, a small sincerity, costs an afternoon. The correlation is the play's audit method. A vow that costs nothing when circumstances turn is revealed, by the cheapness, to have been an intention in ceremonial dress, like Capulet's Act 1 condition, which grief repriced to nothing within the week. The lovers' vow is repriced by banishment, by a forced marriage, and by a faked death, and at every repricing they pay the full amount without negotiation. The play does not argue that paying proves them wise. It argues that paying proves them bound, and that in a city of negotiable words, being bound is the rarest and most dangerous condition a person can be in.

### Q: Why do the servants' undertakings at the opening of the play cheapen the currency every later vow must spend?

The servants' undertakings cheapen the currency because they demonstrate, before any lover speaks, that sworn language in Verona circulates without backing. Sampson and Gregory open the play with swaggering commitments, walls to take, tyrants to play, quarrels to pick, none of it staked on anything but vanity and all of it abandoned the moment real swords arrive. The effect on the audience's ear is the effect inflation has on a market: every later speaker must spend more language to purchase the same credibility. This is the pressure under which the orchard scene labours. Romeo's vows arrive in a theatre whose first scene taught the audience that young men's undertakings are wind, and Juliet's interrogation of his oath is partly a response to that lesson: she has lived in this city, she knows the exchange rate, and she demands collateral precisely because the currency is debased. The play's great sworn words are therefore not spoken into a vacuum of sincerity. They are spoken uphill, against a debased market, and their speakers know it, which is why the lovers' vow is built like a contract and the feud's oaths are backed by ancestry and blood.

### Q: Where does the word "vow" itself fall in the play, and why do its rare appearances gather around Juliet?

The word "vow" falls rarely in the play, and its two great stations are the report of Rosaline in Act 1 Scene 1 and the orchard exchange in Act 2 Scene 2, where it is spoken as verb by Romeo and as noun by Juliet. The gathering around Juliet is a fact of the scene's design. Romeo uses the word as an action, by yonder blessed moon I vow, a gesture of swearing. Juliet takes the same word and makes it a thing, the exchange of thy love's faithful vow for mine, an instrument that can be held, traded, and audited. The transformation of the verb into the noun is the transformation of the whole scene in miniature: feeling becomes structure in her mouth. Around those two stations, the play mostly prefers the humbler vocabulary of promise, faith, and troth for its other sworn business, reserving the solemn word for the woman who refuses to swear it. The reservation looks deliberate. The character who will not swear by the moon is the character in whose speech the word "vow" acquires its weight, because she alone treats the word itself as collateral too precious to spend carelessly.

### Q: Why are the play's women usually sworn about rather than swearing, and where does Juliet break the pattern?

The play's women are usually sworn about because Verona's vow economy is a male market: fathers tender daughters, suitors accept tenders, priests administer marriages arranged by men, and women's spoken part in the machinery is consent, the small part Capulet allots his daughter in Act 1 and withdraws in Act 3. Rosaline swears, but offstage, in report, and her vow is a refusal that the male speakers treat as a problem in their own story. Lady Capulet swears once, a revenge vow over poison, borrowing the feud's masculine grammar for a mother's grief. Juliet breaks the pattern in the orchard, where she does not merely swear but legislates swearing: she rejects the witness, sets the collateral, drafts the exchange, and schedules the performance, occupying in a single scene every office the vow system contains, party, witness-keeper, and judge of form. The break is the scene's deepest claim to fame. Other heroines in the period swear beautifully. Juliet swears constitutionally, designing the instrument that the rest of the play, and this article's ledger, must use to measure everyone else's words.

### Q: Does the Prince ever exact an oath of peace from the two houses, and what fills the place where that oath should stand?

The Prince never exacts an oath of peace, and the place where that oath should stand is filled, at the end, by gold. He opens the play with an edict and a forfeit, closes the feud's worst day with a banishment and a fine, and ends the play with a summary of losses and a pardon for the Friar, but at no point does he administer the one instrument that would bind the houses' future conduct under witness: a sworn reconciliation. The omission fits his record. His instruments bind other people and bend under pressure, as the commuted death sentence for Romeo shows, and an oath of peace would have bound his own enforcement as much as the fathers' conduct. What replaces it in the final scene is the exchange of statue vows, Montague promising Juliet in pure gold, Capulet answering with Romeo at her side. Monuments stand where an oath should be, and the substitution is the play's last word on its sworn economy: a city that would not swear peace purchases remembrance instead, and the purchase, unlike an oath, obliges nobody to anything except admiration.

### Q: What promise does Benvolio make to Montague in Act 1 Scene 1, and how does its quiet keeping judge the louder vows?

Benvolio promises Montague that he will learn the cause of Romeo's sorrow, and he keeps the promise within the act, questioning Romeo and reporting the Rosaline matter faithfully to the father who asked. The keeping is quiet to the point of invisibility: no witness, no formula, no ceremony, and no later scene remembers that a promise was made at all. Its judgment on the louder vows is delivered by contrast. The play's grand sworn words, Tybalt's oath, Capulet's tender, the Friar's sequences, are all spoken with maximum apparatus and all fail or kill. Benvolio's promise has no apparatus and succeeds completely, at the cost of nothing, harming nobody. The ledger keeps his row as its control case, the proof that the play knows what a sound promise looks like and could have filled Verona with them. That the city instead fills with oaths backed by ancestry and contracts backed by gold is the tragedy's premise, and Benvolio, the play's one reliable promise-keeper, is rewarded by being written out of the action, as though reliability itself were undramatic, which in Verona it is.

### Q: How does the language of the lovers' promises change between the orchard night and the wedding morning?

Between the orchard and the wedding, the lovers' promise language changes from the interrogative to the declarative, and the change records what the vow has accomplished. In Act 2 Scene 2 the promises are built from questions: what shall I swear by, what satisfaction canst thou have tonight, when and where and what performance. Juliet conducts the exchange as an examination because the vow must be designed before it can be trusted. In Act 2 Scene 6, before the Friar, the questions are gone. Romeo speaks in absolutes about an exchange of joy that no sorrow can countervail, and Juliet speaks of a love whose worth cannot be summed because it grows as it is given. The declarative mode is not carelessness; it is the sound of a contract already signed. The play marks the cost of the change immediately, since the Friar's warning goes unheard inside their certainty, but the ledger's judgment is double: the interrogative made the vow sound, and the declarative makes it mortal, because speakers who no longer examine their words have begun, like everyone else in Verona, to trust them.

### Q: What makes a vow rash in this play: its speed, its witness, or its object?

Rashness in this play is a property of the witness and the object, not of the speed, and the ledger proves it by counterexample in both directions. Tybalt's oath is slow, inherited, generations in the making, and it is the play's most destructive sworn word, because its object is another man's death and its witness is an ancestry that permits no reconsideration. Benvolio's undertaking is made and kept within a single scene and harms no one, because its object is a piece of information and its witness is his own word to a worried father. The lovers' vow is the fastest in the play and the soundest, because its object is the speakers' own fidelity and its witness, after Juliet's correction, is the speakers' own selves. Speed enters the question only as a diagnostic: haste is dangerous when it prevents examination, which is the sense of Juliet's lightning image, a flash that ceases before it can be inspected. A vow examined at speed outranks a vow unexamined at leisure, in this play's arithmetic, every time the two are weighed, and the weighing is the difference between the orchard and the feast.

### Q: How should an essay argue that the play trusts small kept promises more than great sworn ones?

An essay should argue the case from the ledger, setting the play's small kept promises beside its great sworn failures and letting the pattern carry the thesis. The evidence arranges itself in ascending order of apparatus and descending order of reliability. Benvolio's undertaking, apparatus zero, is kept perfectly. The lovers' vow, examined and self-staked, is kept at the price of their lives. The sacrament, administered by a priest with a disclaimer, is kept in form and ruined in outcome. Capulet's tender, backed by household authority, is reversed within days. The Prince's edict, backed by the state, bends at its first test. An essay built on that ladder can then engage the obvious objection, that the lovers' kept vow hardly advertises the play's trust, since keeping it kills them, and answer it from the play's own distribution of sympathy: the ending mourns the keepers, pardons the flexible, and monuments the faithful, which is what a culture does with values it honours and cannot afford. The conclusion writes itself from the final scene. Verona trusts small promises so little that it buries the people who keep them, and builds their statues in gold.

### Q: What is the final vow spoken in the play, and why is it a promise to build rather than a promise to act?

The final vows spoken in the play are the fathers' statue promises, Montague vowing to raise Juliet in pure gold and Capulet answering with Romeo beside her, and they are promises to build because building is the one commitment the speakers can make without binding their future conduct. A promise to act, to keep peace, to govern households gently, to exact no more revenges, would have obliged the fathers every day for the rest of their lives, and the play has shown what such obligations are worth in their mouths. A statue obliges them once, at the goldsmith's, and then stands in the public gaze doing the remembering for them. The substitution of monument for conduct is the tragedy's last and driest joke about swearing. The play began with servants spending words they could not back, and it ends with princes of the city spending gold they can, and between those two economies stand the lovers, who spent the only currency that ever held its value in Verona: themselves, staked entire, on a vow they examined before they spoke it and kept after everyone else's words had failed.
