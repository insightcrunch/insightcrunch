The Transgender Persons Act 2019 contains, within a few pages of each other, two statements that cannot both be given full effect. Section 4 declares that a person recognised as transgender shall have a right to self-perceived gender identity. Section 7 then provides that the gender recorded against that person's name in official documents may be changed to male or female only after the person has undergone surgery and produced a certificate from the medical institution where the surgery was performed. The first statement locates the authority over a person's gender in the person. The second locates a decisive part of that authority in a hospital and a district office. Almost everything that has been said for and against this statute, in Parliament, in the press, in the petitions pending before the Supreme Court, and in the protests that met the Bill on the day it passed the Lok Sabha, is an argument about which of those two statements is the real one.

![The Transgender Persons Act 2019 and the certificate route it builds, showing the District Magistrate, the certificate of identity and the surgery condition for a revised certificate - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The statute is short. It has twenty-three sections arranged in nine chapters, and a reader can finish the bare text in under an hour. Its brevity is deceptive, because the Act reaches into nearly every register in which the state records a person: the birth certificate, the school roll, the ration card, the passport, the employment register, the hospital ward. Before this Act, Indian law had almost nothing to say about transgender persons as holders of rights, and what it had said historically was said in the language of criminal surveillance. The 2019 Act is the first central statute to define a transgender person, to prohibit discrimination against such a person across education, employment, healthcare, residence, movement and access to public life, to create a national institution for the community, and to make certain acts against a transgender person criminal offences in themselves. It does all of this while routing legal recognition through an application to a District Magistrate, and that routing, more than any single welfare provision, is what the argument about the Act is about.

This article is the statute pillar for the Act in this series. It states what the Act says, provision by provision and in the sequence the Act itself uses, because the sequence is the argument. It sets out the certificate machinery as enacted and as the Rules of 2020 operate it, since that machinery is the part of the Act a person actually meets. It states the welfare obligations, the duties of establishments, the composition and functions of the National Council for Transgender Persons, and the offence provision with its punishment range stated exactly. It then states, at full strength and in their proponents' own terms, the two readings of the statute that divide its defenders from the community it governs, and it records the amendment of 2026, which rewrote the definition, deleted the self-perceived identity clause and inserted a medical authority into the certification process, and which is itself under challenge. The judgment of 2014 that made this statute necessary is stated here only in its outcome, because a companion article owns it; the pillar's business is the statute.

## What stood in the statute book before the Transgender Persons Act 2019

For most of the period in which Indian law has existed in codified form, the transgender person appeared in it, when the person appeared at all, as an object of policing. The Criminal Tribes Act of 1871, in the part added for the registration of eunuchs, empowered local governments to keep registers of persons reasonably suspected of kidnapping or castrating children or of committing unnatural offences, and it restricted the civil capacities of those registered, including their ability to make gifts, wills and adoptions. The provision belonged to a colonial apparatus that treated entire communities as criminal by birth, and although the Criminal Tribes Act was repealed in 1952, the habits of surveillance it authorised survived in state police enactments and in the treatment of hijra communities under vagrancy and public nuisance provisions. The point matters for reading the 2019 Act: the statute was not written onto a blank page. It was written over a record in which the state's documentary attention to this population had been, for a century and a half, almost entirely penal.

The civil law, for its part, simply had no category. Marriage statutes, succession statutes, adoption law and the criminal law were all drafted on the assumption that every person is male or female, and the assumption was built into their operative words. A transgender person seeking to marry, to inherit as a son or a daughter, to be counted in a household, or to complain of a sexual offence found that the texts either did not contemplate the person or contemplated the person only under a gender the person did not claim. The Indian Penal Code's definition of rape, to take the instance that has carried the heaviest practical weight, is written around a man and a woman; the protection it gives against sexual violence was therefore not available, on the face of the text, to a transgender complainant at all. This is the gap that the brief for this article calls the position for minors and adults alike under the general criminal law, and it is the gap into which the offence provision of the 2019 Act was later inserted.

The change in the legal position came from the courts, and it came in a single judgment. In April 2014, in National Legal Services Authority v. Union of India, reported at (2014) 5 SCC 438, the Supreme Court held that transgender persons are entitled to recognition as a third gender for the purposes of the Constitution and the laws made under it; that a person's self-identified gender, male, female or third gender, is a matter of the person's own perception and not of medical certification, the Court stating expressly that insistence on sex reassignment surgery for recognition of gender identity is illegal; and that the Centre and the State Governments must treat transgender persons as socially and educationally backward classes and extend to them reservation in admission to educational institutions and in public appointments. The full account of that judgment, its reasoning and its directions is given in the companion article on [the NALSA judgment and transgender rights](/2017/10/01/nalsa-judgment-transgender-rights/), which owns the case in this series; the pillar records the outcome because the statute cannot be understood except as the legislature's answer to it. The judgment created rights by declaration and directed the state to build the machinery of inclusion. It did not itself create a certificate, a council, an offence or a welfare duty. Those required a statute, and the statute took five years and four bills to arrive.

Administrative practice had begun, in fragments, to move before the courts spoke. The Election Commission permitted electors who did not wish to register as male or female to enrol in the residual category of Others from 2009, so that the electoral roll became the first large state register to carry a third entry. The Census of 2011 likewise offered a category other than male and female and recorded about 4.88 lakh persons in it, a figure treated on all sides as an undercount, since disclosure to an enumerator carried risks that disclosure on a form did not remove. Tamil Nadu constituted a welfare board for transgender persons in 2008, the first state to build an institution of the kind, and Kerala adopted a state policy for transgender persons in 2015 that rested expressly on self-identification. These fragments matter to the pillar for a reason beyond colour. When Parliament came to legislate, it was not choosing between recognition and no recognition in the abstract. It was choosing the terms on which an existing, partial, administratively varied recognition would be unified, and the states that had moved first had unified it, where they moved, around the person's own declaration.

One further piece of the pre-statute record belongs here, because it shows what the executive's own advisers had concluded before the drafting choices were made. The Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment, the ministry responsible for the subject, constituted an expert committee whose report, submitted in January 2014, recommended that gender recognition rest on the person's self-identification, that a person be permitted to record the gender of the person's choice, and that no medical procedure be made a condition of recognition. That recommendation, made by the government's own committee in the same season as the judgment, is the benchmark against which the community measures the enacted text, and it is the first appearance of the divergence that runs through everything that follows: the principle of self-identification was accepted at every stage of the official record, and the machinery enacted to deliver it was, at every stage, something else.

## Four bills and a standing committee: how the Act reached the statute book

The legislative history matters in this pillar for an unusual reason. For most statutes, the passage record explains details. For this statute, the passage record is the evidence in the central dispute, because the critics' case is that the enacted text retreats from positions that Parliament's own processes had already endorsed. The sequence therefore has to be stated with care.

The first bill was a private member's bill. The Rights of Transgender Persons Bill, 2014, moved in the Rajya Sabha by Tiruchi Siva of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, was passed by that House on 24 April 2015. A private member's bill passing any House is a rare event in Indian parliamentary practice; this one passed the Rajya Sabha unanimously, and it provided for recognition of a person's gender as the person perceived it, for national and state commissions, and for reservation. The bill was transmitted to the Lok Sabha, where it was never taken up, and it lapsed. The government, rather than adopt it, published its own draft. The Ministry placed a draft Rights of Transgender Persons Bill on its website in December 2015 for public comment; that draft retained much of the private member's architecture but dropped the commissions and the special courts the earlier bill had contemplated.

The government bill that followed departed sharply from both predecessors. The Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Bill, 2016, introduced in the Lok Sabha in August 2016, defined a transgender person in biological terms, as a person who is neither wholly female nor wholly male, or a combination of female and male, or neither female nor male. It proposed that identity be certified not on the person's application alone but through a District Screening Committee, a body of five that was to include a Chief Medical Officer, a psychiatrist or psychologist, a district welfare officer, a representative of the transgender community and a government officer. It criminalised compelling or enticing a transgender person to beg. Each of these choices drew immediate and organised objection from transgender persons and organisations, on the grounds that the definition described a body rather than an identity, that a screening committee converted self-identification into an examination, and that a begging offence would be turned against a community for whom traditional alms-seeking had been a livelihood and a ritual role. The bill was referred to the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Social Justice and Empowerment.

The Standing Committee's report is the hinge of the whole history. The committee, chaired by Ramesh Bais, presented its report on the 2016 Bill in July 2017. It recommended that the definition be redrafted to rest on self-identification; that a transgender person have the option to identify as man, woman or transgender, and to exercise that option independent of surgery or hormone therapy; that discrimination be defined and a grievance mechanism provided; that reservation be extended to transgender persons as the 2014 judgment had directed; and that rights in marriage, partnership, divorce and adoption be recognised as governed by the person's other applicable law. The report was, in substance, a recommendation to bring the bill back into line with the judgment and with the expert committee's advice of 2014. The enacted statute adopted the committee's definitional direction in part and its institutional recommendations in part, and it did not adopt the recommendations on self-identification independent of certification, on reservation, or on the option of recording male or female without surgery. That pattern, adoption in part, is the factual core of the later argument, and it is not in dispute between the sides. What is in dispute is what the pattern means.

The movement between the bill of 2016 and the Act of 2019 is worth isolating, because it shows the process working in both directions at once. Three of the 2016 Bill's most criticised features disappeared. The biological definition was replaced by the mismatch formula of Section 2(k), which follows identity rather than anatomy. The District Screening Committee, the five-member body that would have examined applicants, was dropped, and certification was placed on application to the District Magistrate alone. The offence of compelling or enticing a transgender person to beg was removed. Two other features survived every round of criticism intact. The certificate remained the gateway to recorded identity, so that recognition continued to run through an administrative act rather than resting on declaration; and the revised certificate for male or female remained tied to surgery, the condition the Standing Committee had recommended abandoning. A reader who wants the shortest accurate account of the drafting history can hold it in that form: the enacted Act conceded the definition, the screening committee and the begging offence, and conceded nothing on certification or surgery, which were the two points on which the community's case and the judgment's logic principally turned.

The 2016 Bill was not passed. A successor bill was passed by the Lok Sabha in December 2018, with amendments that removed the begging offence and revised the definition, but it lapsed when the Sixteenth Lok Sabha was dissolved. After the general election, the government introduced the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Bill, 2019 in the Lok Sabha, which passed it on 5 August 2019; the Rajya Sabha passed the bill on 26 November 2019 by voice vote, a motion to refer it to a select committee having been defeated, and the President assented on 5 December 2019. The Act, numbered 40 of 2019, was brought into force on 10 January 2020 by notification S.O. 135(E). The day the Lok Sabha passed the bill was described by community organisations protesting it as a day of mourning for gender justice, a description recorded here because it belongs to the passage record: the statute began its life opposed, in terms, by a large and organised part of the population it was enacted to protect. No previous article in this series has had to record that of a welfare statute, and the fact shapes how the Act has been read ever since.

## The architecture of the Act: nine chapters and one short text

The Act is arranged in nine chapters, and the arrangement itself tells the reader where the weight lies. Chapter One contains the short title, extent and commencement in Section 1 and the definitions in Section 2. Chapter Two is a single section, Section 3, containing the prohibition of discrimination. Chapter Three, Sections 4 to 7, deals with recognition of identity, and it is the chapter on which the statute stands or falls. Chapter Four is again a single section, Section 8, containing the obligations of the appropriate Government to take welfare measures. Chapter Five, Sections 9 to 12, imposes obligations on establishments and other persons: non-discrimination in employment, compliance by establishments, the grievance mechanism, and the right of residence. Chapter Six, Sections 13 to 15, covers education, social security and health. Chapter Seven, Sections 16 and 17, creates the National Council for Transgender Persons and states its functions. Chapter Eight is Section 18, the offences and penalties. Chapter Nine, Sections 19 to 23, is miscellaneous: grants by the Central Government to the Council, the provision that the Act is in addition to and not in derogation of other laws, protection for action taken in good faith, the rule-making power, and the power to remove difficulties within two years of commencement.

Three structural features deserve notice before the provisions are taken in turn. The first is how much of the Act is cast in the language of obligation without a stated consequence. The discrimination prohibition in Section 3 is addressed to every person and every establishment, yet the Act attaches no penalty to a breach of Section 3 as such; the criminal provision, Section 18, covers a different and narrower set of acts. The welfare provisions are likewise framed as duties of the appropriate Government, a term the Act defines to mean the Central Government for its own establishments and the State Government for state establishments and local authorities, but the duties are programmatic in form: the Government shall take steps, shall formulate schemes, shall take measures. A reader who comes to this Act from the rights-and-remedies end will therefore find a statute whose prohibitions are broad and whose enforcement machinery is thin, and much of the commentary on the Act is an argument about whether that thinness is a drafting failure or the ordinary character of a first-generation welfare statute.

The second feature is the Act's reliance on delegated legislation. The certificate procedure, the form and manner of applications, the period within which certificates must issue, the facilities establishments must provide, and the welfare measures themselves are all left to be prescribed by rules. The parent Act therefore cannot be applied at all without the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Rules, 2020, notified in September 2020 under Section 22, and a pillar that stopped at the parent text would misdescribe the law in force. The Rules are treated in this article as part of the statute's machinery wherever the machinery is described.

A fourth feature is the commencement and rule-making design, which is conventional in form and consequential in effect. Section 1 left commencement to notification, and the whole Act was brought into force at once, on 10 January 2020, rather than in stages; there is therefore no commencement puzzle of the kind that complicates several statutes in this series, and every provision discussed in this article has been operative law from that date until the amendment enacted in 2026, which carries its own appointed-day commencement. The rule-making power in Section 22 is given to the appropriate Government, so both the Centre and the States may make rules, with Central rules laid before Parliament and State rules laid before the State Legislature; the operative national procedure is the Centre's Rules of 2020. Section 23 gave the Central Government two years from commencement to remove difficulties by order, a window that closed in January 2022 without any order under the section becoming a significant part of the Act's story.

The fifth feature is the definition of the operative term, establishment, which determines how far the employment and grievance provisions reach.

### Who counts as an establishment under the Act?

An establishment under Section 2 of the Act means any body or authority established by or under a Central or State Act, any authority or body owned, controlled or aided by the Government, a local authority, a Government company, and equally any company, body corporate, association, firm, cooperative society, trust, agency or institution.

The definition is deliberately exhaustive: a private employer of any size is within it, and the duties of non-discrimination in employment, of compliance, and of designating a complaint officer fall on the corner firm and the multinational alike, with no threshold of employee numbers anywhere in the Act or the Rules.

The breadth of that definition is the reason the employment chapter has practical reach beyond the public sector. Statutes in this field frequently confine their duties to the state and its instrumentalities, leaving private employment to the general law. This Act does not. When Section 9 provides that no establishment shall discriminate against a transgender person in any matter relating to employment, including recruitment and promotion, the prohibition runs against every private employer in the country. When Section 11 requires every establishment to designate a complaint officer, the requirement runs against the same universe. The community's complaint about the employment chapter is not about its coverage, which is wide, but about the absence of any specified consequence for an establishment that ignores it, a point taken up in the assessment later in this article.

## The definitions that carry the Act: Section 2 read as a whole

Section 2 contains eleven clauses, and four of them do structural work beyond the definition of the transgender person itself. Appropriate Government is defined relationally: the Central Government for the Centre and its establishments, the State Government for a State and its establishments and local authorities. Nearly every operative duty in the Act is laid on the appropriate Government or on establishments, so this single definition distributes the statute's obligations across the federation and determines, for any given school, hospital or office, which government answers for it. Establishment, analysed in its own subsection below, fixes the reach of the employment and grievance chapters. Family, defined as a group of people related by blood or marriage or by adoption made in accordance with law, fixes the boundary of the residence rights in Section 12, with the consequences examined there. Institution is defined as an institution, public or private, for the reception, care, protection, education, training or any other service of transgender persons, a definition that connects the Act to the shelter and rehabilitation infrastructure, the Garima Greh homes among them, through which a part of its welfare chapter is delivered. A reader who masters these four definitions and the definition of the transgender person can predict the operation of nearly every section that follows, which is the mark of a tightly, if shortly, drafted definitional chapter.

## The definition: who the Act takes a transgender person to be

Section 2(k) defines a transgender person as a person whose gender does not match with the gender assigned to that person at birth. The definition then includes, expressly, a trans-man or trans-woman, whether or not such person has undergone sex reassignment surgery or hormone therapy or laser therapy or such other therapy; a person with intersex variations; a genderqueer person; and a person having such socio-cultural identities as kinner, hijra, aravani and jogta. Section 2(i) separately defines a person with intersex variations as a person who at birth shows variation in primary sexual characteristics, external genitalia, chromosomes or hormones from the normative standard of male or female body.

Four things in this definition repay attention. First, its organising idea is a mismatch between gender and the gender assigned at birth. The definition is therefore framed around the person's gender, which the person experiences, rather than around the person's body, which was the approach of the 2016 Bill and the approach the Standing Committee had rejected. In that respect the enacted definition followed the committee. Second, the express statement that a trans-man or trans-woman is within the definition whether or not the person has undergone surgery or hormone or laser therapy does real work: it severs inclusion in the protected class from any medical history. A person does not enter the Act's protection by treatment and does not leave it by declining treatment. Third, the inclusion of socio-cultural identities by name, kinner, hijra, aravani and jogta, writes communities with long social histories into the statutory text; these are identities organised around community, ritual role and kinship structures as much as around individual gender, and the definition takes them as it finds them. Fourth, the inclusion of persons with intersex variations within the transgender definition has been objected to by intersex persons and organisations on the ground that an intersex variation is a matter of sex characteristics, not of gender identity, and that many persons with intersex variations identify with the gender assigned at birth; the enacted text places them within the definition all the same, and the objection is part of the record on the definition's design.

The definition also fixes the boundary disputes that the rest of the Act inherits. Because recognition under Chapter Three is recognition as a transgender person, the definition determines who may apply for the certificate at all. Because the offences in Section 18 are offences against a transgender person, the definition determines who may complain of them. And because the definition says nothing about sexual orientation, a matter the 2014 judgment had also kept distinct, the Act's protection runs to gender identity and not to orientation, a boundary that becomes important in the amendment of 2026, which wrote an express exclusion into the substituted definition. As enacted in 2019, however, the definition is the most capacious part of the statute: broad in its organising idea, explicit that medical treatment is irrelevant to membership of the class, and specific in naming the communities it was written for. The contraction, in the critics' account, begins not with who is covered but with how coverage is proved.

The definition has one further consequence that the later argument makes important. It includes the genderqueer person, whose identity is neither male nor female, within the same statutory class as the trans-man and the trans-woman, whose identities are male and female. The certificate machinery of Chapter Three then treats the whole class identically at the first step, recording every member's gender as transgender, and differentiates only at the second step, where the trans-man and trans-woman may exit into a binary marker through surgery and the genderqueer person, by definition, has no binary marker to exit into. A definition that gathers identities pointing in different directions, some toward the binary and some away from it, and a machinery that offers a single first record for all of them, together produce one of the Act's quiet structural effects: the state records as a single category a population whose members' relationships to that category are not the same. The petitions against the amendment of 2026 return to this ground from the opposite direction, complaining that the substituted definition abandons the mismatch idea altogether and recognises only listed identities and verified variations, so that persons whose claim rests on self-perception, including many within the 2019 definition, fall outside it.

## Recognition: Sections 4 to 7 and the sequence that decides the argument

Chapter Three must be read in its own order, because each section qualifies the one before it.

Section 4 is headed recognition of identity of transgender person. Sub-section (1) provides that a transgender person shall have a right to be recognised as such, in accordance with the provisions of this Act. Sub-section (2) provides that a person recognised as transgender under sub-section (1) shall have a right to self-perceived gender identity. The wording deserves the closest attention of any in the statute. The right to be recognised is granted, but it is granted in accordance with the provisions of the Act, which is to say through the machinery the Act itself creates; and the right to self-perceived gender identity is granted to a person recognised as transgender under sub-section (1), which ties the self-perception right, textually, to the completed act of recognition. Read one way, Section 4 states the principle and the following sections merely administer it. Read the other way, Section 4 makes the principle conditional on the machinery. The two readings of this single section are, in miniature, the whole litigation about the Act, and the pillar does not resolve them; it notes only that the text was available to be drafted the other way, as the Standing Committee had recommended, with self-identification stated first and certification following as a record of it.

Section 5 provides that a transgender person may make an application to the District Magistrate for issuing a certificate of identity as a transgender person, in such form and manner and accompanied with such documents as may be prescribed. A proviso states that in the case of a minor child, the application shall be made by a parent or guardian of the child. Section 6 then provides that the District Magistrate shall issue to the applicant a certificate of identity as a transgender person, after following such procedure and in such form and manner and within such time as may be prescribed, indicating the gender of such person as transgender. Sub-section (2) of Section 6 provides that the gender of a transgender person shall be recorded in all official documents in accordance with the certificate issued. Sub-section (3) provides that a certificate issued under the section shall confer rights and be a proof of recognition of identity as a transgender person.

Section 7 is headed change in gender, and it is the provision at the centre of the controversy. It provides that after the issue of a certificate of identity, if a transgender person undergoes surgery to change gender either as a male or female, such person may make an application, along with a certificate issued to that effect by the Medical Superintendent or Chief Medical Officer of the medical institution in which that person has undergone surgery, to the District Magistrate for a revised certificate. The District Magistrate shall, on receipt of the application along with the medical certificate, and on being satisfied with the correctness of such certificate, issue a certificate indicating the change in gender. Sub-section (3) provides that the person who has been issued a certificate of identity or a revised certificate shall be entitled to change the first name in the birth certificate and in all other official documents relating to the identity of such person, and a proviso states that the change in gender and the issue of a revised certificate shall not affect the rights and entitlements of such person under the Act.

The sequence, stated without characterisation, is this. Identity as transgender is recognised through a certificate issued by a District Magistrate on application. The certificate fixes the gender recorded in all official documents as transgender. A person who wishes the documents to record male or female may obtain a revised certificate, and the gateway to the revised certificate is surgery, evidenced by a certificate from the Medical Superintendent or Chief Medical Officer of the institution where the surgery was performed, whose correctness the District Magistrate must be satisfied of before issuing the revision. Hormone therapy, which the definition in Section 2(k) treats as sufficient to leave a person within the class without surgery, is not a gateway to the revised certificate; nor is any period of lived experience in the gender; nor is the person's own declaration. The revised certificate, once issued, carries the entitlement to change the first name across the birth certificate and all other official documents, and the entitlements held under the Act are preserved by the proviso.

Set against the judgment the Act implements, the contrast is exact, and it is the contrast the petitions plead. The Supreme Court in 2014 had held that a person may identify as male, female or third gender on the basis of self-perception, and that insistence on surgery as a condition of recognition is unlawful. The statute gives self-perception as the stated principle in Section 4(2), gives recognition as transgender through an administrative certificate in Sections 5 and 6, and reserves recognition as male or female, which is what many transgender men and women seek, to those who have undergone surgery and can prove it to a District Magistrate's satisfaction. Whether that structure is a reasonable administrative implementation of the judgment or a contradiction of it is the question the courts have been asked and have not answered. The pillar's task is to make the structure impossible to misunderstand, and the table that follows is the article's findable artifact for that purpose: the four steps of the recognition route, with what each step requires.

The table discloses the design decision that the prose analysis keeps returning to. At step 3, the state's instrument is the applicant's own affidavit, and the 2020 Rules forbid the medical examination that the 2016 Bill's screening committees would have conducted; the certificate as transgender is, in its evidentiary demand, close to self-declaration recorded by the state. At step 4, the evidentiary demand changes character entirely: the fact to be proved is a surgical event, proved by an institution, to the satisfaction of an administrator. A statute that trusted the person's word for the third gender did not trust it for the first two. That asymmetry, and not any dispute about what the sections say, is what the phrase self-identification with a certificate, the namable claim of this article, is intended to capture.

Two points of construction in Chapter Three deserve record before the machinery is described in operation. The first is the open texture of Section 6(3). The certificate, it says, shall confer rights and be a proof of recognition of identity as a transgender person. The Act nowhere enumerates the rights the certificate confers, beyond the recording of gender in official documents under Section 6(2) and the entitlements that other provisions attach to transgender persons generally. The certificate is therefore best understood as an evidentiary key rather than a source of rights: the rights are held by the person as a transgender person, and the certificate is the state's own proof of the status that makes them administrable. That reading matters in disputes, because an authority that treats the absence of a certificate as the absence of the underlying rights reverses the section's logic; the certificate proves recognition, and Section 4 vests the right to be recognised, so the document is the record of a status and not its origin. The community's objection, it will be seen, accepts this analysis and draws the opposite institutional conclusion from it: if the certificate merely records a status the person already holds, the case for interposing an administrator, and later a medical board, between the person and the record is weaker, not stronger.

The second point is the standard Section 7 sets for the District Magistrate on the revised certificate. The Magistrate issues it on being satisfied with the correctness of the medical certificate. Correctness is not defined, and the design of the provision, as the ministry's own guidance to district authorities under the Rules reflects, contemplates verification with the issuing institution where doubt arises: the Magistrate's satisfaction is an authenticity check on a document, not a fresh medical judgment, and the provision gives the Magistrate no power to examine the applicant or to second-guess the surgery's adequacy. Even so confined, the standard inserts an administrative satisfaction between the medical fact and the legal record, and it is the only place in the Act where an officer's satisfaction is the operative condition of a person's recorded gender. The challenges fasten on exactly that insertion.

## The certificate in practice: the Rules of 2020 and the National Portal

The Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Rules, 2020, notified in September 2020 in exercise of the power under Section 22 of the Act, supply the procedure the parent Act left to prescription, and in one respect they soften the parent Act's design. Rule 4 governs the application: the applicant applies to the District Magistrate in Form-1, supported by an affidavit in Form-2, and the Rules state the procedure on the basis of the affidavit without requiring any medical examination for the certificate of identity as transgender. Rule 5 requires the District Magistrate to issue the certificate of identity in Form-3 within thirty days of receipt of a duly filled application with the affidavit, and to issue at the same time a transgender identity card in Form-5. The certificate is then the basis on which the name, gender and photograph of the person are to be changed in official documents; the Rules list the documents in an annexure, and the authority that issued a document is to make the change within fifteen days of application, keeping the same serial or reference number as the original document, with benefits held under any earlier state-issued identity card preserved. Rule 6 and Rule 7 govern the revised certificate: the application for change of gender to male or female is accompanied by the certificate of the medical institution, and the District Magistrate is to issue the revised certificate and revised identity card within fifteen days. Rule 8 requires the District Magistrate, on rejecting an application, to inform the applicant of the reasons within thirty days, and empowers the Magistrate to review the rejection on the basis of the applicant's reply within sixty days. Rule 9 gives the applicant a right of appeal, within ninety days of the intimation of rejection, to an appellate authority designated by the appropriate Government by notification.

The remaining Rules build the administrative surround. Rule 10 requires the appropriate Government to constitute a welfare board for transgender persons. Rule 11 requires a Transgender Protection Cell under the charge of the District Magistrate at the district level and under the Director General of Police at the state level, to monitor offences against transgender persons and to ensure timely registration, investigation and prosecution. Rule 12 requires establishments to frame and publish an equal opportunity policy for transgender persons. Rule 13 requires every establishment to designate a complaint officer under Section 11 within thirty days of the Rules taking effect, and requires the complaint officer to enquire into a complaint within fifteen days and the head of the establishment to take action within thirty days of the complaint. The Rules also direct governments to review existing schemes across education, social security, health, vocational training and self-employment, and to provide for facilities such as separate wards and washrooms in establishments within two years.

Alongside the Rules, the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment launched a National Portal for Transgender Persons on 25 November 2020, through which an application for the certificate and identity card may be filed digitally from anywhere in the country, tracked by the applicant, and answered by the issuing authority within the timelines the Rules prescribe, with a grievance facility for delay or rejection. The portal converted the certificate from an office visit into a tracked transaction, and ministerial statements at its launch presented that conversion as the practical meaning of self-perceived identity under the Act: the applicant applies on the applicant's own declaration and does not attend for examination. The description in this article states the general position under the Act and the Rules and is not advice on an individual application; a person whose application is rejected should take advice promptly, because the appeal period in the Rules runs from the date on which the rejection is communicated and is unforgiving of delay. An applicant is also well served by keeping the complete paper trail of the application together, the affidavit, the portal acknowledgment, the certificate and the correspondence on every document change, since each later correction is proved from the earlier one; many applicants [keep the affidavit, the application acknowledgment and every certificate copy together in a private encrypted file](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) for exactly that reason.

One transitional provision in the Rules deserves notice, because it answers the first question many early applicants asked. Several states had issued identity cards or certificates to transgender persons under their own schemes before the Act, Tamil Nadu's welfare board being the oldest such machinery, and a holder of such a card needed to know whether the new certificate displaced it and whether benefits drawn on the old card survived. Rule 5 answers both points. The certificate of identity issued under the Rules becomes the basis for changing name, gender and photograph across official documents, and a proviso to the rule preserves the position expressly: all benefits that a transgender person was entitled to on the basis of an identity card issued by a State authority continue to be enjoyed on the basis of the certificate issued under the Rules. The design is a continuity design, under which the national certificate absorbs the state record rather than annulling it, and documents corrected on its basis keep the original serial or reference number for the same reason. The provision matters beyond its immediate purpose for the light it throws on the 2026 dispute: the statutory scheme of 2019 and 2020 was built, at each joint, to carry recognition forward once granted, which is why a later definition that casts doubt on recognition already granted has drawn the particular challenge the Biswas petition makes.

The honest implementation picture is one of a machinery that exists and functions, at a scale well below the population it was built for. The 2011 Census recorded about 4.88 lakh persons, roughly 4.9 lakh, in the residual gender category it offered, a figure community organisations have always treated as an undercount produced by concealment and by the design of the enumeration itself. Certificates issued through the portal and the district offices are counted on the portal's public dashboard, and the pattern those counters show, period after period, is of certificates numbering a small fraction of even the Census figure, with wide variation between states and districts. The reasons given in the reporting on the gap are practical rather than mysterious: District Magistrates' offices that had not designated staff for the work, applicants asked for documents the Rules do not require, applicants without the digital access the portal assumes, and persons unwilling to enter a state record of their identity at all. The last reason connects the implementation gap back to the design argument. A certificate that is the key to welfare is also a register entry, and a part of the community the Act addresses has historical reasons, reaching back to the registers of the Criminal Tribes Act period, to decline registration. Whether that reluctance is a legacy to be overcome by good administration or a rational response to the design is one of the points on which the two readings of the Act divide, and it is stated here because the implementation figures cannot be understood without it.

## An application traced end to end: what the process asks of a person

The machinery reads differently from inside it, and a pillar that serves the person navigating the process should walk it once in narrative form. An applicant begins with a decision that has no legal form: to seek the certificate at all. The first formal act is the application in Form-1 to the District Magistrate of the district where the applicant resides, filed at the district office or uploaded through the National Portal. With it goes the affidavit in Form-2, in which the applicant declares the applicant's gender identity and residence. The affidavit is the evidentiary heart of the first certificate: there is no medical certificate to obtain, no board to appear before, no witness requirement stated in the Rules, and a District Magistrate's office that demands any of these is demanding what the Rules do not. The applicant who files through the portal receives an acknowledgment and can track the application; the applicant who files in person depends on the office's receipt practice, which is one of the points at which the reported experience of applicants diverges most sharply from the prescribed design.

The thirty days of Rule 5 then run. At their end, in the design, the certificate in Form-3 and the identity card in Form-5 issue, recording the holder's gender as transgender. The certificate's work begins after its issue, because its purpose is to move through the holder's other documents. The holder applies, on the basis of the certificate, to each issuing authority for the correction of name, gender and photograph in the documents the Rules list, and each authority is to make the change within fifteen days, preserving the document's original serial number so that the record remains continuous. In practice this is a sequence of separate applications to separate offices, the birth register, the school or university record, the Aadhaar enrolment, the electoral roll, the bank, the passport office, and the friction of the process concentrates here rather than at the certificate itself, because each office applies the certificate through its own forms and its own habits. The person who later seeks the revised certificate repeats the pattern one level up: surgery in an institution able to perform and certify it, the certificate of the Medical Superintendent or Chief Medical Officer, a fresh application, fifteen days, and then a second circuit of the same document offices with the revised certificate. The two circuits, the first resting on the person's sworn word and the second on an institution's certificate, are the lived form of the asymmetry the artifact table sets out, and they explain why the certificate process, rather than any welfare provision, dominates the practical literature and the grievance reporting on this Act.

## The prohibition of discrimination: Section 3 and its nine grounds

Section 3 provides that no person or establishment shall discriminate against a transgender person on any of nine grounds, which are best understood as nine fields of life in which specified treatment is forbidden. The forbidden treatment is, in each case, the denial or discontinuation of, or unfair treatment in, the field named. The fields are educational establishments and services; employment or occupation, both in unfair treatment and in denial or termination; healthcare services; access to and enjoyment of any goods, accommodation, service, facility, benefit, privilege or opportunity dedicated to the use of the general public or customarily available to the public; the right of movement; the right to reside, purchase, rent or otherwise occupy any property; the opportunity to stand for or hold public or private office; and access to, or removal from, or unfair treatment in, any Government or private establishment in whose care or custody a transgender person may be.

The construction of the section gives it both its strength and its weakness. Its strength is the addressee: no person or establishment. The prohibition is not confined to the state, and it is not confined to employers or service providers in a commercial relationship with the complainant. A landlord refusing a tenancy, a hospital turning a patient away, a school denying admission, a housing society obstructing a purchase, an official body removing a person from its custody on discriminatory grounds, each falls within the words. Its weakness is the remedy. Section 3 declares the prohibition and stops. It does not say what follows from a breach: no compensation, no penalty, no designated forum, no burden of proof rule, no time limit within which a complaint must be decided. The Act's own grievance mechanisms sit elsewhere, in the complaint officer for employment matters under Section 11 and in the National Council's power to redress grievances under Section 17, and the criminal provision in Section 18 covers four specific acts rather than discrimination generally. A person discriminated against in education or housing or healthcare therefore holds a statutory right whose enforcement must be assembled from other instruments: the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts and the Supreme Court, the general law where it happens to apply, the complaint routes of the sector concerned, and the political and administrative pressure that a declared prohibition can support. The community's criticism of Section 3 is not that it is narrow, for it is not, but that it is a prohibition without a sanction, and the defender's answer is that a declared norm of this breadth changes official behaviour and gives courts a text to enforce even before a remedial chapter is built. Both observations are about the same text, and both are accurate descriptions of it.

Two of the nine grounds repay a slower reading, because each reaches a setting that general accounts pass over. The last ground, denial of access to, removal from, or unfair treatment in a Government or private establishment in whose care or custody a transgender person may be, is the custodial ground. It speaks to the person held in a prison, a protective home, a shelter, a hospital ward or a state institution, the settings in which the person has the least practical ability to leave and in which classification decisions, which ward, which block, which register, are taken by the custodian. By naming care and custody establishments expressly, the Act gives a person in such a setting a statutory text addressed to the treatment most likely to define the person's daily life, and it gives inspecting bodies and courts a standard against which custodial practice can be measured. The education ground, the first in the list, operates at the opposite end of life: it covers not only refusal of admission but discontinuation and unfair treatment within services, which reaches the enrolled student pushed out by harassment or by administrative refusal to record the student's name and gender. Read together, the nine grounds form a deliberate map of the documented sites of exclusion, household, school, workplace, clinic, street, property market, public office and institution, and the section's drafting is best understood as that map converted into law.

The section also has a constitutional relationship worth stating. Articles 15 and 16 of the Constitution forbid state discrimination on grounds including sex, and the 2014 judgment read those provisions with the breadth the community's case required; but constitutional rights of that kind run, in the main, against the state. Section 3 is a statutory prohibition running against every person, and its significance lies partly in that horizontal reach: it supplies, in ordinary legislation, the private-sector and interpersonal prohibition that the constitutional text does not directly give. Where a writ court is asked to enforce it against a private party, the claim will typically travel through the state's own duty and the court's constitutional powers rather than through any machinery of the Act, which is one more instance of the pattern this pillar keeps recording: the Act declares in the widest terms and equips sparingly. The declaration still does work that should not be discounted. Official forms, institutional policies and judicial reasoning all cite Section 3 as the statement of the norm, and a norm stated in a central statute is available in every negotiation, inspection and hearing in the field, including the many that never approach a court.

## Work: Sections 9 to 11 and the complaint officer

Chapter Five converts part of the general prohibition into specific duties for the world of work. Section 9 provides that no establishment shall discriminate against any transgender person in any matter relating to employment, including recruitment and promotion and other related issues. Section 10 provides that every establishment shall ensure compliance with the provisions of the Act and provide such facilities to transgender persons as may be prescribed, the prescription being supplied by the Rules' requirement of an equal opportunity policy and of facilities such as separate washrooms. Section 11 provides that every establishment shall designate a person to be a complaint officer to deal with complaints relating to violation of the provisions of the Act, and the Rules add the timelines: designation within thirty days of the Rules taking effect, enquiry into a complaint within fifteen days, and action by the head of the establishment within thirty days of the complaint.

The complaint officer is the Act's most concrete enforcement device, and the one most relevant to employers, who are also among this article's intended readers. Its model is familiar from other Indian workplace statutes: an internal officer, named in advance, to whom a complaint goes first, with short timelines meant to produce a remedy inside the institution rather than a case outside it. Its limits are the limits of that model. The Act does not prescribe the complaint officer's seniority, independence or training; it does not state the relief the officer or the head of establishment may grant; it does not provide an appeal from the establishment to any outside body; and it attaches no consequence to an establishment that designates no officer at all, which the reporting on compliance suggests is a common position among smaller employers who have never heard of the requirement. The provision is therefore best understood as a floor of internal process that a well-advised establishment treats as the beginning of compliance rather than its whole content. An establishment that designates the officer, publishes the equal opportunity policy the Rules require, records complaints and acts on them within the timelines has done what the statute asks; the statute does not ask, in terms, for anything more, and the gap between that ask and the discrimination Section 3 forbids is a fair measure of the Act's enforcement philosophy in the employment field.

The equal opportunity policy that Rule 12 requires deserves a sentence of its own, because it is the point at which the Act enters the internal documents of every establishment. The policy is to address equal opportunity for transgender persons across the establishment's functioning, including infrastructure adjustments, recruitment, employment benefits and promotion, and it is to be published and displayed. For a large employer, the policy sits alongside the equal opportunity and prevention-of-harassment frameworks that other statutes have made familiar, and compliance is a drafting and training exercise. For the small establishment, which is most establishments, the requirement typically arrives, if it arrives at all, as a surprise discovered in an inspection or a dispute. The reporting on the Act's employment chapter returns repeatedly to this asymmetry between the statute's universal address and the regulated population's awareness of it, and it is the practical reason the complaint officer and the policy, though modest instruments, were worth the legislature's attention: they convert a prohibition addressed to everyone into two named artefacts, an officer and a document, whose absence is easy to establish.

## Home: the right of residence in Section 12

Section 12 addresses the setting in which, on the community's evidence, rejection most often begins. Sub-section (1) provides that no child shall be separated from parents or immediate family on the ground of being a transgender child, except on an order of a competent court in the interest of such child. Sub-section (2) gives every transgender person a right to reside in the household where the parents or immediate family members reside, a right not to be excluded from that household or any part of it, and a right to enjoy and use the facilities of the household in a non-discriminatory manner. Sub-section (3) provides that where any parent or member of the immediate family is unable to take care of a transgender person, the competent court shall by order direct that the person be placed in a rehabilitation centre.

The provision protects two different things and exposes one omission, and all three points are live in the argument about the Act. What it protects first is the child against removal: neither the state nor any private party may separate a child from the family on the ground of the child's transgender identity, and only a court, acting in the child's interest, may order separation. What it protects second is the adult's place in the natal household: the right to reside, not to be excluded, and to use the household's facilities without discrimination is a statutory answer to the pattern, described in every account of transgender lives in India, of a young person driven from the family home on disclosure of identity. The omission is the definition of family itself. Section 2(c) defines family as a group of people related by blood or marriage or by adoption made in accordance with law. The hijra gharana, the household of a guru and disciples that has been for generations the institution that receives those whom natal families expel, is not a family within the definition, and the Act's residence rights and protections therefore do not extend to it as a household; nor does the Act recognise any right to reside in a chosen family as such. Community organisations made this point at every stage of the bill process, in terms: the statute protects a person's place in the family the person was born into and is silent about the family that, in the lived pattern, actually shelters the person. The rehabilitation centre in sub-section (3) sharpens the point for the critics, who read a court-ordered placement in an institution as the state's answer to family failure, where the community's own answer, the gharana, receives no recognition at all. Defenders of the text answer that a statute can protect rights in the natal household without legislating for every form of association, and that the gharana's absence from the definition does not prohibit it; it merely leaves it where the general law leaves voluntary associations. The provision and the criticism are both stated here as they stand, because Section 12 is the clause of the Act that most directly touches daily life.

The placement power in sub-section (3) turns on a standard the Act does not define: a parent or family member unable to take care of the transgender person. Unable is not glossed by reference to means, willingness or conduct, and the assessment is the competent court's, made by order. The provision's supporters read it as a child-protection and welfare jurisdiction of a familiar kind, the same court's-order structure that governs placements elsewhere in family and juvenile law, and note that it operates only on inability, not on disapproval of the person's identity, which sub-section (1) expressly excludes as a ground of separation for a child. Its critics read the same words against the documented pattern, in which inability is asserted by families whose objection is to the identity itself, and ask what enquiry a court will make before converting a family's rejection into an institutional placement, and why the receiving institution is a rehabilitation centre rather than the community households that perform this function in practice. The Act answers none of these questions in terms, and the case law on the provision is too thin to answer them from authority; the pillar records the standard's openness because Section 12 will be litigated, when it is litigated, exactly there.

## Welfare, education, livelihood and health: Sections 8 and 13 to 15

The welfare obligations of the state are distributed across the Act rather than gathered in one chapter, and a reader assembling them finds four provisions. Section 8, the whole of Chapter Four, obliges the appropriate Government to take steps to secure the full and effective participation of transgender persons and their inclusion in society; to take such welfare measures as may be prescribed to protect their rights and interests and facilitate their access to welfare schemes framed by that Government; to formulate welfare schemes and programmes which are transgender-sensitive, non-stigmatising and non-discriminatory; to take steps for the rescue, protection and rehabilitation of transgender persons; and to take appropriate measures to promote and protect their right to participate in cultural and recreational activities. Section 13 obliges every educational institution funded or recognised by the appropriate Government to provide inclusive education and opportunities for sports, recreation and leisure to transgender persons without discrimination and on an equal basis with others. Section 14 obliges the appropriate Government to formulate welfare schemes and programmes to facilitate and support the livelihood of transgender persons, including vocational training and self-employment. Section 15, the healthcare provision, is the most detailed of the four and is set out separately below.

These provisions belong to the same family as the welfare chapters of the other statutes in the social justice cluster, and the field as a whole is mapped in the guide to [welfare legislation in India](/2017/07/01/welfare-laws-india-complete-guide/), to which this pillar links for the comparative picture. The character of the obligations is uniform: they are duties to formulate, to take steps and to facilitate, owed by the appropriate Government, and their content in any state at any time is the content of the schemes that government has in fact framed. The Union Ministry's own instruments illustrate the kind: the National Portal and the Garima Greh shelter homes, of which twelve pilot homes were begun with full financial assistance to community-based organisations across nine states, and the sub-scheme for the rehabilitation of transgender persons within the SMILE scheme, Support for Marginalised Individuals for Livelihood and Enterprise, approved by the Standing Finance Committee in November 2021, covering rehabilitation, medical facilities and intervention, counselling, education, skill development and economic linkages. None of these instruments is named in the Act. Each is an exercise of the Act's welfare power, and each could be varied or wound up without amending a line of the statute. That is the standing tension of programmatic welfare drafting, and the Act exhibits it in a pure form: the rights in Chapter Three are individual and justiciable in character, however contested their machinery, while the welfare in Chapters Four and Six is real only to the extent that governments of the day choose to build under it.

One phrase in Section 8 has attracted criticism of a different register from the rest, and a pillar should record it. Sub-section (4) obliges the appropriate Government to take steps for the rescue, protection and rehabilitation of transgender persons. Protection and welfare drew no objection. Rescue and rehabilitation did, at the bill stage and since, on the ground that the vocabulary belongs to the administration of persons treated as problems: adults possessed of rights are protected in the exercise of them, and a statute whose first chapter recognises identity sits oddly with a welfare chapter that speaks of rescuing the holders of that identity from their own lives. Defenders of the drafting answer that the words answer real situations, the minor removed from exploitation, the person in destitution, and that the vocabulary is the standard vocabulary of Indian welfare legislation across many statutes. The exchange is minor beside the argument over certification, and it is recorded here because it illustrates the tonal division that runs under the whole debate: whether the Act's subject is a citizen with a documentation problem or a population to be administered. The community reads the statute's welfare language as evidence for the second; the ministry reads the same language as the ordinary idiom of the welfare state.

### What does inclusive education mean under Section 13?

Inclusive education is a defined term in the Act: a system of education in which transgender students learn together with other students without fear of discrimination, neglect, harassment or intimidation, with the teaching system suitably adapted to their learning needs.

Section 13 obliges every institution funded or recognised by the appropriate Government to provide that education, with sports, recreation and leisure on an equal basis.

The definition does more work than definitions usually do. By adopting inclusion, learning together, as the statutory model, and by naming the harms to be excluded, discrimination, neglect, harassment and intimidation, the Act rejects the two alternatives that institutional practice might otherwise have chosen: exclusion of transgender students from ordinary institutions, and their confinement to separate institutions of their own. The obligation also runs to institutions recognised by government and not only to those funded by it, which brings the large sector of recognised private education within the duty. What Section 13 does not do is prescribe the content of inclusion: the Act leaves curriculum, facilities, admission records and the gender in which a student is enrolled to institutional practice and to the certificate machinery, so a student whose documents record one gender and whose identity is another will find the practical answer in the institution's admission process rather than in the section. The Rules direct governments to review educational schemes and to create an inclusive curriculum, and the distance between that direction and a classroom is, once again, the distance this Act repeatedly leaves to administration.

Section 15 requires the appropriate Government to take a specified set of healthcare measures, and its list is the most concrete in the Act. The Government is to set up separate human immunodeficiency virus sero-surveillance centres to conduct sero-surveillance for transgender persons in accordance with the guidelines of the National AIDS Control Organisation; to provide medical care facilities including sex reassignment surgery and hormonal therapy; to provide counselling before and after sex reassignment surgery and hormonal therapy; to bring out a health manual related to sex reassignment surgery in accordance with the guidelines of the World Professional Association for Transgender Health; to review the medical curriculum and research for doctors to address the specific health issues of transgender persons; to facilitate access to hospitals and other healthcare institutions and centres; and to provide for coverage of medical expenses by an insurance scheme covering sex reassignment surgery, hormonal therapy, laser therapy and other health issues of transgender persons.

Two features of this list deserve analysis. The first is its genealogy: the sero-surveillance centre is a direct descendant of the 2014 judgment, which had directed separate HIV sero-surveillance centres, and the health manual keyed to the standards of the World Professional Association for Transgender Health imports an international clinical standard into a statutory duty. The second is the relationship between Section 15 and Section 7, which is the relationship on which a central criticism fastens. Section 15 obliges the state to provide and insure the very surgery that Section 7 makes the gateway to a revised certificate. Read together, the state undertakes to make the operation available and then requires it, for the person who wants documents as male or female. The government's reading treats the two provisions as complementary halves of a supported transition pathway: healthcare provided, recognition following on proof. The community's reading treats the same pair as the contradiction in institutional form: a medical procedure that ought to be a matter of personal choice, supported by the state for those who choose it, becomes instead a toll charged for legal recognition, payable only by those who can reach the hospitals where such surgery is performed, which are few and urban. The pillar records the pairing because it is textual and because every party to the argument relies on it.

The insurance limb of Section 15 shows the provision moving from text to instrument. The Department of Social Justice and Empowerment and the National Health Authority arranged for the national health assurance scheme to be extended to transgender persons holding certificates under the Act, through a dedicated card under the SMILE umbrella, with a benefit package that includes gender-affirming procedures among the treatments covered. The arrangement is instructive for the reading of the whole welfare chapter. Section 15(g) obliges the appropriate Government to provide for coverage of medical expenses by an insurance scheme; it names no scheme, no sum and no beneficiary machinery. The obligation was discharged, in the event, by bolting the Act's certificate-holders onto an existing national assurance platform, which made the certificate itself the eligibility document for the health benefit. Healthcare under the Act therefore runs on the same documentary foundation as everything else in the statute: the person whom the certificate recognises is the person the scheme can pay for, and the person outside the certificate stands outside the insurance the section promises, whatever the section's own words, addressed to governments, might suggest.

## The National Council for Transgender Persons: Sections 16 and 17

Chapter Seven creates the Act's one institution. Section 16 requires the Central Government to constitute, by notification, a National Council for Transgender Persons. Its composition is fixed by the section. The Union Minister in charge of the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment is Chairperson, ex officio. The Minister of State in that Ministry is Vice-Chairperson, ex officio. The Secretary of the Ministry is a member, ex officio. One representative each, not below the rank of Joint Secretary, sits for the Ministries of Health and Family Welfare, Home Affairs, Housing and Urban Affairs, Minority Affairs, Human Resource Development, Rural Development, and Labour and Employment, and for the Departments of Legal Affairs and of Pensions and Pensioners Welfare, and for the National Institute for Transforming India, the NITI Aayog. One representative each sits for the National Human Rights Commission and the National Commission for Women. Representatives of State Governments and Union territories sit by rotation, one each from the North, South, East, West and North-East regions, nominated by the Central Government. Five representatives of the transgender community sit by rotation, one each from the same five regions, nominated by the Central Government. Five experts representing non-governmental organisations or associations working for the welfare of transgender persons sit, nominated by the Central Government. The Joint Secretary of the Ministry dealing with the welfare of transgender persons is Member Secretary, ex officio. A member other than an ex officio member holds office for three years from nomination. The Council was first constituted by notification dated 21 August 2020, and Section 19 funds it by grants credited by the Central Government after appropriation by Parliament.

Section 17 states the functions. The Council is to advise the Central Government on the formulation of policies, programmes, legislation and projects with respect to transgender persons; to monitor and evaluate the impact of policies and programmes designed for achieving equality and full participation of transgender persons; to review and coordinate the activities of all departments of Government and other governmental and non-governmental organisations dealing with matters relating to transgender persons; to redress the grievances of transgender persons; and to perform such other functions as may be prescribed by the Central Government.

The institutional design invites the same structural reading the series applies to every commission and council it profiles. The Council is an advisory body housed inside the ministry it advises: its chair is the Minister, its secretariat is the Ministry, its budget is a grant from the Central Government under Section 19, and its functions are to advise, monitor, review, coordinate and redress, none of which includes a power to direct, to adjudicate with binding effect, or to require a response within a stated time. Community representation is real but structurally minor: five members of the community and five non-governmental experts sit among a body dominated by ex officio official membership, and every non-official member, including every community member, is nominated by the Central Government rather than elected by the constituency or selected by its organisations. The grievance function in Section 17 is the Council's most distinctive power and its least specified: the Act does not prescribe how a grievance is filed, investigated or answered, and the Council's redress therefore operates as escalation into the ministry rather than as adjudication. Against this, the Council's defenders point to the coordination function as the genuine article: the problems of transgender persons run across health, home affairs, housing, education, labour and pensions, and a body that seats those ministries at one table under the responsible Minister can move files that no external commission could. The Council is best understood, on its text, as an inter-ministerial coordination and advisory mechanism with community membership, not as a rights commission; the 2014 private member's bill had proposed commissions with stronger powers, and the enacted design chose otherwise.

The grievance function deserves one more paragraph, because it overlaps with the Act's other grievance route and the overlap is unresolved by the text. A transgender employee with a complaint about an establishment goes, under Section 11, to the establishment's complaint officer, whose enquiry the Rules time-bound. A transgender person with a grievance about anything, including the same establishment, may also approach the National Council, which Section 17 charges with redressing the grievances of transgender persons. The Act does not say whether the Council's route is an appeal from the complaint officer, an alternative to it, or a channel for grievances against government alone; it prescribes no form, no enquiry power and no timeline for the Council as it does, through the Rules, for the complaint officer. In the institutional practice that has grown up, the Council's grievance role operates as a referral and escalation mechanism into the ministries and state governments concerned, which is consistent with its composition and secretariat. A reader with a live grievance should therefore understand the two routes as different in kind: the complaint officer is a first forum with timelines and a named duty-holder, and the Council is a route for raising what the first forum, or the absence of one, has failed to resolve.

The Council also has to be read against what the Act does not create. There is no state council: the institutional chapter stops at the national body, and the corresponding state-level machinery exists only because Rule 10 of the 2020 Rules directs the constitution of welfare boards for transgender persons in the States and Union territories. The comparison with the commissions elsewhere in this series is instructive. The National Human Rights Commission, whose representative sits on the Council, is a statutory body with inquiry powers and a reporting line to Parliament; the National Council for Transgender Persons has neither an inquiry power nor a report that any legislature must receive. Its outputs are advice to the government that constitutes it, coordination among the departments that compose it, and such grievance redress as escalation within the ministry can achieve. None of that makes the Council useless; inter-ministerial coordination is the chronic failure point of welfare delivery for a population whose needs cross every departmental boundary, and the Council is the one forum in which health, home affairs, housing, labour and education sit with the responsible Minister and with community members in the room. But a reader measuring the institution against the grievance function in Section 17 should understand the measure: the Council can take a grievance up; it cannot decide one, and the Act gives the grievant no statement of what the Council must do, or by when, once the grievance is with it. The funding line completes the picture. Section 19's grants are credited after appropriation by Parliament, which makes the Council's working budget an annual act of the same government it advises, a dependence the institution profiles in this series repeatedly identify as the quiet determinant of what such bodies become.

## Offences and penalties: Section 18 and the comparison it invites

Chapter Eight contains a single section. Section 18 provides that whoever compels or entices a transgender person to indulge in the act of forced or bonded labour, other than any compulsory service for public purposes imposed by Government; or denies a transgender person the right of passage to a public place or obstructs such person from using or having access to a public place to which other members have access or a right to use; or forces or causes a transgender person to leave household, village or other place of residence; or harms or injures or endangers the life, safety, health or well-being, whether mental or physical, of a transgender person, or tends to do acts including causing physical abuse, sexual abuse, verbal and emotional abuse and economic abuse, shall be punishable with imprisonment for a term which shall not be less than six months but which may extend to two years, and with fine.

The section does three things that had not been done before. It makes the expulsion of a transgender person from household or village a criminal offence, which converts the pattern behind Section 12 into a punishable act. It makes obstruction of passage to and use of public places an offence, the statutory answer to exclusion from transport, markets, offices and public facilities. And in clause (d) it creates a general offence of harm to a transgender person, expressed to include physical abuse, sexual abuse, verbal and emotional abuse and economic abuse. For sexual abuse in particular, this was the first provision of general application under which a transgender complainant could prosecute at all, the rape provision of the Penal Code being written for a woman complainant; the wider question of how the old code and its successor divide these offences is traced in the [comparison of the IPC and the BNS](/2011/02/01/ipc-vs-bns-comparison/). The Act's supporters state the point plainly: before Section 18, the criminal law's silence was total, and an offence of two years is not nothing measured against nothing.

The comparison the critics make is not with nothing. It is with the punishments the same legal system prescribes for the same acts against other victims, and the provisions are stated here so that the reader can make the comparison without assistance. Rape under Section 376 of the Indian Penal Code, as the provision stood when the 2019 Act was passed, is punishable with rigorous imprisonment for a term not less than ten years, extending to imprisonment for life, and with fine. Sexual abuse of a transgender person under clause (d) of Section 18 of this Act is punishable with imprisonment of not less than six months, extending to two years, and with fine. Forced or bonded labour, obstruction of public passage, expulsion from home, and sexual abuse all sit inside the same six-months-to-two-years frame, with no gradation by gravity anywhere in the section. The petition filed by Swati Bidhan Baruah in the Supreme Court, on which the Court issued notice in January 2020, pleads the disparity directly: the plea states that several provisions of the Act evince and reinforce the very prejudice that the legislation ought to have aimed at eliminating, and that the punishment for sexual abuse of a transgender person at a maximum of two years, against the punishment for rape of a woman, discriminates on the very ground the Act exists to remedy. The government's answer, made in the passage debates and repeated in its defence of the Act, has two limbs: that Section 18 creates offences where none existed, so the correct comparison is with the previous silence; and that Section 20 of the Act provides that its provisions are in addition to and not in derogation of any other law, so that conduct punishable more heavily under another statute remains punishable there. The second limb is accurate as far as it goes and invites the reply the petitioners make: for the paradigmatic case of sexual violence, the other law does not in fact reach the transgender complainant, because the offences in the Penal Code and its successor code that carry the heavy punishments are drafted around a woman victim, a pattern visible across the body of [women's rights laws in India](/2015/11/01/womens-rights-laws-india-guide/), where protective statutes are written for one gender alone. The Allahabad High Court, in a bail order dealing with Section 69 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, the offence of sexual intercourse by deceitful means, held that a transgender woman is not within the expression woman in that section even after surgery, while recording that the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Home Affairs, in its Report No. 246 on the new codes, had recommended bringing transgender women within the rape provisions and that Parliament had not accepted the recommendation. The order was confined by its terms to the provision before the court. It stands in the record as the clearest judicial statement of the gap into which Section 18's two-year ceiling falls.

Clause (d) also raises a drafting question of the opposite kind from the punishment debate: not that it does too little, but that it is drawn without edges. The clause punishes whoever harms or injures or endangers the life, safety, health or well-being, mental or physical, of a transgender person, or tends to do acts including the listed forms of abuse. Neither abuse category is defined anywhere in the Act; verbal abuse and economic abuse, in particular, are expressions without a statutory measure, and the phrase tends to do acts extends the clause toward inchoate conduct that the general criminal law usually handles through distinct attempt provisions. Prosecutors and courts are therefore left to construct the clause's boundaries case by case, and commentators who support the Act's objects have criticised the clause for a vagueness that invites both under-use, by officers unsure what it covers, and contest, by accused persons entitled to ask what it forbids. The forced and bonded labour clause at the head of the section is, by contrast, tightly drawn: it requires compulsion or enticement by another person, and its carve-out for compulsory service for public purposes imposed by Government tracks the exception in Article 23 of the Constitution, which permits compulsory public service without discrimination. The section thus contains, side by side, the Act's most precise offence and its least precise one.

The procedural character of Section 18 completes the picture, and it is fixed by silence. The Act does not classify its offences as cognizable or non-cognizable, bailable or non-bailable, and it creates no special court, no special public prosecutor and no victim compensation provision of its own. Classification therefore falls to the general scheme of the criminal procedure codes, under which offences under other laws punishable with less than three years' imprisonment are non-cognizable and bailable: a police officer may not investigate a Section 18 offence without a magistrate's order, and bail follows as a matter of course on arrest. Whatever the view taken of the two-year ceiling, the procedural classification has its own consequence, because non-cognizable offences are, in the documented pattern of such provisions, registered late and investigated less; the Transgender Protection Cells that Rule 11 requires exist precisely to watch registration, investigation and prosecution of offences against transgender persons, which is an administrative admission that the ordinary course of the criminal process could not be assumed. Compensation must likewise be sought outside the Act, through the victim compensation schemes that the procedure codes oblige states to maintain for victims of crime generally. The offence chapter therefore reproduces, in criminal dress, the design of the whole statute: a new and genuinely unprecedented set of prohibitions, carried on machinery borrowed from the general law and weighted, at the point of enforcement, toward the weaker end of that law's scale.

### Does the Act criminalise begging?

No. The Act as enacted contains no offence of begging, by a transgender person or by anyone else. The 2016 Bill had proposed to punish compelling or enticing a transgender person to beg, and the clause did not survive into the enacted text.

Community organisations objected that such a clause would be used against transgender persons themselves in a community where alms-seeking is an established livelihood. What Section 18 punishes is compelled forced or bonded labour, a different and graver matter.

The distinction matters beyond tidiness. Commentary on the Act repeatedly attributes a begging offence to it, by inheritance from the bill-stage coverage of 2016, and the attribution then does duty in arguments both for the Act, as protection against exploitation, and against it, as criminalisation of a livelihood. The enacted protection against exploitation is the forced and bonded labour clause, whose gravamen is compulsion by another person, and whose compulsory-service carve-out for public purposes mirrors the constitutional text on forced labour. A reader examining any account of Section 18 should test it against the four clauses of the section itself, which are short enough to check.

### Does the Act override other laws protecting a transgender person?

No, and the direction of the provision is the opposite. Section 20 provides that the provisions of the Act shall be in addition to, and not in derogation of, the provisions of any other law for the time being in force.

A transgender person therefore keeps every right and remedy available under any other statute, from the Protection of Human Rights Act to the general civil and criminal law, and the 2019 Act adds its own rights on top rather than substituting for them.

The practical importance of Section 20 runs in both directions. For a complainant, it means that the two-year ceiling in Section 18 is a ceiling only on Section 18: where the same conduct constitutes a graver offence under another law that does reach the facts, that law remains available, as with assault, wrongful confinement or extortion under the general criminal code, which are not gender-confined. For a court, it means the Act is read cumulatively with the constitutional remedies and with statutes such as the one governing domestic violence only where those statutes' own definitions reach the complainant, which, as the comparison in the offences section shows, they frequently do not. Section 20 is therefore a preserving clause, not a solution to the disparity problem; it preserves a landscape in which the deepest protections remain distributed by gender, and the Act's own provision sits at two years.

## The direction that was not implemented: reservation

One element of the 2014 judgment has no counterpart in the Act at all, and its absence is the community's second standing charge after the surgery condition. The Supreme Court directed the Centre and the State Governments to treat transgender persons as socially and educationally backward classes and to extend all kinds of reservation in admission to educational institutions and in public appointments. The longer record of that direction, and of the reservation architecture into which it was meant to fit, is set out in the [social justice laws guide](/2017/02/01/social-justice-laws-india-guide/) for this series. The Act contains no reservation provision: no clause of Chapter Four, Five or Six reserves a seat, a post or a proportion of either, and neither the Rules nor the equal opportunity policy required of establishments supplies one. The Standing Committee on Social Justice and Empowerment had recommended in 2017 that reservation be extended; the recommendation was not adopted. The petitions challenging the Act and, later, the amendment of 2026 both plead the omission as a continuing failure to comply with the judgment, and the Tripathi petition frames it as an issue in terms, asking whether the continued failure to provide reservation constitutes non-compliance with the operative paragraphs of the 2014 judgment.

The responses available to the government are of two kinds, and both have been made. The first is that reservation is not the only route to inclusion and that the Act chose the route of non-discrimination, welfare schemes and institutional coordination; a direction to treat a class as backward for reservation purposes, on this view, is implemented by policy across the whole field rather than by a quota clause in this statute. The second is that such reservation as exists has in fact been built at the state level and through scheme design rather than through the Act, which is accurate as a description of where the movement has occurred. Karnataka amended its civil services general recruitment rules in 2021 to provide one per cent reservation for transgender persons in public employment, a horizontal reservation cutting across the vertical categories, following proceedings in the Karnataka High Court on the subject. The example is double-edged in the argument, and both edges are used: it shows that the direction of 2014 is capable of implementation in an ordinary recruitment rule, and it shows that implementation arrived from a state government under judicial pressure rather than from the central statute that the judgment's direction principally addressed. The pillar records the position without resolving it: on the text of the Act, the direction stands unimplemented by the Act, and the Act's defenders do not seriously argue otherwise; they argue about what follows from that.

## The two cases, stated as their proponents state them

The controversy over this Act is unusual in Indian legislative debate because the two sides do not substantially disagree about what the text says. They disagree about what the text means, and the disagreement is best presented in the form each side would choose.

The government's case, as made by the Ministry in the passage debates and in defence of the Act since, is a case about operability. A right that cannot be documented cannot be administered: welfare schemes need beneficiary lists, identity documents need a recorded gender, reservations and targeted benefits, if they come, need a register of the class, and a non-discrimination norm needs a defined class to protect. The certificate is that documentary basis. It is issued, under the Act as the Rules operate it, on the applicant's own affidavit, without medical examination, through a portal that requires no attendance at any office, within thirty days. The District Magistrate is not an exotic choice of certifier: the district administration issues the caste certificates, income certificates, domicile certificates and disability certificates on which the entire welfare state runs, and identity documentation for a welfare class belongs, administratively, with the officer who issues every other such document. The surgery condition for the revised certificate answers a different need: a change of the recorded gender to male or female engages legal regimes far beyond this Act, marriage, succession, adoption, sex-segregated facilities and offences drafted by gender, and the state is entitled to tie so consequential a change to a verifiable event rather than to declaration alone, particularly where the declaration route for the third gender remains open to every applicant without any medical gate. On the punishment comparison, the government case is that Section 18 created offences where the criminal law was silent, that Section 20 preserves every heavier offence that reaches the facts, and that a first statute's penalty range is a starting point for the ordinary process of legislative revision rather than a valuation of the victim. On reservation, the case is the one stated in the preceding section: inclusion is pursued through the whole instrument, and quota design is a matter for the recruitment rules and the states as much as for this Act.

The community's case, as made by transgender persons and organisations from the bill stage onward and carried into the petitions, begins from the judgment. The state was told in 2014, by the highest court, that gender identity is self-perceived, that a person may identify as male, female or third gender, and that insistence on surgery for recognition is illegal. Grace Banu, the activist who was among those who worked on the drafting questions from the community side, stated the objection to the 2026 amendments in terms that repeat the objection made to the 2019 Act itself: that the changes were brought without consultation with the community they govern. The Baruah petition of 2020 puts the constitutional form of the objection: that the Act replaced self-identification with state-identification, that the certification process is invasive and deprives transpersons of privacy, and that the Act, in the petition's words, contains provisions which evince and reinforce the very prejudice that the legislation ought to have aimed at eliminating. On the certificate, the objection is that no other Indian is required to obtain a magistrate's certificate of what the person is as a condition of the state's recording it: caste and income certificates certify facts the state can verify, while a certificate of gender identity certifies the one fact that, on the judgment, only the person can state. On surgery, the objection is factual as much as principled: surgery is expensive, is available in few centres, is unwanted by many who live and identify as male or female without it, and a rule that makes recognition wait on it makes recognition a function of wealth and geography. On punishment, the objection is the comparison itself, stated in the offences section. On consultation, the objection is procedural and cumulative: the community's positions were recorded by the expert committee of 2014, by the private member's bill of 2015 and by the Standing Committee of 2017, and at each point where the process narrowed toward enactment, those positions were set aside.

The pillar adopts neither case, because the dispute between them is, at its centre, a dispute the courts have been asked to settle and have not: whether administrative certification of identity, and a surgical condition on binary recognition, are permissible implementations of the right declared in 2014 or violations of it. What can be said without taking that side is narrower, and it is said in the assessment that follows the amendment record: the statute's principle and its machinery point in different directions, the divergence was visible at every stage of the drafting, and Parliament enacted the machinery with the divergence fully argued before it.

## The amendment of 2026: the definition rewritten and the principle removed

The amendment arc of this Act is short and steep. For six years the Act stood as enacted, amended by no legislation, while the Rules and the portal carried its administration and the 2020 petitions waited. The Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Amendment Act, 2026, Act 3 of 2026, changed that position fundamentally. It was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 13 March 2026 by the Union Minister for Social Justice and Empowerment, Virendra Kumar, passed by the Lok Sabha on 24 March 2026 and by the Rajya Sabha on 25 March 2026 amid protests and an opposition walkout as reported of the passage, and received the President's assent on 30 March 2026. It comes into force on a date to be appointed by the Central Government by notification. Its stated object, as the government presented it, was corrective: to remove ambiguity and implementation failure in the 2019 Act. Its content, provision by provision, is as follows.

The definition in Section 2(k) is substituted. In place of the organising idea of a gender that does not match the gender assigned at birth, the substituted definition rests on named socio-cultural identities, kinner, hijra, aravani, jogta and eunuch, and on persons with intersex variations or congenital variations at birth, specified by characteristics: primary sexual characteristics, external genitalia, chromosome pattern, gonadal development and endogenous hormone production. It also includes persons who have been made to adopt or assume a transgender identity by force, inducement or undue influence, with or without consent, through mutilation, emasculation, castration, amputation or surgical or hormonal procedure. A proviso excludes persons with different sexual orientations and self-perceived sexual identities from the definition. The separate definition of a person with intersex variations in Section 2(i) is omitted, its content absorbed into the substituted clause. One further amendment is the deletion, outright, of Section 4(2): the words of the 2019 Act declaring that a person recognised as transgender shall have a right to self-perceived gender identity no longer stand in the statute.

The certification machinery is then rebuilt around a new body. A new clause in Section 2 defines an authority, a medical board headed by the Chief Medical Officer or a Deputy Chief Medical Officer, appointed by the appropriate Government. Section 6 is amended so that the District Magistrate issues the certificate of identity after examining the recommendation of the authority, with power, where the Magistrate considers it necessary or desirable, to take the assistance of other medical experts. Section 7 is amended so that the person who undergoes surgery to change gender shall, in place of may, apply for the revised certificate: the application becomes compulsory rather than optional. A new sub-section requires the medical institution in which such surgery is performed to furnish the details of the person to the District Magistrate and to the authority, in the prescribed form and manner. The sub-section of Section 7 that carried the entitlement to change the first name, with its protecting proviso, is omitted, and a corresponding name-change entitlement for holders of the Section 6 certificate is inserted in Section 6 itself. Section 8 is amended with exceptions, pleaded in the challenges, for religious institutions and for public morality. A new Section 4A introduces a mandatory National Transgender Registry. Section 16 is amended. Section 18 is replaced, retaining the offences against transgender persons while adding new penal clauses, Sections 18(e) and 18(f) among them, directed at compelling a person to outwardly present a transgender identity and connected conduct, and a new Section 18A creates an offence of impersonation.

### What is the authority that the Amendment Act of 2026 inserts into certification?

The authority is a medical board defined by the Amendment Act in Section 2 of the principal Act, headed by the Chief Medical Officer or a Deputy Chief Medical Officer of the district concerned and appointed by the appropriate Government.

Under the amended Section 6, no certificate of identity issues on the District Magistrate's own examination of the application: the Magistrate acts after examining the recommendation of this authority, and may take the assistance of further medical experts where the Magistrate thinks it necessary or desirable.

The distance travelled can be measured against the three designs this article has described. The 2016 Bill proposed a five-member District Screening Committee with medical membership, and the proposal drew the objection that identity was being made examinable. The 2019 Act dropped the committee and certified on application, which the Rules of 2020 rested on the applicant's affidavit without medical examination. The Amendment Act of 2026 reintroduces a medical body, not as an adviser to the process but as the source of a recommendation the District Magistrate must examine before certifying. Supporters of the amendment describe the board as a safeguard against fraudulent claims on welfare and documents. Its opponents describe it as the screening committee restored in a stronger form, and the petitions use the phrase medical gatekeeping. The description in this article confines itself to the mechanism: under the amended text, a favourable recommendation from a board headed by the district's chief medical officer stands between the applicant and the certificate.

The challenges followed immediately and are pending. In the Supreme Court, Laxmi Narayan Tripathi and Zainab Javid Patel petitioned under Article 32, contending that the Amendment Act causes irreparable constitutional injury to the rights of transgender persons under Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21, that Parliament cannot by ordinary legislation omit a provision codifying a right the Court has declared fundamental, and that the substitution of biological and socio-medical classification for lived identity is the question the Court must answer; the petition attacks in terms the substituted Section 2(k) and its proviso, the deletion of Section 4(2), the amended Sections 6 and 7 including the hospital-reporting sub-section, the amended Section 16, and the replaced Section 18 insofar as it retains a two-year maximum for offences including sexual abuse while prescribing far higher penalties for the new trafficking-type offences. A further petition by the advocate Ankání Biswas attacks the proviso to Section 2(k) from the position of a person who already holds a transgender identity card, on the ground that the exclusion of self-perceived identities throws existing recognition into doubt; the Supreme Court issued notice on that petition and tagged it with the others. In the Delhi High Court, a public interest petition by Chandresh Jain challenges Sections 2(aa), 2(k), 4(2), 6, 7 and 18(e) and 18(f) as amended, describing the amendment as a legislative rollback of a recognised fundamental right, and the Court issued notice to the Union Government. Before assent, the Indian Association of Aesthetic Plastic Surgeons, a body of more than thirteen hundred specialists, had warned that the clause punishing the compelling of a person to present a transgender identity was drawn widely enough to chill legitimate gender-affirmative care; the Minister of State, answering in Parliament, said the provision did not target legitimate gender-affirmative care, without defining the term. The Supreme Court has issued notice to the Centre and the States on the challenge petitions and has sought the Union's response, including, in the reported exchange, on whether self-identification could be misused. No court has decided the validity of the Amendment Act, and no court has decided the challenges to the 2019 Act that preceded it; the whole constitutional argument described in this article stands where the petitions leave it, open.

The petitions also tell the reader where the argument will be sourced when it is heard, and the sourcing is part of the record a pillar should keep. The Tripathi petition frames its issues partly in international materials: the Yogyakarta Principles on the application of international human rights law to sexual orientation and gender identity, invoked on the vagueness of the impersonation offence; the decisions of the European Court of Human Rights in Y.Y. v. Turkey and in A.P., Garçon and Nicot v. France, in which requirements of medical intervention or sterilisation as conditions of legal gender recognition were held to violate the right to respect for private life; a finding of the United Nations Special Rapporteur on torture on forced or coerced medical procedures; and the doctrine of non-retrogression under the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, invoked against the deletion of a recognised right. International materials of this kind are persuasive, not binding, in Indian constitutional adjudication, and the 2014 judgment itself drew on comparative and international sources in reaching self-identification. The government's defence, when the matters are heard, can be expected to rest on the legislative competence to design welfare administration, on the fraud and documentation concerns it has stated, and on the margin a legislature holds in structuring a recognition regime. Which frame prevails is the litigation's whole content, and nothing in this article predicts it.

Two features of the amended scheme deserve separate statement, because each generates a distinct line of challenge. The first is the treatment of persons already recognised. Certificates issued under the 2019 Act and the Rules recorded their holders' gender as transgender on the strength of self-declaration, and documents across the state were corrected on that basis. The substituted definition, with its proviso excluding self-perceived identities, is pleaded by the Biswas petition to throw the foundation of those certificates into question: a person recognised in one year under a definition resting on self-perception holds a record whose statutory basis the next definition disowns. Whether an amendment of a definition can unsettle completed administrative acts of recognition is a question of general importance in Indian public law, and it is squarely raised here, on facts that affect identity documents, professional records and welfare entitlements at once. The second feature is the penal additions. Sections 18(e) and 18(f), and the impersonation offence in Section 18A, move the Act's criminal chapter into territory the 2019 text did not enter, the policing of presentation and of claims to identity; the petitioners attack the impersonation offence as unconstitutionally vague, invoking the standard of the Shreya Singhal judgment on vague penal speech provisions, and attack the compelled-presentation clauses on the ground recorded earlier, that their width reaches the medical practice of gender-affirmative care itself. The Amendment Act's commencement, left to notification, adds a final structural point: the substituted scheme's practical operation depends on the appointment of the authorities it creates and on rules to run them, so the distance between the amended text and the amended reality is, as with the parent Act, a distance that administration will decide.

## Assessment: what the Act does, and where its own text argues with itself

A statute pillar ends with a verdict, and the verdict on this Act has to be given at two levels, because the Act succeeds and fails at different ones. At the level of existence, the Act is a genuine first: it defines the class, forbids discrimination across the fields of ordinary life in terms binding on every person and every establishment, creates offences where the criminal law was silent, builds a national institution, and obliges governments to frame welfare, education, livelihood and healthcare provision for a population the statute book previously addressed only through penal surveillance. Measured against the prior position, which was the Criminal Tribes Act's register and the Penal Code's silence, the distance travelled in one short statute is large, and the community's own advocacy never denied it; the argument was always that the judgment of 2014 had promised more, and differently.

At the level of mechanism, the Act is at war with its own stated principle, and the war is visible on the face of the text without any assistance from its critics. Section 4(2) vested the right in self-perception. Sections 5 and 6 routed its proof through an administrator, acceptably, on affidavit, for the third gender. Section 7 reserved the binary genders to those who could produce a surgeon's certificate to an administrator's satisfaction. The reservation direction of the same judgment was left out altogether. The discrimination prohibition was left without a sanction, and the offence provision was pitched, for sexual abuse, at a small fraction of the punishment the general law prescribes for the same act against a woman. Each of these was a choice, each was argued at the time, and the Standing Committee's report shows that Parliament had the alternatives in writing before it. The amendment of 2026 then resolved the statute's internal tension in the direction of the machinery: the self-perceived identity clause was deleted, the definition was recast in classifications a board can verify, and certification was placed behind a medical recommendation. Whatever the courts decide about validity, the legislative direction is unambiguous, and it is the opposite of the direction the judgment, the expert committee and the Standing Committee had each indicated.

For the reader who needs the Act rather than the argument, the operative summary is the sequence of Chapter Three and the machinery of the Rules: an application on affidavit, a certificate in thirty days, documents corrected on its basis, a revised certificate only through the surgical gateway, and, under the amendment as enacted and awaiting commencement and judgment, a medical board's recommendation interposed at the first step as well. For the advocate, the live questions are the validity of the certification and surgery conditions and of the 2026 substitution, all pending. For the researcher, the Act is this series' clearest instance of its recurring pattern, a statute whose principle is stated in one section and qualified into its opposite by another, with the qualification, not the principle, carrying the practical consequences. The cluster's companion articles take the parts the pillar has deliberately left in brief: the 2014 judgment in its own article, and the wider welfare field in its guide. The pillar's closing observation is the one the record supports without assistance from either side. A community asked the state for recognition of what its members are. The state answered with a certificate, and the argument about the certificate, in the legislature and then in the courts, has become the argument about the recognition itself.

## The Act beyond the challenges: courts and states at work

The constitutional challenges dominate the judicial record of this Act, but they are not the whole of it, and a pillar should note the quieter line of cases in which courts have used the post-2014 legal position, the Act included, to decide concrete disputes. The Madras High Court's judgment in S. Sushma v. Commissioner of Police, delivered in 2021 by Justice N. Anand Venkatesh, arose from a complaint by a lesbian couple facing harassment and became the occasion for guidelines on the protection of LGBTQIA+ persons: directions against attempts to medically cure or change sexual orientation or gender identity, for the sensitisation of police and public authorities, and for the competent bodies to act against conversion practices in the medical profession. The judgment belongs to the wider jurisprudence of dignity and autonomy rather than to the construction of this Act, and it is recorded here for a narrower reason: it shows the High Courts building, case by case, the protective practice that the Act's own enforcement machinery does not supply, in the same years in which the Act's validity stood undecided before the Supreme Court. The Karnataka proceedings that produced the 2021 recruitment-rule amendment point the same way from the direction of reservation: the direction of 2014 was carried into a working rule by a state under judicial supervision, while the central statute that omitted reservation stood unamended.

The state level is also where the Act's federal distribution of labour shows itself. Section 22 empowers every appropriate Government to make rules; the national procedure is the Centre's Rules of 2020, and welfare boards under Rule 10 and protection cells under Rule 11 are state and district constructions, reported by the Ministry as constituted across most States and Union territories, with the completeness of that coverage varying in the way of all such returns. States with earlier instruments, Tamil Nadu with its welfare board of 2008 and Kerala with its policy of 2015, folded pre-existing machinery into the Act's frame; states without them began from the Rules. The result is a single statutory text administered through visibly different state apparatuses, which is the normal condition of Indian welfare federalism and which any account of the Act's operation has to hold alongside the text: two applicants in two states hold the same Section 6 right and meet different offices, different timelines in practice, and different welfare offers behind the certificate.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who is a transgender person under the 2019 Act?

Section 2(k) of the Act defines a transgender person as a person whose gender does not match with the gender assigned to that person at birth. The definition expressly includes a trans-man or trans-woman, whether or not the person has undergone sex reassignment surgery or hormone therapy or laser therapy or any such therapy; a person with intersex variations, meaning a person who at birth shows variation in primary sexual characteristics, external genitalia, chromosomes or hormones from the normative standard of a male or female body; a genderqueer person; and a person having such socio-cultural identities as kinner, hijra, aravani and jogta. Membership of the class therefore does not depend on any medical treatment, undertaken or declined.

### Q: How does a person obtain a certificate of identity?

A transgender person applies to the District Magistrate for a certificate of identity as a transgender person under Section 5 of the Act. Under the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Rules, 2020, the application is made in Form-1 with a supporting affidavit in Form-2, either at the district office or digitally through the National Portal for Transgender Persons, and no medical examination may be required for this certificate. The District Magistrate issues the certificate in Form-3, with a transgender identity card in Form-5, within thirty days of a duly completed application. The certificate confers rights and is proof of recognition of identity as a transgender person, and the gender recorded in official documents follows it under Section 6.

### Q: Why does changing the gender marker require surgery?

The Act itself gives no reason; the requirement is structural. Section 6 certificates record the holder's gender as transgender, and Section 7 opens the only route to a revised certificate recording male or female, and that route runs through surgery to change gender, proved by a certificate from the Medical Superintendent or Chief Medical Officer of the institution where the surgery was performed, to the District Magistrate's satisfaction. The government defended the design on the ground that a binary gender marker engages other legal regimes, such as marriage, succession and gender-drafted offences, and should rest on a verifiable event. Critics answer that the 2014 judgment made self-perception sufficient for recognition as male or female, and that the condition contradicts Section 4 within the same statute.

### Q: What discrimination does the transgender Act prohibit?

Section 3 prohibits any person or establishment from discriminating against a transgender person by denial, discontinuation or unfair treatment across nine fields: educational establishments and services; employment or occupation, including denial or termination; healthcare services; access to and enjoyment of goods, accommodation, services, facilities, benefits, privileges and opportunities available to the general public; the right of movement; the right to reside, purchase, rent or otherwise occupy property; the opportunity to stand for or hold public or private office; and access to, removal from or unfair treatment in any Government or private establishment in whose care or custody a transgender person may be. The prohibition is broad in coverage, though the Act prescribes no separate penalty for a breach of Section 3 as such.

### Q: What offences does the transgender Act create and what are the penalties?

Section 18 creates four offences against a transgender person: compelling or enticing the person into forced or bonded labour, other than compulsory public service imposed by Government; denying the person the right of passage to a public place or obstructing the person's access to or use of such a place; forcing or causing the person to leave a household, village or other place of residence; and harming, injuring or endangering the life, safety, health or well-being, mental or physical, of the person, including through physical abuse, sexual abuse, verbal and emotional abuse and economic abuse. Every one of these offences carries the same punishment: imprisonment for a term not less than six months and extending to two years, together with a fine.

### Q: Does the transgender Act provide reservation?

No. The Act contains no provision reserving seats in educational institutions or posts in public employment for transgender persons. That is so although the Supreme Court, in the 2014 judgment the Act implements, directed the Centre and the States to treat transgender persons as socially and educationally backward classes and to extend reservation in admission to educational institutions and in public appointments, and although the Parliamentary Standing Committee recommended reservation in its 2017 report on the earlier bill. Such reservation as exists has been created outside the Act, for example by Karnataka's amendment of its civil services recruitment rules in 2021 providing one per cent horizontal reservation in public employment. The omission is pleaded in the challenges to the Act.

### Q: What is the National Council for Transgender Persons?

The National Council for Transgender Persons is the body constituted by the Central Government under Section 16 of the Act, first notified on 21 August 2020. It is chaired ex officio by the Union Minister for Social Justice and Empowerment, with the Minister of State as Vice-Chairperson, and includes the Ministry's Secretary, representatives of the principal ministries and departments and of the NITI Aayog, representatives of the National Human Rights Commission and the National Commission for Women, State and Union territory representatives by regional rotation, five representatives of the transgender community by regional rotation, and five experts from non-governmental organisations, with non-official members holding office for three years. Under Section 17 it advises the Central Government, monitors and evaluates policies, coordinates departments and organisations, redresses grievances, and performs prescribed functions.

### Q: What do trans communities criticise in the transgender Act?

The criticisms, stated by transgender persons and organisations through the bill process and in the court petitions, are five. Certification by a District Magistrate subjects identity to administrative determination although the 2014 judgment rested recognition on self-perception. The surgery condition in Section 7 contradicts the right to self-perceived identity declared in Section 4 and the judgment's holding that insistence on surgery is unlawful. The maximum punishment of two years under Section 18 is far below the punishments the general criminal law prescribes for comparable offences, including a minimum of ten years for rape. The Act omits the reservation the judgment directed. And consultation with the community in drafting was inadequate, with positions recorded by the expert committee of 2014 and the Standing Committee of 2017 set aside at enactment.

### Q: What is a revised certificate under the Transgender Persons Act, and how is it different from a certificate of identity?

A certificate of identity, issued under Section 6, records the holder's gender as transgender and is obtained on application with an affidavit, without any medical evidence. A revised certificate, issued under Section 7, records a change of gender to male or to female. It is available only to a person who already holds a certificate of identity and has afterwards undergone surgery to change gender, and the application must be accompanied by a certificate from the Medical Superintendent or Chief Medical Officer of the medical institution where the surgery was performed, whose correctness the District Magistrate must be satisfied of. Under the 2020 Rules the revised certificate and a revised identity card are to issue within fifteen days. The two documents therefore rest on entirely different evidentiary foundations: declaration for the first, a certified surgical event for the second.

### Q: Can a transgender person change the first name in official documents under the Act?

Yes. Section 7(3) of the Act as enacted entitles a person who has been issued a certificate of identity, or a revised certificate, to change the first name in the birth certificate and in all other official documents relating to the person's identity, and the proviso protects the person's rights and entitlements under the Act against any effect of the change. The 2020 Rules carry the entitlement into administration: the certificate of identity is the basis for changing the name, gender and photograph in the official documents listed in the Rules' annexure, the issuing authority is to make the change within fifteen days of application, and the revised document bears the same serial or reference number as the original. The Amendment Act of 2026 relocates the name-change entitlement into Section 6 itself.

### Q: What must every establishment do under the Transgender Persons Act?

Three duties fall on every establishment, a term the Act defines to include government bodies, companies, firms, trusts, societies, institutions and every other form of employer, without any size threshold. Section 9 forbids discrimination against a transgender person in any matter relating to employment, including recruitment and promotion. Section 10 requires every establishment to ensure compliance with the Act and to provide such facilities as are prescribed, which the 2020 Rules supply in the form of an equal opportunity policy that the establishment must frame and publish, and facilities such as separate washrooms. Section 11 requires every establishment to designate a complaint officer to deal with complaints of violation of the Act; the Rules require designation within thirty days, enquiry into a complaint within fifteen days, and action by the head of the establishment within thirty days.

### Q: What healthcare facilities does Section 15 of the Act require the government to provide?

Section 15 obliges the appropriate Government to set up separate human immunodeficiency virus sero-surveillance centres for transgender persons, following the guidelines of the National AIDS Control Organisation; to provide medical care facilities including sex reassignment surgery and hormonal therapy; to provide counselling before and after such surgery and therapy; to bring out a health manual on sex reassignment surgery in accordance with the guidelines of the World Professional Association for Transgender Health; to review the medical curriculum and research for doctors so as to address the specific health issues of transgender persons; to facilitate access to hospitals and other healthcare institutions; and to provide coverage of medical expenses through an insurance scheme extending to sex reassignment surgery, hormonal therapy, laser therapy and other health issues of transgender persons. The duties are framed as obligations of government, to be worked out through schemes and facilities.

### Q: What does the Act say about a transgender person's right to live with family?

Section 12 protects the person's place in the natal household in three ways. No child may be separated from parents or immediate family on the ground of being a transgender child, except by an order of a competent court made in the child's interest. Every transgender person has a right to reside in the household where the parents or immediate family members reside, a right not to be excluded from the household or any part of it, and a right to enjoy and use its facilities in a non-discriminatory manner. Where a parent or immediate family member is unable to take care of the person, a competent court may order placement in a rehabilitation centre. Family is defined by Section 2(c) as persons related by blood, marriage or lawful adoption, so chosen families and community households, including the hijra gharana, are not within the protection as defined.

### Q: What happens if an application for a certificate of identity is rejected?

The 2020 Rules build a two-stage answer. Under Rule 8, the District Magistrate who rejects an application must inform the applicant of the reasons for rejection within thirty days, and may review the rejection on the basis of the applicant's reply, within sixty days. Under Rule 9, the applicant has a right of appeal against the rejection to an appellate authority designated by the appropriate Government by notification, and the appeal must be filed within ninety days of the date on which the rejection was intimated. Separately, Section 18 of the Act is no part of this process, and the National Council's grievance function under Section 17 offers an escalation route of a different kind. Because the appeal period runs from intimation of the rejection, an applicant who misses the review stage should still count the ninety days from the original communication.

### Q: How much time do the 2020 Rules give the District Magistrate to issue a certificate?

Rule 5 of the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Rules, 2020 requires the certificate of identity to be issued within thirty days of receipt of a duly filled application together with the affidavit, in Form-3, with the transgender identity card in Form-5 issued at the same time. For the revised certificate after surgery, Rules 6 and 7 set a shorter period: the revised certificate and revised identity card are to issue within fifteen days of the application accompanied by the medical institution's certificate. The Rules also time-bound the consequences: an authority holding an official document of the applicant is to change the name, gender or photograph in it within fifteen days of application based on the certificate, and a rejection must be communicated with reasons within thirty days.

### Q: Can a parent or guardian apply for a certificate of identity for a minor child?

Yes. Section 5 of the Act contains a proviso that in the case of a minor child, the application for a certificate of identity shall be made by a parent or guardian of the child. The ordinary certificate machinery then applies: application to the District Magistrate in the prescribed form, the certificate recording the child's gender as transgender, and the recording of gender in official documents in accordance with the certificate under Section 6. The Act makes no separate provision for the child's own application on attaining majority, and it does not address how a certificate obtained in childhood is to be revisited, a silence that sits alongside Section 12's protection against separating a transgender child from the family except by a court's order in the child's interest.

### Q: What welfare duties does Section 8 place on the appropriate Government?

Section 8, which is the whole of Chapter Four of the Act, obliges the appropriate Government, meaning the Central Government for central establishments and the State Government for state establishments and local authorities, to take steps to secure the full and effective participation of transgender persons and their inclusion in society; to take such welfare measures as may be prescribed to protect the rights and interests of transgender persons and to facilitate their access to welfare schemes framed by that Government; to formulate welfare schemes and programmes which are transgender-sensitive, non-stigmatising and non-discriminatory; to take steps for the rescue, protection and rehabilitation of transgender persons; and to take appropriate measures to promote and protect the right of transgender persons to participate in cultural and recreational activities. Section 14 adds the specific duty to frame schemes for livelihood, vocational training and self-employment.

### Q: What did the Transgender Persons Amendment Act of 2026 change?

The Amendment Act, Act 3 of 2026, assented to on 30 March 2026 and awaiting commencement by notification, rewrites the recognition scheme. It substitutes the definition in Section 2(k), resting it on named socio-cultural identities and on intersex and congenital variations specified by physical characteristics, including persons made to assume a transgender identity by coercion, and excluding by proviso persons with different sexual orientations and self-perceived sexual identities. It omits Section 4(2), the declaration of a right to self-perceived gender identity. It defines an authority, a medical board headed by the Chief Medical Officer, whose recommendation the District Magistrate must examine before issuing a certificate under the amended Section 6. It makes the post-surgery application for a revised certificate compulsory, requires hospitals to report surgeries to the District Magistrate and the authority, introduces a mandatory National Transgender Registry in a new Section 4A, and replaces Section 18 with additional offences including an impersonation offence in a new Section 18A.

### Q: What is the Transgender Protection Cell that the 2020 Rules require?

Rule 11 of the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Rules, 2020 directs the setting up of a Transgender Protection Cell under the charge of the District Magistrate in each district and under the Director General of Police at the state level. Its function is to monitor cases of offences against transgender persons and to ensure timely registration, investigation and prosecution of those offences. The Cell is therefore the administrative bridge between the offence provision in Section 18 of the Act and the police machinery that must act on it: offences against transgender persons had historically gone unregistered or unrecorded, and the Cell exists to watch the registration and investigation of such cases rather than to investigate them itself. It sits within a set of Rules that also require welfare boards under Rule 10 and equal opportunity policies under Rule 12.

### Q: Has the constitutional challenge to the Transgender Persons Act been decided?

No. The challenges are pending and undecided, in two generations. The first attacks the Act of 2019 itself: the petition of Swati Bidhan Baruah, on which the Supreme Court issued notice to the Central Government in January 2020, contends among other things that certification replaced self-identification with state-identification and that the punishment structure discriminates. The second attacks the Amendment Act of 2026: petitions by Laxmi Narayan Tripathi and Zainab Javid Patel and by the advocate Ankání Biswas in the Supreme Court, on which notice has issued and the matters tagged together, and a public interest petition by Chandresh Jain in the Delhi High Court, which has also issued notice. No court has pronounced on the validity of the certification machinery, the surgery condition, the substituted definition or the deletion of Section 4(2). Any account that reports these questions as settled misstates the record.
