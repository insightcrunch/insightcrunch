A reconstruction is an argument built in timber, and the rebuilt Shakespeare's Globe on Bankside is the most consequential argument ever made about how Romeo and Juliet works in performance. The verdict of this article is stated at the threshold, because everything after it is evidence. Globe reconstruction productions of the tragedy test functions rather than recover an event: they demonstrate, in working conditions, what shared daylight does to direct address, what a thrust platform does to scene speed, what a standing yard does to comedy, and what period clothing and period music do to the social legibility of the feud. They cannot demonstrate what the first audiences of the middle 1590s felt, because those audiences are the one component no architect can rebuild. A production that honours that distinction earns the name of experiment. A production that blurs it sells a diorama. The Globe's own record contains both temptations, and the play has been its sharpest test case, because no other tragedy in the repertory leans so hard on the exact conditions the building restores: darkness described in daylight, privacy performed in public, speed enforced by architecture, and a chorus that speaks to the crowd as a crowd.

The distinction matters because the popular account of the rebuilt playhouse runs in the opposite direction. The popular account treats the building as a time machine, as if oak, lime plaster and thatch could return a modern audience to 1599 and let it watch the tragedy with Elizabethan eyes. The record supports a colder and more interesting claim. The timbers are new, the site stands some two hundred metres from the original plot, the capacity is roughly half the original house, the yard holds a fraction of the original crowd, and the spectators arrive with school editions, film memories and four centuries of accumulated commentary in their heads. What survives the crossing is not an experience but a set of physical conditions: light shared between stage and yard, a platform wrapped on three sides by spectators, an upper level, two doors, a trap, no scenery worth the name, and weather. Conditions can be tested. Experiences cannot be inherited. The whole argument of the sections that follow hangs on keeping those two nouns separate.

The play makes an ideal instrument for the test because its text keeps announcing the conditions it was built for. The Chorus opens by addressing a gathered crowd directly, "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene" (Prologue, lines 1 to 2), and the fiction then proceeds to describe night after night that the afternoon sun refuses to supply. Romeo looks up at a window in full daylight and asks, "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2), and the line only makes its full sense when the brightness is supplied by language rather than by a lighting desk. The Friar enters at dawn that exists only in his speech, "The grey-eyed morn smiles on the frowning night" (2.3), while the yard stands in whatever light a London afternoon provides. A darkened modern auditorium hides these transactions inside an illusion. The reconstructed house exposes them as transactions, and exposure is the beginning of knowledge.

Read the third column as the article in miniature. Every row divides into a function that the reconstruction genuinely exercises and a residue that it cannot touch. The building exercises geometry: sightlines from the yard, the wrap of galleries on three sides, the distance from the stage edge to the farthest bench. It cannot exercise the original congregation, because the original congregation included apprentices on a holiday afternoon, watermen from the river stairs, pickpockets working the crowd, and playgoers for whom the feud's language of honour, service and household discipline described the furniture of daily life. The clothing exercises legibility: a modern crowd can learn, within one scene, to read a cap, a mourning band and a livery as fluently as it reads a suit or a uniform. It cannot exercise the original sumptuary reflex, the instant social alarm that a servant in a master's colours would have triggered in 1595. The profile grades each element so that enthusiasm never outruns evidence.

The profile also records what changed across the experiment's own history, because the reconstruction did not stand still. The first seasons under Mark Rylance treated the house as a laboratory with a repertory attached, and the original-practices programme reached its fullest statement in the middle of the following decade, when clothing, music, dance and pronunciation were all placed under period discipline in the same production. Later seasons under Dominic Dromgoole folded the lessons back into a working repertory, keeping the building's conditions while relaxing the period disciplines one by one. Still later practice relaxed further, admitting design interventions and amplification debates that the first artistic regime would have refused. The profile's rows therefore describe a spectrum rather than a single method, and the productions of the tragedy sit at different points along it, which is precisely what makes the play a useful measure of the whole enterprise.

## The Building as Argument: Oak, Thatch and Conjectural Geometry

The rebuilt Globe opened in June 1997 with Henry V, and the opening itself carried the argument in embryo: the first artistic director, Mark Rylance, played the king in an original-practices production, so the building and the method were announced together, as a single claim about how the plays should meet their audiences. The project's founder, the American actor Sam Wanamaker, had campaigned from 1970, having found that the most famous playhouse in English history was marked in London by a plaque on a brewery wall. Wanamaker died in 1993, before the frame rose, and the building that opened four years later is in part his memorial. Its scholarship, however, belongs to a team, and chiefly to the historical adviser Andrew Gurr and the master carpenter's researchers who turned excavation evidence, the surviving contract for the Fortune playhouse, a single drawing of the Swan, and a handful of travellers' accounts into a buildable polygon of twenty sides.

Every dimension of that polygon is a graded inference, and the honest literature of the project says so. The Rose playhouse excavations of 1989 fixed the scale of a Bankside amphitheatre. The Fortune contract supplied stage measurements from a documented commission. The Swan drawing supplied the look of a stage house: two doors, a gallery above, a hut, a flag. What no source supplied was the Globe itself, and the reconstruction is therefore a composite, the best-supported member of a family of possible Globes. Theatre historian Andrew Gurr, whose decades of work on the original playhouses underwrote the design, defended the composite method on the ground that playing conditions converge even where carpentry details diverge: a thrust platform inside a polygon of galleries behaves in a limited number of ways, and those behaviours, rather than any single measurement, are what a production tests.

The concessions to the modern state stand in the building like footnotes. Fire regulations required a sprinkler system above the thatch, the first thatched roof permitted in London since the Great Fire, treated and guarded. Exits widen where the original stairs were narrow. Capacity settles near fourteen hundred against an original figure often estimated at three thousand, and the yard holds about seven hundred standing spectators where the original yard may have held a thousand or more. The site itself concedes the largest point of all: the original plot lies under protected Georgian housing and a road, unreachable, so the reconstruction stands nearby rather than upon it. A visitor who understands the concessions understands the method. The building is a scholarly hypothesis that admits spectators, and its productions are the hypothesis at work.

### How close is the rebuilt Globe to the playhouse of 1599?

The rebuilt Globe is close in geometry and distant in almost everything else, and the distance is documented rather than hidden. Its polygon, platform, doors, upper level, trap, thatch and open yard follow the best convergent evidence for the 1599 house, assembled from excavation, contract and drawing.

Its timbers are new oak framed by modern scholarship, its site stands some two hundred metres from the original plot, its capacity runs at roughly half the original figure, and its crowd arrives with modern expectations. The building reproduces the conditions of playing with high fidelity and the occasion of playing not at all. That division, geometry recovered and occasion unrecoverable, governs every claim the productions can honestly make, and the strongest work in the house has always observed it.

The geometry deserves its due before the qualifications multiply. A platform stage thrust into a yard, wrapped by three tiers of galleries, produces a playing space with no bad position for address and no hiding place for an actor. Speech thrown outward reaches the galleries; speech thrown downward reaches the yard; speech thrown across the platform reaches both and implicates them in different ways. The Chorus of this play, a single figure walking forward to narrate, is unintelligible as a device in a proscenium house and inevitable in this one: the platform's edge is a lectern built into the architecture. The window scenes gain their geometry from the upper level, the tomb scenes from the trap and the discovery space, the street scenes from the two doors whose opposed entries turn a platform into a city with factions. When the sections below speak of functions tested, these are the mechanisms meant.

## Original Practices: The Method and Its Disciplines

Original practices named a discipline before it named a style. In the Globe's usage the term covered the material conditions of production: hand-made clothing constructed by the methods of the period wardrobe, under the direction of costume historian Jenny Tiramani; music composed and played on period instruments, under Claire van Kampen; dance reconstructed from period sources; and, at the experiment's edge, pronunciation reconstructed from the evidence of rhyme, spelling and contemporary description. The discipline's core rule was subtraction. No amplification, no lighting plot, no scenery beyond the building itself, no directorial concept imposed between the text and the conditions. The actor's preparation changed with the wardrobe: a hand-sewn garment of silk, wool and linen, interlined and structured, alters posture, gesture and the felt geography of rank before a word is spoken, and Tiramani's accounts of the wardrobe work treat clothing as the production's first act of interpretation.

The method's theatrical logic ran against the grain of the subsidised stage. A modern production typically begins from a concept and bends the building to serve it. An original-practices production began from the building and the wardrobe and let the play disclose what those conditions made playable. The jig that closed performances, danced by the whole company after the deaths, belonged to the same logic: the earliest texts and accounts attach dancing to the end of tragedies as a release and a social reconnection, and the reconstructed house restored the practice as a matter of course, so that the Prince's closing sentences gave way to communal movement rather than to a blackout and a hush. Audiences educated by the darkened auditorium often reported the jig as the moment the experiment either justified itself or broke faith, which is itself a datum about how completely modern taste has fused tragedy with silence.

The discipline reached its height in the seasons when several original-practices productions ran in repertory, sharing wardrobes, musicians and a company trained in the method's habits. That repertory condition mattered, because it reproduced one more original circumstance that a single production cannot: the company as a standing body, playing many parts across many plays, known to its audience by face and by type. The tragedy gained from the arrangement in a specific way. Verona is a small world of doubled loyalties, and a repertory crowd watching the same actor serve as servant in one play and prince in another read the household scenes with a fluency that a one-off casting cannot manufacture.

The method also carried a built-in limit, which its serious practitioners stated plainly. Clothing can be replicated stitch by stitch because garments leave patterns, portraits and accounts. Music can be approximated because instruments and dance manuals survive. Pronunciation can be reconstructed within a band of probability because the evidence is linguistic and cross-checkable. Behaviour cannot be replicated at all, because behaviour leaves no artefact, and the inner disciplines of the original company, its rehearsal economy, its cue-script learning, its improvisation licences, survive only as inference from texts and lawsuits. Original practices at its best knew exactly where its evidence ran out, and the quarrel examined later in this article begins where practitioners or publicists forgot.

## The 2004 Production: Period Dress and the Sound of the Text

The landmark staging of the tragedy in the reconstruction's first decade came in 2004, in the Season of Star-Crossed Lovers, when Tim Carroll directed the play as an original-practices production with Tom Burke as Romeo and Kananu Kirimi as Juliet, in hand-made period clothing, with period music and dance, in a mixed company of men and women. The production's most discussed experiment ran for a single weekend within the run: a set of performances in Original Pronunciation, the reconstructed sound of the play's first English, prepared with the linguist David Crystal, whose book on the experiment remains its fullest record. The rest of the run spoke in the company's ordinary stage voices, which makes the production a controlled comparison in itself, the same staging heard in two sound systems.

The pronunciation experiment deserves precise description, because legend has inflated it. Crystal's reconstruction drew on the standard evidence: rhymes that fail in modern speech and succeed in the reconstructed sound, puns that revive when vowels shift, spellings that record pronunciation, and the descriptions of sixteenth-century orthoepists. The company rehearsed from transcriptions of the distinctive words rather than from a full phonetic script for most of the text, a compromise forced by the calendar and recorded honestly in Crystal's account. The reported effects were concrete. Rhymes landed that modern delivery loses. Wordplay sharpened, particularly in the prose exchanges where speed and vulgarity carry the comedy. The verse acquired a muscular, rapid character that several listeners compared to Irish or West Country colouring, and the lovers' shared sonnet at the feast gained an audible click as its rhymes closed. None of this proved how the play sounded in 1595. All of it proved that the text's sound-patterning contains information that modern pronunciation discards, and that an audience can hear the difference within minutes.

The clothing carried an equal share of the production's argument. Period dress at the Globe was not decoration but information: the feud is a war of households, and households in the 1590s wore their allegiances. Liveries, colours, mourning dress and festive dress sorted the stage picture into factions before the dialogue began, so that the opening brawl read as a collision of liveried servants rather than as generic street violence, and the feast read as a display of household wealth into which a masked rival walked. The mask itself became a production emblem, passed between the lovers as a token, a small invention inside the discipline that showed the method's real character: not a freeze but a frame, inside which actors still made theatre.

The reviews divided along the line this article adjudicates throughout. Admirers praised the urgency, the speed, the sense of a play running at the pace its plotting demands, with scene following scene in farcical proximity until the farce curdled. Sceptics found the central passion underpowered, coltish rather than consuming, and treated the conditions as a frame in search of a fire. Both responses are evidence. The speed finding recurs across the reconstruction's history and belongs to the building: a platform with two doors and no scenery enforces continuity, and continuity exposes how much of the tragedy is engineered as acceleration. The passion finding belongs to casting and rehearsal, variables the method does not control. A careful verdict credits the house with the first and declines to blame it for the second.

### What did the Original Pronunciation weekend actually prove?

The Original Pronunciation performances proved that the text carries sound-pattern information which modern stage speech discards, and that a modern audience can recover the pattern by ear within a single scene. Rhymes that limp in received pronunciation closed cleanly, puns in the prose exchanges regained their point, and the shared sonnet clicked shut as its makers designed it to close.

The experiment did not prove that the first audiences heard those exact vowels, because the reconstruction of a dead pronunciation yields a band of probability rather than a recording. David Crystal's own account frames the result in those terms: a working hypothesis about sound, tested in the only laboratory where sound can be tested, a theatre full of listeners following a story.

One further finding from 2004 tends to drop out of the legend, and it matters for the authenticity debate. The production was original-practices in clothing, music and dance, and it was not original in casting: women played the women's parts, as in every main-house Globe production of the era, while the wider programme housed all-male experiments on other titles. The selectivity was principled rather than evasive. The Globe's method was never a vow to reproduce every condition; it was a decision to test the conditions that could be tested responsibly, one variable at a time where possible. Critics who attacked the production for inconsistency mistook a laboratory protocol for a broken promise, and defenders who marketed it as a window onto 1595 made the mirror-image error. The production itself, examined closely, supports the protocol reading.

## Daylight: Shared Light and the Play of Described Night

Daylight is the reconstruction's most radical instrument, and the tragedy is the repertory's most daylight-dependent play. The fiction runs on darkness: a torchlit feast, a night orchard, a dawn parting, a night ride to Mantua, a torchlit tomb. The original stage played all of it under the afternoon sky, signifying night by torch, by dress and by speech, and the rebuilt house restores exactly that transaction. The effect on the verse is systematic. Lines that a lighting designer can illustrate, the daylight stage must enact. When Juliet calls for night as a lover's accomplice, "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds, / Towards Phoebus' lodging" (3.2), the speech is not description but conjuring, an attempt to talk the visible sky into changing, and a daylight audience watches the attempt fail beautifully as the speech succeeds. The gap between the seen sky and the spoken night is not a defect of the conditions. It is the conditions' central lesson: the early modern stage ran on collaborative imagination, and the verse was its machinery.

Shared light also rewires the soliloquy. In a darkened house a soliloquy is a thought overheard; the actor speaks into a void and the audience eavesdrops. On the daylight platform the audience is visible to the actor and to itself, and the soliloquy becomes an address, a confidence placed with named witnesses. Romeo's speeches in the orchard scene play to the yard as much as to the window, and the yard's answering stillness or laughter becomes part of the scene's timing. Friar Laurence's meditation on herbs and men, "Within the infant rind of this weak flower / Poison hath residence and medicine power" (2.3), turns in shared light into something close to a lecture delivered to a gathered crowd, which suits a friar whose function is to explain the play's moral botany to anyone who will listen. The reconstruction did not invent these effects. It removed the technology that had suppressed them.

The comedy registers the change most measurably. Daylight comedy is social: the Nurse's delays, Peter's illiteracy, Mercutio's flights all play outward, recruiting the yard as a participant, and the groundlings' laughter arrives as a physical event the actors can ride, top and answer. The 2004 and 2009 productions both drew notices remarking on the comedy's size, its near-farcical energy, and on the corresponding shock when the comedy stops. That shock is structural. A crowd that has been laughing as a body experiences Mercutio's death as a betrayal of the gathering itself, and the platform's refusal to darken denies it the privacy in which modern audiences usually absorb the turn. The tragedy's famous gear-change, from comedy to catastrophe at the midpoint, is an architectural event in the reconstructed house in a way it cannot be in a black box, where lighting can perform the change on the audience's behalf.

Night scenes also expose the discipline of the torch. Torches at the feast and at the tomb signify darkness while adding real flame to real daylight, and their practical management, who carries them, where they stand, when they are doused, becomes a choreography the audience reads unconsciously as time and place. The Paris at the tomb, the Friar with his lantern, the watch with theirs: the original stage directions distribute light-bearing as information, and a reconstruction that follows the directions discovers that the play's last act is lit like a diagram of its knowledge, each party carrying exactly as much light as it understands. That discovery is available in a study, but it is learned in the body only in the yard.

## The Yard: Groundlings, Weather and the Social Contract

The yard is the reconstruction's second instrument, and the harder one to discuss honestly, because the modern groundling is the element of the experiment most often sentimentalised. The five-pound standing ticket reproduces the price ratio of the penny yard and nothing else. The original groundlings were the city's apprentices, servants, soldiers and idlers, a crowd for whom standing room at a play competed with bear-baiting and the tavern, and whose taste the repertory courted and feared. The modern yard holds students, tourists, teachers and devotees, a self-selected congregation that arrives reverent and leaves evangelical. The function the yard tests is therefore attention under exposure, not the sociology of 1595. Within that limit, the findings are real. Standing spectators lean, shift, laugh louder and tire visibly, and actors pitch to them as to a jury that can walk away. The tragedy's pace, its refusal of intervals in the early seasons, its direct appeals, all presume a crowd that must be held standing, and the presumption becomes legible only when a crowd is actually standing.

Stephen Purcell's research on Globe audiences, gathered across seasons of observation and interview, gives the yard its most careful scholarly portrait. Purcell found a spectatorship that understands itself as participatory, that reads the yard's licence to react as a contract rather than as misbehaviour, and that polices its own boundaries, shushing the disruptive and schooling the newcomer. His account cuts against both mythologies at once: against the heritage fantasy of a boisterous Elizabethan mob reborn, and against the purist complaint that the modern crowd merely performs enthusiasm for its own benefit. What the yard produces, on his evidence, is a distinctively alert audience, aware of itself as part of the show's machinery, and the tragedy's Chorus, which instructs its hearers to work, "Piece out our imperfections with your thoughts" in the companion history play's phrase and its own Prologue's demand for patient ears, addresses exactly that alertness.

Weather completes the contract. Rain at the Globe is not an interruption of the performance but one of its documented conditions, and the tragedy's wet performances have their own literature of anecdote: groundlings in plastic, actors accelerating through the orchard scene under a downpour, the tomb scene played in slanting light and steam. The anecdotes encode a finding. Exposure synchronises a crowd; discomfort shared is attention shared, and the play's own weather of haste and heat lands differently when the audience's bodies are participants in the afternoon rather than observers of a climate-controlled fiction. The original crowd knew weather as a condition of all public life. The modern yard knows it as a chosen ordeal, and the difference in meaning is exactly the difference between a function tested and an occasion unrecovered, stated in meteorological form.

### Why does the comedy play larger in the yard than in a darkened house?

The comedy plays larger because daylight and standing room turn laughter into a public act that the actors can hear, time and answer. In a darkened auditorium laughter is private and simultaneous, a noise without a face, a weather that no actor can read or ride.

In the yard it has faces, directions and leaders, and the Nurse's delays, Peter's confusion and Mercutio's flights are written as bids for a crowd's visible response, timed to land on people who can be seen receiving them. The platform's edge disappears as a boundary, the comic scenes recruit the standing crowd as Verona's street made present, and the jokes grow to fill the space they are given. The corresponding cost arrives at the midpoint, when the same crowd must watch the laughter's favourite die in the same unforgiving light.

Hospitality cuts the other way as well, and honesty requires the entry. A yard that can warm a comedy can also flatten a quiet scene, and the tragedy's stillest passages, the lovers' farewells, the potion soliloquy's terror, compete in the open air against river noise, aircraft on the Heathrow path and the rustle of a thousand programmes. Productions learn to place stillness where the architecture shelters it, upstage near the frons, in the lee of the pillars, and the learning itself reproduces an original problem: the first companies also played against the noise of Bankside, its bears, its boats and its weather. The constraint is authentic even when the sounds are not, which is the reconstruction's characteristic bargain in its purest form.

## Architecture in Motion: Doors, Levels, Trap and the Window

The platform's furniture is minimal and total: two doors in the stage house, a gallery level above with its own openings, a trap in the boards, pillars holding the heavens, and a curtained discovery space whose exact form remains the most argued-over element in the building's scholarship. The tragedy's staging requirements read like a specification written for exactly this furniture. Streets form and dissolve through opposed doors, each house entering from its own side until the factions blur. The feast occupies the platform whole, with the gallery for music. The window scenes stack the lovers vertically, Juliet at the upper level, Romeo below in the yard's own space, so that the scene's geography of desire, height against ground, watcher against watched, is drawn by the building rather than by any designer's elevation. The tomb requires a descent, a discovery, a place that can be a monument, a vault and a bed in succession, and the discovery space plus trap supply the functions in sequence.

The window deserves a paragraph of its own, because the reconstruction corrected the popular picture here more cleanly than anywhere else. No early text of the play contains a balcony, and the upper level of the amphitheatre stage was a working gallery reached from the tiring house, used for windows, walls, music and observation as the scene required. The full staging argument belongs to [the staging of the orchard scene and the absent balcony](/2027/12/31/no-balcony-staging-act-2-scene-2/), and the Elizabethan record behind it belongs to [the play's first staging on the Elizabethan stage](/2027/08/06/romeo-juliet-elizabethan-stage/); the reconstruction's contribution is to make the correction physical. A Juliet at a gallery window, leaning on the rail of a working level with the tiring house behind her, is a girl at the lit window of a house on a street. A Juliet on a scenic balcony is a statue on a monument. The play's danger lives in the first picture, a household window above a public street, with a feud listening below, and the building restores that danger without a single directorial decision.

Speed is the architecture's other gift, and the hardest to overstate. With no scenery to shift and no lighting states to fade, scenes succeed each other at the pace of entrances and exits, and the tragedy's plotting reveals its engineering: nineteen scenes in the first two acts, locations announced in a line and abandoned in a breath, messengers crossing the platform as the plot's connective tissue. The 2004 production's notices returned repeatedly to the production's hurtling pace, and the pace was not an interpretation. It was the building declining to slow down. Modern productions in furnished houses characteristically add transitions, underscoring and dumb shows that stretch the action toward three hours and soften its recklessness into fate. The platform restores recklessness as a tempo, and with it the play's argument that the catastrophe is manufactured by haste rather than decreed by stars.

The trap and the tomb close the architectural account. The last act gathers its bodies downward: Paris at the monument, Romeo descending to the vault, Juliet discovered among the dead, the Friar arriving with lantern and tools, the watch, the families, the Prince. The platform that held a ball holds a charnel, and the transformation is achieved with a cloth, a bier, torches and bodies, in unchanging afternoon light. Witnesses of the reconstruction's tomb scenes consistently describe an effect that darkened houses purchase with machinery: the sense that the same public space which hosted the comedy has been reclassified by grief, and that the crowd which laughed in it must stand in it as mourners. The building makes the audience complicit in the reclassification, because the space is theirs too. That complicity is the tragedy's final staging idea, and it is native to the amphitheatre in a way no subsequent architecture has reproduced.

## Casting the Experiment: Women, Boys and the Mixed Company

Casting is where the reconstruction's selectivity shows most plainly, and where its results are most often misread. The original company played the women's parts with boys and young men, a convention resting on law, custom and the apprentice system, and the tragedy's Juliet was written for a skilled boy actor of perhaps fourteen to seventeen, whose art the text trusts with its most demanding verse. The main-house Globe has played Juliet with women from its opening, Kananu Kirimi in 2004 and Ellie Kendrick in 2009 among them, while the all-male discipline was tested in the wider original-practices programme on other titles, most famously the 2002 Twelfth Night whose Olivia was played by the artistic director himself. The arrangement is sometimes charged as a failure of nerve. It is better understood as the laboratory's protocol again: the main house tests architecture, light, clothing and audience with casting held at the modern norm, while the programme as a whole varies casting on titles where the experiment's risks fall elsewhere.

What the mixed company does test, in this play, is the tragedy's economy of age. Verona's catastrophe is generational, and the text is precise about it: Juliet lacks a fortnight of fourteen, her mother was a mother at her age, Paris is a man, Romeo an older youth already seasoned in a previous devotion. A production that casts genuinely young lovers against genuinely parental elders stages the play's arithmetic of power, and the daylight house enforces the arithmetic without mercy, because shared light forgives no casting lie. Kendrick's Juliet in 2009, played at an age close to the text's figure, drew notices that returned to the same observation from different directions: the child's joy in the part's first half, skipping, climbing, dissolving into the Nurse's arms, repriced the second half's decisions, so that the potion speech read as a schoolgirl's courage summoned against a terror she could name but not measure. That repricing is a casting finding, available in any theatre, but the yard's intimacy and the light's honesty amplified it into the production's central event.

The boy-heroine question returns by the back door, because the all-male experiments elsewhere in the programme supplied the control the main house declined to run. Critics who saw both could compare what a male Juliet-figure does to the part's erotic and social geometry: the doubling of artifice, the audience's awareness of craft over biology, the way the wedding-night scene plays when desire is announced as performance. The comparison's honest conclusion, drawn by several reviewers across the programme's history, is that the convention is a technology with costs and yields like any other, not a sacrament and not a scandal. It sharpens the verse's formal pleasures and cools the scenes of physical intimacy; it estranges the feud's marriage market into an exhibit and loses the market's bodily stakes. Editors of the text, whose notes must translate stage conventions for readers, have their own stake in the question: Jill Levenson's Oxford edition, with its performance-centred introduction, treats the original casting as evidence about the writing itself, a text shaped for a boy's skills and breath, and that editorial stance, performance as textual evidence, is one of the reconstruction era's durable exports into scholarship.

### What changes when Juliet is played at the age the text specifies?

The generational arithmetic of the feud becomes visible as the play's engine rather than as background. A Juliet of nearly fourteen, slight, quick and unformed in manner, turns the marriage plot from romance into a transaction conducted over a child, and every adult in the scenes reprices accordingly.

Capulet's tenderness and brutality become two weathers of the same ownership, the Nurse's complicity becomes a servant's survival strategy, and the Friar's plan becomes an old man's gamble with a girl's body as the stake. The verse does not change; the moral light does. The 2009 Globe production demonstrated the effect at full strength, and its reviewers, whatever their reservations about polish, recorded the same repricing, which suggests the finding belongs to the casting rather than to any single performance.

Doubling, the original company's other casting economy, returns in reconstruction practice as a matter of platform logic. A company of fourteen or so playing some thirty parts sorts the play into principals and functions, and the functions double: servants, watchmen, musicians, citizens, the walking infrastructure of Verona. On the bare platform the doublings are visible and legible, the same face serving both houses, and the legibility carries a meaning the first audiences would have read without instruction: the feud is one city wearing two liveries, staffed by the same pool of men. Modern productions with large casts conceal the point. The reconstruction's economy restores it as an accident of arithmetic, which is how the original stage made most of its meanings, as the by-product of a working system rather than as a director's underline.

## The 2009 Production: From Experiment to Repertory

Dominic Dromgoole's 2009 production, opening the Young Hearts season with Adetomiwa Edun as Romeo and Ellie Kendrick as Juliet, Rawiri Paratene as the Friar and Penny Layden as the Nurse, marks the moment the reconstruction stopped asking permission to exist. The original-practices disciplines were present in diluted form, period-flavoured clothing, live music on Elizabethan-style instruments, the building worked as the building insists on being worked, but the production's confidence was repertory confidence: a young cast, a brisk and unfussy staging, the play offered as the house's signature tragedy rather than as a demonstration. Its afterlife confirms the reading, since the production was filmed and circulated on disc and streaming as the Globe's representative version of the play for a generation of students, the version through which much of the English-speaking classroom meets the tragedy in performance.

The production also displayed, in a single design decision, the exact boundary this article has been policing. Simon Daw's staging set a spiral staircase at the centre of the platform, serving as the window's elevation, the route down to the vault and a sculptural emblem of the plot's descending coil. The staircase is not in the building's evidence and not in the text; it is a designer's intervention of the modern kind, solving in built form what the original stage solved with a gallery and a trap. Reviewers divided over it, some finding it elegant, others asking why Juliet appeared to sleep in the middle of the Capulet living room. The division is the authenticity debate in miniature, and the honest verdict is that the staircase was a foreign body that the production's energies mostly absorbed: the building tolerated the import because the platform's functions, address, speed, verticality, survived it, while the purist objection stands as a reminder that every import spends some of the experiment's capital.

What the 2009 production proved, beyond its own merits, was the durability of the house style the reconstruction had created. A Globe performance had become a recognisable genre: daylight, directness, comedy played to the yard's face, music as social glue, speed as default, the audience cast as citizens of the fiction's city. Genre is what an experiment becomes when its findings are absorbed, and the absorption carries a risk the next sections take up: a house style can harden into a brand, and a brand can begin to certify experiences it no longer tests. The filmed record adds a further twist, because the camera converts the yard's event into a document, flattening the shared light into a screen image and the standing crowd into local colour at the frame's edge. Students who meet the production on a screen meet its readings without its conditions, which is the reconstruction's paradox arriving by post.

## The Indoor Test: Candlelight and the Sam Wanamaker Playhouse

The opening of the Sam Wanamaker Playhouse in 2014 extended the reconstruction's logic to the indoor stage, and the extension clarifies the original experiment by contrast. The Playhouse is a candlelit indoor theatre of some three hundred and forty seats, modelled on the Jacobean indoor houses such as the Blackfriars, where the King's Men played in winter from 1609 and where the repertory, including this tragedy in revival, met a wealthier, seated, enclosed audience. Its evidence base is thinner than the Globe's, its design a composite from a surviving set of drawings of uncertain purpose, its candles real beeswax burning in sconces and chandeliers, tended, trimmed and snuffed as part of the performance's labour. Where the amphitheatre tests the public conditions of the early repertory, the Playhouse tests the private ones: enclosure, artificial light, proximity without a yard, the actor's face lit from below by flame.

The candlelit conditions reorder the tragedy's effects in ways the daylight house cannot approach. Flame makes faces flicker, gilds skin and jewellery, turns the feast into a treasury and the tomb into a reliquary, and it restores the indoor stage's signature transaction, darkness that is real darkness, in which a candle carried is a decision and a candle doused is an event. The indoor experiment also restores the sound of a small room: verse at conversational pressure, prose that can drop to conspiracy, a crowd whose silence is audible. Set beside the amphitheatre, the Playhouse completes the reconstruction project's map of the original company's year, the summer platform and the winter hall, and it disciplines a lazy habit of speech that the Globe's fame encouraged, in which the amphitheatre stands for all early staging. The tragedy lived in both kinds of room across its first decades, and its text's double nature, public rhetoric in private scenes, crowd scenes that shrink to whispers, fits the double inheritance exactly.

The indoor house also sharpens the authenticity quarrel rather than settling it, because its composite evidence is frankly acknowledged and its pleasures are unmistakably modern in their comfort: raked seating, a roof that keeps the weather out, candles managed under modern fire discipline. Nobody leaves the Playhouse believing they have visited 1610, and the building does not ask them to. It asks them to watch what candlelight does to faces and verse, which is a question with an answer, and the contrast with the heritage marketing that sometimes wraps the amphitheatre is instructive. The project's own trajectory, from the Globe's founding rhetoric toward the Playhouse's candour about method, traces the maturation this article's verdict endorses: reconstruction as a way of asking questions in public, with the evidence grades printed in the programme.

## A Family of Reconstructions: Staunton, Auckland, Rome and the Road

The London building is the most famous member of a family, and the family's other members test the method's portability. In Staunton, Virginia, the American Shakespeare Center's Blackfriars Playhouse, opened in 2001, reconstructed the indoor conditions from the same family of evidence and built its company practice on original staging conditions: universal lighting shared between stage and house, actors addressing the audience directly, doubling, cross-gender casting used as a tool rather than a thesis, and spectators seated on the stage itself, inside the fiction's edge. Its long repertory history with the tragedy confirmed the London findings in a different climate and a different theatrical culture: the comedy enlarges under shared light, the soliloquies turn confessional, the pace asserts itself, and audiences trained by the conditions begin to demand them. Portability matters to the argument, because a finding that only one building produces might belong to the building's charisma rather than to the conditions, while a finding reproduced in Virginia, in Auckland's Pop-up Globe, a demountable full-scale replica that played to large crowds from 2016, and in Rome's Silvano Toti Globe, built in 2003 on the London plans, begins to look like a property of the conditions themselves.

The touring and educational circuits carry the same conditions in diluted form wherever a company plays the play in the round, in daylight or under working light, with the house lights up and the audience cast as the city. School parties at the London Globe, the project's education work reaching hundreds of thousands of students, function as the experiment's largest sample: adolescents, the tragedy's exact subject population, standing in the yard, discovering that the play's comedy is rude, its speed exhilarating and its ending an affront they argue about on the pavement outside. No survey instrument has settled what that discovery does to examination results, and the project has wisely stopped promising that it will. What the pavement arguments demonstrate is simpler and sufficient: the conditions make the play an event in the present tense for an audience that print had taught to regard it as a monument.

### Do other reconstructed playhouses confirm the London findings?

The other reconstructions confirm the functional findings and refine them, which is what independent replication is for. Staunton's indoor house, working under universal light in an enclosed room, reproduces the enlarged comedy, the confessional soliloquy and the enforced pace that London's open yard produces, while adding the indoor variables of acoustic intimacy and real darkness available at a switch's notice.

The demountable replicas in Auckland and Rome reproduce the amphitheatre geometry and its crowd effects at festival scale. Where the findings converge across buildings, climates and companies, the credit belongs to the conditions: shared light, thrust geometry, standing or encircling spectators. Where they diverge, the divergence maps the local variable, a roof, a room, a culture of playgoing, and the map itself is knowledge the single building could not have supplied.

The family's disagreements are as informative as its agreements. Staunton's practice embraces audience members on the stage, a liberty the London yard implies but does not take, and its defenders argue that the original indoor houses, with gallants seated on stools at the stage's edge, licence the intrusion. London's practice guards the platform's edge more jealously and locates participation in the yard. Both claim descent from the record, and the record, which describes gallants on stools at the indoor houses and a railed yard at the amphitheatres, supports each in its own house and neither as a universal law. The quarrel is the method working as intended: evidence grades visible, inferences named, rival stagings running the rival inferences in public where audiences can judge the yields.

## The Authenticity Quarrel, Adjudicated

The scholarly quarrel over the reconstruction has run for three decades, and the tragedy supplies its clearest test because the play's Globe productions have been both the method's showcase and its marketing emblem. The positions sort into three. Andrew Gurr, the theatre historian whose scholarship underwrote the building, defended the enterprise as experimental archaeology: the reconstruction embodies the best convergent account of the original conditions, and playing in it tests that account the way a rebuilt ship tests a hull design, by floating or by sinking. W. B. Worthen, the performance theorist, answered that performance cannot take its authority from a reconstructed origin, because every performance is made by its own moment's conventions, training and economics; on his account the Globe's claim to recover original force mistakes the location of meaning, which lives in the event between stage and audience and cannot be inherited from a building. John Drakakis pressed the sceptical case hardest in the project's early years, treating the reconstruction's rhetoric as heritage ideology, a theme park of the national poet dressed as research.

Ann Thompson's survey of staging at the rebuilt house, weighing the patchy evidence for original practices against the company's actual uses of yard, trap, under-stage and gallery, lands where the evidence lands: the building has functioned as a laboratory in some seasons and as a brand in others, and the two functions coexist in the same stones. The adjudication this article defends follows the graded discipline that the series applied to the original stage itself. Gurr is right about conditions: geometry, light, clothing and crowd can be reconstructed within stated margins, and playing tests them. Worthen is right about events: no margin of carpentry recovers the occasion, the audience's inner weather, the meaning the feud's vocabulary carried in a city of households and honour. Drakakis is right about rhetoric and wrong about the whole: where the project's language sold time travel, the sceptics' charge stands, but the charge does not reach the laboratory record, the pronunciation weekend, the wardrobe research, the audience studies, which produced findings that survive their marketing.

The editor's seat in the quarrel belongs to the performance-oriented editing that the reconstruction era normalised. Levenson's Oxford introduction reads the play through its stage history, treating performance choices as evidence about the text's affordances, and the Globe's archive of productions became, under that editorial dispensation, a citable body of evidence: a crux tested in the yard, a doubling pattern proved workable, a scene transition timed. The traffic runs both ways, since productions in turn cite editions, and the tragedy's modern stage life is a conversation between the two practices conducted in public. That conversation, rather than any single production, is the reconstruction era's largest contribution to the play's study, and it connects directly to the broader account of [performance criticism and the living text](/2027/05/07/performance-criticism-living-text/), where the argument that a play's meaning is partly made in its stagings receives its full hearing.

### Can a reconstructed theatre recover an original performance?

A reconstructed theatre cannot recover an original performance, and the claim that it might is the one error the whole enterprise must refuse. A performance is an event compounded of a building, a company, a crowd, a day's weather and a culture's reflexes, and of those components only the building and parts of the company's material practice can be rebuilt.

The crowd of the middle 1590s, its apprentices and watermen, its fluency in honour and household discipline, its unrecorded laughter and silence, is gone beyond recall, and the inner experience of the first Juliet's audience left no residue that oak and thatch can reactivate. What reconstruction recovers is a set of working conditions and the findings they yield about the text's machinery. The distinction is not a disappointment. It is the difference between an experiment and a seance, and the experiment is the achievement.

The quarrel's residue is a rule of speech for everyone who writes about these productions, and this article has observed it throughout: verbs of testing for conditions, verbs of speculation for occasions, and no verb at all that smuggles the second across the border of the first. A production reveals what daylight does to the orchard scene; it suggests what afternoons may have felt like; it demonstrates nothing about what any Elizabethan felt. Critics who keep the verbs sorted find the reconstruction a generous colleague. Critics who blur them, in praise or in attack, end up arguing about a claim the building never made in its scholarly voice, however loudly its souvenir voice may sometimes have made it.

## Running Time, Pace and the Two Hours' Traffic

The Prologue's promise of "the two hours' traffic of our stage" has served the reconstruction as a built-in hypothesis, and the house's productions amount to its longest-running test. The full textual and repertory argument about playing time belongs to [the study of running time and the two hours' traffic](/2028/02/04/two-hours-traffic-running-time/); the reconstruction's contribution is empirical. Platform conditions compress: no scene changes, no lighting cues, entrances at a walk that is nearly a run, prose played at conversational speed to a crowd standing in daylight. Original-practices performances in the early seasons characteristically approached the two-hour span more closely than furnished productions in darkened houses, which habitually stretch toward three. The 2009 production's listed running time of three hours with one interval records the repertory era's compromise, an interval for the modern yard's comfort and a pace relaxed toward the modern norm, and the contrast between the two figures is itself a finding: the interval, unknown to the original continuous staging, is the single largest modern addition to the play's shape, and it lands exactly where the original design refuses a breath, across the hinge between the comedy and the catastrophe.

Pace is not a fetish but an interpretation, because the tragedy's moral physics depends on velocity. The lovers marry within a day of meeting, kill within three, and die within five, and the text's own time scheme, double-counted and impossible, is a machine for denying everyone the interval in which reflection happens. A staging that restores the machine's speed restores the play's argument about itself: that the catastrophe is not a fate inscribed in stars but a sequence of decisions taken too fast by people denied the pause that might have saved them, in a city whose institutions, church, law and household, all answer emergency with procedure. The daylight platform is the only common modern stage that enforces the machine's tempo by architecture rather than by directorial willpower, and that enforcement, measurable in minutes, is among the reconstruction's firmest gifts to the play's criticism.

## What the Globe Productions Teach: The Verdict

The findings can be stated as a short ledger. Globe reconstruction productions teach that the tragedy's comedy is load-bearing, written for a crowd's visible response and diminished when that response is privatised by darkness. They teach that its night is a verbal construction, that daylight exposes the verse as the play's lighting designer, and that audiences accept the convention within minutes and then prefer it. They teach that its speed is structural, that the platform enforces the tempo the plot requires, and that intervals and transitions are interpretations with costs. They teach that its social world is legible in cloth, that liveries and mourning and festivity sort the factions faster than exposition can, and that period clothing is therefore analysis rather than costume. They teach that its sound carries recoverable information, that pronunciation experiments repay their weekend. And they teach the limit with equal clarity: that none of this recovers the first performances, whose crowds and meanings are gone, and that the honest name for the enterprise is not resurrection but experiment.

One corollary of the ledger deserves emphasis, because it reorders the play's reputation. The tragedy is usually described as the most lyrical of the canon, a poem that productions illustrate, and the description survives every architecture that treats the verse as commentary on a pictured action. The reconstruction demotes the picture and promotes the transaction: the verse is the action's machinery, building night out of daylight, rooms out of platforms, crowds out of queues of neighbours, and the lyric heights arrive as the machinery's finest operations rather than as decoration upon it. Critics who leave the yard praising the play's toughness, its engineering, its almost farcical mechanical brilliance in the first acts and its procedural horror in the last, are reporting the demotion accurately. The poem did not shrink. The stage that the poem was built for turned out to be an engine, and engines make poems honest.

Set against the cliche the series set out to test, the verdict reverses the usual direction of flattery. The cliche imagines the original stage as a picture to be admired, a round building with a balcony, quaint and complete. The reconstruction restores a working test in the picture's place: a set of conditions that interrogate the text and are interrogated by it, in front of a crowd whose responses are part of the data. The play comes out of the test looking less like a monument and more like a machine, built by a working company for a standing crowd in shared light, its beauties inseparable from its engineering. That is a harder and better thing to know than the cliche offered, and it is knowable only because an American actor refused a plaque, a team of scholars graded their inferences in public, and a company of actors agreed to be rained on in the service of finding out.


## The Chorus at the Platform's Edge: Direct Address as Machinery

The Chorus is the first test any company meets in the reconstructed house, and the one that sorts productions fastest. A single speaker enters through a door, walks to the platform's edge and narrates, asking the crowd to supply with its thoughts whatever the stage cannot show: two households, a city, a voyage to Mantua, a tomb. In a proscenium house the figure reads as an apology, a relic from a poorer theatre excusing its means. On the platform the figure reads as an officer of the occasion, the crowd's own delegate sent forward to manage its imagination, and the difference is architectural. The yard stands close enough to be negotiated with. The galleries lean in. The Chorus of the sonnet prologues speaks as one citizen to a thousand others, and the sonnet form, that most private of shapes in the study, becomes a public instrument, a contract read aloud before witnesses.

The sonnet prologues repay attention in the daylight house for a second reason: they are the play's own theory of its staging, delivered by the staging itself. The first Prologue promises a two-hour traffic and asks patience; the second, at the head of the second act, reports the plot's turn and warns that the new love stands on the old love's grave. Neither speech advances the action. Both tune the crowd, setting its expectations for speed, for secrecy and for the conversion of comedy into mourning that the afternoon will perform. Productions that hurry the Choruses as formalities discover their error at the midpoint, when a crowd that was never contracted cannot feel betrayed. Productions that play them as negotiations, eye to eye with the yard, bank a credit of attention that the tomb scene spends. The reconstruction made the Choruses' function visible by restoring their addressee: not an abstraction called the audience but a standing crowd that can be watched deciding to listen.

Direct address runs deeper into the play than its two formal Choruses, and the daylight house exposes the whole system. The Prince opens and closes the public action by addressing the city, which the yard momentarily is. Mercutio performs for an onstage circle and an offstage one at once, his wit pitched outward like a street entertainer's patter. The Nurse's speeches recruit confidants wherever they find them. Even the lovers, in the orchard's supposed privacy, play their exchanges half to the window and half to the witnesses below, a doubleness that modern darkened staging suppresses by pretending the witnesses away. Tiffany Stern's scholarship on early modern rehearsal and performance documents the system from the production side: actors prepared from parts containing only their own lines and cues, learned address as a primary skill, and played to the house as a matter of craft rather than as an occasional rupture. The reconstruction did not invent direct address. It rehoused it, and the rehousing reveals how much of the tragedy's writing presumes a visible crowd as a permanent member of the cast.

The tragedy's final speech closes the system with a formality that the yard completes. The Prince's closing lines distribute judgement over the whole city, "A glooming peace this morning with it brings" in the speech's own weather, and the platform's edge turns the judgement outward onto the standing spectators, who are sentenced with the houses they have been watching. In a darkened theatre the moment is observed. In the yard it is undergone, briefly and theatrically, and the jig that follows arrives as the pardon that releases the crowd from its sentence. Critics who find the ending's moralising thin on the page have usually read it as statement. The reconstruction recovers it as procedure: a public sentencing requires a public, and the building supplies one.

## Music, Dance and the Feast: The Social Score

Music at the reconstruction is a working system rather than an atmosphere, and the tragedy is scored more deliberately than its reputation for lyric solitude suggests. Musicians occupy the gallery, sound the sennet and the flourish, play the feast onto the floor and the dancers into their figures, and withdraw into the tragedy's most practical joke, the musicians of the fourth act who will not play for the grief-stricken Peter because no fee attends the order. Claire van Kampen's work as the house's composer and music director across the founding seasons treated the score as social information: instruments graded by occasion, dances as the city's diplomacy conducted in figures, the masque at the Capulet feast as the feud's one licensed crossing, choreographed so that Romeo's trespass is at once a courtesy and an invasion. In the daylight house the dancing is not interlude but evidence, the audience watching a household perform its wealth and its discipline while a masked enemy counts the steps from inside the figure.

The feast scene is the reconstruction's favourite demonstration for a reason beyond spectacle. A furnished modern stage must build the feast, and building takes time, money and a scene change. The platform merely fills: servants crossing with plates, torches posted, musicians above, the two houses sorting themselves by livery and by temper, and the room is made by occupancy rather than by carpentry. Tybalt's recognition of Romeo's voice then lands in its proper acoustic, a whisper overheard inside a noise the audience itself has been enjoying, and Capulet's restraint of his nephew plays as household management conducted in public, the host's honour weighed against the guest's insult under the eyes of Verona. The scene's famous product, the lovers' shared sonnet, is staged by the dance's geometry: the figure separates them, returns them, hands them palm to palm in the middle of the crowd, so that the sonnet's pilgrims and saints are danced before they are spoken. Productions in the reconstructed house keep rediscovering that the scene needs nothing added, because the building plus the bodies already constitute the ball.

Dance also governs the tragedy's exit, and the jig's defence has been made above as a matter of crowd management; its musical substance deserves the same care. The jig was a composed, rehearsed company piece, tune, steps and comic business fitted to the afternoon's cast, and the Globe's musicians and dance masters rebuilt the competence from period sources rather than improvising a romp. When the jig follows this tragedy, its competence is the point: the company demonstrates, in coordinated joy, the social body that the feud has spent five acts tearing, and the crowd's applause ratifies a restoration the fiction itself denies its characters. Scholars of early modern stage dance have used the reconstruction's practice as evidence about tempo, stamina and the playing day's shape, since a company that dances a full jig after a tragedy budgets its energies differently from one that merely survives to the curtain call.

The musicians' scene in the fourth act, so often cut in modern productions as comic relief in doubtful taste, recovers its function on the platform. Peter's demand for music at a house of mourning, and the musicians' refusal, is the tragedy's economics stated in miniature: art is labour, labour follows payment, and grief that cannot pay goes unaccompanied. In a house where the musicians have been visible all afternoon, their refusal registers as a small strike witnessed by the crowd that has employed them as witnesses. The scene also cools the platform after the potion speech's terror, restoring the audience's breath before the fifth act's machinery engages, a pacing function that continuous staging makes structural. Cut the scene and the afternoon's rhythm limps; the reconstruction's discipline, which treats the received text as the first director, keeps demonstrating why the limp was a modern invention.

## Rehearsal Economies: Parts, Cues and the Shortness of Preparation

The original company's rehearsal economy is the part of its practice that modern companies find most foreign, and the reconstruction's experiments with it, though less publicised than the pronunciation weekend, produced findings of the same grade. The early modern actor learned a part from a roll containing his own speeches and the cues that triggered them, met the full text in performance, and prepared in days where a modern company counts weeks. Tiffany Stern's research assembled the documentary basis for this economy, and Globe-adjacent practice, including the company's work with cue-based preparation in workshop and the American centre's standing methods, tested its theatrical yields. For this tragedy the yields are specific. A Romeo who knows his cues but not the whole design plays the part as the character lives it, in ignorance of the letter's failure, and the company's collective ignorance becomes an instrument: ensemble scenes carry the static of partial knowledge, entrances arrive as genuine encounters, and the plot's machinery of missed messages reproduces itself in the rehearsal room's own information economy.

The finding cuts in an unexpected direction, because it rehabilitates a kind of roughness that modern polish eliminates. Early audiences expected expert verse-speaking from professionals who had prepared individually and assembled briefly, and the aesthetic that results prizes clarity of cue, strength of address and the athletic management of surprise over the blended ensemble tone that long rehearsal produces. Globe performances shaped by the economy have a characteristic grain: scenes strike like struck matches, soliloquies land as decisions rather than meditations, and the tragedy's speed acquires a second engine inside the acting itself. The 2004 production's much-noted urgency belonged partly to the building, as established above, and partly to a company working near the original preparation rhythm, a variable the house's early seasons took seriously before repertory economics pushed preparation toward the modern norm.

Honesty about grades applies here with special force, because the rehearsal economy is documented unevenly and romanticised easily. The parts survive for a handful of plays, the rehearsal references in letters and lawsuits are scattered, and no document describes the preparation of this tragedy. What the experiments establish is possibility rather than fact: the text is playable, and playable well, under cue-based preparation at high speed, which falsifies the assumption that modern rehearsal length is a precondition of the verse's effects. Falsifying a necessity claim is a modest and genuine scientific yield, and the reconstruction's scholars, Stern among them, have generally claimed no more. The publicists' version, in which actors channel their predecessors' very process, exceeds the record in the familiar direction, and the excess is corrected by the same rule applied throughout this article: test the function, leave the occasion alone.

The doubling chart is the rehearsal economy's visible fossil, and the platform makes it legible. A company sized to the original sharer-and-hired-man structure cannot people Verona's thirty roles without doubling, and the doublings required by the entrances and exits are recoverable by arithmetic from the text itself. When the same actor serves the Prince's watch and the Capulet kitchen, buries the lovers and entertains the feast, the city's unity under its divisions becomes a casting fact rather than a theme. Modern large-cast productions purchase verisimilitude at the price of the point. Reconstruction practice accepts the arithmetic and banks the meaning, one more case of the original conditions generating interpretation as a by-product, the pattern this article has tracked from the wardrobe to the yard.

## The Fight, the Feast and the Tomb: Set Pieces Under Platform Conditions

The tragedy's three great public sequences, the opening brawl, the feast and the tomb, form a graded series under platform conditions, and the reconstruction's handling of the series amounts to a small theory of the play. The brawl opens the afternoon by filling the platform with liveried servants whose quarrel escalates through masters to the Prince, and the platform's openness turns the escalation into urban geography: factions gather from opposed doors, citizens join from the yard's edge in spirit, and the stage picture teaches the crowd to read Verona's politics as crowd behaviour minutes before the Chorus's summary would have sufficed. Fight direction in the daylight house works without the alibis of darkness or scenery, every pass visible to spectators at arm's length, and the finding across productions is that choreographed realism matters less than legibility: a crowd that can follow who threatens whom, and who intervenes, receives the scene's argument, while virtuoso blur loses it. The deaths of Mercutio and Tybalt at the midpoint repeat the brawl with the comedy stripped, the same doors, the same weapons, the laughter dying in the same mouths that produced it, and the platform's refusal to alter its face between the two scenes is the tragedy's visual thesis: one city, one machinery, two outcomes.

The feast has been treated above as the social score's showcase; the tomb requires its own paragraph because it is the sequence where platform conditions face their hardest test and pass it most strangely. The fiction demands a charnel house at night, entered by torchlight, floored with the dead, and the platform supplies a trap, a discovery space, a bier and torches in the afternoon sun. The sequence plays as a procession of entrances, each party arriving with its own light and its own ignorance, Paris strewing flowers, Romeo prising the monument, the Friar too late with lantern and spade, the watch, the fathers, the Prince, until the platform holds the whole city standing among its dead. Daylight, which should expose the fiction's frailty, instead exposes its structure: the audience watches knowledge arrive too late, door by door, in full view of what it already knows, and the dramatic irony becomes architectural. Reviewers of Globe tomb scenes across the decades return to the same word, exposure, and the word is exact: the scene is a public inquest conducted before the verdict, and the yard, standing in the light among the galleries, is empanelled as its jury.

Between the feast and the tomb the platform stages the play's private scenes with the same furniture and no apology, and the bedroom and cell scenes define the experiment's intimacy standard. A bed discovered, a friar's cell announced by a basket of herbs, a chamber constituted by a girl's stillness in a space the crowd has watched serve as a street: the transformations are instantaneous and total, and they depend on the audience's trained collaboration, which the afternoon has spent two hours training. By the fourth act the yard performs its half of the convention without instruction, accepting a platform corner as a vault and a curtain's opening as a revelation. That trained collaboration is perhaps the reconstruction's most transferable finding, because it describes not an Elizabethan peculiarity but a general law of bare-stage theatre that the furnished stage had allowed the modern audience to forget it possessed.

## Teaching the Play in the Round: Education, Access and the Student Crowd

The reconstruction's largest audience never buys a ticket in the ordinary sense: it arrives by coach, in uniform, with worksheets, as part of the project's education programmes, which have made the building a working extension of the English classroom for a generation. For the tragedy, the school's set text across much of the examination system, the encounter has a specific charge. Students meet on the platform the play they have been annotating in extracts, played whole, fast, funny and rude, by actors who address them as citizens of Verona rather than as examinees. Teachers' accounts of the visits repeat a pattern too consistent to be marketing: the comedy's success surprises the students first, then the pace, then the discovery that the verse is intelligible at speed when it is aimed at listeners rather than displayed. The pattern corroborates the yard findings from the laboratory sections, with the least sentimental audience the experiment ever hosts, adolescents under no obligation to reverence.

Access questions belong in the same ledger, because the reconstruction's crowd is chosen by its economics as surely as the original was. The five-pound yard ticket is the project's proudest survival, a standing place priced near the original penny's ratio, and the yard's demographic mix, students, tourists, regulars, the merely curious, is broader than any seated London house achieves. The galleries, by contrast, price toward the heritage market, and the building's sightline compromises, pillars, posts, the yard's weather, distribute discomfort in ways that map imperfectly onto price. Critics of the heritage reading of the project have pressed the point: the reconstruction democratises the floor and commodifies the view, an arrangement the original amphitheatre, with its penny yard and its lords' rooms, would have found familiar. The familiarity is not a defence, but it is a finding, another case of the conditions reproducing a structure their revivers did not design.

The student crowd also supplies the experiment's most honest test of the tragedy's ending. Adult Globe audiences arrive knowing the ending as culture; school audiences frequently know it as plot summary, which is a different and weaker possession. Their response to the tomb scene, the silence that teachers describe falling over a yard of teenagers who have been laughing for two hours, is reported often enough, across enough productions, to count as evidence of the platform's gear-change working on naive spectators exactly as the design intends. No such report can tell anyone what a yard of apprentices felt in 1595. It can and does tell teachers that the play still works on the audience it describes, the young judging the young, when the conditions let it work at its own speed in shared light.


## The All-Male Question and the Wider Programme

The all-male experiment, housed in the wider original-practices programme rather than in this tragedy's main-house stagings, deserves a full hearing because its results discipline the casting debate with evidence instead of taste. The programme's celebrated all-male productions, mounted with the same wardrobe and music disciplines, demonstrated that a male Juliet-figure in the programme's other titles could hold a house, move it and earn its laughter in the right places, and that the convention's costs were as real as its yields: the erotic scenes played as craft, the marriage market estranged into ceremony, the verse's formal pleasures sharpened as its bodily stakes cooled. Critics divided, predictably, along the article's familiar line, some hailing a recovered technology and others a museum of one, but the productions themselves settled the empirical question underneath the rhetoric. The convention works, in the sense that a skilled company can build a complete and affecting performance inside it, and it works at a price, in the sense that specific effects the modern audience values, physical candour between the lovers, the girl's social peril embodied, are surrendered at the door. A technology with known yields and known prices is a tool. The quarrel persists only where either camp insists the tool is a sacrament or a scandal.

Applied back to the tragedy, the experiment's lesson is about where a production should spend its one great act of casting faith. The 2004 staging spent its faith on sound and cloth, holding casting at the modern norm, and produced the pronunciation findings. The 2009 staging spent its faith on age, casting the lovers near the text's own figures, and produced the generational repricing that its reviewers recorded. A hypothetical main-house staging that spent its faith on the boy-heroine convention would produce findings of a third kind, about the part's construction for a boy's breath and skills, and the programme's evidence suggests they would be genuine findings rather than novelties. The laboratory metaphor survives the thought experiment precisely because the variables are separable: each production in the reconstruction's history can be described by which conditions it held, which it varied and what the variation disclosed, and the tragedy's Globe record, read that way, is a small archive of controlled observations rather than a shelf of souvenirs.

Gender returns through the wardrobe as well as through casting, because period clothing codes the body's social meaning before the actor speaks. Tiramani's research demonstrated that the original wardrobe distinguished rank, occupation, marital state and mourning with a precision that the dialogue assumes and rarely explains, and a modern audience, taught by one feast scene, reads a widow's weeds, a servant's livery and a bride's finery as fluently as it reads a screen's costume plot. The fluency is the finding: the play's social information system is recoverable in its material half even where its casting half is modernised, and productions that treat dress as decoration discard half the evidence the first audiences used to follow a plot of households, portions and alliances. Editors annotate the same system from the other side, glossing the clothing references that readers can no longer see, and the reconstruction's wardrobe archive has become a reference collection for that annotation, one more channel through which the laboratory feeds the study.

## Failure Modes: When the Experiment Becomes a Brand

The reconstruction's failure modes are instructive because they are the negations of its findings, and the tragedy's productions exhibit each of them somewhere in the record. The first failure is the heritage slump, in which the building's pleasures, the oak, the thatch, the sky, the pasty and the gift shop, absorb the audience's attention and the play becomes the afternoon's entertainment inside a famous object. The second is the purity contest, in which companies or critics grade productions by their distance from an imaginary complete authenticity, punishing every concession, the women, the interval, the sprinkler, as betrayal, and thereby mistaking the protocol for the promise. The third is the house-style rut, in which the genre the experiment created, daylight, directness, speed, the jig, hardens into a formula applied without curiosity, so that the conditions stop interrogating the text and start merely certifying it. Each failure has been named inside the project's own literature, by its scholars and its artistic directors, which is the strongest evidence that the laboratory self-image is more than a slogan: institutions that can describe their own failure modes in print are still running the experiment.

The tragedy's record suggests the antidote is rotation of the variable under test. Seasons that varied pronunciation, then casting age, then design discipline, then the indoor house, kept producing findings because each season asked a new question of the same play. Seasons that repeated the house style without a question produced afternoons that audiences enjoyed and scholarship cannot cite. The distinction generalises beyond Bankside to every reconstruction in the family and to every bare-stage company that borrows the conditions: the conditions are a method only while they are aimed at a question, and they decay into a mannerism the moment the aim is dropped. That is the final form of this article's verdict, and it is a hopeful one, because it makes the experiment's future a matter of curiosity rather than of faith. The building will stand for a century. The questions are inexhaustible. The play, which has survived every architecture its centuries built for it, is ready to be asked again.


## Soliloquy Before Witnesses: The Visible Audience as Scene Partner

The soliloquy is where the reconstruction's difference from the darkened house cuts deepest, and the tragedy's great solitary speeches supply the demonstration one by one. Juliet's potion speech, the longest sustained solo in the play, is in the study a bravura aria of fear, a girl imagining the vault's horrors in accelerating detail until she drinks to escape her own imagination. In the yard the speech is a public ordeal conducted before witnesses who cannot intervene, and the actor plays it outward, recruiting the crowd's faces as the mirror in which the character checks her terror. The effect reported across productions is a peculiar intensification: the audience, addressed and unable to answer, experiences its own helplessness as the scene's subject, and the drinking lands as an act performed upon the witnesses as much as upon the character. Nothing in the text changed. The light changed, and the light revealed that the speech was always built for witnesses, its rhetoric the rhetoric of a mind thinking in company, because the company that first heard it thought in public as a matter of daily life.

Romeo's speeches divide under the same examination into kinds the darkened house flattens. The orchard apostrophes are wooing conducted at a window with an audience smuggled inside it, and the daylight yard accepts the smuggling as the scene's joke and its danger at once. The Mantua speech, on the news of the death, is a decision taken aloud, its rhetoric a man persuading himself before witnesses who know the persuasion rests on an error, and the dramatic irony acquires a physical address: the crowd holds the truth the speaker lacks, and shared light lets him almost see it holding. The tomb speeches complete the series, public laments delivered over bodies in a space the crowd has watched become a vault, and their formality, the balanced periods, the apostrophes to death and earth, reads in the yard as ceremony rather than as private grief, which is the reading the Prince's arrival ratifies when he converts the ceremony into an inquest without changing the room.

Mercutio is the system's clown and its proof. The Queen Mab speech, a set piece that modern staging often hurries as decoration, is in the yard a street performance inside the fiction, a wit entertaining his friends and, through them, the standing crowd that his actor can see enjoying him. The speech's length, its acceleration, its turn from bawdy catalogue to something colder, are timed to a live response, and the response is available in daylight as it is not in the dark, where laughter arrives anonymised and late. When the same voice, an hour later, curses both houses with the crowd's laughter still warm in its ears, the platform completes the tragedy's cruellest piece of staging arithmetic: the play's best entertainer dies cursing his audience's city, and the audience, which has been cast as that city all afternoon, takes the curse personally. Darkened houses admire the construction. The yard undergoes it, and the difference between admiration and undergoing is the whole case for the reconstruction stated in a single scene pair.


## The Interval Question: A Modern Addition with Ancient Costs

The interval deserves a section of its own because it is the reconstruction's most normalised concession and its least examined. The original staging ran continuously, the Prologue's two hours unbroken by any pause in which the crowd could leave its role, and the tragedy's design presumes the continuity: the acts interlock like gear teeth, each scene's exit feeding the next scene's entrance, the tempo compounding without a release valve. The early original-practices seasons honoured the continuity and reported its effects on attention: a standing crowd held past the hour mark enters a state of collective endurance in which the catastrophe lands on bodies already tired, already weathered, already implicated, and the ending's exhaustion is shared in fact rather than represented. The repertory era restored the interval for comfort, commerce and the yard's bladders, and the restoration was defensible on every ground except the play's. Placed, as it usually is, near the midpoint, the interval pardons the audience at the exact moment the design intends no pardon, letting the laughter of the comedy be shrugged off in the bar queue instead of curdling in place into the horror of the third act's close.

The finding generalises into the reconstruction's most portable lesson for companies that will never see Bankside. Continuity is a meaning. Any staging of the tragedy, in any architecture, decides by its pauses what kind of event the catastrophe is: a fate beheld across a comfortable distance, refreshed by interval drinks, or a machine whose gears the audience has ridden without stepping off. The platform did not invent the question, but by running the play both ways across its decades it converted the question from a preference into a measurement, complete with running times, audience reports and the testimony of actors who have felt the gear-change land in a held crowd and in a pardoned one. That conversion, from taste to evidence, is the laboratory's signature achievement, and the interval is its clearest case.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which production of the play opened the rebuilt Globe in 1997?

The rebuilt Globe did not open with this tragedy. The theatre opened in June 1997 with Henry V, played as an original-practices production with artistic director Mark Rylance in the title part, so the building and the original-practices method were announced together from the first season. Romeo and Juliet entered the reconstruction's history as a repertory title and received its landmark original-practices staging later, in 2004. The distinction matters for the record: the play was an honoured guest in the new house, not its founding exhibit, and its Globe history is a history of productions across three decades rather than of a single opening gesture.

### Q: Who played the lovers in the Globe's 2004 original-practices production?

Tom Burke played Romeo and Kananu Kirimi played Juliet in Tim Carroll's 2004 production, staged in the Season of Star-Crossed Lovers with hand-made period clothing, period music and dance, and a mixed company of men and women. John McEnery played Friar Laurence. The production's notices praised its urgency and speed while dividing over the central passion, which several reviewers found coltish rather than consuming. That division is itself instructive: the speed belongs largely to the building and the method, while the chemistry of a particular pair of lovers belongs to casting and rehearsal, variables no staging discipline can guarantee.

### Q: What was tested during the Original Pronunciation weekend in 2004?

A handful of performances within the 2004 run were played in Original Pronunciation, the reconstructed sound of the play's first English, prepared with linguist David Crystal. The test was whether sound-pattern information lost in modern stage speech would become audible again: rhymes that limp in received pronunciation, puns dulled by shifted vowels, the click of the lovers' shared sonnet as its rhymes close. Listeners and the company's own accounts reported exactly those recoveries, along with a faster, more muscular delivery. The experiment yielded a band of probability about the text's sound, not a recording of 1595, and Crystal's book on the project frames the result in precisely those terms.

### Q: How many spectators can the rebuilt Globe hold compared with the original?

The rebuilt theatre holds roughly fourteen hundred people, with about seven hundred standing in the yard, against estimates of around three thousand for the original house. The reduction was forced by modern safety regulation, wider exits and the limits of the available site, and it is one of the reconstruction's acknowledged concessions. The consequence for the experiment is precise: crowd proximity and crowd behaviour can be tested, since the yard still wraps the platform and the galleries still rise on three sides, but crowd density and crowd pressure operate at reduced scale, and any finding that depends on the press of three thousand bodies remains an inference rather than a result.

### Q: Why does the rebuilt Globe stand near the original site rather than on it?

The original plot on Bankside is occupied and protected, lying beneath Georgian housing and a modern road that cannot be excavated or demolished for a theatre. The reconstruction therefore stands some two hundred metres away, in the same district, on the same bank of the river, within sight of the original ground. The displacement is the project's largest single concession and its most honest emblem: even the ground beneath the experiment is approximate. Everything the building claims rests on that candour. A reconstruction that admitted its site was borrowed could be trusted when it graded its other inferences, and the project's scholarship has generally maintained the same standard.

### Q: What does the term original practices cover at the Globe?

In the Globe's usage the term covers the material disciplines of production rather than a general claim of time travel: hand-made clothing built by period methods under costume historian Jenny Tiramani, music on period instruments under Claire van Kampen, reconstructed dance, and at the experiment's edge reconstructed pronunciation. Its core rule is subtraction, no amplification, no lighting plot, no scenery beyond the building itself. Its stated limit is equally part of the definition: behaviour, rehearsal culture and audience response cannot be replicated, because they leave no artefacts. The term names a laboratory protocol for the conditions that evidence can support, and the quarrel around it begins where marketing language forgets the limit.

### Q: How does shared daylight change the orchard scene?

Shared daylight turns the orchard scene from an overheard privacy into a public act of conjuring. The fiction requires night, a garden wall, a window above and a watcher below, while the yard stands in afternoon sun, so the darkness must be manufactured by the verse and accepted by a crowd that can see itself accepting it. Romeo's address divides between the window and the visible spectators, Juliet's speeches play to a girl's supposed solitude before hundreds of witnesses, and the scene's danger, a feud listening in the dark, becomes a tension the audience embodies rather than observes. The reconstruction's finding is that the convention takes minutes to establish and then outperforms illusion, because complicity binds tighter than darkness.

### Q: What role does the closing jig play after the tragedy?

The jig, danced by the whole company after the final deaths, restores a documented habit of the early stage, in which dancing closed performances across genres as a release and a social reconnection. In the reconstructed house it performs precise work: the crowd that has stood as mourners is returned to itself as a community, the actors step out of the fiction's wreckage in full view, and the afternoon resumes. Modern spectators often report the jig as the moment the experiment either justified itself or broke faith, which measures how completely darkened-house taste has fused tragedy with silence and blackout. The original stage made no such fusion, and the jig is the evidence danced.

### Q: Who played the lovers in the Globe's 2009 production?

Adetomiwa Edun played Romeo and Ellie Kendrick played Juliet in Dominic Dromgoole's 2009 production, which opened the Young Hearts season, with Rawiri Paratene as Friar Laurence and Penny Layden as the Nurse. Kendrick's age, close to the text's figure of nearly fourteen, became the production's most discussed feature, repricing the marriage plot as a transaction conducted over a child and sharpening every adult scene around her. The production was filmed and circulated widely on disc and streaming, becoming the version through which much of the English-speaking classroom meets the tragedy in performance, an afterlife that carries the house style far beyond the yard.

### Q: What design intervention divided reviewers in the 2009 production?

Designer Simon Daw placed a spiral staircase at the centre of the platform, where it served as the elevation for the window scenes, the route down toward the vault and a sculptural emblem of the plot's descending coil. The staircase appears in no early text and in no account of the original stage house, whose upper level and trap already supply the functions it imitated. Some reviewers found it elegant; others objected that it imported a modern designer's solution into a building whose whole argument is the sufficiency of the original furniture. The production's energies largely absorbed the import, but the objection stands as the authenticity debate in miniature, a reminder that every addition spends the experiment's capital.

### Q: How does the Sam Wanamaker Playhouse extend the reconstruction experiment?

The Playhouse, opened in 2014 beside the amphitheatre, reconstructs the indoor half of the original company's year: a candlelit hall of some three hundred and forty seats modelled on the Jacobean indoor playhouses, where the King's Men played in winter and where the repertory met a wealthier, seated audience. Its conditions invert the yard's: enclosure instead of sky, real darkness instead of described night, flame that gilds faces and must be tended as part of the performance's labour. Set beside the Globe it corrects the lazy habit of treating the amphitheatre as all of early staging, and its frank composite evidence, drawn from drawings of uncertain purpose, models the candour about inference that the whole project at its best maintains.

### Q: What does Stephen Purcell's audience research show about the yard?

Stephen Purcell's research on Globe audiences, built from observation and interviews across seasons, shows a spectatorship that understands itself as participatory and treats the yard's licence to react as a contract rather than as misbehaviour. His audiences police their own boundaries, quieting the disruptive and schooling newcomers, and they read their own visibility as part of the performance's machinery. The portrait cuts against two mythologies at once: the heritage fantasy of a boisterous crowd reborn from 1599, and the purist complaint that modern enthusiasm is merely performed for its own sake. What the yard produces, on his evidence, is an unusually alert audience, aware of itself as a component of the show.

### Q: Why do Globe productions of the play tend to run faster than other stagings?

The platform enforces speed by refusing every device that slows a furnished production down. There is no scenery to shift, no lighting state to fade, no transition music to compose over a change of place, so scenes succeed each other at the pace of entrances and exits through two doors, with place announced in a line and abandoned in a breath. Original-practices performances in the early seasons approached the Prologue's promised two hours more closely than darkened-house productions that stretch toward three. The pace is an interpretation as well as a measurement: the tragedy's catastrophe is engineered as acceleration, a sequence of decisions taken too fast, and the building restores the tempo at which that argument makes sense.

### Q: What is Andrew Gurr's defence of the reconstruction?

Andrew Gurr, the theatre historian whose research underwrote the building's design, defended the reconstruction as experimental archaeology. On his account the design embodies the best convergent evidence for the original playing conditions, drawn from excavation, contract, drawing and travellers' reports, and playing in the building tests that account the way a rebuilt ship tests a hull design, by floating or sinking. The defence is careful about its object: what is tested is a set of conditions, the geometry, light, clothing and crowd within which the plays were built to work, not any single lost afternoon. Gurr's convergence principle holds that conditions can be recovered within stated margins even where carpentry details remain disputed, and the productions are the margins at work.

### Q: What is W. B. Worthen's objection to claims of recovered authenticity?

W. B. Worthen argues that a performance cannot borrow authority from a reconstructed origin, because every performance is made by the conventions, training and economics of its own moment, and its meanings arise in the event between stage and audience rather than descending from a building or a text. On his account, talk of recovering the original force of a staging mistakes where performance meaning lives. The objection does not deny that the reconstruction yields knowledge; it denies that the knowledge is the past's own voice speaking. This article's verdict accepts the objection at the level of events while holding, with Gurr, that conditions can be tested: the building examines the play's machinery without resurrecting its first afternoons.

### Q: How do editors use the Globe's productions as evidence?

Performance-oriented editors treat documented productions as evidence about what the text affords. Jill Levenson's Oxford edition reads the play through its stage history, and under that dispensation the Globe's archive, its doubling patterns, transition timings, tested cruxes and casting experiments, becomes citable material: a staging choice that works in the yard is a datum about the writing, and one that fails is a datum too. The traffic runs both ways, since productions build on editions, their texts, notes and introductions, so the tragedy's modern life is a public conversation between editing and staging. That conversation, sustained over three decades of reconstruction, is among the era's largest contributions to the play's study.

### Q: Does the rebuilt Globe use the upper level as a balcony?

The rebuilt house uses its upper gallery level for the window scenes, as the original stage did, and no part of the building is a balcony in the scenic sense the word later acquired. Juliet appears at a working gallery window, with the tiring house behind her and the street of the platform below, which restores the scene's actual geography: a household window above a public space, with a feud listening underneath. The scenic balcony, a furnished platform projecting from a painted wall, belongs to later centuries of staging. The reconstruction's correction is physical rather than pedantic, and audiences grasp it at sight, because a girl at a window is in danger in a way that a figure on a monument is not.

### Q: How does period clothing function as interpretation in these productions?

Period clothing at the Globe is information before it is decoration. Hand-made garments in silk, wool and linen, built under Jenny Tiramani's wardrobe research, sort the stage into households at sight: liveries mark servants as their masters' property, festive dress displays a house's wealth at its feast, mourning bands reprice a scene before a word is spoken. A modern crowd learns to read the code within a scene, as fluently as it reads uniforms in ordinary life, and the feud becomes visible as a war of dressed allegiances rather than an abstraction in the dialogue. The clothing also disciplines the actors, since structured garments govern posture, gesture and the felt geography of rank, making the wardrobe the production's first act of interpretation.

### Q: What can a reconstruction production not prove, however careful it is?

It cannot prove anything about the inner experience of the original audiences, because that experience is the one component no evidence can rebuild. The crowd of the middle 1590s brought reflexes formed by a city of households, honour codes, apprenticeships and public violence, and it heard the feud's vocabulary as the furniture of daily life. No experiment can restore those reflexes to a modern yard of students and devotees, and no finding about laughter, silence or pace in the rebuilt house transfers automatically to 1595. The discipline this article follows grades every claim accordingly: functions tested, occasions unrecovered. A production that states the limit plainly earns the right to its findings; one that blurs the limit sells a diorama.

### Q: How should a student use the InsightCrunch Globe profile in an essay?

The InsightCrunch Globe profile serves an essay best as a grading instrument rather than as a gallery of facts. Its rows separate what reconstruction productions demonstrate from what they cannot touch, so an argument can be built by taking a single row, the daylight, the clothing, the pronunciation weekend, and following its evidence through a scene analysis, citing the tested function as the claim and the unrecovered occasion as the limit. Essays that observe the distinction write about the Globe with the precision the project's own scholarship models. Essays that ignore it repeat the marketing claim the scholars refused, that a building can hand a modern audience an Elizabethan afternoon, and the profile exists to make that error visible before it is committed.
