A couple in Indore and a couple in Kochi can each decide, in the same week, to adopt a child, and discover that they are not entering the same legal process at all. Adoption law in India is not one system but two, and the door a family walks through is determined by the religion of the parents rather than by anything about the child. One door leads into a regulated public system built by the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, in which the parents register on a national portal, submit to a home study, wait in a seniority queue, accept a referred child they did not choose by name, and receive an adoption order from a District Magistrate. The other door, open only to Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs, leads into the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956, under which an adoption is completed by the physical act of giving and taking the child, with no registration, no home study, no queue and no order from any authority at all. Both doors produce a child who is, in law, the child of the adoptive parents for all purposes. Everything between the decision and that result differs.

This article is written for the person standing in front of those two doors. It explains who may adopt under each system, which children each system can reach, what the procedure involves stage by stage, where the time actually goes, what can be challenged and where, and why the regulated system is simultaneously the only safe route for a stranger's child and the source of the longest waits in Indian family law. It takes the position the record supports: the two systems differ less in the status they confer than in the scrutiny they apply before conferring it, and almost every controversy in this field, the queues, the pendency before District Magistrates, the children in institutions who never become available, the illegal placements that bypass the law altogether, is a controversy about that difference in scrutiny and about where in the pipeline it should sit.

## Two systems, one child: the shape of adoption law in India

The secular system is a creature of the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, which is cited by its short title year but was published as Act 2 of 2016, having received assent on 31 December 2015 and commenced on 15 January 2016. Chapter VIII of that Act, running from Section 56 to Section 73, is a self-contained adoption code for orphan, abandoned and surrendered children, and it applies to any person who wishes to adopt such a child irrespective of religion. Section 56 grounds adoption under the Act in the adoption regulations framed by the Central Adoption Resource Authority, and provides expressly that adoption under the Act is in addition to, and not in derogation of, the personal law route: a Hindu adoption made under the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 stands outside the Chapter VIII machinery by the Act's own terms. The architecture of the whole statute, its two tracks for children in need of care and protection and children in conflict with law, and the place of the adoption chapter inside it are treated in [the Juvenile Justice Act 2015 explained](/2016/05/15/juvenile-justice-act-2015-explained/), which owns the parent statute; this article owns the adoption process that the statute creates.

The personal law system is older and structurally opposite. The Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956, Act 78 of 1956, codified the adoption law of the four communities it governs and made adoption a private legal act performed by the parties themselves. Its conditions concern capacity: who may adopt, who may give a child in adoption, and who may be adopted. It creates no agency, maintains no register of children, and asks no official to approve the match in advance. The State enters later, if at all, when a dispute reaches a court or when a registered adoption deed is produced as evidence. Between these two systems stands a third and older instrument that is not an adoption law at all: the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890, Act 8 of 1890, under which a person whom no adoption statute serves may be appointed guardian of a child by a court, acquiring custody and responsibility without acquiring parentage.

The wider frame in which all three instruments sit, the constitutional direction that the State secure conditions for children to develop in freedom and dignity, the commissions that monitor child rights, and the neighbouring statutes on sexual offences, child labour and juvenile justice, is mapped in [the guide to child protection laws in India](/2016/04/01/child-protection-laws-india-guide/), which places adoption among the protective statutes; the account here stays inside the adoption routes themselves and follows a child and a family through each of them.


## How the secular system was built: from guidelines to a statutory code

The Chapter VIII code of 2015 was the third draft of the secular system, and its predecessors explain its texture. The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2000 first placed adoption of orphan, abandoned and surrendered children on a statutory footing as a measure of rehabilitation, administered through recognised agencies under guidelines framed by the central authority of the day; before that, the field had been governed by the Lakshmi Kant Pandey directions and by executive guidelines alone. The guidelines era had a structural weakness that the whole design of the 2015 Act answers: guidelines bind agencies that choose to be recognised, and bind no one else, so the protective procedure applied precisely to the placements least likely to need it, while the private market in children operated outside any text. The 2015 Act closed that gap by giving the Authority a statutory basis, writing the offences for adoption outside the prescribed procedure into the penal chapter, and grounding the process in regulations with statutory force, made by the Authority under the Act rather than in administrative instructions. The Adoption Regulations, 2017 were the first exercise of that power, building the CARINGS queue, the home study discipline and the eligibility conditions in the form the 2022 Regulations inherited and revised; the 2022 revision's signature changes, the tightened age bands at the youngest end, the widened channelling of larger families, the timelines for document verification and upload of legally free children, and the mandatory counselling obligations, are best read as corrections from five years of operating the queue, directed at the points where the first regulations had let time leak out of the process.

The personal law side of the dual system has an opposite history, of a code written once and then left alone. The Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 was one of the Hindu code enactments of the nineteen fifties, and it displaced a classical law of adoption whose motives had been substantially religious and dynastic, the securing of a son for the lineage and its rites, with a secularised capacity code whose concern is the child's transfer between families; the Act's survival, essentially unamended in its adoption chapter, while the secular system was rebuilt three times, is part of the disparity the series thesis identifies. One system is maintained like infrastructure, audited by annual reports and corrected by regulation; the other rests like a conveyance, drafted for a world of known families and joint property, and questioned only when a succession suit forces a court to open it.


## The secular regime: Chapter VIII and the institutions it builds

The first thing to understand about the secular regime is that it does not begin with the parents. It begins with the child, and with a legal determination that the child is available to be adopted at all. A child enters the adoption pool only through the care track of the Juvenile Justice Act: a child found abandoned, a child surrendered by parents or a guardian before a Child Welfare Committee, or a child whose parents are dead with no one to care for the child, is produced before the Committee, placed in a child care institution or a Specialised Adoption Agency, and becomes adoptable only when the Committee declares the child legally free for adoption under Section 38. That declaration is the hinge of the entire system. Before it, the child is a child in need of care and protection whom the State must restore to family where family exists. After it, the child is a person whom the law permits strangers to raise as their own.

### Which children can be adopted through CARA?

Only an orphan, abandoned or surrendered child whom a Child Welfare Committee has declared legally free for adoption can be adopted through the Central Adoption Resource Authority. Until the declaration is made, no agency may offer the child for adoption and no parent may be referred the child, whatever the child's circumstances in an institution.

The declaration follows an inquiry tailored to the child's route into care: for an abandoned child, an attempt to trace the parents; for a surrendered child, a reconsideration period for the surrendering parent; for an orphan, verification that no parent or guardian survives to care for the child. The Act sets the Committee a clock for this determination, two months from the child's production for a child up to two years of age and four months for an older child, and the adoption regulations frame the surrounding duties: institutions must report children who appear eligible, the District Child Protection Unit must enter the child's details on the designated portal, and the legally free status must be uploaded within a short prescribed period once declared. In practice the declaration stage is where the system's arithmetic is decided, because the number of children who clear it in a year is a small fraction of the number of children living in child care institutions, most of whom are not orphans at all but children placed in care by living families in distress, children whose parents visit, or children whose restoration is still being attempted. The pool of adoptable children is therefore not a measure of how many children lack parental care. It is a measure of how many children's legal status has been finally settled, and those are very different numbers.

Around the declaration, Chapter VIII builds a chain of institutions, each with a defined station. The Specialised Adoption Agency is the licensed institution, ordinarily a registered voluntary organisation or a government home recognised for the purpose, that holds children declared legally free, prepares their child study reports, receives prospective parents for the home study, and shepherds a matched case to the order. The State Adoption Resource Agency, which Section 67 requires every State Government to establish, supervises and coordinates adoption within the state, recognises and inspects the specialised agencies, and maintains the state's side of the record. The District Child Protection Unit, the district arm of the child protection system, verifies documents, supports the Committee, and enters and updates children's records. The Central Adoption Resource Authority stands at the apex: constituted as a statutory body by Section 68 of the Act, governed through a Steering Committee under Section 69, and charged with promoting in-country adoption, regulating inter-country adoption, framing the adoption regulations, accrediting agencies, and operating the centralised system through which referrals are made. Its lineage is older than its statutory status. The Authority began in 1990 as the Central Adoption Resource Agency, an autonomous body created in the wake of the Supreme Court's directions on inter-country adoption, and the 2015 Act converted that administrative creation into a statutory regulator, which is why its functions read like those of a market supervisor: it licenses the participants, runs the matching exchange, polices the boundary between lawful placement and trafficking, and reports annually on the state of the system.

### How does the seniority queue in CARINGS work?

Registration on CARINGS, the Child Adoption Resource Information and Guidance System, fixes a prospective parent's seniority, and children are referred strictly in that order within the preferences the parents have recorded. A referred profile may be reserved and then accepted or declined; declining returns the parents to the queue without erasing their registration.

The system is built against shopping: referrals come as the queue and the available children dictate, not as the parents browse. The home study report must be revalidated every three years, and parents who have received no referral within three years have their seniority counted from the original registration date, a protection against being overtaken indefinitely by the queue's own movement.

The portal is the single doorway. Every adoption of an orphan, abandoned or surrendered child by a resident Indian parent under Section 58 moves through registration on CARINGS, document verification by the District Child Protection Unit, a home study by the Specialised Adoption Agency the parents choose in their state, the wait in seniority, referral, acceptance, a period of pre-adoption foster care with the child placed in the family's physical custody, the application for the adoption order, and the order itself, after which the agency obtains the child's birth certificate bearing the adoptive parents' names and conducts post-adoption follow-up. There is no lawful side entrance: Section 80 of the Act makes adoption of a child in contravention of the prescribed procedure an offence, and Section 81 punishes the sale and procurement of children, so the hospital handover, the private arrangement with an intermediary, and the direct taking of a child from a birth mother outside the Committee and agency process are not informal adoptions but crimes, however benign the family's intentions.


## One Act, many districts: state and district variation

The secular system's uniform code runs through machinery whose quality varies by district, and a rights account that ignored the variation would misdescribe the remedy a family actually holds. The State Adoption Resource Agency, established under Section 67 in every state, is the point at which the national framework becomes a state system: it recognises and inspects the Specialised Adoption Agencies, coordinates the pool within the state, and answers for the state's performance to the Authority. The District Child Protection Unit is the point at which the system touches the ground: it verifies the parents' documents on the five day timeline the 2022 Regulations prescribe, supports the Committee's inquiries, and enters the children's records on which the pool depends. A family in a district where the Unit is staffed and the Committee sits at its prescribed strength experiences the same statute as a sequence of kept clocks; a family in a district where the Unit is an additional charge on an already loaded officer experiences it as a sequence of adjournments, and both experiences are accurate reports of the same law. The Supreme Court's direction of November 2023 that every district have a Specialised Adoption Agency by 31 January 2024 was aimed at the most basic form of the variation, the district with no agency at all, in which the pool's children and the queue's families are both administratively invisible.

The variation also shapes the declaration bottleneck regionally. States differ in the number of children their institutions hold, in the speed at which their Committees complete inquiries, and in the practice by which institutions report children who may be eligible, so the national mismatch, roughly two thousand legally free children against more than thirty thousand waiting parents, is an aggregate of district mismatches, some of them far more severe than the average and some, for older children and children with special needs, running in the opposite direction, with children waiting for families rather than families waiting for children. The Authority's published responses to this geography include the identification exercises the Court ordered, the recognition of additional agencies, and the emphasis on foster adoption, each of which is an attempt to move children from the parts of the system where they are stuck to the parts where families are waiting. For the individual applicant the practical consequence is modest but real: the choice of agency at registration, within the applicant's state, and the preferences recorded for the child's state of placement can both affect the wait materially, and advice at the registration stage, from the agency and the state resource agency, is worth more than any general statement of average timelines, because there is no average district in which any actual adoption takes place.


## The declaration file: how a child becomes legally free for adoption

The least understood and most decisive stage of the secular process is the one in which no prospective parent appears at all. The child arrives before a Child Welfare Committee as a foundling, a lost child, a surrendered infant, or a child whose parents have died, and the Committee's first duty is not to find the child a family but to satisfy itself, on inquiry, that the child's family, in the legal sense, is gone. Section 38 governs the terminal step of that inquiry, the declaration that the child is legally free for adoption, and the structure of the section is a set of protections against a mistake that can never be corrected, because an adoption, unlike foster care, is permanent.

For an abandoned child, the inquiry is an attempt to find the parents. The child is produced before the Committee, placed in a child care institution or a Specialised Adoption Agency for interim care, and the inquiry machinery is set in motion: the police report where a child has been found in circumstances suggesting abandonment, the tracing effort through the child's origin details, the publicity through which a family might learn that its child is with the State, and the waiting period during which the Committee satisfies itself that no claimant exists. For a surrendered child, the inquiry is different in kind: parents or a guardian appear and execute a surrender deed before the Committee, stating that they are unable to care for the child, and Section 35 gives them a reconsideration period of two months from the execution of the deed, during which the surrender is not final and the parent may reclaim the child. The deed is the operative document, and the 2022 Regulations frame its execution with procedural care, certification before the Committee, inquiry into whether the surrender is informed and uncoerced, and attention to the particular vulnerability of unmarried mothers and trafficked women, who may be surrendering under the pressure of precisely the circumstances that make State care the safest place for the child. For an orphan child, the inquiry is evidentiary: proof that both parents are dead, or that the surviving parent is incapable of care, and that no fit person or extended family member can be found to take the child, because restoration to kinship, even distant kinship, is preferred to the permanent severance adoption effects.

Only when the Committee is satisfied on these inquiries does Section 38 permit the declaration, and the declaration is an order with reasons, not a notation in a file. Its effect is to change the child's legal status from a child in need of care and protection to a person available for permanent placement, and it is that order that the institution reports, the Unit enters on the portal, and the regulations require to be uploaded within the prescribed short period, so that a legally free child in a small district institution becomes visible to the national queue. The Act's clocks, two months for a child up to two years and four months above that age, are routinely missed in practice, and the reasons are administrative rather than legal: Committees with part-time members and heavy dockets, tracing efforts that stall when the police do not respond, institutions that fail to report, and units that enter data late. Every month of delay in a declaration is a month of a child's institutional life that adoption's permanency logic should have ended, and the cumulative effect is the pool mismatch the later sections quantify. It also explains the Supreme Court's choice of remedy in the Temple of Healing proceedings: the directions of November 2023, bimonthly identification drives in institutions and an agency in every district, are aimed at the undeclared file, not at the waiting list, because the waiting list is doing the one thing it is designed to do, which is rationing by seniority, while the undeclared file is doing the one thing no design contemplates, which is holding a child out of the pool by default of process.

Two neighbouring dispositions show the declaration's weight by contrast. Foster care under Section 44 places a child with a family temporarily, and sponsorship under the Act's sponsorship provisions supports a family to keep its child, and both are reversible. The declaration is not. A foster parent may, after the prescribed period of foster care, seek to adopt the child in foster care, a route the 2022 Regulations emphasise and simplify precisely because the bond already exists and the child is already known; but until the Committee's declaration, no foster care bond and no private arrangement can ripen into adoption on their own, which is why the regulations treat the declaration as the single legal event that moves a child from the care system's temporary instruments to the adoption system.


## The other side of the file: surrender, consent and the birth family

Every adoption in the secular system has a second family in its file, and the regulations' treatment of that family is a measure of the system's seriousness, because a process that is careful only with the applicants will produce adoptions that are efficient and indefensible. Surrender is the point of maximum vulnerability. A parent surrendering a child may be acting under poverty, stigma, family pressure or coercion, and Section 35's structure answers that vulnerability in the only way available to it, by slowing the act down: the surrender is made by executing a deed before the Child Welfare Committee, the Committee inquires into the circumstances, and the surrendering parent carries a two month period in which the decision may be reconsidered and the child reclaimed, after which the surrender hardens into the declaration of legal freedom. The reconsideration period is not a courtesy; it is the system's recognition that consent given in a crisis, in a hospital corridor or under family instruction, is exactly the consent most likely to be regretted and most likely to have been manufactured, and that a short, real window for reversal costs the pool almost nothing and protects the adoption's legitimacy for life.

The Act's governing principles frame the same concern at the level of the whole statute. Section 3 of the Juvenile Justice Act lays down the principles that govern every decision under it, including the principle that the primary responsibility of bringing up a child lies with the biological family, the principle of best interest, the principle of family responsibility, the principle of safety, and the principle that institutionalisation is a measure of last resort. Read against adoption, those principles explain the procedure's shape: the tracing inquiry honours the biological family's primacy, the declaration records that the primacy has been exhausted, the home study tests best interest on the receiving side, and the preference for restoration, foster care and sponsorship over institutional care marks adoption as the deliberate last instrument, to be used when the child's need for permanence outweighs every temporary arrangement. They also explain the design's treatment of information: the surrendering parent's identity is protected within the file because a surrender system that exposed its mothers would deter the surrenders that bring children into lawful care and would push the same mothers toward the hospital handover and the intermediary, the two channels the offences exist to close. The birth family's remedies are correspondingly narrow by design: within the reconsideration window, the surrender may be withdrawn; after the declaration and the order, the adoption's finality forecloses reopening, a harshness the law accepts because the alternative, a permanently provisional adoption, would deny the child the very security the system exists to create. Where a surrender was procured by fraud or coercion, the remedy lies against the transaction through the courts, with the child's settled life as the weightiest fact in the balance, which is the law's recurring posture in this field: status first, and challenges to it entertained, where they are entertained at all, through the narrow gate of illegality rather than the wide gate of regret.


## Who may adopt: the eligibility conditions in the Adoption Regulations, 2022

Eligibility under the secular regime lives in two places. Section 57 of the Act states the principle: prospective adoptive parents must be physically fit, financially sound, mentally alert and highly motivated to adopt, and must satisfy the conditions the chapter and the regulations prescribe, including the express bar that a single male is not eligible to adopt a girl child. The operative detail sits in the Adoption Regulations, 2022, notified by the Central Adoption Resource Authority on 23 September 2022 in exercise of the regulation-making power the Act gives it, superseding the Adoption Regulations, 2017 that had governed since the first years of the Act. Regulation 5 of the 2022 Regulations is the text a family must satisfy, and its conditions repay close reading because each one is a policy choice, not a formality.

The foundation condition is capability: the prospective parents must be physically, mentally, emotionally and financially capable, must have no life-threatening medical condition, and must not stand convicted of a criminal offence or accused in a case of child rights violation. Adoption is open irrespective of marital status and irrespective of whether the parents already have a biological son or daughter, but in the case of a married couple the consent of both spouses is required, so a married person cannot adopt alone under this regime against the other spouse's position. A single female may adopt a child of any gender. A single male may adopt, but not a girl child, the restriction carried from the Act itself into the regulations. A couple must show at least two years of stable marital relationship, a condition waived for relative adoption and adoption by a step-parent, on the reasoning that those families are already formed around the child and the waiting does no protective work.

The age architecture is the condition families discover last and plan around least. The regulations count the parents' age on the date of registration and tie the permissible age of the child to a composite ceiling: for a child up to two years of age, the combined age of a couple must not exceed eighty five years and a single parent must not exceed forty; for a child above two and up to four years, the ceilings are ninety and forty five; for a child above four and up to eight years, one hundred for a couple and fifty for a single parent; and for a child above eight and up to eighteen years, one hundred and ten and fifty five. A minimum age difference of twenty five years between the child and either parent applies across every band. The structure deliberately pushes older applicants toward older children, matching the expected length of active parenting to the child's remaining years of dependence, and it means that the wait itself consumes eligibility: a couple that registers at a composite age close to a ceiling may find, years later, that the infant band has closed to them and only an older child's band remains open, one of the quiet cruelties of a queue measured in years.

The family-size condition completes the design. A couple that already has two or more children will be considered only for a child with special needs or a hard to place child, unless the child is a relative or a stepchild. The earlier regulations had barred couples with three or more children from ordinary adoption altogether; the 2022 text converts the bar into a channelling rule, using the scarcity of infants to direct experienced, larger families toward the children the queue serves worst. Age criteria do not apply at all to relative and step-parent adoptions. These are not neutral rules, and they are contested as such: the single male restriction is defended as a child protection measure directed at a statistically identified risk and criticised as a categorical exclusion that judges a class of applicants rather than any individual; the composite age bands are defended as actuarial common sense about parenting into old age and criticised as a rigid proxy that takes no account of health, wealth or family support. The article states them as the law states them, because a prospective parent's first need is to know the gate's actual dimensions; the argument about whether the gate is well placed belongs to the debate the closing sections take up.

### Why do so many children in institutions never enter the adoption pool?

Institutional residence and legal availability are different statuses, and most children in child care institutions are not orphans. A child becomes adoptable only when a Child Welfare Committee, after inquiry, declares the child legally free for adoption, and that inquiry must first exhaust the tracing of parents and the attempt at restoration to family.

A large share of children in institutions have living parents or extended family, are in care because of poverty, illness, abandonment of the marital home or a protection concern, and are candidates for restoration, foster care or sponsorship rather than adoption. The pool is small because the law makes it small deliberately: adoption permanently severs a legal identity, and the State will not sever it while a family claim remains unexplored.

The consequence is the central mismatch of the system, and it is worth stating with the figures the record actually supports. The Central Adoption Resource Authority's published figures record 4,029 adoptions in the year 2023 to 2024, comprising 3,580 in-country adoptions and 449 inter-country adoptions, and 4,515 adoptions in 2024 to 2025, of which 4,155 were in-country. Against an annual throughput of that order, the figures placed before the Supreme Court in the pendency proceedings recorded more than thirty thousand prospective parents registered and waiting on the portal against roughly two thousand children legally free for adoption at that point. The ratio, on any view of the numbers, is more than ten waiting families for every available child, and for the youngest children, whom most applicants seek, the effective ratio is far worse, because the legally free pool skews toward older children, sibling groups and children with special needs, precisely the children the composite age rules and the family-size channel push some applicants toward and most applicants do not initially choose. A system can be honest about this or can pretend the queue is an administrative accident. The regulations, to their credit, are built on the honest version: seniority, channelling and the special needs categories all assume scarcity and attempt to distribute it by rule rather than by influence, money or speed of counsel, which is what an unregulated market in scarce children would distribute it by.


## Reading Regulation 5 against real families

Eligibility rules are easiest to understand applied, and four recurring configurations show where the regulations' lines actually fall. The first is the young couple that has planned its adoption late in its thinking about education and career: registering at a composite age of eighty, both spouses together, they sit inside the infant band's ceiling of eighty five and may wait for a child up to two years; but if their wait runs five years, which for an infant is an ordinary wait, the composite age counted on the date of registration still governs, so their place in the infant band is protected, while their capacity to switch preferences to a younger child later is not improved. The registration date rule is a deliberate mercy: without it, the queue would consume the eligibility it is rationing, and the regulations say so in form, by counting age on the date of registration. The second is the couple registering at a composite age of ninety five. The infant and toddler bands are closed to them from the outset; the bands for children above four years and up to eight, with a ceiling of one hundred, and for children above eight, with a ceiling of one hundred and ten, are open, and their realistic adoption is of an older child. This is the age architecture's intended redirection at work, and agencies treat counselling on it as central, because a couple that registers expecting the system to bend toward an infant will lose years to an expectation the table never promised. The third is the single applicant of forty four who wishes to adopt a child of three. As a single parent the ceiling for the band above two and up to four years is forty five, so the applicant is inside it, but only just, and had the application been for a child of two or below, the ceiling of forty would have excluded the applicant altogether; the applicant's sex then matters in the adjacent way the Act prescribes, a single woman being eligible for a boy or a girl and a single man being eligible for a boy only, so that the same file produces three different answers depending on a few months in the child's age and nothing at all about the applicant's fitness as an individual. The fourth is the family that already has two children, one biological and one adopted, and wishes to adopt again. For an ordinary child the route is closed: the family will be considered only for a child with special needs or a hard to place child, or for a relative or stepchild, and the policy is frankly allocative, a scarce infant being counted as better placed with the childless and the special needs pool, which the queue reaches slowly, being directed to the families whose capacity is proven by the children already in their home.

Beyond these recurring configurations sit two conditions whose application is individual rather than arithmetical. The capability condition, physical, mental, emotional and financial capability with no life-threatening medical condition, is applied through the home study and medical evidence, which is where fine judgments about a specific applicant's health, history and household are properly made, whatever the categorical rules exclude in advance. The marital condition is applied through the state of the relationship rather than its paper age alone: the requirement is two years of stable marital relationship, and the home study examines the relationship's stability as a fact, so a couple inside the two years by the calendar is refused, and a couple beyond the two years whose home study records instability may be refused as well. Separated spouses, applicants in a live-in relationship and applicants whose marriage the law does not recognise as a marriage are outside the couple category altogether, and a single applicant within such a relationship is examined as a single applicant; the regulations' framework is a framework of recognised statuses, and the debate about its boundaries, which the closing sections address, is a debate about family forms and not about administrative convenience.


## The process stage by stage, and where the time goes

A resident Indian adoption under Section 58 begins with registration. The prospective parents register on CARINGS, upload their documents, and select the Specialised Adoption Agency through which their case will run. The documents are the ordinary apparatus of identity and capacity: photographs, proof of date of birth, proof of residence, identity documents, evidence of income and medical fitness, and, for a couple, the marriage certificate by which the two years of stable relationship are shown. The District Child Protection Unit verifies the documents, under timelines the 2022 Regulations tightened to days rather than weeks, and the chosen agency then conducts the home study, in which a social worker visits the home, interviews the family, examines motivation, health, finances, relationships and preparedness, and writes the home study report on which eligibility in the individual case rests. The report, once accepted, is valid for three years and must be revalidated if the wait outlasts it, as for most families seeking an infant it will.

The wait is the process, for most families, and it is a wait with a structure rather than a wait in a void. Seniority runs from registration. As children are declared legally free and uploaded by agencies across the country, the system generates referrals in seniority order against the preferences each family has recorded for the child's age band, gender where the rules permit a preference, health status and state. A family may be shown profiles of children, may reserve one child's profile for a short window while it considers the child study report and medical examination report, may meet the child, and must then accept or decline. Acceptance starts the legal endgame: the child is placed with the family in pre-adoption foster care under a foster care agreement, so that the bonding the order will ratify has already begun in fact; the agency prepares and files the adoption application before the District Magistrate of the district where the agency or the child is located; and the Magistrate, under Section 61 as amended in 2021, disposes of the application and issues the adoption order, the amended provision contemplating disposal within two months of the application. The order is the act that changes status. Section 63 states its effect: the adopted child becomes the child of the adoptive parents for all purposes, the ties with the birth family are replaced by the ties created in the adoptive family, and property rights already vested in the child are preserved. The agency then secures a birth certificate for the child in the adoptive name, and follow-up visits and reports over the following two years close the file, with counselling obligations at the pre-adoption, adoption and post-adoption stages that the 2022 Regulations made mandatory.

The realistic timeline therefore has three segments of very different characters. Registration, verification and home study are measured in weeks to a few months and are largely within the family's and the agency's control. The seniority wait for referral is measured in years for an infant or a child up to two years of age, commonly three years and more, and is within nobody's control, because it is a rationing queue for a scarce pool; it shortens dramatically for an older child, a sibling group or a child with special needs, and it is shortest of all for relative adoption, where the child is identified and the queue does not apply. The order stage, after acceptance, is measured in weeks to months and is the segment the 2021 amendment tried to compress by moving it from the courts to the District Magistrate. Families who budget their expectations by the first and third segments and are ambushed by the second have been mis-served by almost everything written about this process, which is why the route table below separates the stages rather than offering a single reassuring number.


## Referral, the child study file, and acceptance

Between the wait and the order sits the matching event, and its design deserves more attention than the queue's length usually leaves for it. When a child is declared legally free, the institution that holds the child prepares two documents that travel with the child through the process: the child study report, recording what is known of the child's origin, circumstances, development and behaviour, and the medical examination report, recording the child's health position, prepared under the regulations' framework, which assigns the assessment of a child with special needs to the district's Chief Medical Officer. The child is categorised, as an ordinary case, as a child with special needs, or as a hard to place child, a categorisation with consequences, because the special needs and hard to place pools are the pools the family-size channel serves and the pools for which the system permits fee concessions and additional support; the categories exist because the system's honest problem is not distributing infants, who distribute themselves to the head of every queue, but placing the older child, the sibling group that must not be split, and the child whose medical or developmental file a reader of reports may pass over too quickly.

The referral itself is generated by the portal in seniority order against the family's recorded preferences, and the family is given access to the child's profile, the study report and the medical report, with a short window, forty eight hours in the regulations' scheme for reserving a referred child's profile, in which to consider and reserve. The reservation matters: it takes the child out of circulation while the family studies the file, arranges to meet the child at the agency, and decides. Acceptance is recorded on the portal and triggers the pre-adoption foster care stage; a decline, or allowing the reservation to lapse, returns the family to the queue and the child to the pool, and the regulations discipline repeated refusal, because a queue in which senior families could hold and cycle children indefinitely would cease to be a queue at all. The meeting with the child is structured but real, and for an older child the child's own view carries weight in the agency's assessment, a recognition, running through the regulations' counselling provisions, that an adoption of a child old enough to speak is a transaction with three participants rather than two.

Two features of this stage answer questions families ask often and official material answers badly. The first is immediate placement: a category through which children who have waited without referral, frequently older children and children with special needs, may be offered to families willing to consider them outside the ordinary seniority matching, so that a family that widens its preferences can complete an adoption quickly, not by queue-jumping but by choosing from the part of the pool the queue does not reach. The second is the sibling rule applied in practice: sibling groups are placed together as a unit, which places them in the harder end of the pool despite each child in the group being young, and explains why the arithmetic of the pool, roughly two thousand children against thirty thousand families, coexists with long waits for those same children; the scarcity and the surplus are both real, sorted by age, health and family configuration into almost separate markets that the single queue serves unevenly.


## Money in adoption: the fees that may move and the payments that may not

Scarcity creates price pressure, and the regulations' treatment of money is a load-bearing part of the anti-trafficking design. The only money that may lawfully move in a secular adoption is the scheduled fee structure: the regulations prescribe the charges an agency may levy, for registration, for the home study, and toward the child care corpus that supports the institution holding the children, with different, higher schedules for inter-country cases reflecting the dossier and follow-up work involved, and the charging of anything beyond the schedule is a ground of action against the agency. The design premise is that adoption must never become a purchase, for two distinct reasons. The first is the child's: a market in which a child has a price will allocate children to money and will generate supply by the means scarcity markets always generate it, through pressure on surrendering mothers, on hospitals and on intermediaries. The second is the queue's: seniority distributes children by waiting, and any payment that could improve a family's position would convert the rationing rule into an auction. This is also why the regulations and the Act react so severely to the informal handover: the payment in such a case is rarely the only defect, but it is the defect that proves the transaction was a placement market rather than a family arrangement, and families approached by any person offering to arrange a child for consideration should treat the offer as the offence it describes. Within the lawful system, the financial burden is deliberately kept modest relative to the stakes, and concessions and support attach at the special needs end of the pool, so that the money in the system flows, as far as the design can make it, toward the care of children rather than toward their acquisition.

## The personal law route: adoption under the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956

The Hindu statute governs a different transaction. Chapter VIII of the Juvenile Justice Act allocates children whom the State holds. The Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 regulates a transfer between families: a child with parents or a guardian who can give the child, and an adopter with the capacity to take the child. Section 2 fixes its reach to Hindus in every form, including followers of the Brahmo, Prarthana and Arya Samaj movements, to Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs, and to any other person who is not a Muslim, Christian, Parsi or Jew and is governed by Hindu law in these matters. Section 5 makes Chapter II of the Act the exclusive code of the subject for those it governs: an adoption made in contravention of the chapter is void, and Section 6 states the requisites of a valid adoption, that the person adopting has the capacity and the right to take in adoption, that the person giving has the capacity to give, that the person adopted is capable of being taken, and that the adoption complies with the chapter's other conditions.

Capacity to take is dealt with in Sections 7 and 8. A male Hindu of sound mind who is not a minor may adopt, but if he has a wife living, he may not adopt except with her consent, unless she has renounced the world, ceased to be a Hindu or been declared of unsound mind; where he has more than one wife living, the consent of all is required. A female Hindu of sound mind who is not a minor may adopt if she is unmarried, or if her marriage has dissolved, or her husband is dead, has renounced the world, has ceased to be a Hindu or has been declared of unsound mind. Capacity to give, under Section 9, belongs to the father, then the mother, and then a guardian only with the previous permission of the court and on the conditions the section imposes; the father's right is exercisable only with the mother's consent save in defined exceptions, and the mother may give where the father is dead or disqualified in the section's terms. Section 10 states who may be adopted: a person who is a Hindu, who has not already been adopted, who has not been married unless a custom governing the parties permits it, and who has not completed fifteen years of age unless such a custom permits. Section 11 adds the conditions that protect the existing family: a son cannot be adopted where a Hindu son, son's son or son's son's son is living, a daughter cannot be adopted where a daughter or son's daughter is living, and where a male adopts a female or a female adopts a male, the adopter must be at least twenty one years older than the child. The adoption is completed, Section 11 finally requires in its scheme, by the actual giving and taking of the child with the intention of transferring the child from the family of birth to the family of adoption; the ceremony matters because it is the operative act, and Section 16 gives a registered adoption deed a presumption of validity that families ignore at their cost when disputes later arise.

The effect, under Section 12, matches the secular regime's endpoint: the adopted child is deemed to be the child of the adoptive parents for all purposes from the date of adoption, and all ties with the family of birth are deemed replaced by those created in the adoptive family, subject to provisos that preserve the bar on marrying those the child could not have married in the birth family, preserve property already vested in the child, and prevent the adoption from divesting any person of an estate vested before the adoption. Section 15 completes the design: a valid adoption cannot be cancelled by the adoptive parents or the child, and the child cannot renounce the status and return to the birth family. The adopted child's resulting position in the adoptive family's property, including coparcenary rights and the comparison with a natural-born child's share, is a question the succession rules answer, and it is treated in [the account of the Hindu Succession amendment of 2005](/2016/10/15/hindu-succession-amendment-2005/), which owns that ground.

Set the two systems side by side and the disparity the series thesis points to is plain. The secular route examines the parents before the child arrives: identity, health, income, marriage, home, motivation, and it rations the child by queue. The personal law route examines almost nothing in advance, because its historical subject was a known child moving between known families within a community, and it leaves verification to the deed and to litigation if the adoption is ever challenged. For a child surrendered by identified parents to identified relatives or family friends, the personal law machinery is proportionate and swift. For a child whose origins are unknown or whose transfer involves strangers, intermediaries or money, the same machinery offers no checkpoint at which trafficking could be detected, which is precisely why the Juvenile Justice Act reserves orphan, abandoned and surrendered children to its own process and why Section 56 keeps the personal law exemption for genuine Hindu adoptions while the offences in Chapter IX police the boundary. The two systems are not rivals for the same child in the ordinary case. They become rivals at the boundary, where a child who should travel through the State's declaration process is instead moved privately under colour of a personal law adoption or a simple handover, and it is at that boundary that the criminal law stands.


## When a personal law adoption is challenged

The personal law route's speed is purchased by deferring its scrutiny to litigation, and the shape of that litigation is part of the route's real cost. A Hindu adoption is typically challenged years after the event, when succession opens and a reversioner or a displaced heir discovers that the adoption, if valid, diverts the estate; the challenger alleges that a condition of Chapter II was not met, that there was no genuine giving and taking, that the adopter lacked capacity or consent, or that the child was ineligible. The burden in such suits is structured by the registered deed: Section 16 requires a court to presume, until disproved, that a registered document purporting to record an adoption was made in compliance with the Act, so the family that executed and registered a deed at the time litigates from a presumption, while the family that relied on ceremony and memory alone must prove the fact and validity of the adoption like any other contested fact, with witnesses to an event that may be decades old. The courts' settled approach treats the physical act of giving and taking as the essential ceremony and the religious rites that commonly attend it as non-essential: the Supreme Court held in L. Debi Prasad v. Tribeni Devi that the datta homam, the fire rite of classical law, is not essential to a valid adoption under the Act, while the actual delivery of the child by the giver into the adopter's hands, with the intention Section 11 requires, is indispensable. A challenge also commonly turns on Section 10 and Section 11 facts, the child's age and marital status, the existence of a living son in the line that blocks a son's adoption, or the twenty one year gap where the adoption crosses sexes, and on the consent conditions in Sections 7 and 9, the wife whose consent was never taken or the mother bypassed by a father's unilateral giving. The lesson for practice runs the opposite way from the route's common image: the family's protection is built at the moment of adoption, in consent obtained and documented, a deed executed and registered, and the ceremony performed in a form witnesses can later describe, and the system that appeared to ask for nothing at the door collects its evidentiary price, with interest, at the succession.

Two boundary disputes recur often enough to name. The first concerns the child adopted under the Hindu statute whose adoption is later said to have been, in truth, an institutional child moved through a manufactured ceremony; where the child was an orphan, abandoned or surrendered child in the State's system, the Juvenile Justice Act's process is exclusive for that child, and a personal law label does not cure the want of a declaration, a point the offences in Sections 80 and 81 enforce from the other side. The second concerns the interplay with succession that the deed's presumption only postpones: an adopted child's rights relate back, under the Section 12 scheme, to the date of adoption, and property arrangements made in the interval, partitions, alienations, subsequent adoptions, are tested against the adoption once it is established, which is why the succession litigation over an adoption is rarely only about the adoption. A family that understands the route understands that its two decisions are front-loaded: to document at the ceremony, and to expect the adoption to be proved, if it is ever questioned, from the record made on the day rather than from the conviction of the family's account.


## The third position: personal laws without adoption, and the guardianship route

For a Muslim, Christian, Parsi or Jewish family, the personal law historically offered no adoption at all. Muslim law does not recognise adoption as a mode of filiation, and the Christian and Parsi communities were left to the general law rather than to a personal adoption code. The instrument available was the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890, under which the court appoints a guardian for the person and property of a minor, weighing the welfare of the minor as the governing consideration. Guardianship under that Act is real protection but a different legal creature from adoption. The guardian does not become the parent; the ward does not take the guardian's name or family status by operation of the appointment; the relationship does not create the mutual rights of inheritance that adoption creates; and the guardianship determines when the ward attains majority, which, where a guardian has been appointed by a court, the Majority Act framework fixes at twenty one years rather than eighteen. Families raised children for decades inside this half-status, and its limits were practical as much as doctrinal: a guardian's authority over the child's property is supervised by the court, major transactions need permission, and the child the family raised could, in strict law, remain a stranger to its line of succession.

The secular regime changed that position fundamentally, and the Supreme Court settled the relationship between the regimes in Shabnam Hashmi v. Union of India in 2014. The petitioner, a Muslim social activist who had raised an adopted child, asked the Court to declare a right to adopt as a fundamental right and to strike down the obstacles that personal law placed in the way. The Court declined to elevate adoption to a fundamental right, but it held the point that matters for every family in this position: the Juvenile Justice legislation is an enabling statute that permits any person, irrespective of religion, to adopt a child from the State's pool under its procedure, and personal law neither confers nor removes that statutory facility. A person governed by a personal law that does not know adoption may therefore elect the secular route and adopt in full, acquiring a child in law and not merely a ward; equally, a person governed by the Hindu statute may elect the secular route for an orphan, abandoned or surrendered child, a choice with practical consequences, since the secular adoption follows the regulations' eligibility and queue while the personal law adoption does not, and the two adoptions, though equal in the status they confer on the child under Section 63 of the one Act and Section 12 of the other, are reached by roads of entirely different lengths. The election doctrine is what makes the Indian position a genuine dual system rather than a partition: religion decides which doors exist, but since the 2015 Act and the Shabnam Hashmi reading of its predecessors, it no longer decides which door a willing family must use for a child in the State's care.

A remnant of the older inter-country machinery also explains itself here. Before a statutory adoption code existed for children in institutions, a foreigner or a person outside the Hindu statute who wished to take an Indian child proceeded by guardianship, and the Supreme Court's decision in Lakshmi Kant Pandey v. Union of India in 1984 built the protective procedure for that world: moved by a letter alleging that children were being trafficked abroad through voluntary agencies, the Court laid down that a foreign applicant must be sponsored by a recognised agency in the receiving country, that a home study and child study must precede any placement, that the child must be legally free, and that the guardianship appointment in India would be followed by adoption in the applicant's own country under that court's supervision, with progress reports in the interval. The directions were judge-made procedure in a statutory vacuum, and their institutional child was the central adoption authority created in 1990 to operate the safeguards. Reading Chapter VIII against that history shows how much of the modern code is the Lakshmi Kant Pandey framework written into statute and extended to domestic adoption: the agency licensing, the home study, the legally free requirement and the follow-up reports all descend from a case about the dangers of the unregulated route, which is the strongest internal evidence that the regulated system's scrutiny is not bureaucratic taste but a response to a documented failure mode.


## Relative adoption and step-parent adoption: the identified-child routes

The relative adoption route is the secular system's acknowledgement that not every adoption is a placement of a stranger's child. Where the child is a relative, defined in the Act by a schedule of relationships, or the child of a spouse, the adoption proceeds as an identified case: the parties are known to each other, the child's identity and circumstances are not in issue, and the queue, the declaration and the pool have no application. The regulations adapt the machinery accordingly. The age criteria that police the pool routes do not apply; the home study is conducted with reference to an existing household into which the child is already woven or will directly enter; the consent of the biological parents, or of the guardian who stands in their place, is the central document; and the case moves from registration to the District Magistrate for the order without seniority, because no other family's claim on the child exists to be rationed. The same logic governs adoption by a step-parent, where the adoption regularises a household the child already lives in, most often after the death of a biological parent or a remarriage, converting a social parenthood into a legal one with its succession consequences.

The route is widely misunderstood in two directions. Families assume it is a formality that dispenses with the statutory channel altogether, and are surprised to find registration, a home study and an order still required; the system's answer is that a child crossing into a new legal identity requires an order whoever the parties are, and that relative placements are not immune to the pressures, inheritance manoeuvres, coerced consents within families, disputed custody dressed as adoption, that the documentary process exists to surface. The second misunderstanding runs the other way: that a relative adoption under the secular route is the only way to adopt a relative's child. For families within the Hindu statute, the personal law route reaches the same identified child with no order at all, and the choice between the routes, where both exist, is a choice between the deed and the order as the child's proof of status, between the succession consequences mapped by the personal law and those mapped by Section 63, and between a status that may need to be proved from ceremony and a status that an order establishes on its face. Where the child is to travel, study or claim rights across jurisdictions and registries, the order's documentary strength is a practical advantage families weigh; where the adoption is entirely within a family and its property, the deed's simplicity has its own. Foster adoption belongs in the same family of routes: a child in foster care with a family, declared legally free, may be adopted by the foster parents with priority born of the existing bond, and the 2022 Regulations' emphasis on foster adoption, including the recognition of adoption from foster care after a period of two years, is the system teaching itself to convert its temporary instruments into permanent ones where the child's attachment has already formed, the single most child-centred development in the regulations' scheme.


## Inter-country adoption and the Hague framework

Inter-country adoption under the 2015 Act, principally in Sections 59 and 60, is the secular regime pointed outward, and it is disciplined by treaty. India ratified the Hague Convention on Protection of Children and Co-operation in Respect of Intercountry Adoption, 1993 in 2003, and the Central Adoption Resource Authority is India's designated Central Authority under the Convention, which fixes the grammar of every case: adoption across borders is subsidiary to placement within the country of origin, the child must be legally free, the receiving state's authorities must certify the parents' eligibility and suitability, the Central Authorities of both states must agree that the adoption may proceed, and no improper financial gain may drive the placement. The preference order inside the Indian process reflects the subsidiarity principle in operational form. A child declared legally free is first exposed to the in-country queue of resident Indian parents; only after the prescribed window for in-country placement, sixty days from the declaration under the regulations' scheme, does the child become available for referral to non-resident Indian and overseas citizen parents living abroad, who are treated with a measure of parity in the queue, and then to foreign parents, who apply through an authorised foreign adoption agency in their country of residence, file their dossier with the Authority, receive a referral like any other applicant in seniority, and complete the adoption on the Authority's no objection certificate, which Section 68's scheme makes a condition of the order, followed by the conformity certification and the child's travel on an Indian passport with the receiving country's immigration clearance.

Section 60 covers the special case of inter-country relative adoption, where the child is going to family abroad; the machinery is lighter because the placement is identified, but the order, the Authority's processing and the child's documentation still pass through the statutory channel, since a child crossing a border on an adoption is exactly the movement the Hague system exists to make visible. In-country relative adoption sits at the other end of the same logic: Section 56 recognises adoption of a child by a relative, and adoption by a step-parent, as adoptions under the Act irrespective of the parties' religion, routed through the District Magistrate rather than the pool, with the age criteria disapplied and the home study adapted to a family that already exists. The volume pattern in the Authority's figures shows the shape of the whole system: of the 4,029 adoptions completed in 2023 to 2024, 449 were inter-country, a minority stream running at roughly a ninth of the total, which is what a subsidiarity regime is supposed to produce; the inter-country route is a residual safety valve for children the domestic queue does not reach, disproportionately older children and children with special needs, and not a parallel market in infants.


## The inter-country machinery in operation

The inter-country stream is small in volume but dense in machinery, because every case must satisfy two legal systems and one treaty at once. The foreign applicant does not approach India directly: the application is sponsored by an authorised foreign adoption agency, an agency in the receiving country that the Authority has accredited for the purpose, and the dossier, the home study prepared under the receiving country's law, the eligibility certificates of its authorities, the undertaking of post-adoption follow-up, is transmitted through that agency to the Authority. The Authority's no objection certificate is the gate through which the matched case passes to the District Magistrate for the order, and after the order the Authority issues the conformity certificate by which the adoption is certified as a Hague adoption, entitling it to recognition in the receiving state under the Convention's scheme; the child's passport and exit follow on that certificate, and the receiving agency owes the Authority progress reports on the child's adjustment over the two years that follow the placement, the follow-up obligation descending directly from the Lakshmi Kant Pandey directions, in which the foreign adoption was completed abroad under a foreign court's eye and the reports were the only Indian sight of the child. Where a placement abroad disrupts, the machinery is tested hard, because the child is by then a resident of another jurisdiction, and the system's answer is prevention at the accreditation and home study stages rather than remedy after the event, which is the practical reason the inter-country stream is kept residual and heavily documented relative to its size.

The non-resident Indian position within this machinery deserves its separate statement, because it is the position diaspora families most often misread. A non-resident Indian or overseas citizen applicant is, by the regulations' design, treated with parity in eligibility and near parity in the queue with a resident Indian, reflecting the judgment that an Indian child placed with an Indian family abroad has not crossed the cultural border the Hague subsidiarity principle guards in the same way; but the applicant living abroad must still complete the process as an inter-country case, through an authorised agency or the Indian mission's channel where the regulations permit, with the receiving country's certification of eligibility, because the child will leave India's jurisdiction and the treaty machinery attaches to the movement rather than to the passport of the applicant. The distinction between the resident, the non-resident Indian and the foreign applicant is thus a distinction of queue position and of paperwork, not of the order or its effect, and the families who plan on the wrong side of it, assuming that an Indian passport abroad converts the case into a domestic one, lose time to a classification the regulations state plainly.


## The adopted child: status, succession and identity

Adoption law is usually written from the adults' side of the counter, but the order's most consequential effects belong to the child, and a rights and remedies account should state them as the child's rights. Under Section 63 of the Juvenile Justice Act, the adopted child is the child of the adoptive parents for all purposes from the order; under Section 12 of the Hindu statute, the adopted child is deemed the child of the adoptive parents from the date of the adoption. Both formulations carry the same cargo: the child takes the adoptive family's name, inherits from the adoptive parents and their relations as a natural-born child would, and is owed maintenance, care and succession by the family that took the child, while the corresponding claims against the birth family are extinguished, subject to the preservation of property already vested in the child and, under the Hindu statute, the enduring bar on marriage within the birth family's prohibited degrees. It is this completeness that distinguishes adoption from every neighbouring institution, foster care, guardianship, sponsorship, each of which gives the child a protector for a season, where adoption gives the child a family in law for life, and it is why the system's gates are placed where they are: a status this total, created by an order or an act that neither side can later cancel, is not one the law permits to be created casually.

The child's remedial position follows from the same design. An adoption that was void, for want of a condition in the Hindu chapter or for want of the Juvenile Justice procedure, can be declared so by a court, and the child's status then falls to be re-determined, a remedy that protects legality at the price of unsettling the child's identity, which is why courts approach the annulment of a settled adoption with caution and why the system's preference is to test placements before the order rather than after it. An adoption that was valid cannot be undone by regret, by the adoptive parents or by the child on attaining majority, the Hindu statute saying so expressly and the secular scheme producing the same result through the order's finality and the dissolution procedure's narrowness. The adopted person's documentary identity is rebuilt on the adoption: a birth certificate issuing in the adoptive family's name, school and identity records following it, and, in an inter-country adoption, the child's citizenship and passport position settled by the receiving state's law on the strength of the Indian order and the conformity certificate.

The one question the system answers least comfortably concerns origins. The records of an adoption, the child study report, the surrender deed, the declaration file, are confidential in character, and the Juvenile Justice Act's general protection of a child's identity in its proceedings reinforces the confidentiality with which the agencies hold the file. The adopted adult who wishes to trace birth family meets a framework designed for the child's protection and the birth mother's privacy rather than for the adult's search, and the tension between an adopted person's claim to know their origins and the surrendering parent's claim to anonymity is a live and unresolved one in Indian adoption practice, argued by adoptee advocates as a question of identity and by the agencies as a question of the confidence on which surrenders depend. The honest statement of the position is that the law secures the adopted child's status completely and the adopted adult's knowledge of origins hardly at all, and the reform debate about a regulated roots search, conducted with consent and counselling on both sides, belongs to the same unfinished agenda as the declaration backlog: both concern what happens to the child's file, the event on which everything else in this field depends.


## The order moved to the District Magistrate: the 2021 amendment and its aftermath

As enacted in 2015, Section 61 placed the adoption order where adoption orders had always sat in the guardianship tradition: with the courts. The Juvenile Justice (Amendment) Act, 2021, Act 23 of 2021, assented to on 7 August 2021 and brought into force on 1 September 2022, transferred the power to the District Magistrate, with an appeal against the Magistrate's order lying to the Divisional Commissioner, and the Adoption Regulations, 2022 reworked the procedure around the new forum in the same season. The stated objective was speed and decongestion: adoption applications are, in the standard case, uncontested, documentary and administrative in character, the argument ran, and routing them through overburdened civil courts added years to a process whose human cost of delay falls on a child waiting in an institution and a family waiting at home; a district executive officer with a statutory two month disposal horizon, already charged with reviewing the district's child protection machinery, could convert the order stage from a litigation queue into an administrative act.

The aftermath is the contested part, and the honest account must hold both halves of it. District Magistrates are the busiest generalist officers in Indian administration, and a function transferred to an office is not thereby given time; adoption applications accumulated before Magistrates in significant numbers, and the transfer moved the queue rather than abolishing it, from a forum whose delay was notorious to one whose delay was at least new. The deeper objection is about the character of the scrutiny. An adoption order is not a licence: it creates parentage, severs a prior legal identity and is, for the child, effectively irreversible, and the case for keeping it with a court was never merely sentimental. Judicial officers are trained and institutionally insulated to test documents, detect collusive or purchased placements and ask the question an administrator processing a file may not ask, which is whether the transaction in front of them is the transaction it purports to be. Against that stands the record of the court era itself, in which adoption petitions waited months and years for a hearing that, in the genuine case, occupied minutes, and in which the scrutiny the theory promised was often a formality performed slowly. Transferring a function from a court to an executive officer changes both the speed and the character of the scrutiny, and the defensible verdict on the 2021 transfer is that it solved the problem it named, the listing and hearing queue, while creating the problem it did not name, pendency inside an office with a hundred competing duties and an appeal route to another executive officer, the Divisional Commissioner, rather than to a judicial forum.

The system's delays then drew the Supreme Court in directly. In proceedings on a public interest petition by The Temple of Healing, a charitable trust whose founder Piyush Saxena pressed the case for simplification, the Court described the adoption process as very tedious, observed that couples were being made to wait three to four years for a single child while children remained in institutions, and treated the mismatch between registered parents and legally free children as the structural fact to be attacked. By its order of 20 November 2023, a bench of the Chief Justice, D. Y. Chandrachud, with Justices J. B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra, directed every State and Union territory to run a drive every two months to identify orphan, abandoned and surrendered children in child care institutions who could enter the adoption pool, set a first deadline of 7 December 2023 for that exercise, required a Specialised Adoption Agency in every district by 31 January 2024, and directed the States to compile and send the Authority the data on adoptions under the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 by the same date, a direction that itself illustrates the dual system problem, since the personal law stream of adoptions is so far outside the State's record that the Court had to order it counted. The directions attack the declaration bottleneck rather than the matching stage, which is where this article has located the constraint throughout: the Court's remedy assumes, as the regulations assume, that the queue of parents is not the problem to be solved but the evidence of it, and that the children who should be in the pool are identifiable inside institutions whose residents have never been carried through the legally free inquiry.

Implementation of the Act, including the adoption machinery, is monitored by the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights under Section 109, working with the State Commissions, and the Commission's inquiries into adoption irregularities and institutional care form part of the oversight record described in [the profile of the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights](/2016/08/01/ncpcr-child-rights-commission-india/). For the individual family, the remedial map is narrower and should be stated plainly. An applicant aggrieved by an adoption order, or by a refusal, has the statutory appeal to the Divisional Commissioner against the District Magistrate's order, and beyond the statutory route the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts remains available for the classic public law complaints of delay, arbitrariness and refusal to perform a statutory duty, which is the route by which stalled applications and unreasoned rejections are in practice moved. Grievances about an agency's conduct run to the State Adoption Resource Agency and the Authority, which license, inspect and can proceed against the agencies they recognise. The general account in this article is legal information drawn from the statutes and regulations; it is not advice on an individual case, and a family whose application turns on a contested fact, a disputed consent or a limitation-sensitive step should take advice on its own circumstances, because adoption orders are designed to be final and errors at the order stage are correspondingly hard to unwind.

## After the order, and the mistakes that undo families

### What happens after the adoption order is passed?

The order completes the adoption: under Section 63 the child is the child of the adoptive parents for all purposes and the birth family's legal ties are replaced. The adoption is not a probationary arrangement that ripens later; status changes on the order itself, and everything after it is documentation and support.

The agency obtains a birth certificate naming the adoptive parents, the child takes the family's name, and the Specialised Adoption Agency follows the placement up with visits and reports for two years, with counselling available at each stage.

Post-adoption life has a legal shadow that families should understand before they begin, because the system's remaining interventions all live there. The 2022 Regulations strengthened the provisions on disruption, the breakdown of a placement before the order, and dissolution, the breakdown of an adoption after the order, requiring counselling, agency involvement and, where a placement fails, the child's return to the pool through the Committee rather than any private re-placement by the family. A failed adoption is a child protection event, not a private rearrangement: a family that hands an adopted child onward to another household, however informally and however kindly meant, steps outside the Act and into the territory of its offences. The same finality governs the personal law route in mirror image, where Section 15 of the Hindu statute forbids cancellation of a valid adoption by either side, so that in both systems the law's design choice is the same: adoption is deliberately built as a one-way door, and the scrutiny the secular system front-loads is the price of that irreversibility.

The common mistakes are worth naming because they recur. The first is the private handover dressed as adoption: a child taken directly from a hospital, a birth mother or an intermediary without the Committee's declaration and the agency process, which under Section 80 is an offence punishable with imprisonment and fine, and which leaves the child without legal status in the family and the family exposed to the child's removal; the exposure falls hardest on precisely the families whose intentions were best, because the law cannot distinguish their handover from a purchased placement by looking at it. The second is the colourable personal law adoption of a child who is not being given by anyone entitled to give: the Hindu statute requires a giver with capacity under Section 9, and a transaction in which an institution or a stranger manufactures the giving and taking for an unrelated child is not saved by performing a ceremony, since the adoption would be void for contravention of the chapter and the participants would be inside the Juvenile Justice Act's offences rather than outside them. The third is documentary carelessness in the genuine case: an unregistered deed where registration was available, missing proof of consent, a home study allowed to lapse past its three year validity while the queue runs, or preferences recorded on the portal so narrowly that referrals pass the family by. Families who keep their registration record, home study report, referral papers and the order itself in one ordered file, using a single viewer such as [the ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) for the papers and presentations that agencies circulate during counselling and training, are measurably better placed when a verification, a revalidation or an appeal asks them to produce the record in sequence. The fourth mistake is strategic: registering for the youngest band alone while composite age quietly closes it, where a realistic reading of the age table at registration would have produced a plan the family could actually complete.


## If the system says no: adverse reports, refusals and second attempts

A remedies account must also cover the refusals that occur without any court ever being seized. The first gate at which a family can be turned away is the home study: an adverse home study report records that the agency is not satisfied on capability, stability, motivation or the household's preparedness, and without a favourable report the registration cannot proceed to referral. The family's practical responses are sequential: to understand the reasons recorded, since an adverse report grounded in a correctable circumstance, an unresolved health question, an incomplete document, a household member's unaddressed objection, is a different event from an adverse report grounded in a settled judgment of unfitness; to seek review through the State Adoption Resource Agency and the Authority, which supervise the agencies and can examine whether the study was conducted and reasoned properly; and, where the objection is to the lawfulness of the process rather than to its outcome, to invoke the writ jurisdiction, which polices the manner of administrative decision rather than substituting the court's own assessment of parental fitness. A second refusal point is verification: documents that cannot be verified, a marriage that cannot be proved to the Unit's satisfaction, a medical position that requires the Chief Medical Officer's assessment in a special needs case, each of which is curable by evidence rather than by argument, and each of which rewards the family that treats the file as the case. The third is the order stage itself: a District Magistrate who refuses an application, on a defect in the declaration, the consent or the agency's processing, produces the statutory appeal to the Divisional Commissioner, and the appeal's framing matters, because the appellate authority can correct errors of law and procedure and can direct the completion of a step the Magistrate wrongly treated as fatal. What the system does not contain is a general merits appeal against the queue, the eligibility bands or a child's categorisation; those are challenges to the regulations themselves, open only as constitutional or administrative law challenges to the instrument, and no family should be advised to expect an individual exception from a composite age ceiling that the regulations impose in terms. The discipline this imposes on applicants is unglamorous and real: the remediable failures are documentary and procedural, the structural exclusions are not remediable in the individual case at all, and a family's effort is best spent at registration, on the accuracy of the file and the realism of the preferences, where the system's actual discretion lives.


## The criminal boundary: Sections 80 and 81 and the market they police

The offences are not an appendix to the system; they are the reason the system can claim exclusivity at all. Section 80 of the Juvenile Justice Act provides that whoever adopts, or undertakes the care of, an orphan, abandoned or surrendered child in contravention of the Act's prescribed procedure commits an offence punishable with imprisonment that may extend to three years, or a fine of one lakh rupees, or both, and where the offender is a recognised adoption agency, the Act adds the withdrawal of its registration, the corporate death penalty of this field. Section 81 addresses the market beneath the procedure: whoever sells or buys a child for any purpose is punishable with rigorous imprisonment that may extend to five years and a fine of one lakh rupees, and where the offence is committed by a person having the actual charge of the child, including the staff of a hospital, nursing home or maternity home, the imprisonment runs from three years to seven. The placement of the aggravated category is the statute's memory at work: the recurring Indian illegal adoption case is not a stranger snatching a child but an institution's insider diverting a child, a surrendered infant recorded as dead or as placed elsewhere, a newborn handed from a maternity ward to a waiting purchaser, a child moved on forged papers through a chain that ends in a respectable home, and the Act reserves its heaviest adoption-adjacent punishment for the person who abused a position of care to feed that chain.

The enforcement record shows the boundary's real shape, which is uneven. Prosecutions cluster where an institutional scandal exposes a diversion, a hospital or agency whose records cannot account for its children, and they are rarest exactly where the offence is most common and most sympathetic, the single private handover between a birth family and a receiving family who will raise the child well. That asymmetry produces the field's most uncomfortable remedial question. The child placed illegally is, after years in the receiving home, a child with one family in fact and none in law; the criminal law's answer is prosecution of the adults, but the child's answer cannot be removal into the institution the system failed to place them from, and courts faced with the accomplished fact have to weigh legality against a best interest that by then points at the illegal home. The system's design response is preventive rather than corrective: make the lawful route's existence unmistakable, punish the professional intermediaries who profit from the unlawful one, and keep the lawful route's own delays under enough pressure that the unlawful shortcut loses its excuse. It is a further reason the declaration backlog matters beyond its arithmetic: every year of avoidable waiting is a subsidy to the market Sections 80 and 81 exist to suppress, a point the Supreme Court's impatience in the Temple of Healing proceedings makes from the bench, connecting the tediousness of the lawful process to the temptations of the unlawful one in a single observation about couples driven to desperation.

For the receiving family, the boundary's practical meaning should be stated without varnish. There is no doctrine of regularisation for a private placement of a pool child: time does not cure it, the child's integration does not cure it, and a later approach to the Committee does not convert the handover into an adoption, because the Act's process cannot be run retrospectively over a child already placed. A family that has taken a child outside the process holds a fragile position that no deed can repair, and its lawful options are the unattractive ones, disclosure to the authorities and submission to the system's judgment on the child's placement, which is why the single most valuable sentence in this article for a family at the point of decision is the one the offences compel: if the child is an orphan, abandoned or surrendered child, or is being offered by anyone other than a parent or guardian entitled to give the child in a genuine personal law adoption, there is no lawful shortcut, and any person offering one is offering a crime with a child attached to it.


## Adoption among the routes to parenthood

Adoption is one of three regulated routes by which a person who cannot, or does not, conceive a child becomes a parent in Indian law, and the eligibility philosophies of the three routes diverge instructively. The surrogacy and assisted reproduction statutes of 2021 gate their route by medical indication and marital status, reserving surrogacy for intending couples and a narrow class of intending women with a demonstrated medical need and surrounding the arrangement with eligibility certificates at both ends, as [the account of the Surrogacy and ART Acts of 2021](/2015/10/01/surrogacy-art-acts-2021-explained/) sets out. Adoption gates its route by the welfare of an existing child rather than by the medical position of the adults: it asks not whether the applicants need a child but whether the child would be safe and well parented by them, which is why a single person may adopt while a single person may not commission a surrogacy, and why adoption's eligibility conditions are about age, stability and capability rather than infertility. The guardianship route, oldest of the three, gates by judicial satisfaction in the individual case and confers the least. A family choosing between the routes is therefore not choosing between procedures for the same legal event; it is choosing between different legal relationships, different children, and different theories of what the State is entitled to examine before parenthood begins.

## The argument about slowness, taken seriously on both sides

The case for the safeguards, in its strongest form, is historical before it is theoretical. Inter-country adoption before Lakshmi Kant Pandey operated with children moved across borders on the word of voluntary agencies, and the Court's intervention was a response to trafficking risk that was documented rather than imagined; the domestic black market in children, hospital handovers and purchased surrenders that the offences in Sections 80 and 81 exist against, has never disappeared, and every checkpoint in the CARINGS process answers a known evasion. A registry with seniority prevents the two corruptions a scarcity market guarantees: selection of children by the wealth and influence of applicants, and payment flowing to whoever can supply a child. A home study is the minimum inquiry a legal system can make before handing a defenceless child, permanently and irreversibly, to strangers it has never met. On this view the queue is not the system's failure but its integrity made visible: the wait is long because the children are few, and a reform that shortened the wait by weakening the inquiry would be buying speed with children's safety.

The case for reform, in its strongest form, accepts the safeguards and attacks the arithmetic. The Temple of Healing's petition and the Supreme Court's observations in its proceedings make the core of it: only about four thousand adoptions are completed in a year, the wait for an infant runs to three or four years, a couple that registers at twenty six may be thirty when a child arrives, with composite age bands closing beneath them as they wait, and children spend the years in which attachment is formed inside institutions while legally available parents wait outside them. The reformers' sharper point, which the evidence supports, is about where the delay sits. The matching stage, for all its frustrations, is a queue doing rationing work. The declaration stage is a backlog doing none: children in institutions whose tracing is complete, whose surrender is final or whose abandonment is years old remain legally unfree because Committees are understaffed, inquiries are not closed, and no officer owns the file, and it is that unprocessed status, not the seniority system, that keeps the pool at roughly two thousand children against thirty thousand waiting families. The Supreme Court's 2023 directions, bimonthly identification drives, an agency in every district, timelines enforced, are drafted on exactly this diagnosis.

One further piece of evidence disciplines both sides of the slowness argument, and it is the composition of the adoptions the system completes. The Authority's published material records that a substantial share of the children in the legally free pool carry a special needs categorisation, and that adoptions of children with special needs, while rising, remain the minority of completed adoptions; the 2022 Regulations answer with machinery, medical assessment by the Chief Medical Officer within a fixed period, fee relief and the channelling of larger families, rather than with exhortation. The pattern matters because it locates part of the wait not in the State's processing at all but in the demand side's preferences, in the distance between the child the median applicant describes and the child the median institution holds. A reform programme that addressed only processing would shorten the declaration stage and then meet this second wall, which no timeline can dissolve; the system's honest instruments for it are the ones the regulations deploy, information at counselling, the immediate placement category that puts waiting children in front of waiting families, and a queue design that rewards, with speed, the family that widens the child it will welcome. The debate is therefore three-cornered in the end: the State must process faster, the law must scrutinise properly, and the applicants' collective preferences decide how much of the pool the faster processing will actually clear, and any verdict that assigns the whole delay to a single corner has not understood the machine.


The verdict this article defends follows from the diagnosis. The regulated system's design, registration, scrutiny, seniority, an order from a public authority, is the correct architecture for placing stranger children, and the personal law route's survival for identified children within families is defensible precisely because its cases are the ones where scrutiny has the least to bite on. What is not defensible is a declaration process that leaves the pool artificially small and then points to the queue as proof that demand is the problem. Speed in adoption should be bought where the Supreme Court directed it to be bought, in identifying children, completing inquiries and staffing the Committees and agencies, and not at the two points where reform proposals perennially aim, the home study and the seniority queue, which are the load-bearing walls. The District Magistrate transfer belongs to the same assessment: it rearranged the last stage of the process while the first stage, the child's journey to legal freedom, remained the constraint, which is why its promise of speed has been contested in outcomes even by those who accepted its logic.


## Who defends the gates, and who presses against them

The contested design choices in the regulations have identifiable defenders and identifiable critics, and the stance of this article requires that both be named rather than summarised. The defence of the system's rigour is made institutionally by the Ministry of Women and Child Development and the Authority in Parliament and in their published material: the 2022 Regulations were presented, in the Ministry's statement to the Lok Sabha, as a streamlining instrument, with the District Magistrate's order replacing the court's, the upload of legally free children fixed to a maximum period of ten days, document verification by the District Child Protection Unit fixed at five days, health assessment of special needs children fixed at fifteen days, and counselling made mandatory at every stage; the official position is that delay is a problem of timelines and identification, to be solved by prescribing clocks and counting children, and that the eligibility gates are child protection in its plainest form. The critique from the families' side is represented by adoptive parents' organisations, of which the Families of Joy Foundation, a body of adoptive families that published a clause-by-clause commentary on the 2022 Regulations, is representative: its commentary presses the drafting gaps that families meet in practice, asking what happens to the seniority of parents who cross the composite age of one hundred and ten while waiting without a referral, and whether additional days prescribed for the legally free declaration beyond the Act's own two and four month periods serve the child who spends them in an institution. The litigation critique, carried by The Temple of Healing and its founder Piyush Saxena, is broader and more impatient: the petition framed the system's throughput, about four thousand adoptions a year in a country with a very large population of children in institutions, as the indictment, and sought simplification of the process itself rather than better administration of it; the Supreme Court's response, in the hearings of 2022 and 2023 and the directions of November 2023, accepted the throughput critique and the mismatch evidence while directing its remedies at identification, infrastructure and the declaration stage, declining to treat the eligibility and scrutiny apparatus as the obstacle. A fourth position is held within the child protection establishment itself and should be distinguished from all three: that the system's true defect is neither rigour nor drafting but the condition of the institutions through which children pass before the declaration, Committees without full-time members, agencies without trained social workers, units without data staff, so that every clock the regulations prescribe is set against machinery that was never staffed to keep it. Each of these four positions identifies a different bottleneck, and the article's verdict can be stated against them: the fourth is the best evidenced, the Court's directions endorse it, and reforms addressed to the first three without the fourth will continue to produce the pattern the record shows, a well-designed queue in front of an under-built door.


## Conclusion: the question to ask of any adoption

A reader who has followed the two systems to this point can answer the only question that organises the field: which system a given child can lawfully travel through, and what scrutiny that journey applies before the child's status changes for good. For the orphan, abandoned or surrendered child, the answer is the Juvenile Justice Act and nothing else, with the Committee's declaration as the gateway, CARINGS as the road, and the District Magistrate's order as the destination. For the child given by parents or a guardian within the Hindu, Buddhist, Jain and Sikh communities, the answer is the personal law of 1956, swift and unscrutinised, valid on its conditions and void without them. For everyone else, the secular route stands open by election, which is the quiet revolution Shabnam Hashmi confirmed: adoption in India is no longer rationed by the accident of which personal law a family was born into. What remains rationed, by scarcity and by an unfinished administrative pipeline, is the supply of children the law will allow to be adopted at all, and until the declaration stage is resourced to match the queue, the two systems will continue to produce their anomalous result: the most scrutinised route in Indian family law, and the longest wait, imposed on the families adopting the children with the fewest people in the world to object.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does legal adoption work in India?

Legal adoption works through one of two systems. For an orphan, abandoned or surrendered child, the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015 provides the route: a Child Welfare Committee declares the child legally free for adoption, the prospective parents register on the CARINGS portal of the Central Adoption Resource Authority, a Specialised Adoption Agency conducts a home study, a child is referred in seniority order, the parents accept and take the child in pre-adoption foster care, and a District Magistrate issues the adoption order, on which the child becomes the parents' child for all purposes. For Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs, the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 also permits adoption by giving and taking between families, with no authority's order.

### Q: Who is eligible to adopt a child in India?

Under the Adoption Regulations, 2022, prospective parents must be physically, mentally, emotionally and financially capable, with no life-threatening medical condition. A married couple needs the consent of both spouses and at least two years of stable marital relationship. Age is counted on the date of registration against composite ceilings that rise with the child's age, from eighty five years combined for a child up to two years to one hundred and ten for a child above eight, with single parent ceilings of forty to fifty five years and a minimum age difference of twenty five years. A couple with two or more children is considered only for a child with special needs or a hard to place child, relatives and stepchildren apart.

### Q: What does the Central Adoption Resource Authority do?

The Central Adoption Resource Authority, known as CARA, is the statutory body constituted by Section 68 of the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015, and it is the regulator of the secular adoption system. It frames the Adoption Regulations that govern eligibility and procedure, operates the CARINGS portal through which parents register and children are referred in seniority, recognises and oversees the State Adoption Resource Agencies and Specialised Adoption Agencies, regulates inter-country adoption and issues the no objection certificate without which an inter-country adoption cannot be completed, accredits authorised foreign adoption agencies, and serves as India's designated Central Authority under the Hague Convention on inter-country adoption of 1993, which India ratified in 2003.

### Q: Can a single person adopt in India?

Yes. Under the Adoption Regulations, 2022 a single person may adopt irrespective of whether they have a biological child, subject to the capability conditions, the single parent age ceilings, which run from forty years for a child up to two years to fifty five years for a child above eight years, and the minimum age difference of twenty five years between parent and child. A single female may adopt a child of any gender. A single male may adopt a boy but is not eligible to adopt a girl child, a restriction that appears in Section 57 of the Juvenile Justice Act itself and is carried into the regulations. The home study examines the single applicant's household and support arrangements as it would any applicant's.

### Q: Who issues an adoption order now?

The District Magistrate issues the adoption order under the Juvenile Justice Act. As enacted in 2015, Section 61 placed the order with the courts, but the Juvenile Justice (Amendment) Act, 2021, in force from 1 September 2022, transferred the power to the District Magistrate of the district, who is to dispose of the agency's application and issue the order, the amended provision contemplating disposal within two months of the application. An appeal against the District Magistrate's order lies to the Divisional Commissioner. No order is needed at all under the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956, where the adoption is effected by the act of giving and taking itself.

### Q: How long does adoption take in India?

The honest answer is stage-wise. Registration, document verification and the home study take weeks to a few months. The seniority wait for a referral is the long segment: for an infant or a child up to two years of age it commonly runs to three years or more, because the Authority's figures record about four thousand adoptions a year against more than thirty thousand registered waiting parents and a legally free pool of roughly two thousand children. The wait shortens sharply for an older child, a sibling group or a child with special needs, and relative adoption, which has no queue, is measured in months. After acceptance, the order stage before the District Magistrate is measured in weeks to months.

### Q: What is the difference between adoption and guardianship?

Adoption creates parentage; guardianship creates custody and responsibility without parentage. An adopted child becomes the child of the adoptive parents for all purposes, takes their name, inherits from them as a natural-born child, and is severed in law from the birth family, permanently and irreversibly. A guardian appointed under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 is a court-supervised caretaker of the minor's person and property: the ward does not become the guardian's child, acquires no right of inheritance from the guardian by virtue of the appointment, remains legally a member of the birth family, and the relationship ends when the ward attains majority. Guardianship was the only route historically open to persons whose personal law had no adoption; the Juvenile Justice Act route has since made full adoption available irrespective of religion.

### Q: Can relatives adopt a child directly?

Yes, and the relative route is deliberately lighter because the child is identified rather than drawn from the pool. Under the Juvenile Justice Act and the Adoption Regulations, 2022, a relative may adopt a relative's child, and a step-parent may adopt a stepchild, through the statutory channel: registration, an adapted home study, the consents of the biological parents or guardian, and an adoption order from the District Magistrate, with no seniority queue and with the composite age criteria disapplied. Families governed by the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 may instead complete the same adoption by giving and taking, recorded in a registered deed, with no order at all. What relatives cannot do is take a child by private arrangement outside both routes, which the Act treats as an offence.

### Q: What documents do prospective adoptive parents need to register for adoption in India?

Registration on CARINGS requires the family's identity and capacity record: a current family photograph, identity documents such as the PAN card, proof of date of birth, proof of residence, evidence of income, and medical fitness certification, with the marriage certificate added for a couple to show the two years of stable marital relationship the regulations require. The District Child Protection Unit verifies the documents, under a five day timeline in the Adoption Regulations, 2022, after which the chosen Specialised Adoption Agency conducts the home study. The documents are not a formality: age is counted from the registration record against the composite ceilings, and errors or gaps at this stage are the most common cause of avoidable delay and of referrals lost to a lapsed file.

### Q: What is a home study report and who prepares it?

The home study report is the assessment on which an individual family's eligibility in fact rests. It is prepared by a social worker of the Specialised Adoption Agency the parents have chosen, after visiting the home and interviewing the family about motivation, health, finances, relationships, the household's composition and support, and preparedness for an adopted child, including the counselling the regulations make mandatory at the pre-adoption stage. The report records whether the family satisfies the capability conditions of Regulation 5 of the Adoption Regulations, 2022: physical, mental, emotional and financial capability and no life-threatening medical condition. Once accepted, the report is valid for three years and must be revalidated if the seniority wait outlasts it, as it commonly does for families seeking an infant.

### Q: Can a married couple adopt if one spouse does not consent?

No, not under the Juvenile Justice Act route. Regulation 5 of the Adoption Regulations, 2022 requires the consent of both spouses in the case of a married couple, and the home study examines the couple's relationship as a unit, so an adoption by one spouse against the other's position cannot proceed; the couple must also show at least two years of stable marital relationship, a condition waived only for relative and step-parent adoptions. The Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 reaches a parallel result by its own route: a married male Hindu may not adopt without his wife's consent, save where she has renounced the world, ceased to be a Hindu or been declared of unsound mind, and where more than one wife is living, the consent of all is required.

### Q: Can NRIs and OCI cardholders adopt a child from India?

Yes. Non-resident Indian and overseas citizen of India applicants are eligible under the Adoption Regulations, 2022 and are treated with parity in eligibility and near parity in the seniority queue with resident Indians. Because the child will leave India, however, the case is processed as an inter-country adoption: the applicants apply through an authorised foreign adoption agency in their country of residence or the channel the regulations prescribe, their home study and eligibility are certified by that country's authorities under the Hague Convention framework, the child becomes available to them after the in-country window has run, the Central Adoption Resource Authority issues its no objection certificate, and the District Magistrate issues the adoption order, followed by the Authority's conformity certificate for recognition abroad.

### Q: What is the difference between in-country and inter-country adoption?

In-country adoption is the placement of an Indian child with parents resident in India, under Sections 58 of the Juvenile Justice Act, through registration, home study, seniority referral and a District Magistrate's order. Inter-country adoption, under Sections 59 and 60, is the placement of an Indian child with parents resident abroad, whether foreign nationals, non-resident Indians or overseas citizens, and it carries an additional treaty layer: India is a party to the Hague Convention of 1993, the Central Adoption Resource Authority acts as Central Authority, the receiving state's authorities certify the parents, the child is offered first to the in-country queue and becomes available for inter-country referral after the prescribed window, and the Authority's no objection certificate and conformity certificate frame the order and the child's travel. The child's status after the order is identical; the machinery and the wait are not.

### Q: Can a Hindu couple adopt under the Juvenile Justice Act instead of Hindu law?

Yes. The two routes are alternatives, not compartments. In Shabnam Hashmi v. Union of India, decided in 2014, the Supreme Court held that the Juvenile Justice legislation is an enabling facility open to any person irrespective of religion, and that personal law neither confers nor removes it. A Hindu couple may therefore adopt an orphan, abandoned or surrendered child through CARA registration, home study, seniority and a District Magistrate's order, instead of adopting by giving and taking under the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956. The choice matters practically: the secular route reaches children in the State's pool whom the personal law cannot reach at all, applies the regulations' eligibility conditions and queue, and produces an order as proof of status, while the personal law route is faster but works only where a person entitled to give the child exists.

### Q: What happens if an adoption is done without following the CARA process?

For an orphan, abandoned or surrendered child, an adoption outside the prescribed procedure is an offence, not an informality. Section 80 of the Juvenile Justice Act punishes adopting or taking charge of such a child in contravention of the procedure with imprisonment that may extend to three years, or a fine of one lakh rupees, or both, and a recognised agency that offends risks withdrawal of its registration. Section 81 punishes selling or buying a child with rigorous imprisonment that may extend to five years and a fine of one lakh rupees, rising to three to seven years where the offender had charge of the child, as hospital staff may. The child also acquires no lawful status in the receiving family, the placement cannot be regularised retrospectively, and the family is exposed to the child's removal.

### Q: Can an adoption order be challenged or appealed?

An adoption order of a District Magistrate under the Juvenile Justice Act is appealable to the Divisional Commissioner, the route the 2021 amendment created when it moved the order from the courts to the Magistrate. Beyond the statutory appeal, the High Courts' writ jurisdiction remains available against delay, arbitrariness or refusal to perform a statutory duty in the process leading to the order. A completed adoption is otherwise designed to be final: the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 states expressly that a valid adoption cannot be cancelled by the adoptive parents or the child, and an adoption's validity can be impeached in court only on the ground that it was never lawfully made, for example because a condition of the governing statute was not met or the child's transfer bypassed the mandatory procedure.

### Q: What is pre-adoption foster care?

Pre-adoption foster care is the stage between acceptance and the adoption order in the Juvenile Justice Act process. When prospective parents accept a referred child, the child is placed in their physical custody under a foster care agreement with the Specialised Adoption Agency, so that the child begins living with the family, and the bonding the order will recognise, while the agency prepares and files the adoption application before the District Magistrate. The arrangement is foster care in form, time-limited and supervised, but its purpose is adoptive: it is not a trial that either side may casually end, and if the placement breaks down the child returns to the pool through the agency and the Committee rather than being placed anywhere else by the family. On the order, the foster placement converts into full adoption under Section 63.

### Q: Do adoptive parents get a new birth certificate for the adopted child?

Yes. After the adoption order, the Specialised Adoption Agency arranges for a birth certificate for the child to be issued by the local birth registration authority recording the adoptive parents as the child's parents and the child's adoptive name, on the basis of the adoption order. The certificate is the child's working identity document for school admission, passport and other records, and it is drawn from the order rather than from the birth family's record, which remains in the confidential adoption file held by the agency and the Authority. In an inter-country adoption the same order supports the child's passport and the receiving country's recognition through the Authority's conformity certificate under the Hague framework.

### Q: What is the role of a Specialised Adoption Agency?

A Specialised Adoption Agency is the licensed institution at the working centre of the secular adoption system, recognised under the Juvenile Justice framework and supervised by the State Adoption Resource Agency. It holds children declared legally free for adoption, prepares their child study reports and medical examination reports, uploads their details to the portal, receives prospective parents, conducts the home study through its social workers, manages the referral meeting and acceptance, places the child in pre-adoption foster care, prepares and files the adoption application before the District Magistrate, obtains the child's birth certificate after the order, and conducts post-adoption follow-up visits and reports for two years. The Supreme Court's directions of November 2023 required such an agency in every district, because a district without one cannot run the process at all.

### Q: Can foster parents adopt the child in their care?

Yes, and the Adoption Regulations, 2022 deliberately encourage it. A child placed in foster care under the Juvenile Justice Act who is declared legally free for adoption may be adopted by the foster parents, converting an existing bond into permanent status rather than moving the child to the head of the general queue, and the regulations emphasise adoption from foster care, including recognition of the route after a period of two years in foster care. The foster parents must still satisfy the eligibility conditions and complete the home study and order process; what the bond earns them is priority and continuity, not exemption. The route matters disproportionately for older children, for whom a known foster home is often the only realistic adoptive home the system will find.
