The Chorus is the most consequential nobody in Romeo and Juliet. A single unnamed figure walks out before the action begins, delivers fourteen lines of verse in the form of a sonnet, withdraws, returns once more before Act 2 with a second sonnet, and is then never heard again in a play that runs for three further acts. The figure has no name, no costume specified in any early text, no interlocutor, and no part in the story being told. Yet the twenty-eight lines this voice speaks contain the whole plot in embryo: the feud, the lovers, the deaths, and the reconciliation those deaths purchase. The verdict argued through this article is plain. The Chorus is not a character and was never meant to be one. It is the playing company speaking in its own person, a professional spokesman hired by the fiction for two specific jobs, the establishment of foreknowledge before the play opens and the management of a difficult transition before Act 2. When those jobs are done, the office lapses, and its disappearance is a mark of design rather than of damage. The textual oddities that surround the figure, above all the complete absence of both speeches from the First Folio of 1623, record the history of the playhouse and the printing house. They do not record an authorial second thought, and they do not make the second sonnet spurious.

That verdict has to be earned against a genuinely tangled record. The two Chorus speeches survive in the early quartos, in the abbreviated First Quarto of 1597 and in the authoritative Second Quarto of 1599, and vanish together from the Folio. The second sonnet has had its authorship doubted in the older editorial tradition, partly because it recapitulates rather than prophesies, partly because its manner is more conventional than the first sonnet's, and partly because the Folio omission was once read as evidence against both speeches. Modern editors, Rene Weis in the Arden third series, Jill L. Levenson in the Oxford edition, and G. Blakemore Evans in the Cambridge edition among them, print both speeches from the Second Quarto and treat the Folio absence as a theatrical fact rather than a textual verdict. The disagreement is worth staging in full, because the Chorus question turns out to be a model of how this play's text reaches a reader: filtered through performance, abridgement, and the economics of print. Who is speaking, in the end, is a question with three answers layered inside one another. Within the fiction, nobody is speaking, because the Chorus stands outside the fiction's world. Within the theatre, an actor is speaking, one member of the company stepping forward in his own clothes and his own person. Within the text, a quarto voice is speaking, one the Folio silenced and modern editing restored. The article takes the three answers in order.

## Who Is the Chorus in Romeo and Juliet?

The first fact to fix is the strangest: the Chorus is one person. The word chorus descends from the collective singing and dancing body of Greek tragedy, and a reader meeting the speech prefix for the first time may picture a file of robed commentators entering together. The early texts of this play give no warrant for that picture. The Second Quarto, the edition on which modern texts rest, opens with a stage direction for a single entrance and a single speech heading, and the speech that follows is cast in a singular professional voice that speaks of the stage, the scene, and the audience's patience as a master of ceremonies would. Nothing in the quarto suggests attendants, music, or a group. The figure is a function rendered as a person: one actor, alone at the front of the stage, in the interval between the audience's world and Verona's.

The grammar of the first sonnet confirms the office. Its famous opening, "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene" (Prologue, lines 1 to 2, in the lineation of Weis's Arden third series text), turns on a pronoun no character inside the story could use. The "we" of "where we lay our scene" is the company: the players who have chosen this story, built this stage picture, and now invite attention to it. When the sonnet closes by asking what "our toil shall strive to mend" and requesting the audience's "patient ears" (Prologue, lines 13 to 14), the voice is negotiating a contract on the company's behalf, promising labour and asking forbearance. A citizen of Verona could not lay a scene or mend a performance. The Chorus speaks for the theatre about the play, which is why the figure can know the ending: the company has rehearsed it. Foreknowledge, in this voice, is not prophecy. It is a programme note delivered in verse.

This is also why the Chorus has no psychology to analyse. Criticism that asks after the figure's motives, reliability, or feelings is asking questions the text has deliberately made unaskable. The Chorus does not approve or disapprove of the lovers, does not warn or plead, and offers no moral beyond the bare causal chain of grudge, love, death, and reconciliation. T. J. B. Spencer, introducing the play in the New Penguin edition, treated the choric figure as a formal inheritance rather than a person, a presenter inherited from earlier Tudor drama and put to unusually concentrated work. Brian Gibbons, in the Arden second series edition, likewise read the speeches as presentational machinery, the playwright's way of compressing a long narrative source into stageworthy shape while keeping the audience oriented. The two editors differ in emphasis, Spencer stressing the device's ancestry and Gibbons its utility, but they converge on the essential point: the Chorus is an office, and offices are defined by their duties rather than their inner lives. The duties here are exposition without dramatisation, orientation without participation.

One practical consequence follows for performance. Because the office is detachable from the story, the actor who speaks it can be, and in the early theatre almost certainly was, an actor with another part to play later in the performance. Doubling was the economic grammar of a company of a dozen or so sharers and hired men mounting a play with more than thirty speaking roles. A figure who appears only before Acts 1 and 2, and never while the main cast is engaged elsewhere in costume, is an ideal doubling assignment, free at exactly the moments when every principal is offstage. No casting record survives to name the original speaker, and any specific assignment remains conjecture, but the structure of the part is itself evidence: it was built to be carried by a member of the company with other work to do. The Chorus is therefore best pictured not as a robed abstraction but as a working actor, very possibly in his ordinary stage dress, stepping out of the assembling fiction to address the house before stepping back into it in another name.

### Is the Chorus one actor or a group of singers?

The Chorus is one actor. Both early quartos present a single entrance and a single speech, the Second Quarto heading the speech with the singular prefix "Chorus," and the sonnet's own pronouns, "we lay our scene," are the company's collective voice placed in one mouth for convenience.

Nothing in any early text mentions attendants, song, or dance. The Greek collective body behind the name had already shrunk, in Tudor practice, to a presenter, and Shakespeare's figure completes that shrinkage: a company spokesman, alone before the curtain line, speaking the firm's introduction.

A final distinction completes the identity. The Chorus is not the Prologue, though the two words are often used interchangeably in casual talk about the play. A prologue is a speech, a kind of text; a chorus is a speaker, a kind of office. The first sonnet is a prologue delivered by the Chorus, and the heading in the Second Quarto reflects exactly that division of labour: the speech is entered to a figure, not floated as an anonymous preface. Keeping the two terms separate matters because the puzzle of this article attaches to the speaker. Sonnets can be cut, moved, or reprinted without mystery. A speaker who appears twice and then falls silent, in a text that elsewhere accounts for its persons with care, poses a question about intention. The next sections take the two speeches in turn, then the ancestry that explains the office, then the printed record that has made the office look more mysterious than the stage ever found it.

## The First Prologue and the Contract of Foreknowledge

The opening sonnet's content has its own dedicated treatment elsewhere in this series, and its argument, its quatrains, and its famous phrases are compressed here to what the question of the speaker requires. Readers who want the speech itself anatomised line by line should turn to [the Prologue sonnet that spoils the ending](/2022/03/18/romeo-and-juliet-prologue-sonnet-analysis/), which owns that ground. What matters for the Chorus is what the speech does to the room. In fourteen lines it abolishes suspense about outcomes and replaces it with attention to process. The audience is told that the lovers will die, that their deaths will end the feud, and that the performance will last two hours. Every subsequent pleasure of the play is thereby converted from the pleasure of discovery to the pleasure of watching a known machine move: how the feud reignites, how the love ignites faster, how each step toward the announced grave is taken by people who cannot hear the announcement.

That conversion is the Chorus's first and largest contribution, and it is achieved by the voice's position outside the story. A character who prophesied the ending would become part of the causal web, a prophet to be heeded or ignored, and the play would have to manage him. The Chorus cannot be heeded, because the Chorus does not exist for the persons onstage. When Romeo later cries out against the stars, he is not defying a warning he heard; no one in Verona heard the Prologue. The foreknowledge belongs to the audience alone, held in trust by a figure who vanishes before it can be spent. This asymmetry, the house knowing what the stage cannot, is the engine of the play's dramatic irony, and it is installed entirely by an office that speaks for fourteen lines and retires.

The sonnet form is the contract's seal. A sonnet in the 1590s was the period's most recognisable instrument of lyric compression and the fashionable currency of desire, the form in which the decade's young men addressed unattainable ladies. To cast the play's summary as a sonnet is to announce, before a word of dialogue is spoken, that this tragedy will be conducted in the idiom of love poetry, and that the idiom will be made to carry narrative weight it was not built for: genealogy, geography, body count. The Chorus thus performs a quiet act of formal criticism. It demonstrates the sonnet as a container, filled here with plot. When the lovers later meet and improvise a perfect sonnet between them at the feast, the audience has already been taught the form by the company's spokesman, and can hear the meeting as an answering act: the form the Chorus used to summarise the story from outside is taken up, from inside, by the two people the story will kill. The play's celebrated self-consciousness about its own forms begins not with any character but with this hired voice, and it begins before the fiction does.

There is a further, plainer service in the speech: it maps the social ground. "Two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue, line 1) distributes the play's world into matched camps of equal standing, and the feud that drives the catastrophe is framed from the first line as a quarrel between peers rather than a rebellion against authority. That framing governs how an audience reads every later act of violence, and it is the premise on which the play's treatment of power in Verona rests, examined in this series' study of [the two households and the power and class order of Verona](/2022/04/22/two-households-class-power-verona/). A presenter who can be trusted to state the premise in one balanced line is doing world-building of the most economical kind. The Chorus's neutrality is the point: because the voice belongs to neither household, its symmetry is credible, and the tragedy can proceed as a disaster of equals.

### Does the Chorus speak to any character in the play?

The Chorus speaks to no character at all. Both sonnets are addressed outward, to the audience, over the heads of a Verona that never hears them; no figure onstage acknowledges the voice, answers it, or acts on its information. This one-way address is the clearest proof of the office's position outside the fiction.

The lovers cannot defy the Prologue because they cannot hear it, and the audience's foreknowledge stays quarantined in the house, which is exactly where the play's irony needs it to live.

The first speech, then, is a masterpiece of delegated work. Fourteen lines establish place, premise, outcome, duration, and the interpretive posture the audience is asked to adopt, and they do so in the voice of the only speaker who could establish all five without entangling the fiction. The second speech has a narrower brief and, as the next section shows, a different temper, and the difference between the two is where the authorship argument begins.

## The Act 2 Chorus: A Second Sonnet with a Different Job

The voice that returns before Act 2 is recognisably the same office performing a new task. Where the first sonnet looked forward across the whole action, the second looks backward across one act and forward across a single difficulty. Its opening is a small funeral: "Now old desire doth in his deathbed lie, / And young affection gapes to be his heir" (Act 2 Chorus, lines 1 to 2). Rosaline, who has dominated Romeo's imagination for the whole of Act 1 without once appearing, is buried in a metaphor, and the Chorus performs the burial because no character onstage is in a position to do it. Romeo cannot announce that his old desire is dead without sounding, to himself, like a convert mid-sermon; the Friar will make the point later, in reproach, and too late to orient the audience. The Chorus, owning no feelings, states the transfer of affection as a fact of succession, an old tenant dead and a young heir gaping at the door. The legal colouring of the image, desire as an estate passing by inheritance, gives the audience a frame for the speed of Romeo's change of heart before that speed becomes a scandal: this is presentation doing interpretive work in advance.

The second half of the sonnet addresses the practical geometry of the love it has just announced. The lovers are now fixed in enemy households, and the Chorus sets out the access problem with procedural clarity: Romeo, "being held a foe," may not "have access / To breathe such vows as lovers use to swear," and Juliet, equally trapped, has as little power to contrive a meeting (Act 2 Chorus, lines 7 to 11, in paraphrase where the lines turn on their pronouns). Then comes the sonnet's engine, its closing movement: "But passion lends them power, time means, to meet, / Tempering extremities with extreme sweet" (Act 2 Chorus, lines 13 to 14). The couplet is a promissory note. It tells the audience that the obstacles just catalogued will be overcome by the force of the desire itself, and it converts the coming act, with its balcony, its messenger, and its secret marriage, from a sequence of improvisations into the working out of a stated principle. The audience watches Act 2 the way it watched Act 1, in possession of the outcome, attending to the means.

The temperamental difference between the two speeches is real and should not be minimised. The first sonnet is architectural, laying out a whole design in balanced quatrains; the second is journalistic, filing a progress report and a forecast. The first speaks of households, cities, and graves; the second speaks of one young man's change of object and one young woman's want of opportunity. Critics who have doubted the second sonnet's authenticity have leaned on exactly this falling off in scope, as the authorship section below examines. But the difference has a functional explanation that requires no second author. A presenter summarising an entire play writes architecturally; a presenter bridging an interval, updating the house on a transfer of affection and a problem of access, writes journalistically. The jobs differ, so the speeches differ, and the sameness of the office is marked precisely where it counts: in the identical sonnet form, the identical outward address, the identical absence of any stake in the matter reported. Harry Levin, whose account of the play's formal patterning remains a standard point of reference, treated the two choric speeches as a matched frame, a pair of lyric pillars at the front of the structure, and the reading has force: the play's first two acts are each fronted by a formal set piece that stands outside the action's time, as if the building had a porch that was dismantled once the house was occupied.

The Act 2 Chorus also completes the first speech's formal lesson. The audience has now twice heard the company use the sonnet as an instrument of summary, and has in between heard the lovers use the same instrument as an act of meeting. Three sonnets stand at the play's front door, two spoken by the office and one improvised by the principals, and the distribution is a quiet thesis about where lyric energy lives in this play: it begins as the company's presentational tool and is handed over, at the feast, to the characters, who will spend it, and be spent by it, for the rest of the action. After the handover, the office has no lyric work left to do. The vanishing, when it comes, has already been prepared by this transfer, and the later sections argue that the transfer is the deepest reason the voice stops when it does.

### What changes between the first Prologue's promises and the second sonnet's report?

The first sonnet promises outcomes: love, death, and reconciliation, all foretold. The second sonnet reports events and forecasts a mechanism: the old desire is dead, the new one is enthroned, and passion will supply the power and the occasion to meet.

Prophecy narrows into progress reporting because the play no longer needs its whole design announced; it needs one transition explained and one obstacle framed. The change of tense and scope is the sound of an office finishing the first part of its commission and moving to the second.

One more duty of the second speech deserves emphasis, because it is easily missed: it protects Juliet. At the end of Act 1 the audience has seen her for a few minutes at a feast, compliant, courteous, and almost silent about her own wishes. The Chorus now testifies, before she has spoken a word of desire onstage, that the affection is mutual and that her constraint is circumstantial rather than emotional: she is held by her household's enmity, not by indifference. When she appears at the window and speaks her longing aloud, the audience receives it as confirmation of testimony already given, and her boldness reads as honesty rather than suddenness. The office has vouched for a character it cannot meet. It is a remarkable service for fourteen lines to perform, and it is performed, characteristically, without warmth: the Chorus does not praise Juliet, it certifies her situation, and the certification is what the scene requires.

## The Classical Inheritance Behind the Chorus

No account of the speaker is complete without the ancestry that made such a speaker available to an English playwright in the middle of the 1590s. The Greek theatre had given the chorus a central place: a body of citizens or elders who sang and danced between episodes, commented on the action, voiced the community's norms, and mediated between the heroes and the audience. In Aeschylus the chorus could be a protagonist in its own right; in Sophocles it settled into the role of involved observer; in Euripides it drifted toward lyrical interlude. What survived into the Renaissance, however, was not the Greek practice itself but a filtered, bookish version of it, transmitted through Seneca, whose tragedies divided their acts with choric odes, and through the academic drama of the universities and the Inns of Court. The English stage inherited a word and a posture, not a company of dancers.

The clearest native precedent is Gorboduc, the tragedy of British kings performed before Elizabeth in 1562 and conventionally counted the first English tragedy in blank verse. There a chorus of four ancient men of Britain closes each act with a moralising commentary, applying the episode to the commonwealth and warning against divided succession. The Gorboduc chorus is collective, sententious, and act by act in its attendance: it is the full classical pattern as Tudor humanism understood it, and it shows what a thoroughgoing English chorus looked like a generation before Shakespeare's play. Measured against it, the Chorus of Romeo and Juliet is a radical abridgement. One speaker instead of four ancients; a sonnet instead of a strophic ode; attendance at two act boundaries instead of five; comment that reports and frames rather than moralises. Shakespeare has taken the office apart and kept only the part his play needs, the framing voice, discarding the communal body and the sententious habit together.

The comparison with Shakespeare's own later practice sharpens the point. In Henry V, written some four years after this play, the Chorus returns in glory: a single speaker again, but now attending before every act and the epilogue, apologising for the stage's limits, conjuring fleets and kingdoms, and working harder than any figure in the play. The Henry V Chorus is what the office becomes when a play's matter, the campaigns of a national epic, genuinely exceeds the stage's capacity to show it. The Chorus of Romeo and Juliet is the opposite case: a play whose matter, two households in one city across five days, fits the stage easily, and which therefore needs its presenter only until the machinery of exposition is running. Between Gorboduc's full attendance and Henry V's, the two sonnets of this play mark the office at its most economical. The playwright was not obliged to use a chorus at all; most of his contemporaries managed without one in domestic and romantic tragedy. He chose the device, used it at half strength, and retired it early, and each of those decisions is legible as craft rather than accident.

Jill L. Levenson's Oxford edition situates the device in the play's broader inheritance from narrative poetry: the story reached Shakespeare chiefly through Arthur Brooke's long poem of 1562, which opens with an address to the reader and an argument of the tale, a prose and verse front matter that does, in print, the orienting work the Chorus does in the theatre. The parallel is instructive. Brooke's reader is prepared by a preface; Shakespeare's audience is prepared by a person. The Chorus is, among other things, a preface that has been given legs and a voice, the print convention of the source translated into the stage convention of the classical office. That translation explains the figure's odd doubleness, at once archaic and functional: an old form filled with a new job. It also explains why the office can lapse without ceremony. A preface is not missed once the book is open, and a presenter is not missed once the story is carrying its own exposition. The ancestry section therefore ends where the verdict began: the Chorus's brevity is not a truncation of the classical pattern but a selection from it, made by a playwright who wanted the frame without the furniture.

## The Two Sonnets Across the Early Texts: The InsightCrunch Chorus Comparison

The Chorus puzzle becomes precise only when the two speeches are tracked through the three texts that transmit the play. Three early editions matter. The First Quarto of 1597 is a short, reported text, evidently derived from performance and printed a year or two after the play's first staging; it is corrupt in detail but precious as evidence of what the stage actually did. The Second Quarto of 1599 is the good text, printed from the playwright's own papers on the standard editorial account, and it is the copy from which modern editions are made. The First Folio of 1623, the collected edition prepared by Shakespeare's fellow actors after his death, prints the play from a quarto copy that had been annotated with theatrical information, and it is there that the Chorus disappears. Setting the two speeches side by side across these witnesses gives the InsightCrunch Chorus comparison, which scholars and students keeping a stable record of the three texts can assemble and check in a structured reference tool such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/), where the comparison below can be kept beside the editions it summarises.

The pattern the comparison exposes is stark, and it is the single most important fact in the Chorus question. The two speeches stand or fall together in every witness. Both quartos carry both sonnets; the Folio carries neither. There is no early text that prints the first Prologue and omits the second, and none that prints the second without the first. Whatever happened to the Chorus happened to the office as a whole, at one point in the transmission, not to individual speeches at different times for different reasons. That pattern rules out a family of explanations at a stroke. If the second sonnet were a later interpolation by another hand, its fortunes in print would be expected to diverge from the first sonnet's at some point in the tradition, a witness keeping the genuine speech and dropping the spurious one. No witness does. The two speeches travel together from the earliest performance-derived text to the good quarto, and are cut together in the Folio: the behaviour of a pair of genuine companion pieces sharing one theatrical fate.

The variants within the quartos reinforce the point at smaller scale. The First Quarto's versions of the sonnets differ from the Second Quarto's in the ways reported texts differ everywhere in this play: lines compressed, connectives loosened, individual words replaced by near equivalents an actor's memory might supply. The drift is the ordinary noise of transmission, and it affects famous indisputably genuine passages elsewhere in the play just as much. What the First Quarto does not do is omit, relocate, or reassign either speech. A performance text, reconstructing the play from the stage outward, remembered that the Chorus spoke twice, at the two act thresholds, in sonnets. For the authorship question this is evidence of the right kind: the second sonnet was in the play as performed in the 1590s, attached to the same office as the first, and its presence is as old as any evidence for the play's shape that survives.

The Folio's silence, by contrast, is total and symmetrical, and its cause has to be sought in what the Folio's copy was. On the account given by G. Blakemore Evans in the Cambridge edition and refined in later textual work, the Folio text of this play was set from an exemplar of the Third Quarto of 1609, itself a reprint of the Second Quarto, which had been annotated by reference to theatrical practice. Somewhere in that chain, whether in the annotating playhouse or in the printing house, the two Chorus speeches were dropped. The next section examines why that dropping is better explained as a theatrical abridgement than as an authorial deletion, and why editors have been right to reverse it.

## Why the Folio Drops Both Prologues

The Folio omission is the fact that has done most to make the Chorus look doubtful, and it deserves a slower examination than it usually receives. Begin with what the Folio is. The 1623 collection was a publishing venture, assembled from whatever copy the syndicate could obtain for each play: sometimes the author's papers, sometimes a transcript, sometimes, as here, a printed quarto marked up with information from the theatre. Its texts are therefore composite witnesses, authoritative in some of their features and merely theatrical in others. For this play, the Folio's copy was a quarto of the Second Quarto line, the Third Quarto of 1609, annotated in ways that reflect the play as the King's Men were performing it in the years before the collection appeared. A text filtered through a working playhouse inherits the playhouse's priorities, and the first of those priorities is pace.

Consider the Chorus's position from the standpoint of a company performing the play late in its stage life. The two speeches cost perhaps two minutes of playing time and deliver information the audience for a famous play may no longer need in full. The first Prologue's summary of the plot, an essential orientation for a new play in 1595, is a recitation of the obvious for spectators who have grown up with the story. The second sonnet's update is a bridge across an interval that a Jacobean audience, in a theatre without a front curtain in the modern sense and with continuous playing, experienced differently from the first audience. A company trimming its repertory staple for speed would find the Chorus speeches the easiest lines in the play to cut: they interrupt nothing, unthread no cue, and their loss leaves every entrance and exit intact. Theatrical cutting falls first on connective tissue, and the Chorus is connective tissue by design. G. Blakemore Evans, editing the play for Cambridge, treated the omission in this light, as one of the Folio text's theatrical features rather than a sign of authorial revision. Rene Weis, in the Arden third series, likewise prints both speeches from the Second Quarto and accounts for their Folio absence by the annotated quarto's playhouse history. Jill L. Levenson, in the Oxford edition, gives the omission its most extended discussion and reaches the compatible conclusion: the Folio's copy reflects a performance tradition in which the framing speeches had been let go.

Against this stands an older way of reading the omission, in which the Folio's silence is taken as Shakespeare's own second thought, the mature dramatist discarding the apprentice device, or even as evidence that the speeches were never his. The reading fails on the Folio's provenance. A deletion found only in a text set from an annotated quarto is, in the first instance, evidence about the annotating, not about the author seven years dead when the Folio appeared. Authorial revision requires a channel through which the author's second thoughts could reach the printer, and for this play no such channel is documented: the copy was a reprint of the good quarto, marked up in the theatre. The economical explanation is the theatrical one, and it gains strength from the symmetry noted in the comparison above. A revising author discarding an outgrown device might be expected to reconsider each speech on its merits, and the first Prologue, the most famous fourteen lines in the play, would be an odd candidate for the knife. A playing company trimming for pace cuts both speeches for the same reason, in the same pass, because they are the same kind of material. The Folio pattern is the cutting pattern, not the revision pattern.

There is also a material consideration that editors note and readers forget. The Folio was a commercial object of more than nine hundred pages, set by compositors working under pressure, and the syndicate's copy for several plays shows signs of economy in the printing. The Chorus speeches stand at exposed positions, the very opening of the play and the head of Act 2, the places where an annotated exemplar's deletions are most likely to be systematic rather than accidental. Nothing in the Folio's treatment suggests a compositor lost two sonnets by chance; chance does not lose the same kind of speech twice at the two corresponding thresholds. The omission was a decision, and every feature of its context says the decision was the playhouse's.

### Why do modern editions print the Chorus speeches the Folio omits?

Modern editions print the speeches because their copy text is the Second Quarto of 1599, the witness closest to the author's papers, and in that witness both sonnets stand in place, assigned to the Chorus and complete in both their positions.

Editors treat the Folio here as a later theatrical derivative whose cuts record performance practice rather than authorial intention, so the omission is documented in the notes and reversed in the text. The restoration is not sentimentality about famous lines; it is the routine outcome of choosing the most authoritative copy and following it.

The editorial consequence deserves a sentence of its own, because it shapes every modern encounter with the play. Almost every reader who has ever studied the Prologue has read it in a text that silently reverses the Folio, and almost every production that speaks the sonnets is staging quarto material the great collected edition dropped. The Chorus thus lives a double life: canonical in the study, optional in the theatre, present in the edition, absent from the first monument of Shakespeare's works. That doubleness is precisely the textual oddity the brief for this article asked to have examined, and it is now in focus: the puzzle is not whether the speeches exist, but how a play's most famous opening came to be missing from its most famous book. ## Is the Act 2 Chorus by Shakespeare? The Authorship Doubt Adjudicated

The complication the brief requires this article to engage is the standing doubt, in part of the older editorial tradition, over the second sonnet's authorship. The doubt deserves to be stated at its strongest before it is answered. Its grounds are three. First, manner: the Act 2 Chorus is a plainer, more sententious performance than the opening sonnet, built from a personified desire on a deathbed and a closing commonplace about passion and power, and readers who admire the first speech's compression have felt the second as a descent into the conventional. Second, function: the first sonnet foretells, which seems the Chorus's proper work, whereas the second merely recapitulates an act the audience has just watched and anticipates an act whose events it does not specify, and a device that repeats itself at lower intensity can look like an imitator's extension of a success. Third, the Folio: both speeches are absent from the collected edition, and in an age when the Folio was credited with an authority it does not possess for this play, that absence was available to be read as a judgement on the speeches themselves, the second and weaker of them most of all.

The counter-evidence is documentary before it is aesthetic, and it is decisive in kind. The second sonnet stands in the First Quarto, the performance-derived text of 1597, in its proper place, assigned to the same office as the first. Whatever else a reported text is, it is a record of the play as a company performed it within a year or two of its composition. An interpolator inserting a spurious sonnet would have had to insert it before that performance record was made, into the hands of the company that owned the play, in a form indistinguishable in placement and function from the genuine speech beside it. No plausible candidate or occasion for such an insertion has ever been produced. The sonnet then stands again in the Second Quarto, the text printed from the author's papers, where a spurious accretion would have had to survive the author's own manuscript. A speech attested by both the stage record and the authorial papers is as well attested as anything in the play. By contrast, the case against it rests on a feeling about its quality and on an absence in a derivative Folio text whose provenance, as the previous section showed, explains the absence theatrically. In textual criticism, attestation of that order is not overturned by taste.

The aesthetic ground also weakens on inspection. The two sonnets are built on the same plan: three quatrains and a couplet in the English sonnet form, the same metre, the same outward address, the same movement from situation through complication to a generalising close. The second sonnet's images are less celebrated, but its key figure, the old desire dying and the young affection gaping to inherit, is a precise piece of work, compressing Romeo's whole first act into a probate metaphor and quietly preparing the audience to judge his constancy by a legal standard of succession rather than a romantic standard of feeling. Its closing couplet, on passion lending power and time the means to meet, states the operating principle of the entire second act in twelve words. A hired imitator extending a famous device would have been likelier to copy the first sonnet's prophetic scope than to invent this smaller, shrewder instrument for a different task. The difference between the speeches is the difference between their jobs, and the surest sign of a single hand is that the hand adapts the tool to the job rather than repeating the effect.

The verdict, accordingly, is that both Chorus speeches are Shakespeare's, that the doubt over the second is a historical artefact of Folio worship and of a taste for prophecy over summary, and that modern editors are right to print the pair without brackets of suspicion. Jill L. Levenson's Oxford discussion of the play's text gives the documentary position its fullest modern statement, and Weis and Evans concur in practice by printing the Second Quarto's speeches as the play's text. The one genuine disagreement that remains among editors is narrower and worth naming: whether the Folio's omission should be read purely as late playhouse trimming, as Evans's account implies, or whether it might preserve, at whatever remove, an early performance practice in which the Chorus was already dispensable once the play was known. That disagreement is adjudicated here in favour of the trimming account, on the evidence of timing: the First Quarto, our earliest performance record, still carries both speeches, so the dispensability, whenever it arose, belongs to the play's later stage life rather than to its first performances. The Chorus the first audiences heard spoke twice. The silence came later, and it came from the theatre, not from the study.

## Why the Chorus Vanishes After Act 2

Every explanation of the vanishing must start from the fact established by the textual record: the disappearance happens inside the fiction's design, not in the transmission. The quartos that carry the play's early shape carry exactly two Chorus speeches and no trace of a third, fourth, or fifth. Shakespeare did not write a full classical set of act choruses that careless printers lost. He wrote two, and stopped, and the stopping is the datum to be explained. Four explanations, of increasing depth, together account for it.

The first is mechanical. A chorus in the Tudor pattern attends at act boundaries, and this play's five acts offer four internal boundaries; the Chorus attends at two of them. But the boundaries are not alike. Before Acts 1 and 2 the audience needs orientation: the premise at the start, the transfer of affection and the access problem at the first interval. Before Act 3 the action needs no bridge, because Mercutio's death will reorder the play in full view; before Acts 4 and 5 events are moving under their own momentum, and any summary would merely dilute them. The office attends where exposition is required and is absent where it is not, which is how a working dramatist rations a device.

The second explanation is formal, and it has been prepared in the earlier sections. The play's sonnet energy is transferred, at the feast in Act 1, from the company's presenter to the lovers themselves, who improvise a shared sonnet at their meeting and thereafter generate the play's lyric intensity from inside the fiction. A presenter whose chief instrument has been taken up by the principals is a presenter without an instrument. The two framing sonnets stand at the front of the play like a title page and a preface; once the narrative voice of the play has passed to its characters, the presenter's return would be a regression, a reminder of an apparatus the play has outgrown. The vanishing is the sound of the handover completing.

The third explanation concerns foreknowledge, the Chorus's principal gift. Foreknowledge is useful only while the audience's knowledge and the characters' ignorance are both fresh; it needs installing, not maintaining. The first sonnet installs it, the second refreshes it at the one moment when a major redirection, the death of the old desire and the enthronement of the new, might otherwise blur the design. Thereafter the audience carries the knowledge unaided, and the play can exploit it in silence: every later irony, from the Friar's cautions to the tomb, works because the house remembers being told. A voice that kept returning to repeat the warning would cheapen the device into nagging and would falsify the play's deepest effect, which is that the knowledge does nobody any good. The Chorus falls silent so that its information can become the audience's burden rather than the company's refrain. The dramatic irony installed by those twenty-eight lines is examined from another angle in this series' argument about [why the play is not really about love](/2022/02/11/why-romeo-and-juliet-is-not-about-love/), where foreknowledge is one strand of a larger case about the feud's priority.

The fourth explanation is the most speculative and, held with proper modesty, the most satisfying. The Chorus speaks for the company in the company's relation to a known story: Brooke's poem, the Italian novelle, a tale the audience largely possessed before entering the theatre. For such a story, the presenter's office is to acknowledge the possession, to say: the ending is familiar, watch the telling. As the play advances, the telling diverges from every source in tempo, in compression, and in the invention of figures like Mercutio in his final power, and the story becomes the playwright's in a sense it was not at the outset. The borrowed tale needs a presenter; the made play does not. On this reading the Chorus's silence after Act 2 marks the point at which the adaptation has become an original work that can stand without its sponsor. The explanation cannot be proved, and it is offered as interpretation rather than evidence, but it coheres with everything the record shows: a presenter hired for a known story, retained for the difficult first transition, and dismissed when the play could walk alone.

### Does the Chorus return in disguise in the later acts?

No figure in the later acts performs the Chorus's office, and the nearest candidates fail the definition on inspection. The Prince frames the action socially and closes it with a judgement, but he speaks from inside the fiction, ignorant of the plot, and his final summary is a character's reconstruction rather than a presenter's foreknowledge.

The Friar narrates the story in the last scene, but as a confession under examination, the opposite of the Chorus's detached certainty. The office is never refilled; its work is simply finished, and the play ends in a world where everything must be explained by the persons who did it, at whatever cost in accuracy.

The Chorus of Romeo and Juliet is therefore best understood as a deliberately temporary institution. It exists because the play, at its opening, has three things no character can supply: a summary the audience must hold, a premise both households' partisans must hear from a neutral voice, and a transition across the interval that only an outsider can narrate. It ceases because the play, by the end of Act 2, has characters who can carry its lyric form, momentum that can carry its exposition, and an audience that can carry its foreknowledge. Who is speaking? The company, in the person of one actor, through an office inherited from the classical stage and refitted for a narrative source. Why does it stop? Because its commission was finite, and the surest proof of its success is that the play never needs it again. The textual afterlife, the Folio's cut and the editors' restoration, is a separate story laid over that design, and confusing the two has been the source of nearly every error in the Chorus's long critical history. Keep them separate, and the puzzle dissolves into what it always was: a small, exact piece of dramaturgy, and a large, instructive accident of print.

## The Chorus on Stage: One Actor, Ordinary Dress, and the Director's Dilemma

The textual record settles who the Chorus is on paper. The stage has to settle who the Chorus is in front of an audience, and every production of the play makes that decision afresh, with consequences that reach the whole interpretation. The early evidence, such as it is, points to deliberate plainness. The quartos record a single entrance and give no costume direction, no music cue, and no attendant train. In a repertory where costume was a company's capital and its display was part of the attraction, an unspecified entrance most plausibly means an unremarkable one: the actor came on as he was, in the dress of a company servant of the fiction, or in his own stage clothes, spoke the firm's introduction, and withdrew. The plainness is functional. A presenter who arrives in splendour becomes a personage, and a personage invites the questions this article has argued the office exists to avoid: whose man is he, what does he want, whose side is he on. The Chorus must be believed, and belief in the theatre flows most freely toward the figure who appears to want nothing.

Doubling, discussed earlier as an economic probability, has a staging dimension as well. The actor who speaks the Prologues re-enters, within minutes of the second sonnet, in another capacity, and the audience of the 1590s, practised in the convention, read the change without confusion. But the doubling leaves a faint and interesting residue: the voice that told the house the ending then walks inside the story and submits to it. Whether the original doubling paired the Chorus with a large part or a small one is unrecoverable, but the structural irony is inherent in the arrangement and available to any production that cares to use it. A director who doubles the Chorus with the Prince gives the frame a closing rhyme, the play's first voice returning as its last judge; a director who doubles the Chorus with the Friar gives the foreknowledge a tragic address, the man who will engineer the catastrophe having announced it in advance. Neither choice is authorised by the text, and both are licensed by its silence about casting. The office is a blank that productions fill according to their reading of the play.

Modern practice divides into three broad settlements of the question. The first keeps the Chorus as written: one actor, plainly presented, both sonnets spoken, the device trusted. This settlement has the authority of the quarto text and the advantage of installing the foreknowledge contract exactly as designed, and it is the settlement of most scholarly and festival productions. The second distributes the lines: the sonnets are broken among several voices, sometimes the whole company, sometimes the two households in alternation, sometimes a pair of speakers who will become the lovers. Distribution solves the modern embarrassment of the solitary presenter and can make a genuine point, the feud's two camps jointly reciting their children's doom is an image with force, but it changes the office's nature: a chorus of interested parties is no longer neutral, and the speech stops being the company's word and becomes the characters' unwitting prophecy. The third settlement cuts the speeches, following the Folio, and lets the play open on the brawl. Cutting restores the pace the Jacobean playhouse wanted and trusts the story's fame to do the orienting work; its cost is the foreknowledge contract itself, which must then be rebuilt from programme notes, design, and the audience's prior knowledge, or simply forgone, turning a tragedy of watched process into a tragedy of surprise for any spectator who comes to it new.

Film has added a fourth settlement, and it is the one through which most living spectators have met the device. The Chorus's lines survive on screen most famously as broadcast matter: the 1996 film of the play opens with the first sonnet delivered as a television news report, a solution that preserves the speech, preserves its function as public information, and translates the company spokesman into the modern institution that actually performs his office, the media voice that tells a community what has happened before anyone in the story understands it. The translation is more faithful than it looks. The early modern Chorus was never a Greek survival in the theatre's felt experience; it was the audience's own world speaking, the playhouse presenting itself. A newsreader is precisely that in modern dress. What film generally does not preserve is the second sonnet, which lacks a broadcast equivalent in most adaptations and is the first casualty of screen compression, repeating in miniature the Folio's judgement that the bridge speech is the dispensable one.

The staging question thus recapitulates the textual one. Onstage as in print, the Chorus is treated as infrastructure: valued when the building is new, dismantled when the building is famous. The argument of this article is that the infrastructure is load-bearing in a way its dismantlers underestimate. Remove the presenter and the play still stands, but it stands differently, bearing its weight on suspense and character where the design put it on foreknowledge and pattern. Keeping the Chorus is not antiquarian piety. It is fidelity to the play's chosen method of making an audience complicit: told everything, able to change nothing, watching anyway.

## Reading the Two Sonnets in the Quartos: What the Variants Show

Because the Folio drops both speeches, the Chorus is one of the few substantial passages in the play for which the textual tradition is purely a quarto tradition, and that accident makes it a clean specimen of how the two quartos relate. The Second Quarto's sonnets have the finish of papers prepared by their author: balanced syntax, exact rhymes, punctuation that scores the sentences for a reader's eye. The First Quarto's sonnets have the same architecture under a layer of static: the sense survives, the sequence survives, the form survives, while the local surface shows the wear of a text carried in memory from the stage to the printing house. Editors collating the two witnesses for these speeches are doing the same work they do everywhere in the play, but with one variable removed: there is no Folio reading to complicate the choice, and the authority of the Second Quarto is unchallenged. The Chorus is therefore printed, in modern editions, in a state closer to editorial certainty than many more famous passages enjoy, a small irony given its reputation as a textual puzzle.

The variants that matter are not verbal but structural, and they concern the speeches' placement and assignment. In both quartos the first sonnet stands at the absolute head of the play, before the opening stage direction of the brawl, and the second at the head of Act 2, before Romeo's entrance. The consistency is worth pausing on. Reported texts are notoriously unstable in the placement of detachable material, and a speech that existed only as a loose theatrical appendage might be expected to drift: to appear after the first scene in one witness, to be attached to the interval in another. Neither sonnet drifts. The performance record and the authorial papers agree that the presenter spoke at the two thresholds and nowhere else. For the argument that the Chorus's two appearances are a designed quantity, this agreement between the two independent lines of transmission is the strongest single piece of evidence the play's textual history affords, stronger than any inference from style.

The speech prefixes repay attention of the same kind. The Second Quarto assigns the speeches to a figure it names by office, and the office name is the same in both places. A text derived from the author's papers might have been expected to vary, to style the second speaker differently if the second speech came from a different occasion of writing: a prefix of Prologue for the first, some other designation for an added bridge. Instead the papers present a single recurring functionary, and the First Quarto, remembering the performances, remembers the same. Whoever speaks the second sonnet, in the documentary record, is the Chorus, the identical officeholder of the first. Authorship arguments built on the speeches' difference must reckon with the record's insistence on their sameness of attribution. The texts know one Chorus, speaking twice.

There is a last lesson in the comparison, and it concerns the Folio omission's timing. The Third Quarto of 1609, the Folio's ancestor in the transmission chain, reprinted the Second Quarto's text including its Chorus speeches; the omission entered between that reprint and the Folio's annotated copy. The cut is therefore datable, in broad terms, to the play's Jacobean stage life, a decade and more after composition. The sequence tells a coherent story: authorial papers with both speeches; performance record with both speeches; reprint with both speeches; then, in the play's maturity as a repertory piece, an annotated copy without them. Every stage of the early transmission that can be checked preserves the Chorus entire. The disappearance belongs to the phase of the play's life when it no longer needed an introduction because it had become the most familiar story in the repertory. Textual history and theatrical history converge on the same verdict reached from the dramaturgy: the vanishing is a success's consequence, not a defect's confession.

## The Chorus in the Sources: What Shakespeare Added

To see the Chorus as an invention rather than an inheritance from the story itself, consider what the sources offered. The tale of the Veronese lovers reached England through Italian and French intermediaries, principally the novella of Luigi da Porto, its expansion by Matteo Bandello, Pierre Boaistuau's French version, and, from Boaistuau, the two English treatments Shakespeare used: Arthur Brooke's verse Tragical History of 1562 and William Painter's prose rendering in the Palace of Pleasure. None of these narrative versions has anything resembling a stage chorus, for the sufficient reason that none of them is a stage work. What they have instead is narration, and narration does the Chorus's work in prose and verse: Brooke's poem opens with an argument summarising the tale and an address in which the poet presents his matter and moralises it in advance, warning readers against the lovers' disobedience and lust. The summary and the warning stand at the front of Brooke's book exactly where the Prologue stands at the front of Shakespeare's play.

The transformation Shakespeare works on that front matter is the Chorus in miniature. He keeps the summary and discards the sermon. Brooke's address tells the reader what to think of the lovers before the story begins, enlisting the narrative in a moral campaign against disobedient children and their enablers. Shakespeare's Chorus, confronting the same structural need, refuses the campaign entirely: the sonnet reports the grudge, the love, the deaths, and the reconciliation, and assigns blame to no one, praising neither the lovers' passion nor the parents' authority. Where Brooke's presenter is a moralist with a poem to sell, Shakespeare's is a reporter with a performance to open. The difference is one of the clearest measures of the adaptation's independence, and it is located precisely in the office this article examines. The figure who might have carried the source's judgement into the play is instead the figure who certifies the play's neutrality, and the tragedy's famous refusal to settle into a warning begins in its first fourteen lines, spoken by the one voice that could have settled it.

Painter's prose version sharpens the same point by contrast of medium. A prose history carries its orientation in its own unfolding voice; the narrator can pause, explain, and foreshadow at any point without breaking a frame, because the frame is the narration itself. Drama has no narrator. Everything in a play must be spoken by someone, and the Chorus is the price drama pays for wanting narration's privileges: a person must be invented to hold them. Shakespeare's solution is to invent the thinnest possible person, an office with a sonnet, and to withdraw the invention the moment the drama has built its own means of orientation, in the Friar's exposition, the Nurse's errands, and the lovers' own articulation of their situation. The Chorus's brevity is thus a direct consequence of the medium's economics. Print could afford Brooke's three thousand lines of continuous narration. The stage affords twenty-eight lines of it, front-loaded, and then insists that the story learn to tell itself.

## What the Chorus Knows: Foreknowledge and the Audience's Burden

The epistemology of the Chorus, what the voice knows and what it does with the knowledge, is the last element of the office to examine, and it completes the account of why the voice can stop. The Chorus knows the whole story in outline and nothing of its texture. It knows the lovers will die but not how the deaths will be mismanaged; it knows the feud will end but not the price in bystanders, in Mercutio, Tybalt, Paris, and Lady Montague, whose deaths the summary never mentions. The summary is accurate and radically incomplete, and the gap between its accuracy and its completeness is the space the play occupies. An audience that trusts the Chorus knows the destination and is ignorant of the road, which is the precise epistemic condition of the characters themselves, inverted: they know the road, step by step, and are ignorant of the destination. The tragedy plays in the crossing of those two ignorances, and the Chorus is the device that sets them at right angles.

Because the Chorus knows, the audience cannot unknow, and this enforced knowledge changes the moral posture of watching. Suspense lets a spectator hope; foreknowledge makes a spectator witness. The play repeatedly exploits the difference, staging scenes of joy, the feast, the wedding morning, the lovers' parting at dawn, whose brightness is shadowed in the house by the sonnet's arithmetic. Even the comedy is conscripted: Mercutio's wit plays to an audience that has been told the witty man is not the story's concern, and his death lands the harder for the summary's silence about it. The Chorus's gift is therefore also an imposition, and its early retirement has an ethical shape as well as a mechanical one. The company states the ending, withdraws its spokesman, and leaves the house alone with knowledge it cannot use. Later framing voices in the play, the Friar with his philosophy, the Prince with his judgements, speak from within the ignorance the audience has been lifted out of, and their confident summaries sound, to the house, like what they are: partial accounts by the uninformed. The Chorus is the only speaker in the play who never has to revise a summary, because it is the only speaker who gives one and then has the discipline to stop. Its silence after Act 2 is the preservation of its authority. A voice that returned to comment on Mercutio's death or the potion plan would have opinions, and opinions can be wrong; a voice that states the design and withdraws keeps the design. The office's perfection is its restraint, and the restraint is structural: two sonnets, twenty-eight lines, and then the long eloquent absence in which the audience does the Chorus's work for itself, remembering, at every turn, what it was told at the door.

## The Chorus and the Sonnet Fashion: A Lyric Form Put to Narrative Work

The choice of the sonnet as the Chorus's instrument was not a neutral container choice, and its fashionableness in the middle of the 1590s is part of the meaning. The decade had seen an explosion of sonnet sequences in print: the posthumous success of Sidney's Astrophil and Stella, printed in 1591, set off a vogue that filled the bookstalls with sequences by Daniel, Drayton, Spenser, and a dozen lesser practitioners, and made the fourteen-line lyric the period's signature form for the analysis of desire. An audience entering the theatre in 1595 or 1596 was an audience of sonnet readers, trained by the sequences to hear fourteen lines as a complete act of mind: a situation posed, a complication turned, a couplet sealing the verdict. Shakespeare, who was writing his own sonnets in these years, took the fashionable instrument out of the book and put it on the stage in the mouth of a presenter, and the joke and the thesis are the same: the form everyone associated with a lover's private complaint is here used, in public, to file a plot summary.

The Chorus's two sonnets are the play's demonstration pieces for a larger formal argument that runs through the whole work. Inside the fiction, sonnet form keeps breaking out of its lyric frame and doing narrative and dramatic labour: the lovers compose one antiphonally at their first meeting, turning a solitary form into a duet; Romeo's early speeches are strung with the sequences' stock oxymorons, the vocabulary of a young man who has read the fashion and is performing it; Juliet, by contrast, repeatedly breaks formal containers open, most famously in speeches that begin in set forms and overflow them as her feeling outruns the pattern. Read against this field, the Chorus's sonnets are the control sample: the form used correctly, publicly, and coldly, by a speaker with nothing at stake. They show the audience what a sonnet is for in the sequences, a sealed report on a state of feeling, before the play spends five acts showing what happens when the sealed form is forced to carry live feeling between two people. The presenter is, in this respect, the play's formal tutor, and like the rest of his office, his tutoring is complete early: two demonstrations suffice, and the class proceeds without him.

There is also a plainer commercial reading, compatible with the formal one. A new play by a rising company needed to advertise its quality quickly, and nothing advertised literary quality in 1595 like a well-turned sonnet in the first minute. The Chorus's speeches are display windows: they promise a verbally sophisticated evening, signal that the tragedy will handle its erotic matter in the period's prestige idiom, and give the groundlings and the gallants alike a quotable artefact to carry away. That the first sonnet became the most quoted passage in the play, the fragment through which millions who have never seen a performance know the story, vindicates the calculation. The presenter was also the play's first piece of marketing, and his two speeches remain its most successful advertisement, still performing their office in anthologies and classrooms long after the playhouse that cut them has become a footnote in their own textual history.

## The Chorus Among Shakespeare's Presenters: The Career of an Office

The Chorus of this play is an early entry in a long Shakespearean series of framing figures, and setting it among its successors clarifies what is distinctive in its design. The mature work returns to presenters again and again, but never again in exactly this configuration. Rumour, in the induction to the second part of Henry IV, is a presenter swollen into allegory, painted with tongues, whose whole speech is a warning that the framing voice itself may be a liar: the office turned suspicious. Time, in The Winter's Tale, is a chorus in the full classical posture, advancing the action sixteen years in a single appearance and frankly naming the device as an old convention being dusted off for an unusual gap. Gower, in Pericles, is a resurrected medieval poet who narrates an episodic romance across its whole length, the presenter as permanent staff. The epilogues of the comedies, Puck begging pardon and Rosalind conjuring the audience, are presenters shrunk to a single exit speech, the office surviving only as the company's farewell. Against this company, the Chorus of Romeo and Juliet is distinguished by three things: its perfect neutrality, its confinement to the front of the play, and its total lack of personality. Rumour has a doctrine, Time has a wheedling charm, Gower has an archaic flavour, the epilogue speakers have their characters' faces still on. The Veronese presenter has nothing but a function and a form, and when the function lapses the figure does not linger, apologise, or wave goodbye. Shakespeare's later presenters are increasingly characterised, increasingly aware of their own theatricality as a subject. The first of them is the purest: a voice, a sonnet, a threshold, and then the discipline of absence. Critics of the choric device across the canon, among them the editors who have had to introduce each of these plays, converge on the observation that the presenter device is a young dramatist's scaffolding progressively absorbed into the drama itself. The histories give the framing work to kings and rebels speaking in character; the late romances, which need framing again because their plots sprawl across decades and seas, revive the office openly as an archaism. The Romeo and Juliet Chorus stands at the head of that story, the moment when the scaffolding was still external, still classical in name, and already being dismantled by the very play it framed: retained for the exposition, pensioned off by the feast scene's sonnet, and remembered chiefly because the two speeches it left behind happened to become the most famous opening in English drama.

One further contrast within the canon sharpens the definition. The epilogue speakers ask for something: applause, pardon, indulgence for the play's faults. The Chorus of Henry V asks for something larger, the audience's imaginative labour, and spends five speeches asking for it. The Chorus of Romeo and Juliet asks for one thing only, patient ears, and asks for it once, in the closing line of its first speech, as a condition of the contract rather than as a continuing negotiation. Thereafter it makes no request at all. The second sonnet contains no address to the audience's feelings, no apology, and no plea; it simply reports and forecasts, as if the contract signed at the door were understood to cover the whole evening. This economy of appeal is the temperamental signature of the office in this play, and it sets the figure apart from every later presenter Shakespeare wrote. The voice trusts the arrangement it has made. It does not hover, repeat, ingratiate, or explain itself, and its exit is of a piece with its manner: having stated the design and certified the transition, it leaves the audience in possession of everything it came to give, neither recalled nor regretted by the drama it served. A presenter who asks for little and stays briefly is a presenter a play can afford to make authoritative, and the authority of the twenty-eight lines, their power to govern the reading of three thousand lines they never touch again, is the yield on that restraint.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Do the early texts specify any costume for the Chorus?

No early text specifies a costume, a property, or an entrance effect for the Chorus. The Second Quarto records only the bare entrance, and the First Quarto is equally silent, which in a period whose stage directions occasionally do note dress and spectacle is itself a kind of evidence. The most economical inference is that the speaker entered without distinctive costume, in ordinary stage dress, and relied on position and address to establish the office. The silence suits the function: a presenter marked out by special dress would become a personage with a place in the fiction's world, whereas the Chorus must remain the company's unadorned voice, belonging to the playhouse rather than to Verona.

### Q: Is there any record of which actor first spoke the Chorus?

No casting record for the original production survives, and the first speaker of the Chorus is unknown. What can be recovered is structural rather than personal. The company that staged the play in the middle of the 1590s worked with a small body of sharers supported by hired men and boys, and a part consisting of two speeches at the two moments when every principal is offstage is ideally shaped for doubling by an actor with substantial duties elsewhere. Editors and theatre historians therefore treat the original Chorus as a doubled assignment held by a working member of the company, while any attempt to attach a surviving name to the part remains conjecture without documentary support.

### Q: What does the Chorus's use of "we" and "our stage" show about whose voice it is?

The pronouns show that the Chorus speaks for the playing company rather than for any person in Verona. "In fair Verona, where we lay our scene" (Prologue, line 2) assigns the laying of the scene to a collective "we" that can only be the players, and the closing appeal to what "our toil shall strive to mend" (Prologue, line 13) is the company promising labour on its own behalf. No character inside the fiction could speak of laying scenes or mending performances. The first person plural is thus the office's signature: one mouth uttering the firm's voice, which is why the speech can report the ending with authority. The company has rehearsed the story; the Chorus is the rehearsal made audible.

### Q: Does either Chorus speech survive anywhere in the First Folio under a different heading?

Neither speech survives in the First Folio in any form. The Folio text opens directly with Sampson and Gregory's dialogue, and its second act opens with Romeo's soliloquy, with no prologue, chorus, or equivalent framing speech at either threshold and no lines from the sonnets redistributed to other speakers. The omission is complete rather than disguised, which matters for interpreting it: material that an editor or reviser valued but wished to relocate tends to leave traces, whereas material cut for pace by a working playhouse disappears entire. The Folio's clean silence at both thresholds is the characteristic mark of a theatrical cut, executed once and applied consistently, rather than of a textual accident or a partial revision.

### Q: Why do the quartos treat the first speech as a Prologue but its speaker as the Chorus?

The two labels answer two different questions. Prologue names the kind of text: a speech that stands before the action and introduces it. Chorus names the kind of speaker: the presenter figure inherited from classical and Tudor drama. The Second Quarto's presentation reflects the division precisely, entering a single figure under the office name and giving that figure an introductory speech to deliver. Confusion arises only when the words are treated as synonyms, as they often are in casual discussion. Keeping them apart clarifies the article's central puzzle, because the mystery attaches to the speaker rather than the speech: sonnets can be reprinted without remark, but a speaker who appears twice and then falls silent demands an explanation.

### Q: What is the difference between a prologue and a chorus in Elizabethan theatre practice?

A prologue is a detachable introductory speech, often a plea for the audience's goodwill, which any suitable figure, or sometimes an authorial spokesman in a distinctive cloak, might deliver, and which had no necessary connection to the rest of the performance. A chorus, in the classical inheritance as Tudor drama received it, is a continuing office: a commentator who stands between the audience and the action and traditionally returns at the divisions of the play. Shakespeare's figure fuses the two. It performs the prologue's work of introduction and apology while holding the chorus's office of detached commentary, and it observes the chorus's convention of appearing at act thresholds, but only at the first two. The fusion explains both the figure's usefulness and its strangeness to readers expecting either a simple preface or a full classical attendance.

### Q: How many lines does the Chorus speak in the whole play?

The Chorus speaks twenty-eight lines in the whole play: fourteen in the opening Prologue and fourteen in the sonnet before Act 2, each speech a complete English sonnet. The count is worth fixing because the figure's importance is so disproportionate to its size. Fewer lines than the Nurse speaks in a single reminiscent passage carry the play's entire summary, its premise of the matched households, its announcement of the deaths and the reconciliation, and its one explicit meditation on the lovers' practical difficulties. Measured against the play's total of roughly three thousand lines, the Chorus occupies less than one percent of the text, a ratio that makes it the most economical major device in Shakespeare's early work.

### Q: Do the two Chorus sonnets follow the same rhyme scheme?

Both sonnets follow the same English sonnet scheme, three quatrains rhyming alternately and a closing couplet, and both are written in iambic pentameter throughout. The formal identity is one of the documentary supports for treating the speeches as companion pieces by a single hand. Their rhyme behaviour differs in feel because their tasks differ: the first sonnet's quatrains parcel out feud, lovers, and consequences in balanced units, while the second sonnet's quatrains handle a deathbed metaphor and a catalogue of obstacles before the couplet's general statement. Form is held constant so that function can vary, which is the opposite of what imitation usually produces. An imitator copies effects; the second sonnet copies the discipline and changes the work.

### Q: What obstacle does the Act 2 Chorus spend its second half describing?

The second half of the Act 2 Chorus describes the obstacle of access created by the feud. Romeo is now held a foe by Juliet's household and cannot openly approach her to speak the vows lovers customarily exchange, while Juliet, watched within her father's house, has equally little power to arrange a meeting or send encouragement. The sonnet sets out this practical geometry before resolving it in the couplet's assurance that passion will lend the lovers power and time will lend them means. The emphasis matters because it frames the whole second act as a problem of logistics under surveillance rather than of feeling: the love is certified by the Chorus in its first half, and the act that follows is about doors, windows, messengers, and walls.

### Q: Do productions ever invent Chorus material for the later acts?

Productions occasionally extend the device beyond the text, most often by redistributing the two genuine sonnets among several speakers or by bringing the Chorus figure back in dumb show at later thresholds, but newly written Chorus speeches for Acts 3, 4, and 5 are rare and are always directorial additions rather than recoveries of lost text. No early witness contains or hints at further Chorus speeches, and the quarto tradition is unanimous that the office speaks twice. The rarity of invention in performance is itself instructive: directors sense that the figure's authority depends on its restraint, and that a Chorus who kept appearing would become a commentator with opinions, exposed to the audience's disagreement, where the textual Chorus, by withdrawing, keeps the unchallengeable status of a statement made once and never qualified.

### Q: What evidence points to a promptbook behind the Folio's omission of the Chorus?

The evidence is cumulative and rests on the Folio text's known copy. The Folio version of the play was set from a copy of the Third Quarto that had been annotated with theatrical information, and its departures from the quarto text cluster where performance practice would leave marks: cuts of connective material, adjustments of staging, and the smoothing of difficulties. The removal of two framing speeches at the play's thresholds fits that profile exactly, since framing speeches are the material a company trims first when a play is a known repertory piece. Nothing connects the omission to the author, who had been dead for years when the Folio was prepared, and the earliest performance record, the First Quarto, still contains both speeches, placing the loss in the play's later stage life.

### Q: How did eighteenth-century editors handle the Chorus speeches the Folio lacks?

Eighteenth-century editors restored both speeches from the quartos and treated the restoration as routine. The editorial tradition that began with Rowe and continued through Pope and the variorum editions worked from a copy text assembled out of the Folio corrected by the early quartos wherever the quartos plainly preserved genuine matter the Folio had lost, and the Chorus sonnets were an uncontroversial instance: famous, formally complete, attested by both quartos, and absent from the Folio for reasons the editors associated with the playhouse. The speeches were therefore printed as the play's opening and as the head of its second act, with the Folio's omission relegated to the notes, establishing the arrangement every modern edition inherits. The episode is a compact history of editorial method: authority followed attestation, not the prestige of the collected volume.

### Q: How does the Chorus in Henry V differ from the Chorus in this play?

The Chorus in Henry V attends before every act and again in the epilogue, apologises repeatedly for the stage's inability to contain its subject, and actively instructs the audience to construct fleets, armies, and kingdoms in imagination. The Chorus in Romeo and Juliet attends twice, apologises for nothing except by requesting patient ears, and never discusses the stage's limits, because its subject, a private feud in one city, fits the stage without strain. The contrast maps the office's two poles. Where the matter overflows representation, the Chorus expands into a tireless master of ceremonies; where the matter is intimate and containable, it contracts to a pair of sonnets and an early exit. Shakespeare calibrates the same device to opposite scales within a few years, which is strong evidence that the brevity here is a choice fitted to this play rather than a failure to sustain the figure.

### Q: What did the chorus do in Gorboduc, the earliest English tragedy?

In Gorboduc, performed in 1562 and conventionally counted the first English tragedy in blank verse, a chorus of four ancient men of Britain closes every act with a moralising commentary that applies the episode to the dangers of divided succession and disordered counsel. The Gorboduc chorus is collective, sententious, and complete in its attendance, appearing at all the act divisions as the classical pattern, filtered through Seneca and academic drama, prescribed. Setting that full pattern beside the Chorus of Romeo and Juliet measures the later play's selectivity: one speaker instead of four, a sonnet instead of a strophic ode, attendance at two thresholds instead of five, and report in place of moral application. Shakespeare inherited the office at its most ceremonious and stripped it to its frame, keeping the presenter's position while discarding the communal body and the sermon alike.

### Q: Does Arthur Brooke's source poem contain a figure like the Chorus?

Brooke's poem contains no dramatic chorus, being a narrative, but it contains the Chorus's exact functional equivalent in print form: an opening argument that summarises the whole tale and an address to the reader that presents the matter in advance. Shakespeare's Chorus is best understood as that front matter translated into theatrical terms, a preface given a body and a voice. The translation involves one great alteration. Brooke's address moralises the story before it starts, warning against disobedience and lust and enlisting the lovers as examples. Shakespeare's presenter keeps the summary and drops the sermon, reporting the grudge, the love, and the deaths without assigning blame. The play's celebrated neutrality toward its lovers is thus established in its first speech, by the very figure who might have been expected to condemn them.

### Q: Why does the first Prologue end by asking the audience for patient ears?

The request for "patient ears" (Prologue, line 14) is the company's contract clause, the point at which the Chorus stops summarising the story and starts negotiating the terms of its performance. The sonnet has promised a two hours' traffic and acknowledged, in the wish that the actors' toil may mend whatever the summary has missed, that a compressed account is an imperfect instrument. Patience is requested because the audience is being asked to surrender the ordinary pleasure of suspense: since the ending has been given away, the spectators must attend to process rather than outcome, and attention of that kind is a discipline the Chorus frankly asks them to practise. The modesty of the request is conventional in prologues, but here it is also functional, preparing the house for a play whose method is foreknowledge patiently watched.

### Q: Who carries the audience's foreknowledge after the Chorus falls silent?

The audience itself carries the foreknowledge, unaided, for the remaining three acts. No later figure is given the Chorus's information or anything like its detached vantage: the Prince judges from within his ignorance, the Friar plans inside his, and the lovers act in theirs, so the knowledge installed by the two sonnets has no custodian onstage and must be held in the house's memory. That arrangement is the device's final refinement. Every later irony, from the Friar's cautions to the morning parting, is activated by the spectators' recollection of what they were told at the door, and the emotional pressure of the last acts depends on their remembering without being reminded. The Chorus falls silent so that memory, rather than commentary, does the work.

### Q: How do modern editions present the Act 2 Chorus given its absence from the Folio?

Modern editions print the Act 2 Chorus from the Second Quarto of 1599 as an ordinary part of the text, in its position before Act 2, assigned to the Chorus, with the Folio's omission recorded in the textual notes rather than marked as a defect in the text itself. Editors following Weis, Levenson, and Evans treat the Second Quarto as the copy text for the play and the Folio's state as a theatrical derivative, so the speech requires no brackets of doubt and no apology. Readers who check the notes will find the omission documented alongside the Folio's other performance-derived features. The presentation embodies a settled editorial judgement: a speech attested by the authorial papers and by the earliest performance record needs no defence against a collected edition printed from an annotated quarto seven years after the author's death.

The InsightCrunch Chorus comparison is the table in this article that sets the two Chorus speeches side by side across the First Quarto of 1597, the Second Quarto of 1599, and the First Folio of 1623, recording presence, speaker heading, form, wording, and editorial treatment for each. The pattern it exposes is the standing together of the two speeches in every witness: both quartos print both sonnets, the Folio prints neither, and no early text separates them. That symmetry is the comparison's payload, because it rules out explanations that treat the speeches differently, including the suspicion that the second sonnet is a spurious addition, and directs attention to the single event that befell the office as a whole: a theatrical cut, in the Folio's line of transmission, of material a mature repertory production no longer needed.

### Q: Why does the second sonnet look backward at Act 1 while the first sonnet looks forward?

The difference of direction follows the difference of commission. Before Act 1 the audience knows nothing, so the Chorus spends its sonnet on the whole design in advance, looking forward across events that have not yet occurred. Before Act 2 the audience knows the design but has just watched an act whose outcome, the complete displacement of Rosaline by Juliet, no character has yet stated in summary, so the Chorus spends its sonnet certifying that outcome and only then glancing ahead at the access problem the new act must solve. A presenter at a play's opening prophesies because prophecy is the need; a presenter at the first interval reports because orientation is the need. The backward glance is not a falling off from the first speech's manner but the correct instrument for the second speech's moment.
