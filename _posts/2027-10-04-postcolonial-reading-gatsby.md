A postcolonial reading of The Great Gatsby begins with a question that a plain reading of the novel rarely thinks to ask: whose land is the dream standing on? Read for plot and character, Fitzgerald's book is a story about love, money, and the impossibility of repeating the past. Read for its own final image, it becomes something stranger and older. The last paragraphs pull back from Long Island to the whole continent and to the first Europeans who ever looked at it, and in that pullback the private tragedy of one man reaching for a green light widens into a national story about arrival, ownership, and the ground that ownership rests on. The postcolonial lens is the tool that keeps the reader's eye on that ground.

This is not the interpretation most students meet first. The novel does not obviously belong to the literature of empire. There are no colonies in it, no distant administrators, no conquered peoples on the page. Its world is jazz, gin, and the manicured lawns of the newly rich. And yet the closing vision reaches back to a colonial encounter, the moment when Dutch sailors first saw the coast of a continent they would take, and it names the American Dream itself as the inheritor of that moment. To read the novel postcolonially is to take that gesture seriously and follow it, to ask what it means that a book about American aspiration ends by remembering conquest.

## What a postcolonial reading of The Great Gatsby brings into view

A postcolonial reading is not a hunt for hidden villains or a demand that a 1925 novel meet the political standards of a later century. It is a method, a way of asking a specific set of questions of a text so that features invisible under other approaches come forward. Applied to Fitzgerald, the method foregrounds three things that a plain reading tends to pass over.

The first is the frontier. The American Dream that Gatsby embodies is usually described in the language of self-invention: the poor boy who remakes himself, the belief that anyone can become anyone. That is true as far as it goes, but the self-invention has a stage, and the stage is a continent that was settled by displacing the people already living on it. The dream of unlimited opportunity is inseparable from the dream of unlimited land, and the land was not empty. A postcolonial reading holds those two dreams together and asks what the novel does with their connection.

The second is the direction of the story. The book is set in the East, at the two Eggs of Long Island and in Manhattan, but its characters are all Westerners, and its narrator tells us at the end that the whole thing has been, in his words, [a story of the East and West](/2024/10/28/gatsby-east-and-west/) and finally a story of the West. The westering impulse, the movement toward the frontier and the sunset, is the deep grammar of the American myth, and it is a grammar of expansion into other people's territory. When Nick frames his tale as a Western one, he is invoking the settler narrative whether he means to or not.

The third is the closing vision. In the final paragraphs the narrator imagines the island as it was before any house stood on it, "flowered once for Dutch sailors' eyes," and calls that first sight of the continent the great dream from which all the smaller American dreams descend. This is the single most important passage for a postcolonial reading, and it is remarkable that Fitzgerald put it there at all. He did not have to end a novel about a bootlegger and a debutante by remembering the seventeenth-century colonial encounter. That he did is the textual warrant for the whole approach.

Bring these three elements together and a shape emerges. The dream the novel mourns is a settler's dream. Its promise of a fresh start on open land is the promise the continent seemed to make to the first Europeans who saw it, and the novel's tragedy is not only that Gatsby cannot reach Daisy but that the dream itself was built on a taking that can never be undone. That is what a postcolonial reading brings into view: the conquest at the root of the American Dream.

### What is a postcolonial reading of a novel?

A postcolonial reading interprets a text through the histories and legacies of empire, conquest, and colonization. It asks how a work represents or forgets colonized peoples, how it treats land and ownership, and how the myths it inherits are shaped by settlement and displacement, even when no colony appears directly on the page.

## Postcolonial theory in plain terms

Before applying the lens, it helps to know what it is. Postcolonial theory is the body of criticism that studies literature and culture in relation to the history of European colonialism and its aftermath. It grew up in the second half of the twentieth century, as former colonies gained independence and writers and scholars from those places began to interrogate the ways empire had shaped both the colonized world and the imagination of the colonizers.

Its founding texts give a sense of its concerns. Edward Said's study of how Western writing constructed an imagined, inferior East taught critics to notice how texts produce and naturalize the power relations of empire. Frantz Fanon wrote about the psychology of colonizer and colonized, the way domination shapes the inner life of both. Later thinkers such as Gayatri Spivak asked whether the colonized subject can speak within the archives and languages of the powerful, and Homi Bhabha explored the unstable, hybrid identities that colonial contact produces. These writers disagree on much, but they share a starting assumption: that empire is not a background detail of modern literature but one of its deep structures, and that reading with empire in mind changes what a text means.

For a novel like The Great Gatsby, the relevant strand of the theory is the analysis of the settler colony. A settler colony is different from an administered one. In an administered colony the imperial power extracts wealth and rules a native majority from a distance. In a settler colony the colonizers come to stay, to take the land and make it their own, and the native population is displaced, removed, or destroyed rather than governed. The United States is the settler colony par excellence, and the frontier myth is the settler colony's story about itself: a story in which the land is empty or wild, the settler is a pioneer rather than a conqueror, and the taking is reframed as discovery, cultivation, and destiny.

The study of settler colonialism has developed its own vocabulary, and a few of its terms make the reading of Gatsby sharper. One is the legal fiction by which colonizers declared a settled continent to be empty land, unowned and available, so that taking it could be called discovery rather than seizure. The frontier myth is the cultural version of that fiction: it pictures the West as wilderness waiting for the pioneer, a blank that becomes valuable only when the settler improves it. Another term names the way settler societies imagine the original inhabitants as fading, a people already vanishing whose disappearance the settler merely witnesses rather than causes. This is the myth of the vanishing native, and it lets the settler mourn what the settler destroys. A third describes settler colonialism as a structure rather than a single event, a logic that keeps operating long after the initial conquest, organizing law, property, and imagination for generations. These concepts matter for the novel because they name exactly the moves its imagination makes: land pictured as offering itself, prior inhabitants present only as absence, and a settled order that treats its own founding taking as natural belonging.

A postcolonial reading of an American text, then, does not look for a distant empire. It looks for the settler myth working from the inside, shaping how the characters imagine land, opportunity, and their own arrival. The question is not whether Fitzgerald wrote about colonialism on purpose. The question is whether the myth he inherited, and the dream he set out to anatomize, carries the settler colony's structure inside it. The claim of this reading is that it does, and that the novel, almost against its own surface, ends by saying so.

### What is the difference between a settler colony and an administered one?

An administered colony is ruled from afar so an imperial power can extract wealth from a native majority. A settler colony is one the colonizers move to permanently, taking the land and displacing or destroying the original inhabitants. The American frontier myth is the settler colony's flattering story about that taking.

## The questions a postcolonial lens asks

Every critical lens is a set of questions, and the questions are what make it a method rather than a mood. A reader applying the postcolonial lens to Gatsby is not looking for a predetermined verdict; the reader is running the text through a series of interrogations and seeing what answers the prose gives back.

The first question is about land. Where does the land in this novel come from, and how does the text describe its acquisition? Ownership is everywhere in Gatsby, in the mansions, the lawns, the shore places, the very division of the island into East Egg and West Egg. A postcolonial reading asks whether the novel ever remembers that this land had earlier owners, and it finds that in the final pages it does, when the narrator strips away the "inessential houses" and sees the island as it was before settlement.

The second question is about the myth of the fresh start. The American Dream promises reinvention, a clean slate, a self freed from the past. A postcolonial reading asks what the clean slate was cleaned of. A continent does not become open by nature; it is made open by the removal of the people who were there. So when the novel treats the West and the frontier as a space of possibility, the lens asks what possibility rests on, and for whom the slate was ever clean.

The third question is about the direction and desire of the narrative. Whose point of view organizes the story, and what does that point of view take for granted? Nick Carraway narrates from within the settler culture; he descends from a family that came West three generations back and built its fortune there. The lens asks how his unexamined assumptions about land, family, and belonging structure the tale he tells, and whether the novel ever lets those assumptions become visible as assumptions.

The fourth question is about complicity and critique. Does the text endorse the myths it depicts, or does it hold them up for examination? This is the hardest and most interesting question, because the answer is not simple. Fitzgerald is often a critic of the American Dream; the novel is famously an indictment of the carelessness and hollowness of the rich. But a postcolonial reading asks a sharper version of the question. Even where the novel criticizes the dream, does it criticize the conquest at the dream's root, or does it mourn the dream as a lost innocence, treating the settler's first vision of the continent as something pure that was later betrayed? The reading this article defends argues that the novel does both at once, and that the tension between them is the source of its final power.

These four questions, about land, about the fresh start, about narrative point of view, and about complicity, are the engine of the reading. They are portable; a student can carry them to any American text and find that they open it in unexpected ways. Applied to Gatsby they lead directly to the passages where the settler myth surfaces, and it is to those passages that the reading now turns.

## The lens applied: three passages

A critical lens proves itself on specific text, not on general claims. Here are three passages from the novel read through the postcolonial questions, moving from the most explicit to the most buried.

### The Dutch sailors and the fresh green breast of the new world

The clearest passage is the famous one, and it belongs at the center of any postcolonial reading. In the novel's last movement the narrator sits on the beach at night, the summer's events behind him, and lets the modern scene dissolve. He becomes aware of the island as it was before the houses, the island that "flowered once for Dutch sailors' eyes," and he names that first sight of the continent as a moment when European wonder met a new world.

Read plainly, the passage is elegiac and beautiful, a meditation on wonder and loss. Read through the postcolonial lens, every phrase turns. The sailors are Dutch, which locates the passage precisely: the Dutch colonized the region that became New York, and the island the narrator sits on was taken in exactly the settlement the vision remembers. The continent that flowered for their eyes was not empty; it flowered for eyes that would claim it. The passage calls this first sight "the last and greatest of all human dreams," and here the reading finds its central knot. The great human dream, the parent of every American dream that follows, is the dream of a colonizer looking at land he intends to take. The wonder is real, and so is the taking, and the novel puts them in the same sentence.

The lens also notices what the passage does not say. The people who lived on the island before the Dutch are absent. The vision is of a continent that seems to offer itself to European wonder, "material without being real," a phrase the novel uses a few pages earlier for a different scene but that resonates here: the land is imagined as a screen for a dream rather than as an inhabited place. A postcolonial reading marks that absence not as a failure of the novel to know better but as the very structure of the settler imagination, which sees the land as new precisely by forgetting who was already on it. Fitzgerald reproduces that structure and, by dating it to the Dutch and calling it the root of all later dreams, half exposes it.

### The story of the West

The second passage is quieter and comes a few pages earlier. Reflecting on everyone in his tale, the narrator concludes that "this has been a story of the West, after all," that Gatsby, Tom, Daisy, Jordan, and he himself were all Westerners who proved "unadaptable to Eastern life." On the surface this is a comment about temperament and belonging, about Midwesterners who came East and did not fit.

Through the lens, the sentence opens onto the frontier myth. To call the book a story of the West is to place it in the oldest American narrative, the movement of settlement across the continent toward the setting sun. The West is not just a region here; it is the direction of the settler dream, the place opportunity is imagined to lie, the frontier that American identity was forged against. The narrator's own family enacts this. He tells us early that the Carraways are "prominent, well-to-do people in this Middle Western city," that his family came West and "started the wholesale hardware business" on which its comfort rests. Nick's stability, his money, his sense of belonging to a place where houses carry a family's name for decades, is the settled second act of a westward movement. He narrates from inside the settlement, from the comfort it produced, and the novel lets him call this a story of the West without quite letting him see the conquest the word contains. The [frontier and westering myth](/2024/11/11/gatsby-frontier-myth/) is the deep structure the phrase reaches back to, and the postcolonial lens reads Nick's Western story as a settler's story told from the far side of settlement.

### The dream already behind him

The third passage is the most buried, because it does not mention land or conquest at all. It is the sentence, near the very end, about Gatsby's dream being already lost: he did not know that the green light was "already behind him," somewhere back in "the dark fields of the republic" rolling on under the night. Plainly, this is about time and the impossibility of recapturing the past; the dream is behind Gatsby because the moment he wanted to relive is gone.

The postcolonial reading hears something else in the geography of the sentence. The dream is behind him in space as well as time, back in the continental interior, in "the dark fields of the republic," the settled agricultural heartland won by the westward expansion the novel has just named. The republic's dark fields are the frontier after it has been closed and farmed, the settler dream realized and turned into ordinary ground. To say the dream is back there is to say that the fresh start has already happened and cannot happen again; the continent has been taken, the frontier is closed, and what is left is the receding green light of a promise that was spent in the taking. The lens reads Gatsby's personal failure to repeat the past as a figure for the national condition after the frontier: the dream of open land is structurally over, because the land is no longer open, because it was taken. This is why the reading can hold Gatsby's private tragedy and the settler story together. His reach for an unrepeatable moment is the reach of a whole culture for a frontier that closed behind it.

## The settler's anxiety: Tom Buchanan and the Coloured Empires

If the closing vision shows the settler dream from the side of wonder, an early scene shows it from the side of fear, and the two sides belong together. In the novel's first chapter, over dinner at the Buchanans', Tom announces that "Civilization's going to pieces" and presses on Nick a book he has been reading, "The Rise of the Coloured Empires" by a man named Goddard. The idea of the book, Tom explains, is that unless the white race looks out it will be "utterly submerged," and that it falls to those "who are the dominant race" to watch out or "these other races will have control of things." Daisy mocks him lightly and then, half-playing, whispers that "We've got to beat them down."

A plain reading takes this as characterization: Tom is a brute and a bigot, and the scene establishes his ugliness early so the reader will not mistake his later moralizing for principle. That reading is correct, and the postcolonial lens does not cancel it. But the lens hears in Tom's panic something structural, something that connects him to the novel's closing vision rather than isolating him as a private villain. Tom is a settler afraid of being settled upon. The empire he fears is the mirror image of the empire that made him: a rising of the colonized against the dominant, a reversal of the conquest that established the world he rules. His anxiety is the conquest returning as dread, the taking imagined as a taking that might be done to him in turn.

This is why the passage matters for the reading of the American Dream as conquest. The dream's promise of open land and a fresh start depends on a taking, and a taking always leaves the fear that what was taken might be taken back. Tom's crude fantasy of submersion is the settler's nightmare, the underside of the settler's dream. Where the closing vision remembers the arrival on the continent as wonder, Tom's outburst remembers it as domination that must be defended, a possession that other races might seize. The two passages are the two faces of the same colonial structure, and a postcolonial reading that used only the beautiful closing vision would miss the ugliness the beauty rests on.

The scene also names the ideology that made settlement feel legitimate. Tom insists the book is scientific, that it has been proved, and later that the argument is about being Nordics who produced everything that makes civilization. This is the language of racial hierarchy dressed as science, the pseudo-scholarship that justified both the conquest of the continent and the immigration panics of the decade the novel was written in. The postcolonial lens connects Tom's book to the broader machinery of empire, which always produces a story in which the conqueror is civilization and the conquered are its threat or its raw material. Tom is a fool who half-remembers a bad book, but the bad book is a real cultural artifact of settler self-justification, and Fitzgerald places it in his mouth on the first evening as a marker of the world's foundation.

### Why does Tom Buchanan fear the Coloured Empires?

Tom fears the rising of other races because he is a settler afraid of being settled upon. His dread of the white race being submerged is the conquest returning as anxiety: the taking that built his world imagined as a taking that might be done to him. His panic is the ugly underside of the dream's promise of possession.

Reading Tom this way changes his relation to Gatsby. The two men are usually opposed as old money against new, the entrenched aristocrat against the self-made striver. The postcolonial lens finds them sharing a foundation. Tom holds the land and fears losing it; Gatsby wants the land and believes he can take it by will. Both live inside the settler logic, the one defending a possession, the other pursuing one, and both treat the continent and its rewards as things that exist to be held or seized. The dream and the dread are two positions within a single conquest, and the novel gives Nick both to narrate without ever letting him name the conquest that binds them.

## Gatsby as a frontier self: Dan Cody and the myth of self-making

The American Dream's promise of self-invention is the frontier's promise turned inward, and Gatsby is its purest expression. The novel tells us that Jay Gatsby "sprang from his Platonic conception of himself," that his real parents were "shiftless and unsuccessful farm people" whom his imagination refused, and that at seventeen he invented the self he would be faithful to for the rest of his life. Read plainly, this is the story of self-making, the poor boy who becomes his own author. Read through the postcolonial lens, it is the settler ideology of the fresh start applied to a person: Gatsby treats himself as open land, a blank on which anything can be built, and he clears away his origins the way the frontier myth clears away the continent's prior inhabitants, by refusing to see them.

The figure who launches Gatsby into this self-making deserves attention, because Fitzgerald made him a frontiersman. Dan Cody, the millionaire whose yacht Gatsby rows out to and whose service becomes his education, is described as "a product of the Nevada silver fields," and "of the Yukon, of every rush for metal since seventy-five." Cody is the frontier prospector, the pioneer of the mining rushes, the man who made his fortune by extracting wealth from the newly taken and newly opened West. He is the American Dream in its raw, first-generation form, the conqueror of the mineral frontier, and he is the model on which the young Gatsby forms himself before "beating his way along the south shore of Lake Superior" toward the life he imagines. The lens reads Cody as the settler dream's origin figure and Gatsby as its inheritor, the frontier's wealth passing from the man who dug it out of taken land to the boy who will spend it chasing a green light.

This genealogy grounds the dream's conquest in the novel's own backstory rather than only in its closing image. The green light and the Dutch sailors give the reading its frame; Cody and Gatsby give it a human line of descent. The continent is taken by the prospectors of the metal rushes, the wealth is accumulated, and it passes to a new generation who no longer dig but dream, who inherit the frontier's fortune and turn it toward romance and display. Gatsby's tragedy, in this light, is the tragedy of the dream's late phase: the frontier that made Cody rich is closed, the raw taking is over, and what remains is the inherited longing with nothing left to conquer but the past. Gatsby tries to take Daisy the way Cody took silver, by will and force of desire, and the attempt fails because the object is not open land but a person and a vanished moment, and because the age of taking is over.

### What does Dan Cody reveal in a postcolonial reading?

Dan Cody is the frontier prospector, a product of the Nevada silver fields and every metal rush since the eighteen-seventies. As Gatsby's mentor and the source of his first fortune, he grounds the American Dream in the conquest of the West, so the wealth Gatsby inherits descends directly from the taking of newly opened land.

So the self-made man, the dream's hero, turns out on inspection to be the settler's ideal in individual form. To make oneself from nothing is to treat oneself as a frontier, a space to be cleared and claimed, and the model for that self-making is the actual frontier, the metal rushes and the taken land that produced the fortunes the dream remembers. Gatsby's refusal of his parents and his springing from his own conception of himself are not only a psychological portrait; they are the frontier myth internalized, the settler's clean slate rewritten as a clean self. The lens reads Gatsby's greatness and his doom together as the greatness and doom of the settler dream: magnificent in its will to remake everything, undone by its dependence on a taking that has already exhausted the ground it stood on.

## The threshold of the city: race and the settler order

A brief passage on the drive into Manhattan lets the postcolonial lens catch the settler order at a moment of instability. Crossing the Queensboro Bridge with Gatsby, Nick sees the city rise across the river and reflects that from the bridge it is always "the city seen for the first time," in "its first wild promise of all the mystery and the beauty in the world." The bridge is a threshold, a place where the ordinary hierarchies feel briefly suspended and anything seems possible. Nick's thought is exactly that: "Anything can happen now that we've slid over this bridge," and then, half in wonder and half in unease, "Even Gatsby could happen."

What triggers this sense of a world unloosed is a small scene Nick witnesses on the crossing. He passes a funeral procession of southeastern European immigrants and, a moment later, a limousine carrying wealthy Black passengers driven by a white chauffeur. The novel renders both moments through Nick's condescension, and its language for the Black passengers is ugly and dehumanizing in the manner of its period. The postcolonial lens does not excuse that language; it reads what the scene reveals about the narrator and the order he speaks from. Nick's amused shock at seeing Black riders served by a white driver exposes his assumption about who is supposed to be served and who is supposed to serve, an assumption the settler racial hierarchy installs so deeply that its reversal reads to him as comedy, as proof that the city dissolves the natural order rather than as evidence that the order was never natural.

The lens reads the bridge, then, as a threshold where the settler world's certainties waver. The immigrants and the wealthy Black passengers are the people the settler order admits only at its margins, the ethnic and racial others who populate the city the dream built without belonging to the dream's imagined community. That Nick can see Gatsby as belonging to the same category of the unlikely, that "even Gatsby could happen" in the same breath, quietly places Gatsby among the arrivistes and outsiders rather than among the settled dominant. The passage is minor in the plot and major for the reading, because it shows the settler perspective encountering the people it usually forgets and registering their presence as a marvel or a joke. The forgetting the closing vision performs at the level of the continent, this scene performs at the level of the city street, and the narrator's discomfort is the settler imagination brushing against what it prefers not to see.

## The waste beneath the dream: the valley of ashes

Every dream of accumulation produces a place where its waste collects, and in The Great Gatsby that place is the valley of ashes. Halfway between West Egg and the city lies "a certain desolate area of land," which Nick calls a valley of ashes, "a fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat" into ridges and grotesque gardens, peopled by "ash-grey men, who move dimly and already crumbling" through the powdery air, under "the spasms of bleak dust which drift endlessly over it." Read plainly, the valley is the novel's image of what industrial wealth costs, the gray underside of the glittering parties, the ash the burning of the age leaves behind.

The postcolonial lens reads the valley as the frontier's remainder. The settler dream imagines the land as a fresh green breast, fertile and offering itself, but the taking and the building do not leave fertility behind them; they leave a farm that grows ashes instead of wheat, a parody of cultivation. The phrase is exact and terrible: a farm where ashes grow like wheat is the settler's harvest turned to waste, the promise of the fertile continent inverted into a gray field that yields only more gray. The men who work it are already crumbling, worn to ash by the labor that keeps the dream's wealth flowing. If the closing vision shows the land as it was before the taking, green and whole, the valley shows the land as the taking leaves it, spent and gray, and the two images bracket the dream between its fertile beginning and its ashen end.

### What does the valley of ashes reveal about the American Dream?

The valley of ashes is the frontier's waste made visible. Where the closing vision shows the continent as a fresh green breast offered to the settler, the valley shows the land the taking leaves behind, a farm that grows ashes instead of wheat. It is the dream's fertile promise inverted into gray exhaustion.

This is where the postcolonial reading and the novel's more familiar social criticism meet. Critics have long read the valley as the cost of the American Dream, the poverty and ruin on which the rich build their pleasure. The lens adds a dimension: the valley is not only the cost of class inequality but the cost of the settler dream itself, the waste that accumulation on taken land finally produces. The fresh green breast and the ash-gray farm are the same continent at two ends of the dream, and Fitzgerald gives us both, the one at the novel's close and the other at its middle, so that the reader who holds them together sees the whole arc: a land taken as if it were pure gift, worked and burned for its wealth, and left as ashes drifting endlessly over the ground it was.

## The reading it produces: the dream as a conquest story

Put the three passages together and the reading produces a single claim, which can be stated plainly: The Great Gatsby reads, through the postcolonial lens, as a conquest story wearing the clothes of a love story. The American Dream the novel anatomizes is not first of all about romance or money; those are its late, private forms. It is first of all about land, about the promise a continent seemed to make to the Europeans who arrived to take it, and the novel says as much in its final image, where the great dream is dated to the Dutch sailors and the fresh green breast of the new world.

This claim reorganizes the book. The green light, usually read as the symbol of Gatsby's longing for Daisy and for the future, becomes also the light of the frontier, the receding promise of open land that recedes because it has been consumed. The novel itself invites this widening. In the same closing pages where it names the Dutch sailors, it turns from Gatsby's private green light to a green that belonged to the whole continent, the fresh green breast that flowered for the first Europeans, so that the small green light at the end of Daisy's dock rhymes with the great green promise of the land. The lens reads the two greens as one symbol at two scales: the personal longing and the national dream, both organized around a green that beckons and retreats. Gatsby stretches his arms toward a light across the water the way the settler culture stretched toward a horizon of open land, and in both cases the reaching is toward something the reacher believes is ahead but that has in fact already been passed, spent, or taken. The green light is the frontier's promise miniaturized into one man's yearning, which is why the novel can move from his outstretched arms to the whole republic's dark fields without any seam. Gatsby's belief that he can repeat the past becomes the settler culture's belief that the frontier can always be reopened, that there is always more continent, a belief that the closing of the frontier and the taking of the land render false. The novel's mourning tone, its sense of a dream betrayed, becomes double: it mourns the personal dream that Gatsby cannot reach, and beneath that, it mourns and half exposes the settler dream whose fulfillment required a conquest the novel remembers only as wonder.

The reading also clarifies what makes the book great by a postcolonial measure and what makes it limited by one. It is great because it does something rare for a text of its moment: it reaches back past the Jazz Age to name the colonial encounter as the origin of the American Dream, holding the wonder and the taking in a single vision. It is limited because it names the taking mostly as loss and absence rather than as violence done to specific peoples; the original inhabitants of the fresh green breast are present in the passage only as the ones the vision forgets. A postcolonial reading does not have to choose between admiring the reach and marking the limit. It can do both, and the honest reading does.

The findable claim, the one a student can carry into an essay, is this: in The Great Gatsby the American Dream is a settler dream, and the novel's closing vision reveals its colonial root by dating the great dream to the Dutch sailors' first sight of a continent they would take, so that the land of opportunity is always already taken from someone. ## The limits of the lens and the strongest objection

An honest reading names the objection to itself before a critic can. The strongest objection to a postcolonial reading of Gatsby is that the novel simply is not a colonial text, and that the reading imports a framework the book does not support. There are no colonies in it, the objection runs, no colonized peoples, no imperial administration; it is a novel about a small set of rich white Americans on Long Island, and to read it through the history of empire is to force a fashionable lens onto a text that resists it.

This objection deserves a serious answer rather than a dismissal, because part of it is correct. The novel is not about colonialism in the way that a novel set in a colony is about colonialism. If a reading pretended that Gatsby is centrally, consciously a critique of settler conquest, it would be overclaiming, and a careful reader would be right to reject it. The postcolonial reading advanced here does not make that claim. It does not say the novel is about conquest in the way it is about Daisy or money. It says something narrower and more defensible: that the myth the novel anatomizes, the American Dream, carries the settler colony's structure inside it, and that the novel's own closing vision reaches back to the colonial encounter and names it as the dream's origin. The warrant for the reading is not imported; it is on the page, in the Dutch sailors and the fresh green breast of the new world. A reading that follows a text's own final image to its historical source is not forcing a lens; it is taking the text at its word.

### Is The Great Gatsby a colonial text?

Not in the ordinary sense; it contains no colonies, colonized peoples, or imperial rule, and it is not consciously about empire. It is a colonial text in a narrower sense: the American Dream it anatomizes carries the settler colony's structure, and its closing vision dates that dream to the colonial encounter with the continent.

The limit of the lens is worth stating alongside its power. The postcolonial reading is strongest on the myth and weakest on the flesh-and-blood history it points to. Because the original inhabitants of the continent appear in the novel only as an absence, the lens can name the structure of forgetting but cannot fill in what was forgotten from the text itself; it has to bring that history from outside. This is a real limit, and a reading that hides it is weaker for hiding it. The way to use the limit well in an essay is to be explicit: the novel gives us the settler's vision and the settler's forgetting, and the critic supplies, from history, the peoples and the violence that the vision leaves out. Naming that division of labor honestly is more persuasive than pretending the novel contains what it only gestures toward.

There is also the question of anachronism, the worry that a reading built from theory developed decades after 1925 is projecting later concerns onto Fitzgerald. The answer is that the postcolonial lens does not claim to recover Fitzgerald's intentions; it claims to analyze the myth he worked with, and that myth, the frontier and the settler dream, was fully available to him and much older than his novel. The Dutch sailors were not a later critic's invention. Fitzgerald put them in the last paragraph. Reading them for what they carry is interpretation, not projection.

## How to build an essay around the postcolonial lens

A critical lens is most useful to a student when it becomes an essay. Here is how to turn this reading into an argument that holds.

Start with the counterintuitive thesis, because the strength of a postcolonial essay on Gatsby is that it argues for something a plain reading misses. A workable thesis names the lens, the claim, and the key evidence in one sentence: that a postcolonial reading of The Great Gatsby, following the novel's closing vision back to the Dutch sailors, exposes the American Dream as a settler dream built on conquest. That thesis is arguable, specific, and anchored to a passage, which is exactly what an examiner rewards.

Build the body on passages, not on generalities about colonialism. The three passages read above give a ready structure: the Dutch sailors and the fresh green breast as the explicit warrant, the story of the West as the frontier myth surfacing, and the dream already behind Gatsby in the dark fields of the republic as the closing of the frontier figured as personal loss. Each paragraph should quote a short phrase, read it closely, and connect it back to the thesis, so the argument grows out of the text rather than floating above it. An essay that keeps returning to Fitzgerald's actual words will always beat one that recites postcolonial concepts in the abstract.

Address the objection inside the essay rather than hoping the examiner forgets it. Devote a paragraph to the strongest counter-reading, that the novel is not a colonial text, and answer it as this article does: by narrowing the claim to the myth and the closing vision and by pointing to the warrant on the page. An essay that anticipates and defeats its objection reads as thought rather than assertion, and the postcolonial reading is more convincing when it admits what it cannot claim.

A longer essay can build the fuller case, adding Tom's reverse-conquest anxiety as the dream's fearful underside, Dan Cody and Gatsby's self-making as the frontier internalized, and the valley of ashes as the taking's waste, so that the settler structure appears at every level of the book, from the continent to the city to the single self. The advantage of the wider scope is that it answers the objection by weight of evidence: one passage might be a coincidence, but a pattern that runs through the opening dinner, the drive into the city, Gatsby's backstory, the valley, and the final page is harder to dismiss as a lens imposed from outside.

Keep the prose grounded and avoid the two failure modes that sink postcolonial essays. The first is abstraction, an essay that recites the names of theorists and the vocabulary of empire without ever touching Fitzgerald's sentences; the cure is to quote a short phrase in every paragraph and read it. The second is overclaiming, an essay that treats the novel as a conscious anti-colonial tract and ignores its blind spots; the cure is to keep saying what the reading does and does not assert, that the novel names the settler dream's origin while forgetting the peoples the dream displaced. An essay that stays close to the words and honest about the limits will read as the strongest kind of literary argument, one that finds something real in the text rather than something imported into it.

## Closing verdict

The postcolonial lens does not replace the other ways of reading The Great Gatsby; it sits alongside them and catches what they let pass. A reading for love catches the ache of Gatsby's reach for Daisy. A reading for class catches the cruelty of old money toward new. The postcolonial reading catches the ground beneath both, the continent the dream stands on, and it does so by taking the novel's own last image at its word. Fitzgerald chose to end a story of the Jazz Age by remembering the Dutch sailors and the fresh green breast of the new world, by dating the greatest of human dreams to a moment of colonial arrival. A reading that follows that choice is not straining the book; it is reading the book's own conclusion about itself.

What that reading finds is a conquest story inside a love story. The American Dream that the novel mourns is a settler's dream, its promise of a fresh start on open land inseparable from the taking that made the land seem open. Gatsby's tragedy, his belief that the past can be repeated, becomes the culture's tragedy after the frontier closed: the dream of endless new beginnings running up against a continent already taken, a green light that recedes because it was spent. The novel half sees this and half looks away, mourning the dream as lost innocence even as it names the conquest at its root. That doubleness is not a flaw to resolve; it is the exact place where the postcolonial reading does its work, holding wonder and taking in a single vision, as Fitzgerald does in the last paragraph, so that the land of opportunity stands revealed as land that was always already taken from someone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is a postcolonial reading of The Great Gatsby?

A postcolonial reading of The Great Gatsby interprets the novel through the history and legacy of European colonialism, especially the settler colonization of North America. It does not treat the book as a story about a distant empire, since it has none, but asks how the American Dream the novel anatomizes carries the structure of settlement inside it. The reading focuses on land, conquest, the frontier myth, and displacement, and it takes its warrant from the novel's own closing vision, where the narrator reaches back to the Dutch sailors' first sight of the continent and names that colonial encounter as the origin of the great American dream. The method treats the book as a text that half exposes the settler imagination it inherits.

### Q: How does a postcolonial lens read the American Dream as conquest?

The lens argues that the American Dream, before it becomes a private dream of love or wealth, is a dream about land: the promise a continent seemed to make to the Europeans who arrived to take it. Self-invention needs a stage, and the stage was land made to seem open by the removal of the people already on it. The novel supports this reading in its final pages, where the great dream is dated to the Dutch sailors and the fresh green breast of the new world, so that every later American dream descends from a colonial arrival. Under this lens Gatsby's belief that the past can be repeated mirrors the settler culture's belief in an endlessly reopenable frontier, a belief the closed frontier and the taken land prove false. The dream and the conquest turn out to be the same story told twice.

### Q: What does the Dutch-sailors closing vision reveal in a postcolonial reading?

The closing vision is the single most important passage for the reading. The narrator imagines the island as it was before its houses, land that "flowered once for Dutch sailors' eyes," and calls that first European sight of the continent "the last and greatest of all human dreams." A postcolonial lens notices that the sailors are Dutch, which locates the passage in the actual colonization of the region that became New York, and that the great human dream is therefore dated to a moment of arrival and taking. The lens also notices the absence: the people who lived on the island before the Dutch appear only as what the vision forgets. Fitzgerald reproduces the settler imagination, which makes land seem new by forgetting its inhabitants, and by naming this the root of all later dreams he half exposes it. The wonder and the taking sit in the same sentence.

### Q: How does the postcolonial lens read the frontier and displacement?

The lens reads the frontier as the settler colony's flattering story about its own expansion, in which the land is imagined as empty or wild and the settler is a pioneer rather than a conqueror. In the novel the narrator calls his tale a story of the West and describes his family coming West and building its fortune there, so the American Dream is organized by the direction of settlement across taken land. Displacement enters the reading as an absence rather than a depiction: the original inhabitants of the continent are present in the closing vision only as the ones it forgets. Near the end the dream is placed "already behind" Gatsby, back in the dark fields of the republic, which the lens reads as the frontier closed and farmed, the fresh start used up because the land was taken once and cannot be taken new again. The frontier myth and its forgotten cost are the deep structure the lens brings forward.

### Q: Is The Great Gatsby a colonial text?

Not in the ordinary sense. The novel contains no colonies, no colonized peoples, and no imperial administration, and it is not consciously about empire; it is a story about a small set of rich white Americans on Long Island. To call it a colonial text without qualification would overclaim. It is a colonial text in a narrower and defensible sense: the American Dream it anatomizes carries the settler colony's structure, and its own closing vision dates that dream to the colonial encounter, the Dutch sailors seeing the fresh green breast of the new world. So the postcolonial reading does not import a framework the book resists; it follows the book's final image back to its historical source. The honest position is that the novel is not centrally about colonialism the way it is about love or money, but that it names, in its last paragraph, the conquest at the root of the dream it mourns.

### Q: How is the dream's land always already taken from someone?

The phrase captures the reading's central claim. The American Dream promises a fresh start on open land, but a continent does not become open by nature; it is made open by the removal of the people who were there. The clean slate the dream imagines was cleaned of its earlier inhabitants. So the land of opportunity is never a neutral blank; it is ground that was already someone's, taken before the dreamer arrives to build on it. The novel gestures at this in its closing vision, where the land flowers for the Dutch sailors' eyes as if offering itself, while the people who lived there go unmentioned. The settler imagination experiences the land as new precisely by forgetting the taking, and the postcolonial lens names that forgetting. The dream's promise of open land is haunted by the prior ownership the promise depends on erasing.

### Q: Who are the major thinkers behind postcolonial literary criticism?

Postcolonial criticism draws on several foundational figures whose concerns a reader can carry to a text like Gatsby. Edward Said studied how Western writing constructed an imagined, inferior East and taught critics to see how texts naturalize the power relations of empire. Frantz Fanon wrote about the psychology of colonizer and colonized and the way domination shapes the inner life of both. Gayatri Spivak asked whether the colonized subject can speak within the archives and languages of the powerful, and Homi Bhabha explored the unstable, hybrid identities that colonial contact produces. For an American settler text the most relevant strand is the analysis of settler colonialism, which distinguishes the colony people move to permanently, taking the land, from the colony ruled from afar. These thinkers disagree on much, but they share the assumption that empire is a deep structure of modern literature, not a background detail, which is the assumption the reading in this article applies.

### Q: How does a postcolonial reading differ from a Marxist reading of Gatsby?

The two lenses overlap but ask different questions. A Marxist reading focuses on class, capital, and economic power; it reads the novel as a study of the relations between old money, new money, and labor, and of how wealth structures the characters' lives and cruelties. A postcolonial reading focuses on empire, land, and conquest; it reads the American Dream as a settler dream and asks how the novel imagines the acquisition of the continent. Where a Marxist critic sees the exploitation of the working figures behind the wealth, a postcolonial critic sees the taking of the land the wealth stands on. The readings are complementary rather than opposed: both interrogate the myths that make inequality and ownership look natural, and a strong essay can use them together, letting the postcolonial reading supply the continental ground beneath the class drama the Marxist reading maps.

### Q: Does a postcolonial reading require the novel to name colonized peoples?

No, and this is a common misunderstanding. A settler text characteristically does not name the peoples it displaced; the settler imagination makes land seem new precisely by forgetting who was there. So the absence of colonized peoples on the page is not evidence against a postcolonial reading; it is one of the things the reading analyzes. The lens can name the structure of forgetting even when the text supplies only the forgetting and not the forgotten. This is also where the reading meets its limit and must be honest: because the original inhabitants appear only as an absence, the critic has to bring the specific history from outside the novel rather than draw it from the text. The novel gives the settler's vision and the settler's blind spot; the reader supplies, from history, the peoples and the violence the vision leaves out. Naming that division of labor makes the reading stronger, not weaker.

### Q: How does the westering myth work as a settler narrative in the novel?

The westering myth is the American story of movement toward the frontier and the setting sun, and it is the settler colony's account of its own expansion across the continent. In the novel the myth surfaces when the narrator declares the whole book a story of the West and calls the main characters Westerners, unadaptable to Eastern life. The West here is not only a region but a direction, the way the settler dream imagines opportunity to lie. The narrator's own family enacts the myth in miniature: the Carraways came West three generations back and built their fortune there, so his comfort and sense of belonging are the settled second act of a westward movement. The postcolonial reading takes the myth's flattering surface, discovery and destiny, and turns it over to show the conquest it renames, so Nick's Western story becomes a settler's story told from the far side of settlement, its origins in taking half hidden by the language of home.

### Q: What does Nick's Middle Western background contribute to a postcolonial reading?

Nick's background makes him the settler narrator, and his point of view is part of what the lens examines. He tells us the Carraways are prominent, well-to-do people in a Middle Western city, that his family came West and started a wholesale hardware business, and that he grew up in a house whose name endures through decades. That stability is the realized end of a westward settlement, and Nick narrates from inside it, treating his belonging as natural rather than as the product of a taking. A postcolonial reading asks how his unexamined assumptions about land, family, and home structure the tale, and it finds that the novel lets him call the book a story of the West without quite letting him see the conquest the word contains. His comfortable settledness is the vantage from which the settler dream can be mourned as innocence, which is exactly the blind spot the lens is designed to bring into view.

### Q: Is a postcolonial reading of Gatsby anachronistic projection?

This is the sharpest objection, and it has a clear answer. The postcolonial lens does not claim to recover Fitzgerald's conscious intentions or to hold a 1925 novel to a later century's politics. It claims to analyze the myth the novel works with, and that myth, the frontier and the settler dream, was fully available to Fitzgerald and far older than his book. The Dutch sailors are not a later critic's invention imposed on the text; Fitzgerald put them in the final paragraph and named their vision the root of all human dreams. Reading that image for the colonial history it carries is interpretation, not projection, in the same way that reading the green light for what it symbolizes is interpretation. The theory that gives the reading its vocabulary came later, but the material it interprets is on the page. An anachronism charge would stick only if the reading claimed the novel meant to critique conquest; it makes the narrower claim that the novel's own images carry the settler structure, which they do.

### Q: How does the fresh green breast of the new world image work postcolonially?

The image is the hinge of the whole reading. Fitzgerald describes the island as it appeared to the first European arrivals, land that flowered for Dutch sailors' eyes as a fresh green breast of the new world, offering itself to wonder. Read postcolonially, the image both records and conceals a conquest. It records it because the sailors are Dutch and the land is the one that would be colonized and built over, so the vision remembers the moment of arrival that made all later settlement possible. It conceals it because the land is imagined as offering itself, nurturing and new, with no earlier inhabitants in view, which is exactly how the settler imagination turns taking into discovery. The image names the colonial encounter as the greatest of human dreams, and in doing so it holds wonder and taking together in one figure. The postcolonial reading does not add the conquest to the image; it draws out the conquest the image already carries.

### Q: What settler-colonial ideas help explain The Great Gatsby?

Several concepts from the study of settler colonialism sharpen the reading. The first is the fiction of empty land, by which colonizers declared a settled continent unowned and available so that taking it could be called discovery; the frontier myth is its cultural form, picturing the West as wilderness waiting for the pioneer. The second is the myth of the vanishing native, in which settler societies imagine the original inhabitants as already fading, so the settler can mourn what the settler destroys. The third is the idea that settler colonialism is a structure rather than a single past event, a logic that keeps shaping law, property, and imagination for generations. Each names a move the novel's imagination makes: the closing vision pictures the land as offering itself, the prior inhabitants appear only as an absence, and the settled world of the Buchanans treats its founding taking as natural belonging. These ideas let a reader show that the settler pattern is built into the book rather than added to it.

### Q: How does the postcolonial reading connect to race in the novel?

Race enters the reading most directly through Tom's recital of a book about the rise of the Coloured Empires, in which he frets that the white race will be submerged and insists it falls to the dominant race to keep control. The postcolonial lens reads this as the settler's reverse-conquest anxiety, the fear that the taking which built his world might be turned against him, and it notes that Tom dresses the fear as science in the manner of the era's racial pseudo-scholarship. Race surfaces again on the drive across the Queensboro Bridge, where Nick's condescending amusement at the immigrants and wealthy Black passengers he sees exposes his assumptions about who belongs and who serves. The novel's language in that scene is ugly and of its period, and the lens reads what the ugliness reveals about the narrator rather than endorsing it. In both moments the racial hierarchy of the settler order becomes visible, either as panic or as a marvel, which is exactly what the lens is designed to bring forward.
