Mercutio and Romeo are the play's two theorists of love, and the tragedy begins as their argument. One friend insists that desire is a bodily appetite, to be fed, mocked, and discharged before it makes a fool of the man who feels it. The other insists that desire is a form of worship, to be suffered, decorated, and obeyed until it remakes the world around the beloved's face. Shakespeare gives each man a complete vocabulary, a set of set pieces in which to perform it, and a best friend positioned to hear the performance and answer it. The debate is not decoration laid over the plot. It is the plot's engine room, because every catastrophe in the play can be traced to a moment when one theory of love was applied where the other, or a third that neither man holds, was required.

The verdict of this article can be stated at the outset. The play endorses neither friend. Mercutio's reduction of love to appetite is exposed as a defense that cannot survive contact with his own death, and Romeo's elevation of love to worship is exposed as a literary habit that cannot survive contact with a real girl answering back. What the play puts in their place is the third position embodied by Juliet: a frank tenderness that wants the body, says so in plain comparisons, and binds desire to terms, conditions, and a wedding. The tension among the three positions is never resolved into a doctrine, and that refusal is the point. Verona's tragedy is what happens when two half-truths about love are each acted on as though they were whole.

Every quotation in what follows is taken from René Weis's Arden Third Series edition of the play, and line references follow that text. The argument proceeds by scenes, because the play itself proceeds by scenes: the two theories are stated in Act 1, tested against Rosaline's absence and Juliet's presence in Act 2, broken against each other in Act 3, and judged by what remains of them in Act 5. Joseph Porter's study of Mercutio, Harry Levin's account of the play's formal design, and Heather Dubrow's work on Petrarchism and its counterdiscourses supply the critical frame, and where Porter and Levin disagree about what Mercutio's wit is doing, the disagreement will be settled rather than merely reported.

The flattening this series exists to resist has a particular shape in the case of love. The received Romeo is a composite assembled from the balcony's greatest hits, a young man whose feeling is presented as self evident, unanimous, and shared by the play that contains him, as though the tragedy were a valentine with an unfortunate ending. The composite survives by amputation. Mercutio must go first, since his presence in any summary instantly complicates the valentine past recovery: a play whose second most memorable voice holds that the hero's passion is a case of the itch cannot be summarized as a hymn to that passion without discussion. Juliet's terms must go next, since a heroine who sets conditions, audits oaths, and schedules the wedding by messenger fits no greeting card yet printed. What remains after the amputations is Romeo's Act 1 register mistaken for the play's doctrine, the sighing of the Rosaline phase promoted to a philosophy, and the promotion is the cliché's whole mechanism. Restore the amputated voices and the play's actual structure reappears: a debate, conducted by two friends who are each half right, supervised by a girl who is mostly right, and judged by events that decline to ratify even the mostly right. The sections that follow perform the restoration in the play's own order. Readers pressed for the conclusion may take it from the verdict already given. Readers with the length of the play in them will find the conclusion worth less than the argument, which is, in the end, the play's opinion of conclusions as well.

A note on terms will keep the argument honest. A theory of love, as the phrase is used here, is not a philosophy seminar position but a working account a character acts on: what desire is, what it is for, what conduct it licenses, and what evidence would count against it. Stated that way, the play contains exactly three working accounts with holders, plus the appraisal account Benvolio carries as a control and the arrangement account Paris embodies as a null case, and the action can be read as the accounts' collision under laboratory conditions the feud supplies. The debate has three great sessions. In the first, on the way to the feast, the two friends state their accounts in pure form, undisturbed by evidence. In the second, in the orchard, a third account is drafted by the one participant the friends' theories were both about and neither had consulted. In the third, in the piazza, the accounts are audited by violence, and the audit's findings occupy the rest of the tragedy. The sections below follow that order, spending the most time where the play spends its: on the statements, the drafting, and the audit, in that proportion.

## Mercutio vs Romeo: Two Theories of Love Stated Plainly

The cleanest statement of the opposition comes before either man has met the obstacle that will ruin him, in the street outside Capulet's house in Act 1 Scene 4. Romeo, lovesick for Rosaline, announces that he cannot dance: "I have a soul of lead / So stakes me to the ground I cannot move" (1.4.15 to 16). The image is worth pausing over. The idealist experiences desire as weight, as a metal poured into the soul that pins the body to the earth. Mercutio's answer is the body's whole philosophy compressed into a taunt: "You are a lover. Borrow Cupid's wings / And soar with them above a common bound" (1.4.17 to 18). Where Romeo feels lead, Mercutio prescribes wings, and the wings belong to Cupid, the one god both men acknowledge and neither man understands in the same way. For Romeo, Cupid is a tyrant whose arrows inflict a sacred wound. For Mercutio, Cupid is equipment, a device a clever man borrows and returns.

The exchange that follows is the debate in miniature. Romeo asks, "Is love a tender thing? It is too rough, / Too rude, too boisterous, and it pricks like thorn" (1.4.25 to 26). The question is genuine and the answer he gives himself is a complaint: love, experienced from inside worship, hurts in a way worship was not supposed to hurt. Mercutio's reply converts the complaint into a prescription: "If love be rough with you, be rough with love; / Prick love for pricking, and you beat love down" (1.4.27 to 28). Every term has been translated. Tenderness has become roughness, suffering has become strategy, and the passive wound of the Petrarchan lover has become an active counterblow. Mercutio does not deny that love pricks. He denies that the pricking deserves reverence. Treat the feeling as an opponent in a scuffle, answer in kind, and the feeling loses its throne.

Set the two speeches side by side and the theories resolve into their elements. Romeo's love is vertical: it looks up at a lady, down at a self, and measures the distance in sighs. Its characteristic images are religious and celestial, its characteristic posture is kneeling, and its characteristic product is poetry about the lover's own condition. Mercutio's love is horizontal: it moves between bodies at ground level, and its characteristic images are anatomical and commercial, its characteristic posture is the leer shared with the audience, and its characteristic product is the joke that dissolves the feeling into appetite before the feeling can organize a life around itself. Neither man, in Act 1, has yet loved anybody in a way that required the other man's theory. That is why both speeches sound complete. The play's cruelty is to give each theory exactly the test it cannot pass.

### Does Mercutio believe his own reduction of love to appetite?

Not wholly. The doctrine is performed with an energy that keeps outrunning its content, and the performance is the point: bawdy lets Mercutio stay inside every conversation about desire while committing himself to none of it. His reductions are sincere as far as they go. They simply stop where his own feeling for Romeo would have to begin.

The sections that follow take the theories one at a time and at full strength, because the play does. Mercutio's case deserves its strongest form before it is judged: a reader who treats the bawdy as mere comic relief will miss the fact that Mercutio is often right about Romeo, right about Rosaline, and right about the literary fraudulence of the sighing lover. Romeo's case deserves the same courtesy: a reader who treats the idealism as mere Petrarchan posing will miss the moments when the pose breaks open into something the pose was only ever rehearsing. The play holds the two cases in tension, tests a third against both, and delivers its judgment not in a speech but in a body count.

A control case runs quietly beneath the debate, and his name is Benvolio. Benvolio holds the appraisal theory described later in these pages, the belief that desire is a judgement correctable by comparison, and he holds it without Mercutio's brilliance or Romeo's suffering, as a piece of practical hygiene. The play's treatment of the control is instructive: Benvolio is right about nearly every fact he touches, his account of the fatal duel will be the most reliable testimony the Prince hears, and he vanishes from the action after Act 3, unmissed by the plot and unremembered by the audience. A theory of love that generates no poetry, no catastrophe, and no loyalty unto death is, in this play's economy, a theory that has opted out of the subject. The friends' half-truths dominate the play because they are lived at full stretch. Benvolio's whole truth about appraisal dominates nothing, and his quiet exit is the play's footnote on the difference between being correct about desire and having a position on love.

The sessions can be scored as they proceed, and the scoring is worth fixing before the evidence sections begin. In the first session Mercutio wins on points: his prescription answers Romeo's complaint, his pageant outshines Romeo's foreboding, and the idealist leaves the street with no reply in hand. In the second session Juliet wins outright, against a field that does not know it is competing: her terms are set, her conditions are met, and both friends' theories are quietly retired as descriptions of the case in progress. In the third session the feud wins, which is the tragedy's way of announcing that the debate was never the only game in Verona and that its stakes, however absolute they felt in the street and the orchard, were always payable in a currency neither disputant controlled. The sections that follow keep that scoring in view, because a verdict that forgets the order of the sessions will mistake the loudest session for the decisive one.

## The Absent Evidence: Rosaline and the Debate Before the Feast

Both theories are built, in the first instance, on a woman who never appears, and the absence is not an accident of plotting but the condition that lets the theories flourish. Rosaline exists in the play as a report: Benvolio's intelligence, Romeo's sighs, Mercutio's anatomy lesson. She has sworn herself to chastity, which means she has removed herself from negotiation, and a beloved who cannot negotiate can be made to carry any doctrine her interpreters bring to her. Romeo loads her silence with worship. Mercutio loads it with anatomy. Neither loading is ever corrected by the lady, and the play's first lesson about theories of love is that they grow rank in proportion to the beloved's absence from the conversation. The orchard will reverse the proportions, since Juliet talks, and the reversal is the plot.

Benvolio deserves a paragraph in the debate's prehistory, because he offers the cure that neither theory can recognize as a rival. His prescription in Act 1 Scene 2 is comparison: examine other beauties, weigh Rosaline against the field, and the swan will downgrade herself to a crow. The cure assumes desire is a judgement of relative value, correctable by better information, and it belongs to a fourth, unglamorous account of love as appraisal, the account the marriage market itself runs on. Romeo rejects the appraisal model with a worshipper's scorn, and Mercutio never bothers to reject it at all, since appraisal is simply appetite with a ledger. The feast will administer Benvolio's cure by accident, and its success poses a problem the debate never quite faces: if the worship transfers to a new object in a single glance across a dance floor, then something in Romeo's creed behaved exactly as Mercutio's doctrine predicts, object indifferent, appetite constant, vocabulary portable. The play knows this, and says so in the Chorus that opens Act 2: "Now old desire doth in his death-bed lie, / And young affection gapes to be his heir" (2.0.1 to 2). Desire has heirs. The line is the doctrine of appetite speaking in the play's own choric voice, and any honest verdict in the friends' debate has to enter it as evidence for Mercutio before Juliet's answering voice can be heard qualifying everything.

The qualification begins with the observation that the transfer, whatever it proves about the old desire, does not behave like appetite once it lands. Romeo at the feast does not do what the doctrine prescribes, which is to take the new object's inventory and proceed. He asks her hand, builds a liturgy around the touching of palms, and submits to correction. The glance across the room is appetite's moment, and the play grants Mercutio that moment entire. Everything after the glance is the controversy.

## The Body First: Mercutio's Doctrine of Appetite

Mercutio's theory rests on a single conviction: desire is an event in the body, and everything else said about it, the worship, the destiny, the celestial machinery, is embroidery stitched over an appetite that would proceed perfectly well without the decoration. His speeches work by translation. Whatever Romeo raises into metaphor, Mercutio lowers into anatomy. Whatever Romeo calls fate, Mercutio calls digestion, weather, or the hour of the day. The method is most visible in the conjuring scene of Act 2 Scene 1, where Romeo has vanished into Capulet's orchard and Mercutio attempts to summon him home with an incantation addressed to Rosaline: "I conjure thee by Rosaline's bright eyes, / By her high forehead and her scarlet lip" (2.1.17 to 18). The spell proceeds downward through the body in catalogue form, and the catalogue is the doctrine. A woman, in Mercutio's liturgy, is an inventory of parts, and a lover is a man bewitched by the inventory into forgetting that an inventory is all it is.

The conjuring also reveals the theory's aggression. Mercutio remarks that it would anger Romeo "to raise a spirit in his mistress' circle" (2.1.23 to 24), a piece of bawdy geometry that reduces the beloved to a location and the lover to an intruder at its perimeter. The joke's cruelty is diagnostic. Mercutio cannot leave Romeo's feeling alone, because the feeling, left alone, would testify against the doctrine: here is a man whose desire has plainly become something more than appetite, who has gone over a wall in the dark for it. The bawdy works hardest exactly where the evidence against it is strongest. That pattern, the escalation of the joke in proportion to the threat, is the single most important fact about Mercutio's theory, and it will matter when the critics disagree about him.

Act 2 Scene 4 supplies the doctrine's happiest hour and its clearest self-portrait. Romeo returns from the Friar's cell restored, witty, and secretive, and Mercutio greets the recovery as a medical event: "Why, is not this better now than groaning for love? Now art thou sociable, now art thou Romeo" (2.4.87 to 89). The diagnosis embedded in the greeting deserves attention. In Mercutio's account, the sighing Petrarchan was not Romeo at all; the real man is the one who trades quips, and love, properly understood, is a temporary fever that had impersonated him. When the catalogue of Rosaline follows, with Romeo reduced to a lover "without his roe, like a dried herring" (2.4.38 to 39), the theory completes itself: desire unconsummated is a fish out of its element, and desire consummated is a meal. "O flesh, flesh, how art thou fishified!" (2.4.37) is funny because it is systematic. Mercutio has a genuine anthropology. The body is the truth of the person, appetite is the truth of the body, and any vocabulary that claims otherwise is a costume the appetite wears to dinner.

Readers who want the full anatomy of that comic system, pun by pun and scene by scene, will find it in the companion study of [Mercutio's bawdy wordplay, its sex jokes, its wit, and its wordplay](/2022/09/16/mercutio-bawdy-wit-wordplay/), which takes the bawdy as its whole subject. Here the bawdy matters for what it argues. Three claims recur through all of it. First, that love's exalted language is a fraud men practice on themselves to dignify wanting. Second, that the wanting itself is simple, recurrent, and indifferent to its object, so that one woman's inventory serves as well as another's. Third, that the man who sees through the fraud is free of it. The play grants the first claim a partial truth, destroys the second claim in the orchard, and executes the third claim in the piazza. Mercutio is not free of desire's empire. He is its most gifted exile, and exile, as the play arranges it, is a fatal posting.

The Nurse's entrance in Act 2 Scene 4 shows the doctrine meeting the one character who shares its premises and refutes its conclusions. The Nurse, too, believes love is a bodily business conducted through errands, appetites, and arrangements. Mercutio greets her with a fan joke and a song about a bawd, treating her errand as a procuress's commission because his theory has no other category for an older woman carrying messages between lovers. Romeo's repair work afterward is quiet and total. He commends himself to Juliet, protests the honour of his intentions, and sends word of a marriage arranged for that afternoon. The contrast is the scene's buried argument: the same errand that Mercutio's theory reads as bawdy logistics, Romeo's conduct reveals as the machinery of a wedding. Appetite theory cannot see a sacrament being scheduled in front of it. It can only see the fan, the sail of a dress, and the joke, and the joke, for once, is on the theorist.

Two further exhibits complete the doctrine's file before the defence opens. The first is Mercutio's ventriloquism of Romeo under the medlar tree in Act 2 Scene 1, where the conjurer, having catalogued Rosaline, composes the speech he believes his friend is making in the dark: Romeo will sit under the tree "and wish his mistress were that kind of fruit / As maids call medlars when they laugh alone" (2.1.34 to 36). The medlar, ripe only when rotten and named in slang for what the joke requires, is appetite theory's ideal beloved, a fruit that asks to be eaten and cannot talk back. What deserves emphasis is the act of composition itself. Mercutio does not merely diagnose Romeo's desire; he scripts it, supplying the frank speech he insists the worshipper is too costumed to make. Porter's reading earns its keep here, because a doctrine confident of its truth does not need to write the patient's lines for him. The ventriloquism confesses that Romeo's actual desire, unheard behind a wall, is the one text Mercutio cannot read, and the conjuring's rising filth is the sound of a theorist drowning out a silence that might refute him.

The second exhibit is Romeo's silence itself. Romeo is onstage, hidden, through the whole conjuring, and his contributions are a handful of asides: an instruction to the night, a remark that the jester speaks well, and the resolve to go forward while Mercutio calls. The play stages the debate as an overhearing. The doctrine of appetite performs at full volume for an audience it believes is absent, and the audience it most wants, the lover himself, listens without converting. When Romeo finally speaks in the scene it is to the darkness and to Juliet's window, in the other theory's tongue. Shakespeare never gives Mercutio the satisfaction of a rebuttal in this scene, and the withholding is structural criticism: appetite doctrine, however brilliant, is a performance for men, delivered in men's hearing, and the beloved's side of the wall is where its jurisdiction ends.

The doctrine's register deserves a final note, since register is where Levin's formal reading and the doctrine meet. Mercutio is the play's great prose speaker among the principals, and his bawdy lives naturally in prose, the medium of appetite, trade, and the tavern, while the Queen Mab flight, his one excursion into the imagination's high country, arrives in verse and collapses back into the admission that dreams are begot of nothing. The pattern flatters neither friend. Romeo's verse, in the Rosaline phase, is as conventional as Mercutio's prose is free, so that form and freedom refuse to align with either theory: the worshipper's tongue is the more enslaved, bound to a stockroom of sighs, while the reductionist's tongue invents at will and binds itself to nothing, not even to the conclusions its inventions reach. When Romeo's verse begins to free itself in the orchard, answering and being answered, the change is audible before it is arguable, and listeners who track the debate by ear will hear the third position arrive as a new music: two voices in one metre, finishing each other's lines, neither sighing in Petrarch's pattern nor leering in Ovid's.

## The Ideal First: Romeo's Petrarchan Worship

Romeo's theory is the mirror image of his friend's, built from the same evidence and read in the opposite direction. Where Mercutio holds that desire is an appetite wearing costumes, Romeo holds that desire is a religion acquiring a communicant. His first speeches in the play are a liturgy of oxymorons performed for Benvolio's benefit and his own: "O brawling love, O loving hate, / O anything of nothing first create!" (1.1.176 to 177). The catalogue continues through heavy lightness, serious vanity, and the famous conclusion that he lives dead, telling the story while already inside it. Critics have sometimes treated the speech as a symptom to be diagnosed, the sound of a young man enjoying his own melancholy. It is better treated as a creed recited by a believer who has memorized the service before ever meeting the god. Rosaline, who never appears, is the perfect object for such a creed, because an absent lady cannot interrupt the worshipper's monologue with facts.

The creed's structure is Petrarchan in the strict sense. The beloved is distant, chaste, and armed: Rosaline has sworn off love and carries Diana's weapons in Romeo's report, so that the lover's suffering is guaranteed a permanent supply. The lover's part is to suffer beautifully, to convert rejection into verse, and to treat the conversion as proof of the feeling's worth. Nothing in the arrangement requires the lady to exist as a person. It requires her to exist as an occasion. Mercutio's catalogue of Rosaline's body parts in the conjuring scene is cruel precisely because it mimics the Petrarchan blazon, the lover's own habit of praising the beloved feature by feature, and drags the blazon down the body past the point where worship can follow. The two friends share a poetic form. One uses it to kneel, the other to leer, and the sharing is Shakespeare's quiet notice that the theories are twins, not opposites: both are ways of talking about a woman instead of with her.

The feast scene breaks the pattern, and the breaking is the most consequential event in the debate. Romeo sees Juliet across the room and the creed survives the impact for exactly one speech: "O, she doth teach the torches to burn bright!" (1.5.43) is still the monologue of a worshipper addressing an image. Then he takes her hand, and the monologue becomes a dialogue. The shared sonnet that follows, beginning "If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this" (1.5.93 to 94), is built entirely from the old vocabulary: shrine, pilgrim, saint, prayer, sin. Every sacred term is in place. What has changed is the grammar. Juliet answers. "Good pilgrim, you do wrong your hand too much, / Which mannerly devotion shows in this" (1.5.96 to 97) is not an interruption of the worship but a correction administered from inside it, by the saint herself, who turns out to have views about the liturgy. Romeo's theory had never budgeted for the beloved talking back, and the sonnet form, which the lovers complete between them line by line, becomes the record of the theory discovering its error in real time.

### Is Romeo's idealism only a literary habit learned from Petrarch?

It begins as one. The Rosaline speeches are assembled from the Petrarchan stockroom, and the Friar later mocks the performance as salt water wasted in brine. Yet the feast sonnet is spoken with another person, in her hearing, and the orchard scene answers back. Habit supplies the vocabulary. Encounter teaches the grammar.

The orchard scene of Act 2 Scene 2 completes the transformation and quietly refutes both friends. Romeo enters it with a line aimed directly at Mercutio's doctrine: "He jests at scars that never felt a wound" (2.2.1). The line is usually read as a dismissal of the absent friend's mockery, and it is that, but it is also a theory of knowledge. Some things, Romeo claims, can only be known from inside the wound, and the jesting spectator's account of them is not a rival truth but a confession of inexperience. Mercutio had argued that the spectator sees the appetite plain because he stands outside the costume. Romeo answers that the outsider sees only the costume, and that the wound is the fact. The play will test that claim savagely, since Romeo's inside knowledge does not save him, but in the orchard it lands, because Juliet is at the window converting worship into negotiation before his eyes.

What Romeo says in the orchard is still idealism, but idealism under new management. "Juliet is the sun" (2.2.3) keeps the celestial register, and "O, speak again, bright angel" (2.2.26) keeps the sacred one, yet the speeches are now addressed to a listener who will interrupt, qualify, refuse the oath he offers, and propose the marriage contract herself. The worshipper's posture has been altered by the worshipped person's participation. Romeo does not abandon his theory in the orchard. He discovers that the theory, practised with a partner instead of at an absence, becomes a different instrument: less a liturgy than a correspondence, in which every image he sends up is received, audited, and returned with amendments. That discovery is why the Rosaline phase matters. Without it, the orchard would look like a style. With it, the orchard looks like an education, and Mercutio's taunt in Act 2 Scene 4, that the recovered Romeo is Romeo again, reads as a misdiagnosis: the man trading quips in the morning is not the old self restored but a new self concealing, even from his dearest friend, the one fact that has changed everything.

One speech from the Rosaline phase deserves separate weighing, because it states the ideal's physics as precisely as Mercutio ever states the body's. Asked to define the thing he suffers, Romeo answers that love is "a smoke raised with the fume of sighs; / Being purged, a fire sparkling in lovers' eyes; / Being vexed, a sea nourished with loving tears" (1.1.190 to 192). The definition climbs away from matter as it proceeds. Smoke is a body evaporating; the fire that survives purging is light without fuel; the sea of tears is the one material image, and even it is made of weeping rather than flesh. Romeo's love, in his own account, is what remains when the body has been boiled off, and the sighs doing the boiling are the lover's chief product. Set beside Mercutio's counterphysics, in which love is a pressure in the loins to be answered with roughness, the definition clarifies the debate's deepest level. The friends disagree about what desire is made of. For one, it is the body's vapour, rising and dispersing. For the other, it is the body's substance, pressing and demanding. Juliet will settle the composition question in the orchard by giving love back its body and its infinity in the same image, the sea restored from tears to bounty, but in Act 1 the two physics stand unreconciled, and Romeo's has the disadvantage of describing an element nobody can eat, touch, or marry.

The orchard also preserves, inside the worshipper's finest speech, a confession his friend's doctrine would have relished. Watching Juliet lean her cheek upon her hand, Romeo wishes he were a glove upon that hand, that he might touch that cheek (2.2.23 to 24). The wish is usually quoted as the balcony scene's most delicate fancy, and it is delicate, but its structure deserves the debate's attention: the idealist, at the summit of his celestial register, expresses his desire as a wish to become an article of clothing in contact with skin. Mercutio could not have drafted the admission better. The worship, examined at its most refined, wants exactly what the appetite wants, contact, and differs from the appetite chiefly in the price it is willing to pay in ceremony for the same touch. The difference is not nothing; ceremony, as the sequel shows, is where the obligations live. But the glove wish is the point in the text where the two theories can be seen occupying a single image, and the play's refusal to choose between them stops looking like indecision and starts looking like reportage. Desire in the orchard is one thing with two descriptions, and the descriptions hate each other, and both are accurate, and the girl at the window is about to supply the third description that neither disputant had thought to attempt: an account of what the desire is for.

## Act 2 as the Debate's Laboratory

Act 2 is where the theories stop being speeches and become experiments, and the act's design submits each position to a test the position did not choose. Romeo's test is secrecy. The man whose worship had been a public performance, sighed in streets and recited to Benvolio, now holds the central fact of his life in silence before the friend whose doctrine claims jurisdiction over exactly that fact. The wit combat of Act 2 Scene 4, in which Romeo defeats Mercutio at his own game and is congratulated for the recovery, is therefore the debate's most ironic passage: the prize is awarded for a cure that never happened, by a physician reading a chart the patient has falsified. Romeo's delight in the scene is real, and so is the exclusion it performs. Mercutio exits the play's knowledge at that moment and never re-enters it, and everything he says afterward about love is said in ignorance of the marriage, a doctrine addressing a world that has already ended. The play is careful to let the audience watch the information fail to travel. Theories of love, in Verona, die of the same disease as letters.

Juliet's test in the same act is logistics, and her handling of it extends the third position into a new register. Waiting for the Nurse in Act 2 Scene 5, she computes the errand's duration with a bookkeeper's fury, three hours since nine o'clock for a journey of half an hour, and abuses the delay in terms that mix the body's impatience with a lover's metaphysics of swift messengers and slow age. The speech matters to the debate because it shows tenderness experiencing time as an obstacle course rather than as a shrine or a joke. Mercutio's appetite knows only the present tense and Romeo's worship knows only eternity; Juliet's love has a schedule, a go-between, a wedding hour, and the capacity to be furious at a servant's knees. When the Nurse finally arrives and withholds the news for the sport of breathlessness, the scene replays the whole argument in miniature: the message of the body delayed by the body, while the person who set the terms waits to hear whether the terms were met. They were. The laboratory returns its result, and the wedding that follows is the third position's first institutional victory, complete with the Friar's warning, already examined, filed as a minority report.

Romeo's last experiment in the act is conducted at the wedding itself, in Act 2 Scene 6, and its result should be entered against his side with the same honesty. Waiting in the cell, he issues a challenge to the universe: let love-devouring death do what it dares, since the mere possession of Juliet's joy outweighs any sorrow that can be sent against it. The Friar's counsel of moderation in the same scene has already been noted, but the challenge deserves its own line in the ledger, because it shows the worshipper's theory mutating under success. The Rosaline creed was built for rejection and knew how to suffer beautifully at a distance. Success leaves it without instructions, and it improvises recklessness, daring fate to audit a happiness it cannot yet protect. Juliet's entrance cuts the improvisation short, and her contribution to the scene is characteristically a measurement, of love grown by counting, the modest prologue to the economics of increase she will state in full elsewhere. The act closes with the marriage performed and all three positions still standing, which is the laboratory's honest report: Act 2 refutes nobody. It merely loads each theory with the weight Act 3 will drop on it.

## Queen Mab as Evidence: Dreams Weighed in the Debate

The Queen Mab speech belongs, as a set piece, to its own corner of the series, and it will be treated here only for what it contributes to the argument between the friends: it is Mercutio's theory of love stated as a theory of everything. Romeo, on the way to the feast, reports a dream and a foreboding. Mercutio answers with a pageant. Queen Mab, "the fairies' midwife" (1.4.53), drives her chariot over sleepers and delivers each dreamer the dream his station and appetite have ordered: lovers dream of love, courtiers of curtsies, lawyers of fees, soldiers of throats cut in ambush. The speech's surface is whimsical and its engine is contempt. Every human longing, the pageant argues, is a appetite in fancy dress, delivered by a parasite no bigger than an agate stone, and the dreamer's dignity is the size of the lie he tells himself about the delivery.

Within the debate, the speech functions as Mercutio's closing argument against Romeo's foreboding and, behind the foreboding, against Romeo's whole way of loving. If dreams are the children of appetite, then a love that feels like destiny is an appetite that has learned to speak solemnly. The lovers of the pageant dream "of love" as mechanically as the parsons dream of benefices, and the mechanism is the message: nothing in the desiring mind is exempt from reduction, neither Romeo's sighs nor his stars. Romeo's interruption is the debate's hinge in small. "Peace, peace, Mercutio, peace! / Thou talk'st of nothing" (1.4.95 to 96) is not a refutation but a plea for mercy from a man who feels the pageant's gaiety turning rancid, and Mercutio's concession is more dangerous than his attack: "True, I talk of dreams, / Which are the children of an idle brain, / Begot of nothing but vain fantasy" (1.4.96 to 98). The theorist of the body, pressed, admits that his own gorgeous speech was fantasy too, woven from the same nothing he mocks. Reduction, pursued far enough, consumes the reducer's authority. The speech that was meant to prove all longing is appetite ends by proving all speech about longing is invention, Mercutio's included.

The play then conducts the experiment the speech proposes. Romeo goes to the feast under the sign of a dream dismissed, meets Juliet, and the feeling that follows is either the pageant's mechanical appetite wearing its finest costume or the thing the pageant said did not exist. The audience is left to grade the experiment, and grading it is the audience's share of the debate. What can be said on the evidence is that the feeling behaves unlike appetite in the one way Mercutio's theory cannot accommodate: it survives the body's satisfaction. The marriage is consummated, the appetite is fed, and the desire, instead of cooling into the contempt the doctrine predicts, intensifies into the orchard's terms, the wedding's haste, and the tomb's fidelity. A theory of love that cannot explain why feeding the hunger deepens it has located a real component of love and mistaken the component for the whole.

The pageant's census deserves one more look, because its filing system is the doctrine in disguise. Mab delivers to each sleeper the dream his trade has ordered, and love appears in the catalogue as one appetite among the professions: the lover dreams of love as the lawyer dreams of fees, the parson of a benefice, the soldier of an ambush and a Spanish blade. The levelling is the argument. Nothing in the desiring imagination is permitted a higher origin than any other entry in the ledger, and Romeo's foreboding, which claims a governed world and a consequence hanging in the stars at 1.4.106 to 107, is precisely the sort of dream the census cannot file, since it arrives unbidden, orders nothing the dreamer wants, and turns out to be accurate. Mercutio's theory handles wishes. It has no drawer for warnings, and the play, which opened with a Chorus filing the ending in advance, keeps producing warnings for the rest of its length.

## Juliet's Third Term: Frank Tenderness Between Body and Ideal

Juliet enters the debate holding neither man's theory, and the play signals her independence in her first exchange as a lover. She completes Romeo's sonnet at the feast, corrects his hand's devotion, grants the kiss the liturgy was angling for, and then delivers the verdict that dismantles his method while accepting his suit: "You kiss by th' book" (1.5.109). The line is fond and lethal at once. It tells the worshipper that his most spontaneous gesture has been recognized as a quotation, that the saint can read the service book as fluently as the pilgrim, and that fluency will be the condition of everything that follows. Mercutio reduces love to the body in order to expose the poetry as fraud. Juliet exposes the poetry as poetry and keeps the love. That is a third operation, unavailable to either friend, and the play builds its alternative theory out of it.

The orchard scene is the third term's full statement. Juliet wants the body with a frankness that outruns Mercutio's bawdy for directness, since the bawdy is always joking and she never is. "Gallop apace" will later give that wanting its great soliloquy, but the orchard already contains the essentials. She refuses the worshipper's oath because the moon he would swear by is inconstant, and a theory of love that begins by forbidding false swearing has already left Petrarch behind: the beloved is not a shrine to be addressed but a party to be satisfied of the contract's honesty. Her central comparison performs the synthesis her position claims. "My bounty is as boundless as the sea, / My love as deep; the more I give to thee, / The more I have, for both are infinite" (2.2.133 to 135). The image is physical, tidal, and material, the sea being the least celestial object available, and the claim it carries is the one neither friend's theory can utter. Mercutio's appetite is a economics of depletion: wanting is spent in the getting. Romeo's worship is an economics of distance: the feeling is preserved by the obstacle. Juliet proposes an economics of increase, in which giving enlarges the stock, and she proposes it not as a metaphor for her soul but as the plain mechanics of her tenderness.

Then she sets the terms. "If that thy bent of love be honourable, / Thy purpose marriage, send me word tomorrow" (2.2.143 to 144) is the most consequential sentence in the debate, because it converts desire into an institution with a schedule. The body is not denied; the wedding night is awaited by her with an impatience the soliloquies make unmistakable. The ideal is not denied either; honourable purpose is the explicit condition. What is denied is each friend's founding premise. Against Mercutio, the speech insists that appetite can be bound by vows without being falsified by them, that the body's wanting can sign a contract and mean it. Against Romeo, it insists that worship must submit to verification, tomorrow, by messenger, in words that can be checked. Juliet's tenderness is frank because it hides nothing: not the wanting, not the condition, not the haste, and not the fear, which she names as plainly as the love when she calls the contract too rash, too unadvised, too sudden, and proceeds with it anyway, eyes open.

### Does the play crown Juliet's tenderness as the correct theory of love?

Nearly, and the distinction matters. Juliet's position is the only one the play tests against reality and finds workable: it names terms, sets conditions, and survives the friar's cell and the potion night. The play still kills her. Endorsement in this tragedy means vindication of the position, never protection of the person holding it.

The third term also reframes the two friends retroactively. Once Juliet has spoken, Mercutio's bawdy reads as a defense mounted by a man who has never been answered by a beloved, and Romeo's worship reads as a draft written by a man who had not yet been answered either. Both theories are monologues. Juliet's position exists only as dialogue, which is why it has no set piece to rival Queen Mab or the balcony liturgy and why careless centuries have credited the play's idea of love to the men. The play's own distribution of outcomes is its commentary. The monologuists' theories each collapse at their first contact with the events they claimed to explain. The dialogic position functions: it produces the marriage, the consummation, the loyalty in exile, the refusal of Paris, and the plan's near success. It is defeated by plague, by a sealed door in Mantua, by the feud's weather, never by its own incoherence. In a tragedy that kills everyone it examines, functioning until accident intervenes is the nearest thing to a verdict the form allows.

The third term can also be traced under pressure, in the scenes where the feud stops debating and starts issuing orders. Commanded to marry Paris, Juliet answers her father with an equivocation that only her position makes possible: she will not marry yet, and when she does, "it shall be Romeo, whom you know I hate" (3.5.121 to 122). The sentence is frank tenderness wearing armour. Its literal content is the contract, stated to the one audience that must not hear it, and its honesty is total in every register except the factual one her father can audit. Neither friend's theory survives translation into that room. Mercutio's appetite would have counselled compliance and a lover on the side, the world's ordinary arrangement, and Romeo's worship would have counselled a beautiful refusal and a swoon. Juliet produces a working sentence instead, one that buys a night, protects a husband, and keeps the tenderness intact by lying about everything except the love. The potion soliloquy extends the same evidence and can be left to its own close readings in the series; what belongs to the debate is only its premise, that the girl who set marriage as desire's condition in the orchard will drink a feigning death rather than break the condition in the cathedral. A theory of love is what its holder will pay for it. Juliet's receipts are the largest in the play, and they are rendered in her own coin, courage, rather than in sighs or in jokes.

Juliet's starting point should be recorded, since the debate tends to recruit her only from the feast onward. In Act 1 Scene 3, asked for her disposition toward marriage, she gives the household a sentence of perfect contractual neutrality: she will look to like, if looking liking moves, and no deeper will she endart her eye than her mother's consent gives strength to make it fly. The sentence is the third position in embryo. Desire is neither worshipped nor leered at; it is treated as a finding to be reached by inspection, subject to ratification, in a process whose stages she can already enumerate at thirteen. The feast then administers the inspection ahead of schedule, and her conduct in the sonnet's second movement shows the same mind working at erotic speed: when the kiss's sin requires absolution, she trades the metaphor back and forth with a lawyer's pleasure in its logic, granting and reclaiming the sin until the Nurse's interruption closes the session. Nothing in the exchange is appetite's sprawl or worship's swoon. It is negotiation conducted as flirtation, which is to say it is her theory already in uniform.

Her registration of the feud's price completes the portrait and separates her position from both friends' at the root. Learning the stranger's name, she computes the catastrophe in one line: her only love sprung from her only hate, seen too early and known too late at 1.5.137 to 138. Neither friend's theory contains a line item for the feud. Mercutio's appetite treats the houses as scenery for jokes until the scenery kills him; Romeo's worship treats the feud as weather, regrettable and external, until it arrives as a sentence of exile. Juliet prices the feud into the love from the first hour, as a cost the contract will have to carry, and the whole of her subsequent conduct, the terms, the schedule, the secrecy, the potion, is the administration of that original computation. The third position is sometimes mistaken for a compromise between the body and the ideal. The computation shows the truth: it is the only position in the play that includes the world in its calculations, and the world repays the compliment by destroying it with the world's own instruments, plague, doors, and daggers, rather than with any flaw in the arithmetic.

## The Friar as the Debate's First Judge

Before the critics take the case, the play submits the debate to an internal judge, and Friar Laurence's rulings are worth recording because he splits his verdict between the friends with a precision the tragedy later confirms. Hearing that Rosaline has been discarded overnight, the Friar delivers Mercutio's doctrine in a cassock, ruling that young men's love lies in their eyes rather than in their hearts (2.3.67 to 68). The sentence concedes the appetite theorist's central evidence, the transferable object, the ocular mechanism, the brine of wasted sighs, and it does so without a single joke, which is how the audience knows the concession is serious. Yet the same judge, in the same scene, refuses Mercutio's conclusion. The feeling in front of him, however ocular its origin, can be regularized, married, and put to social work against the feud, and the Friar proceeds to marry it that afternoon. Appetite is the diagnosis; appetite is not the destiny. The distinction is the whole of Juliet's third position stated by its first institutional ally, and Romeo, who has heard both friends' theories, hears in the cell the first account of his desire that neither reduces it to the body nor abandons it to worship.

The Friar's second ruling comes at the wedding itself, and it judges the lovers' side of the debate as sternly as the first ruling judged Romeo's past. "These violent delights have violent ends" (2.6.9) is aimed at the intensity both young people bring to the cell, and its imagery of fire and powder, consumed in the act of kissing, borrows Mercutio's physics to warn Romeo's heirs. Intensity, the ruling says, is not self certifying; a feeling's violence is evidence about its duration, not its worth. The tragedy proceeds to execute the sentence in the letter rather than the spirit, since the delights are ended by other people's violence, but the ruling stands in the text as the play's own first adjudication: Mercutio is right about the mechanism of young men's love, Romeo is right that the mechanism can open into something the mechanism does not explain, and the something requires slowness, terms, and luck. Two of those three requirements are Juliet's contributions. The third is the one Verona refuses to supply.

## Petrarch Against Ovid: The Traditions Behind the Friends

The debate is older than the friends, and Shakespeare stages it with the literary traditions already assigned to each side. Behind Romeo stands Petrarch: the sonnet sequence as a technology for converting an unattainable lady into a lifetime of exquisite suffering, the beloved's cruelty as the engine of the lover's fame, the whole apparatus of sighs, oxymorons, and celestial comparisons that Romeo recites in Act 1 as though reading from the manual. Behind Mercutio stands Ovid, the other great classical authority on desire available to an Elizabethan schoolboy: the Ovid of the Amores and the Art of Love, for whom desire is a game of pursuit, disguise, and appetite, to be played with technique and enjoyed without metaphysics. Verona's street argument is a curriculum argument. The two friends have each swallowed one of the Renaissance's two authorized accounts of love, and the play is the examination in which both accounts are marked.

Heather Dubrow's account of English Petrarchism and its counterdiscourses is the sharpest tool for this section of the debate, because Dubrow shows that Petrarchan worship was never a stable creed in the period: it carried its counterpressures inside itself, the Ovidian laugh, the fear of effeminacy, the suspicion that the sighing lover is performing for other men. Read through Dubrow, Romeo's Act 1 performance is not failed sincerity but a convention doing its ordinary work, and Mercutio's mockery is not an intrusion on the convention but one of its built-in voices, the counterdiscourse the tradition itself supplies. That reading strengthens the article's verdict. If the two theories arrive as a matched pair inside one literary system, then the play's refusal to endorse either is not indecision but design: the system is the subject, and Juliet, who has read neither Petrarch nor Ovid and conducts her loving in the grammar of contracts, tides, and falconry, is the experiment conducted outside the library.

Joseph Porter's Mercutio is the strongest statement of the opposing critical instinct, the instinct to locate the debate inside a psyche rather than a system. Porter's Mercutio is a man whose bawdy is driven, compulsive, and charged with an investment in Romeo that the jokes are designed to manage. On Porter's reading, the conjuring scene is not a set piece but a symptom: Mercutio cannot stop anatomizing Rosaline because Romeo's desire, aimed anywhere, is the fact his own feeling must keep converting into farce. Harry Levin's account of the play pulls in the other direction, toward form. Levin's tragedy is built on the patterned opposition of high and low, formal and informal, the lovers' ceremonious verse set against the prose and bawdy of the world that surrounds and finally excludes them. In Levin's architecture, Mercutio is less a psyche than a register, the play's low style given legs, wit, and a sword.

The disagreement between Porter and Levin is genuine and worth settling, because the article's verdict depends on it. If Levin is wholly right, the debate is the play's design and the friends are its instruments, and the question of what either man believes dissolves into the question of what the structure juxtaposes. If Porter is wholly right, the debate is a symptom of one man's buried feeling, and the play's philosophy of love shrinks into a case history. The text supports each reading at a different joint. Levin explains the placement of the speeches: the bawdy arrives in blocks, at structurally marked intervals, and stops at the midpoint with a precision no psychology requires. Porter explains the escalation of the speeches: the bawdy intensifies, coarsens, and lengthens exactly in proportion as Romeo's happiness becomes real and exclusive, a curve that formal design alone does not predict. Escalation is the more telling evidence for the question this article asks, because a theory of love is revealed not by where it is stated but by what it does under pressure. Mercutio's doctrine, under the pressure of Romeo's joy, does not hold steady like a register in a design. It strains, overreaches, and finally follows its holder into a duel that has nothing to do with love at all, as though the defense, denied its object, went looking for any available wound. Porter takes the local point. Levin keeps the architecture. The play holds the theories in tension because Shakespeare built the tension into the structure and then paid a character to feel it.

The traditions meet most intimately in the play's handling of the sonnet, the Petrarchan instrument itself. Shakespeare frames the action with sonnets, the two Prologues, and then, at the feast, commits the form's quiet revolution: the sonnet that in Petrarch is one man's fourteen lines addressed to an absent lady becomes fourteen lines shared between two speakers, traded quatrain for quatrain, with the couplet arriving as a kiss negotiated rather than a conclusion imposed. The counterdiscourse Dubrow describes is usually a laugh at the lover's expense from outside the poem. At the feast the counterdiscourse is inside the poem, spoken by its object, in its own metre, improving it. Ovid does not defeat Petrarch in that scene; Juliet edits Petrarch, and the editing is the third position's literary form, corresponding exactly to its erotic one. Even Romeo's private sonneteering is handled with the same doubleness. The play never shows the poems he is said to have made for Rosaline. His idealism before the feast exists as report, sigh, and oxymoron, a literature about a literature, and the absence of the actual verses is Shakespeare's tact and Shakespeare's joke: the worship that fills Act 1 would not survive being read aloud, and the play knows better than to read it.

The traditions also supply each friend with a therapy, and the therapies expose the theories as neatly as the poems do. Ovid wrote the cure as well as the art: the Remedia Amoris prescribes occupation, absence, and the deliberate rehearsal of the beloved's defects, a regimen of disenchantment administered by the patient to himself. Mercutio's prescription in Act 1 Scene 4, roughness returned for roughness until love is beaten down, is the Remedia compressed into street practice and outsourced to a friend, since the patient is in no condition to administer it. The therapy's premise is the doctrine's premise, that the feeling is a condition of the body and yields to handling. Petrarch's corresponding therapy is the refusal of therapy: the sequence converts the incurable case into the career, so that the lover's suffering, properly maintained, becomes fame, the one satisfaction the system guarantees. Romeo in Act 1 is already collecting that satisfaction in advance, performing the case for Benvolio's admiration. Juliet's position, characteristically, has no therapy because it does not regard love as a disease. It regards love as a commitment whose difficulties are logistical and mortal rather than pathological, and its prescriptions are messengers, schedules, and a friar. The debate's three accounts of what love is can thus be told apart by what each proposes to do about it: discharge it, suffer it beautifully, or marry it by tomorrow and send word.

## What Each Theory Cannot Say

A theory of love is convicted most efficiently at the point where its vocabulary runs out, and the play arranges one such point for each position. Mercutio's silence is fidelity. His doctrine can describe wanting, taking, tiring, and mocking, and it cannot describe staying: no speech of his imagines a desire that persists past satisfaction into obligation, which is why the marriage, the play's central fact, is the one event his wit never touches except as a bawdy errand it has misread. The vocabulary's limit becomes the man's limit in the piazza, where the loyalty he cannot theorize acts through him anyway: he draws for his friend, for honour, for the insult of the moment, and dies discovering that his conduct had a theory his mouth had spent three acts denying. The grave man pun he promises at 3.1.97 to 98 is the doctrine's last performance, a joke made over a wound the joke cannot reach, and its pathos is the sound of a vocabulary acknowledging, in the only form it owns, that the world contains more than it can say.

Romeo's silence, in the early acts, is the body's dignity. His worship can describe sighs, shrines, and suns, and it cannot describe wanting without embarrassment, which is why Rosaline's unavailability suited it and why the orchard's negotiations come as an education administered by his teacher's daughter. The interesting fact is that the play cures this silence not by refuting the worshipper but by marrying him: the consummation the liturgy could not mention takes place offstage between the acts, and Romeo emerges from it into Act 3 with a new stake in the world's peace, refusing Tybalt's challenge on grounds he is forbidden to state. The ideal, given a body and a marriage, produces the play's one genuine attempt at peacemaking. Its failure is the feud's work, not the theory's, and the distinction belongs in the verdict alongside the harsher evidence of the collapse that follows Mercutio's fall.

Juliet's silence is the future. Her position can state terms, set schedules, and pay costs, and it cannot imagine the condition its whole conduct presupposes, a world in which the contract could be published. The secrecy that protects the marriage also starves it of every ally except a friar and a nurse, and when the machinery fails there is no public to appeal to, no household obliged to help, because the tenderness was never announced where help is organized. This is not an incoherence in the third position; it is the feud's tax on it, the same tax the verdict section records. But the debate should note the asymmetry the silence creates. Mercutio's theory fails from inside, Romeo's fails from inside, and Juliet's is failed from outside, by weather, plague, and other people's doors. Tragedy grades its theories as it grades its people, and the grading here is the nearest the play comes to an endorsement it is willing to sign.

## The Friendship as the Debate's Stage

The argument needs the friendship, and the friendship, briefly, is this: Mercutio, kinsman to the Prince and intimate of the Montague heir, claims Romeo's company as his native element, and Romeo, in the play's first acts, is endeared to his friends precisely by being the audience his friend's wit performs for. The biography of that bond, its history inside the scenes and its destruction in the piazza, is told in full in the narrative of [Romeo and Mercutio's friendship and what defines it](/2023/03/24/romeo-and-mercutio-friendship/), and the companion portrait of [Mercutio as the friend who steals the play](/2022/08/19/mercutio-character-analysis/) takes the character whole. The bond matters here only as the debate's stage machinery. Each man performs his theory for the other, because the other is the one witness whose conversion would settle the argument, and each performance is therefore also a claim on the friendship's terms: love me, the theories say, in the vocabulary I use for love itself.

### Why does the argument about love need two friends rather than one spokesman?

Because neither theory can expose the other alone. Romeo's worship sounds complete until Mercutio prices it in flesh; Mercutio's appetite sounds complete until Romeo's grief prices it in feeling. Set side by side in one friendship, each becomes the other's evidence. A single spokesman would have made the play a pamphlet.

The machinery explains the debate's peculiar intimacy. Mercutio's bawdy is rarely addressed to the world; it is addressed to Romeo, at Romeo, in the hearing of witnesses whose laughter scores the points. Romeo's confidences flow the same way, to Benvolio in the first scene and, by report and evasion, around Mercutio ever after. Once Romeo has a secret that belongs to Juliet's theory rather than to either friend's, the machinery jams. In Act 2 Scene 4 the recovered Romeo trades quips at championship level and discloses nothing, and Mercutio, delighted, misreads the concealment as the cure his doctrine prescribed. The friendship's stage can host any argument except the one fact that has ended the argument. That is why the debate never receives its final session. Mercutio dies still believing the contest stands at one set all, his friend cured of worship by a wedding Mercutio would have filed under appetite, and the audience alone has heard all three positions stated. The play distributes its philosophy among people who cannot hear each other whole, and the distribution is the tragedy in miniature.

The friendship's final exchange belongs in the evidence, because the debate's two principals conduct it over the wound itself. Mercutio, hurt and not yet knowing the hurt is mortal, turns on the friend who had stepped between the swords: why the devil came Romeo between them, since the wound was taken under Romeo's arm at 3.1.103 to 104. The reproach is the doctrine of appetite receiving its last datum. Romeo had interposed his body, the peacemaking body of a man whose theory had just been given a marriage to protect, and the interposition is what the blade used. Mercutio's dying analysis, that his friend's love made the opening his enemy struck through, is unfair as blame and exact as mechanics, and Romeo accepts the mechanics in his own indictment minutes later. The debate ends, as it began, with the two friends interpreting the same event in their two vocabularies, except that the event is now a death, and one vocabulary is about to fall silent in the middle of its finest performance.

## Who Interprets Romeo: The Wit Combat as a Contest of Jurisdiction

Underneath the debate's doctrines runs a jurisdictional quarrel that explains its heat: the friends are arguing, at every point, over who holds the right to interpret Romeo. Mercutio's theory is also a claim of office. The man who can translate every sigh into appetite appoints himself the sighing man's physician, licensed to diagnose, to prescribe, and to mock the patient's own account of the symptoms as part of the disease. Romeo's counterclaim is the wound principle of the orchard, that the feeling's meaning is available only from inside it, which revokes the physician's licence and installs the patient as sole interpreter. The wit combat of Act 2 Scene 4 is the jurisdictional quarrel performed as sport. Romeo, newly married and radiating a competence Mercutio cannot source, wins exchange after exchange, and Mercutio's delight is genuine and proprietary at once: the patient has recovered, in the physician's reading, because the treatment worked. Every term of the scene reverses under the audience's knowledge. The recovery is real and the diagnosis is false; the wit is Romeo's natural element reclaimed, not appetite's fever breaking; and the secret that powers the performance is the one fact the physician's office never gets shown. Shakespeare builds the scene so that the debate's loser wins it, on points, in public, which is why Mercutio's authority survives the scene intact in his own estimation and why the piazza, an hour later, finds him still practising medicine on a patient whose condition has become a marriage, a feud, and a drawn sword. The jurisdictional reading also settles a small point in the critics' quarrel. Porter's escalating Mercutio is a physician watching his licence being revoked by degrees and raising his voice to keep the office. Levin's formal Mercutio is the office itself, the low style's chair, filled by whoever holds it. The text supports the physician: offices do not take wounds personally, and Mercutio, in the piazza, takes everything personally at last.

## Honour Crashes the Argument: The Duel as the Debate's Audit

The audit scene repays a slow reading, because it shows the debate being misread in real time by its own participants, with fatal consequences for the misreader. Tybalt arrives with a word, villain, that belongs to the feud's vocabulary and to no theory of love at all. Romeo, meeting the word, answers from inside the third position and cannot say so: his protest that he never injured Tybalt, that he loves him better than the challenger can devise, and that the name Capulet is dear to him as his own at 3.1.67 to 71, is Juliet's grammar of contract and kinship attempting public speech. The grounds of the peace are a marriage, the marriage is secret, and so the speech arrives in the piazza as an unexplained softness, a riddle whose key is locked in a friar's cell. Mercutio, auditing the exchange with the only instruments he owns, reads the softness through his doctrine and finds the reading he has always predicted: love as appetite's effeminacy, the worshipper unmanned. His verdict, "O calm, dishonourable, vile submission!" at 3.1.72, is the doctrine of the body delivering its final judgement on the debate, and it is wrong in every particular. The submission is not calm appetite, not dishonour, and not the Rosaline sickness; it is tenderness under orders, the third position keeping its terms at the price of its reputation. Mercutio draws to correct the record his theory has falsified. The sword that answers him is the feud's, and the debate's audit concludes with the auditor bleeding from a wound his categories inflicted as surely as Tybalt's rapier did.

Romeo's part in the audit has been weighed in the verdict section for its collapse, but its first movement belongs here, because the refusal to fight is the debate's one moment of applied philosophy. For perhaps thirty lines, a theory of love governs a man's conduct in the street, against provocation, at real cost, and the theory is neither friend's: Mercutio's appetite would have fought for the laugh of it, and the old worship would have fought for the lady's name in the abstract. The married Romeo declines the duel because the challenger is now his wife's cousin, because kinship has rewritten the street's arithmetic, because tenderness, having signed its contract, discovers obligations the sighing and the leering had never generated. The play lets the application stand just long enough to be destroyed by the man it excluded from its knowledge. Then the collapse, the fury, and the exile proceed as the verdict section records. But the audit's finding should preserve the order of events: the third position worked, publicly, in the worst conditions Verona offers, and it was not refuted. It was interrupted, by its own secrecy and its best friend's theory, acting in that order.

## Where the Play Lands: Body, Ideal, and the Cost of Each

Act 3 Scene 1 is the debate's graveyard, and it buries both theories within a hundred lines. Mercutio, his doctrine of appetite useless in a quarrel about honour, fights on behalf of the friend whose love he had spent three acts discounting, and dies converting the experience into the idiom he has left: "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1.99). The curse is the doctrine's bankruptcy declared in its holder's own voice. The man who explained all human conduct by the body has been opened by a sword under his friend's arm, and the explanation he reaches for is not appetite but malediction, aimed at the social world his theory never had terms for. Romeo, metres away, conducts the ideal's bankruptcy in the same minutes. "O sweet Juliet, / Thy beauty hath made me effeminate / And in my temper softened valour's steel!" (3.1.113 to 115) is the worshipper discovering that worship, as he had practised it, had no instructions for the moment when love and vengeance divide the stage. His correction, "fire-eyed fury be my conduct now" (3.1.123), abandons the theory as completely as Mercutio's curse abandons his, and the two friends exit the debate together, one into death and one into exile, each holding the other's refutation.

The tomb completes the accounting. Romeo's final speeches keep the celestial register of the early worship, the vault made "a feasting presence full of light" (5.3.85 to 86) by Juliet's beauty, yet the content has been through Juliet's school: the man who once sighed at an absence now acts on evidence, addresses the corpse as a party to an agreement, and keeps the appointment the contract set. What survives of the ideal is its courtesy. What survives of Mercutio is nothing spoken, which is its own verdict: the doctrine of appetite has no representative in the final scene, no mourner, no echo, unless the Apothecary's starving body, selling poison because "my poverty, but not my will" consents (5.1.75), counts as appetite's last appearance, and it appears there as victim, not theorist. Juliet's position, by contrast, is the tomb scene's operating system. Her terms, her schedule, her refusal to be comforted out of the contract govern everyone's movements even after her voice stops, and the golden statues the fathers promise are the feud's crude translation of her economics of increase: value created, too late, out of loss.

A sterner version of this article's verdict exists in the series, and honesty requires facing it. The argument that [the play is not really about love at all](/2022/02/11/why-romeo-and-juliet-is-not-about-love/), that the feud is the engine and the romance its most beautiful casualty, would dissolve the friends' debate into a sideshow: two young men philosophizing while the hate machinery warms up. The debate survives the challenge, but only just, and on terms the sterner argument dictates. The theories of love matter in the play exactly as much as the feud permits, which is why both theories are refuted by events neither addresses: honour, plague, a father's marriage market, a friar's sealed door. Shakespeare's final position on the friends' question is therefore double. Between body and ideal, the play leans, by the evidence of what functions, toward Juliet's synthesis of both. Between the whole debate and the world that hosts it, the play delivers the colder judgment that theories of love, even the true one, are luxuries the feud taxes at a hundred percent. The tension the brief for this article asked to be found is real, and it is load-bearing: hold any single view and the play breaks it; hold the three together and the breakage itself becomes the meaning.

Paris completes the evidence by supplying the null case against which the three real positions can be measured. The County loves by arrangement, with courtesy, persistence, and no discernible appetite or worship of his own; he negotiates with the father, accepts the schedule, mourns at the tomb with formal flowers, and dies asking to be laid beside a bride who never chose him. His suit is what Verona's official theory of love looks like when stated plainly: marriage as a transaction between houses, desire irrelevant, worship outsourced to custom. The debate among the three principals is sometimes accused of being a young people's game conducted while the adults arrange the real business, and Paris is the accusation's exhibit. Yet the null case cuts the other way on inspection. Paris's theory, the only one with institutional backing, produces nothing at all: no marriage, no knowledge of its object, no mourner who can say what was lost. The friends' half-truths and Juliet's whole one at least generate a world. The arrangement generates a corpse in somebody else's tomb, laid there, with terrible courtesy, by the husband.

The fathers' closing bargain supplies the debate's final mistranslation, and it should be read with the theories in mind. Over the bodies, Capulet and Montague compete in grief's currency, each promising a statue in pure gold, and the play ends with love priced, cast, and set up in metal by the two men whose feud taxed it to death. Gold is the debate's fourth element arriving too late: Romeo's lead transmuted, Mercutio's appetite sublimed into bullion, Juliet's sea of bounty frozen into a pair of monuments that give nothing and increase nothing. The statues are Verona's official theory of love at last, the arrangement economy honouring in death the desire it would have prosecuted in life, and their useless splendour is the tragedy's last word on every theory in this article. What the young people knew about love, in three incompatible vocabularies, the city can only repeat in one: the vocabulary of price, which all three positions, for three different reasons, had refused.

Exile administers the debate's last experiment, and its result is the saddest in the file. In Mantua, cut off from the partner who had educated his theory, Romeo regresses. The regression begins in the Friar's cell, where the banished man answers philosophy with the orchard's epistemology turned savage, telling his judge that the judge cannot speak of what the judge does not feel (3.3.64), and ends at an apothecary's door with gold offered for a dram of poison. The sequence deserves the debate's full attention because it isolates the variable the verdict turns on. Romeo's worship, sustained by dialogue, had produced peacekeeping, contract keeping, and the tomb's courtesy. Romeo's worship, deprived of dialogue, reverts within days to appetite's economy, purchasing the body's destruction in a market transaction, and calls the poison a cordial. The ideal was never self sufficient; it was the third position's pupil, and the pupil, sent away from the teacher, forgets the lesson in the order it was learned. Mercutio's doctrine, had its holder lived to see Mantua, would have claimed the regression as its vindication, the appetite outlasting the costume at last. The claim would have been half earned, and the play's refusal to let Mercutio make it, his voice three acts silent by then, is the tragedy's tact and its judgement in one: no theory in this play is permitted to collect its winnings.

The manner of the two deaths files the audit's last exhibits, and the filing is exact. Romeo dies by a purchased drug, appetite's economy completing its circuit: gold for poison, the body's chemistry obeying a transaction, while the speech over the body he believes dead is the worshipper's liturgy perfected, courtesy and light imagery intact to the final kiss. Body and ideal, the friends' two elements, share his death between them like executors. Juliet dies by steel, waking to evidence, declining rescue, and performing the contract's final clause with the instrument the feud left lying in the tomb. Neither death is administered by the position its holder professed. The reductionist's heir is killed by a story he was never told; the worshipper is killed by a schedule he never received; the third position's holder is killed by the feud's hardware after every one of her terms has been honoured. The play's distribution of deaths is its peer review, and the review's conclusion is the one this article has argued throughout: the theories were not refuted by better theories. They were overtaken, in descending order of fault, by secrecy, by misreading, and by a city whose only working account of anything was price.

The debate's durability outside the theatre confirms the verdict from an unexpected direction. Every reading culture that adopts the play promptly reconstitutes the two parties: the Mercutios, who hold that the lovers are a hormone event with good publicity, and the Romeos, who hold that the feeling's altitude is its own authentication, and the parties argue with the play's own lines because Shakespeare pre-wrote both briefs. What the reconstitution reliably loses is the third position, since Juliet's tenderness has no slogan, no set piece detachable from its scenes, and no party. It exists only as conduct, and conduct does not anthologize. Recovering it requires the whole play read in sequence with the debate in view, which is the labour this article has attempted. The recovery matters beyond piety toward a character. A culture that can only hear the body and the ideal arguing will keep mistaking the argument for the subject, and will keep being surprised by the tomb. The play's subject is what the argument costs and what the unheard third voice knew: that desire, stated plainly, wanted openly, and bound by terms, was the one account of love in Verona that never lied to anyone, and that its truth was no protection at all.

## The Audience as the Debate's Second Jury

One further court hears the debate at every performance, and its split verdict is part of the play's meaning. Audiences laugh with Mercutio. The bawdy wins rooms, wins intervals, wins the walk home, and generations of playgoers have left the theatre persuaded that the doctrine of appetite is the play's own voice, since it is the voice that entertained them. The same audiences weep with the lovers and remember the weeping as the experience. The split is not hypocrisy; it is the tension the play is built to produce, administered live. Shakespeare arranges for the doctrine of the body to be the most enjoyable thing in the tragedy and for its holder to be removed at the midpoint, so that every audience is made to feel the cost of the entertainment it preferred: the second half proceeds without the laughter, in a Verona suddenly airless, and the deprivation instructs the jury more efficiently than any verdict speech could. A production that plays Mercutio as merely funny rigs the trial, and a production that plays Romeo's worship as merely foolish rigs it the other way. The text's balance is exact, and keeping it is the director's share of the adjudication this article has attempted in print.

## The InsightCrunch Theories-of-Love Map

The three positions reward a side by side reading, and readers assembling a study record of the debate, quotation by quotation and scene by scene, do well to keep the comparison in a durable research notebook of the kind maintained at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/), where the map below can sit beside the passages it summarizes. The InsightCrunch theories-of-love map sets the bodily, the ideal, and Juliet's frank tenderness in parallel columns, so that any scene in the play can be filed against the three readings of it.

| Dimension | Mercutio: love as bodily appetite | Romeo: love as ideal worship | Juliet: love as frank tenderness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Governing claim | Desire is an appetite of the body, and fine language is its costume | Desire is a sacred devotion, and the beloved is its shrine | Desire is a bond to be stated plainly, wanted openly, and bound by terms |
| The beloved is | An inventory of parts, one woman much like another | A saint, a sun, an angel, set above the lover | A partner who answers, corrects, and sets conditions |
| Characteristic speech | Bawdy, puns, catalogues, the conjuring of Rosaline | Oxymorons, sonnets, celestial and religious images | Comparisons from the sea and the household, contracts, schedules |
| View of dreams | Children of an idle brain, appetite in fancy dress | Omens worth fearing, signs of a governed world | Material to be tested, never sworn by, as the moon speech rules |
| What it asks of the lover | Roughness in return, appetite answered and discharged | Sighs, patience, and suffering beautifully borne | Honesty, haste owned as haste, and marriage as the condition |
| Where it is strongest | Exposing the fraud in Romeo's Rosaline performance | The feast sonnet, once the saint begins to answer | The orchard scene, where every term is set and met |
| Where it fails | The piazza, where honour kills and appetite has no terms | Act 3 Scene 1, where worship gives no conduct for vengeance | It does not fail on its own ground; plague and the feud defeat it from outside |
| Last trace in the play | The Apothecary's poverty, appetite as victim rather than theory | The tomb liturgy, courtesy surviving as form | The whole final scene's machinery, still running on her terms |

The map earns its keep when a reader files a scene against it. Take the wit combat of Act 2 Scene 4: Mercutio's column predicts the bawdy and the recovery diagnosis, Romeo's column has gone silent because the worship has become a secret, and Juliet's column, though its holder is offstage, governs the scene's errand and its outcome. Take the piazza: every column fails to predict the event except in the row reserved for failure, which is the map's way of showing that the duel belongs to a fourth system, honour, that the table deliberately excludes and the play deliberately admits. Take the tomb: the columns converge, Romeo's speech filing under worship's imagery and tenderness's terms at once, and the convergence is the map's final lesson, that the positions were never three sealed doctrines but three emphases the tragedy keeps redistributing among its speakers until only one speaker is left to hold all of them, briefly, over a body.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does Mercutio mean by "If love be rough with you, be rough with love" in Act 1 Scene 4?

Mercutio means that desire should be fought on its own level, answered physically and discharged, never worshipped. Romeo has just complained that love pricks like a thorn, and the reply at 1.4.27 to 28 converts the complaint into a strategy: prick love for pricking and beat love down. The prescription assumes love is an opponent in a scuffle rather than a god on an altar, so the correct response to its roughness is counter-roughness, the appetite taken and finished with, instead of sighs offered up as tribute. The whole doctrine of the body is inside the couplet, which is why Romeo's silence after it matters: the idealist has no answer ready, and the scene moves on to Queen Mab.

### Q: What does Romeo's question "Is love a tender thing?" in Act 1 Scene 4 reveal about the idealist's suffering?

The question reveals that Romeo's worship hurts in a way his creed had not predicted, and that the hurt confuses him. At 1.4.25 to 26 he asks whether love is tender and answers his own question with a catalogue of injuries: too rough, too rude, too boisterous, a thorn. A Petrarchan lover expects the beloved's distance to cause the pain and expects the pain to certify the love. Romeo's bewilderment is different, since the pain feels less like noble suffering than like damage, and the speech that follows about his soul of lead admits the feeling has disabled rather than ennobled him. Mercutio hears the crack in the creed and drives his prescription straight through it.

### Q: Why does Romeo say his soul of lead keeps him from dancing in Act 1 Scene 4?

Romeo says it because, in his theory, desire is experienced as weight, and the image at 1.4.15 to 16 makes the theory physical: a soul of lead stakes him to the ground so that he cannot move, let alone dance into a masked ball. Lead is the alchemist's basest metal and the coffin's lining, and the choice of it tells the audience that the Rosaline worship has become a small death carried around in the body. Mercutio's answering taunt about borrowing Cupid's wings only sharpens the opposition. The idealist's love pins down; the body theorist's love is equipment for flight. The scene needs no commentary, since the two physics of desire have stated themselves.

### Q: What changes in Romeo's saint and pilgrim imagery once Juliet answers it at the feast in Act 1 Scene 5?

The imagery stops being a monologue and becomes a negotiation, and its meaning changes with its grammar. Romeo opens the shared sonnet at 1.5.93 with the full Petrarchan apparatus of shrine, pilgrim, and sin, expecting the silence an absent Rosaline always provided. Juliet answers inside the same form, correcting his hand's devotion at 1.5.96 and matching him image for image until the kiss the liturgy was angling for is granted on terms she has helped to draft. Worship addressed to a statue becomes worship conducted with a partner, and the partner's fluency is the point: the beloved turns out to have read the service book too, and to hold editorial rights over it.

### Q: What does Juliet's "You kiss by th' book" expose about Romeo's courtship in Act 1 Scene 5?

The line at 1.5.109 exposes Romeo's most ardent gesture as a performance learned from literature, and it does so with affection rather than contempt. Juliet has granted the kiss the sonnet negotiated, and her verdict identifies its style: correct, formal, by the book, the kiss of a young man whose passion arrives premeditated in quotations. The exposure matters to the debate because it is the first time either theory of love is audited by its object. Mercutio mocks Romeo's worship from outside, as a spectator. Juliet audits it from inside, as the worshipped party, and her audit is the more damaging and the more loving: the form may stay, the line implies, provided the man learns to mean it in person.

### Q: What does Mercutio's catalogue of Rosaline's body in Act 2 Scene 4 leave out?

The catalogue leaves out Rosaline herself: her mind, her refusal, her reasons, and her existence as anything except an arrangement of parts. In Act 2 Scene 4 Mercutio reconstructs the beloved who has supposedly unmanned his friend as a forehead, a lip, a foot, a leg, and the famous demesnes that lie adjacent, and the inventory is the doctrine of appetite drawn to scale. A woman so described cannot say no, which is convenient, since the real Rosaline's no is the fact the catalogue exists to dissolve. The omission is the tell. Mercutio's theory can process any quantity of body and no quantity of person, and the play's heroine is about to be introduced to him, in the same scene, in the shape of an errand he can only misread.

### Q: What does Mercutio mean by "O flesh, flesh, how art thou fishified" in Act 2 Scene 4?

Mercutio means that lovesickness has drained Romeo of his proper element and left him preserved, sexless, and faintly ridiculous, like a fish out of water or a herring in salt. The cry at 2.4.37 launches the dried herring comparison and the whole mock anatomy that follows, and its wit is systematic rather than random. In the doctrine of appetite, an unconsummated desire is a malfunction: the body built for its satisfactions is pickling in its own brine while the soul performs poetry overhead. Fishified Romeo is the Petrarchan lover as his friend sees him, a creature suspended between elements, and the greeting turns warm only when Romeo starts trading quips again, which Mercutio misreads as the patient climbing back into the sea.

### Q: How does Romeo repair Mercutio's mockery of the Nurse in Act 2 Scene 4?

Romeo repairs it by treating the errand with a ceremony Mercutio's theory cannot generate. Mercutio has greeted the Nurse with a bawd's song and a joke about her fan, filing her mission under procurement because appetite doctrine has no other drawer for it. Romeo, left alone with her, commends himself to Juliet, protests the honour of his intention, and confirms the marriage arranged for that afternoon, converting the supposed procurement into the scheduling of a sacrament before the Nurse's eyes. The repair is quiet and complete. It also goes unheard by its intended audience of one, since Mercutio has exited laughing, and the debate loses its chance to be settled by the single most instructive errand in the play.

### Q: What does Romeo mean by "He jests at scars that never felt a wound" in Act 2 Scene 2?

Romeo means that Mercutio's mockery of love is the testimony of an outsider, and that outsider testimony about a wound is worth nothing. The line opens the orchard scene at 2.2.1, aimed backward at the friend who spent the previous scene conjuring the lovesick fool home, and it states a theory of knowledge inside the play's debate about love. Mercutio had claimed the spectator's advantage: standing outside the costume, the jester sees the appetite plain. Romeo reverses the claim. The spectator sees only the costume, the scar's surface, and the jesting is a confession of never having been cut. The play tests the principle cruelly, since inside knowledge does not save Romeo, but the orchard gives the line its force: the wound, this time, is real.

### Q: What does Juliet reject when she stops Romeo from swearing by the moon in Act 2 Scene 2?

Juliet rejects an oath whose guarantor changes nightly, and with it the whole Petrarchan habit of swearing beautifully instead of binding honestly. At 2.2.109 she names the moon inconstant, circling monthly through its sphere, and forbids the vow that would borrow that inconstancy as its security. The rejection is the third theory of love in a single instruction. Mercutio would have laughed the oath off the stage as costume; Romeo offered it in earnest as worship. Juliet neither laughs nor worships. She audits the instrument, finds the security bad, and directs the lover to swear by a steadier collateral, his own gracious self, which the contract can then hold him to in the morning.

### Q: What does Juliet's "My bounty is as boundless as the sea" claim that neither friend's theory claims?

The speech at 2.2.133 to 135 claims that love increases by being given away, an economics neither friend's theory can state. Mercutio's appetite runs on depletion: wanting is a pressure that satisfaction discharges, after which the object is spent and the wit moves on. Romeo's worship runs on distance: the feeling keeps its value because the obstacle keeps the supply short, as Rosaline's refusal had kept him sighing for a season. Juliet's sea abolishes both ledgers. The more she gives, the more she has, both being infinite, and the claim is made in a physical image, tidal and material, so that the third position cannot be mistaken for the ideal wearing a new dress. It is the body's element, the sea, carrying the ideal's infinity, and each friend's doctrine breaks on one half of it.

### Q: Why does Juliet make marriage the condition of love's satisfaction in Act 2 Scene 2?

Juliet makes marriage the condition because her tenderness is frank in both directions at once: the wanting is admitted without disguise, and the terms are stated without apology. At 2.2.143 to 144 she directs that if his bent of love is honourable, his purpose marriage, word must come tomorrow, and the direction answers each friend in turn. Against Mercutio, it insists appetite can sign a contract and mean it, that the body's desire is not falsified by being bound. Against Romeo, it insists worship must submit to verification by messenger, in daylight, in words that can be checked. The condition is not prudery and not romance. It is the third theory's constitution, drafted by a thirteen year old on a balcony in a single sentence.

### Q: What happens to Romeo's idealism in the hour after Mercutio dies in Act 3 Scene 1?

Romeo's idealism collapses into its opposite and is carried offstage in disgrace, by its holder's own verdict. Surveying the duel's wreckage, he blames the worship itself: Juliet's beauty has made him effeminate and softened valour's steel at 3.1.113 to 115, as though tenderness were a solvent that left him unable to act while his friend bled. The correction he applies, fire-eyed fury as his conduct at 3.1.123, abandons the theory wholesale rather than repairing it, and Tybalt is dead within the minute. The sequence is the ideal's refutation from inside. A theory of love that can only be saved by being renounced at the first emergency was a fair weather creed, and Romeo, to his credit as a witness against himself, says so in terms.

### Q: Why is Mercutio's dying speech about houses and plague rather than about love?

Mercutio's dying speech is about houses and plague because the event that kills him belongs to a system his theory never described. He has been wounded under Romeo's arm in a quarrel of honour between families, a machinery of names, challenges, and precedence in which appetite plays no part, and the mind that explained all conduct by the body reaches at the end for the vocabulary that fits the facts: the curse at 3.1.99 on both houses, repeated as the wound declares itself mortal. The omission is the verdict on the doctrine. Love's great reductionist dies talking about the feud, and the talk is magnificent, which only sharpens the point: the theory's finest speaker, given death to explain, explains it in somebody else's terms.

### Q: How does Joseph Porter's account of Mercutio's bawdy change the terms of the debate?

Porter's account changes the debate from a contest of doctrines into a drama of defense, because his Mercutio jokes compulsively and from need. In Porter's reading, the bawdy is driven rather than decorative: Mercutio anatomizes Rosaline, harps on Romeo's desire, and keeps converting his friend's feeling into farce because the feeling itself is the pressure his wit exists to manage. Once that account is admitted, Romeo's side of the argument gains evidence it never states. The idealist does not have to refute the doctrine of appetite if the doctrine's own escalations testify that appetite is not what the speeches are about. Porter does not end the debate, but he moves its centre of gravity from what Mercutio says to why he cannot stop saying it.

### Q: How does Harry Levin's contrast of high and low styles map onto the friends' theories of love?

Levin's contrast maps onto the debate almost exactly, which is both its strength and its limit. In Levin's account of the play's design, the lovers' high, ceremonious verse and the surrounding world's low, informal prose and bawdy form a patterned antithesis, and the friends slot into the pattern without remainder: Romeo carries the high style's theory of love as worship, Mercutio carries the low style's theory of love as appetite, and the play's movement is the high style's world being invaded, mocked, and finally abandoned by the low. The mapping explains the speeches' placement with real precision. What it cannot explain is their escalation, the way Mercutio's low style strains and coarsens as Romeo's happiness becomes real, and that residue is where Porter's psychology takes over from Levin's architecture.

### Q: Which side of the debate does Juliet's "Gallop apace" speech in Act 3 Scene 2 support?

The speech supports neither side, because it is the third position performing itself at full strength. Juliet summons night and her husband in one breath at 3.2.1 and throughout the soliloquy, with a bodily frankness that outdoes Mercutio's bawdy in directness while joking not at all, and a ceremonial splendour that outdoes Romeo's worship while sighing not at all. The wanting is physical, impatient, and entirely hers; the frame around it is a wedding night awaited inside a marriage already made, the contract of the orchard scene arriving at its appointed hour. Filing the speech under appetite ignores its vows, and filing it under worship ignores its hunger. The soliloquy is the strongest single exhibit for the article's verdict that the play built a third term and gave it the best poetry.

### Q: What has happened to Romeo's theory of love by his final speech in Act 5 Scene 3?

Romeo's theory has been educated out of its Petrarchan phase and into Juliet's grammar, while keeping its ceremonial voice. The worshipper who once sighed at an absence now stands in the vault calling it a feasting presence full of light at 5.3.85 to 86, and the light imagery is the old idealism intact, but everything underneath it has changed hands. He acts on evidence rather than omens, addresses the dead as a party to an agreement rather than an altar, and keeps, to the hour, an appointment made under a contract he did not draft. Rosaline's devotee performed love as a monologue about his own condition. The man in the tomb performs it as the execution of terms, and the distance between the two performances is the measure of what the third position taught him.

### Q: How should an essay argue the play's verdict between Mercutio and Romeo on love?

An essay should argue that the play stages the debate in order to refuse both parties and award the question, on the evidence of what functions, to Juliet. The strongest structure opens with the refusal: Mercutio's appetite doctrine is refuted by its escalation under pressure and by a death it has no terms for, while Romeo's worship is refuted by its collapse in Act 3 Scene 1 and by its own holder's verdict against it. The essay should then build the positive case from the orchard scene, where Juliet's terms, sea image, and marriage condition construct a position that survives every test the friends' theories fail, and close with the tragedy's limit on the award: the third position is vindicated, not protected, since plague and the feud kill what incoherence could not. Essays that crown either friend mistake a voice in the argument for the argument's judge.
