Daisy Buchanan tells a story about the moment her daughter was born. She woke from the ether, asked whether it was a girl, and turned her head away and wept, and then she says she was glad, because she hopes the child will be a fool, since "that's the best thing a girl can be in this world, a beautiful little fool." The line is quoted constantly as evidence of the novel's view of women, and it is almost always read as a statement about limitation. Gender theory and The Great Gatsby meet at the word she does not use, which is nature. Daisy is not describing what a girl is. She is describing what a girl is best advised to become, and the difference between those two sentences is the entire lens.

The method's claim is that femininity and masculinity in this novel are not properties the characters possess but roles they execute, with varying skill, under conditions they did not set. Daisy's helplessness is a performance she is good at. Tom's dominance is a performance he is losing his grip on. Jordan's coolness is a performance the era had only recently made available. Once the reading shifts from what these people are to what they are doing, a novel that seems to be about a man's obsession turns into a novel about four or five people executing scripts and paying for the execution.

## What Gender Theory and The Great Gatsby Bring Into View

The claim worth carrying is that gender in this book is performance rather than nature, and the novel supplies unusual evidence for it because it keeps showing the performances being adjusted.

Consider how much of the text is devoted to the mechanics. Daisy modulates her voice and Nick tells us that men who cared for her found it difficult to forget. Myrtle changes her dress three times in a single afternoon and Nick reports that with the change of costume her whole manner altered. Tom objects to a word his wife applies to his body. Jordan holds her shoulders back like a cadet. Gatsby assembles a manner out of Oxford, a war record, and an address he repeats until it grates. None of this is incidental description. It is a novel obsessively interested in the maintenance of appearances, and gender theory supplies the vocabulary for reading maintenance as constitutive rather than decorative.

Call the resulting artifact the Gender Performance Table, laid out below: each concept from the theoretical toolkit paired with the detail in the novel that demonstrates it. The table's purpose is to get a student from the abstraction to the page without an intervening leap, because the most common failure in essays on this topic is a paragraph of theory followed by a paragraph of plot with nothing joining them.

## Gender Theory in Plain Terms

The tradition has several strands and a student should be able to distinguish them, because they answer different questions and an essay that blends them produces mush.

The foundational move belongs to Simone de Beauvoir, writing in 1949, who argued that womanhood is not a biological given but something a person is made into through a social process that begins at birth and never stops. Her formulation, that one is not born but rather becomes a woman, separated the biological fact from the social role and made the second available for analysis. Everything after her is a refinement of that separation.

Gayle Rubin, in an influential 1975 essay, gave the separation a name and a mechanism. She described what she called the sex and gender system: the set of arrangements by which a society converts biological raw material into social roles, and she argued that kinship and marriage function as systems for the exchange of women between men. That framework is directly applicable to a novel whose central conflict is two men disputing which of them a woman belongs to.

Judith Butler, writing in 1990, pushed the argument further and produced the term most students reach for. Butler's claim was that gender is performative: it does not express a prior inner essence but is constituted by the repeated, stylized acts through which it is performed. On this account there is no femininity behind Daisy's behavior that the behavior expresses. The behavior, repeated under social pressure until it appears natural, is the femininity, and the appearance of naturalness is an effect the repetition produces.

Raewyn Connell, in a study published in 1995, supplied the corresponding account of men. Connell argued that masculinity is not one thing but a field of competing versions arranged in a hierarchy, with a dominant or hegemonic form that most men measure themselves against and few embody, and subordinated forms below it. The novel's men are a demonstration set for this argument, and reading Tom, Gatsby, and Wilson as three positions in a ranking is more productive than reading them as three personalities.

One more figure belongs here because she is contemporary with the novel. Joan Riviere, in a paper published in 1929, described women who performed an exaggerated femininity as a kind of protective covering, adopting the appearance of harmlessness in order to deflect the retaliation their competence might otherwise attract. Riviere called this womanliness as masquerade, and she was writing four years after the novel appeared, about the same social world.

### What does gender theory mean by performance?

Gender theory holds that femininity and masculinity are constituted by repeated social acts rather than expressing an inner essence. On this account there is no womanhood behind a woman's behavior that the behavior reveals. The behavior, repeated under pressure until it appears natural, is the gender, and the naturalness is the repetition's effect.

The historical setting matters as much as the theory. The novel is set in 1922 and published in 1925, which places it in the middle of the sharpest renegotiation of American gender norms in a century. The Nineteenth Amendment had been ratified in 1920. Women had entered wartime employment and in many cases stayed. The figure the period called the flapper had made short hair, shorter skirts, public smoking, and unchaperoned socializing into visible markers of a new script. Fitzgerald was among the writers who defined the type for the public and then spent years complicating it. A reader who treats the novel's gender arrangements as timeless has missed that they were being contested on the page as they were being contested in the streets.

## The Key Questions the Lens Asks

Four questions do the analytical work.

The first asks what the script is. Every performance implies a set of expectations it is measured against, and identifying the script for a given character in a given scene is the first move. The script for a young married woman of Daisy's class in 1922 is not the same as the script for a professional golfer, and the difference is where the analysis lives.

The second asks where the seams show. A performance that succeeded completely would be invisible, so the analytically useful moments are the ones where effort becomes perceptible: a costume change, a word objected to, a laugh that arrives at the wrong volume, a manner that is applied too evenly.

The third asks who enforces. Norms do not maintain themselves. Somebody polices them, and the policing leaves textual traces in the form of corrections, mockery, physical violence, and social exclusion.

The fourth asks what the performance costs. This is the question that separates a strong essay from a moralizing one, because it applies to the beneficiaries as well as the victims. Tom's position gives him almost everything he wants and requires him to be a certain kind of man continuously, and the novel shows the requirement wearing him down.

## The Lens Applied: Four Passages

### Daisy in chapter one

The beautiful little fool speech is the most quoted gender passage in the novel and the most frequently flattened. Read closely, it contains three distinct moves.

First, Daisy reports a physical fact and an emotional one: she woke from the ether, asked the nurse, learned it was a girl, and "turned my head away and wept." Second, she converts the weeping into a position: "I'm glad it's a girl. And I hope she'll be a fool." Third, she supplies the reasoning, that a fool is "the best thing a girl can be in this world."

The lens notes what kind of sentence the third one is. It is advice about strategy, not a description of essence. Daisy is not saying girls are foolish; she is saying that within the arrangement she knows, foolishness is the most survivable available option. That is an account of a script and its incentives, delivered by someone who has been performing it successfully for years and understands the terms.

The delivery matters as much as the content. Nick records that she said all this in a way that made him feel the insincerity of what she had said, and that her smirk asserted membership in a rather distinguished secret society to which she and Tom belonged. The performance is visible to the narrator even as it works on him. Two paragraphs earlier he has described her voice as the kind men who cared for her found difficult to forget, and later Gatsby delivers the novel's most economical account of the whole apparatus: "Her voice is full of money."

That line is the single best piece of evidence for the constructionist reading. A voice is a body, and Gatsby hears a social position inside it. The femininity Daisy performs is not separable from the class that trained it, which is why gender theory applied to this novel keeps colliding with class and should be allowed to.

### Jordan in chapter one

Nick's introduction of Jordan Baker is a physical description that reads as a description of a script in transition. She is "a slender, small-breasted girl, with an erect carriage," and she accentuates the carriage by "throwing her body backward at the shoulders like a young cadet." Later, in the chapter four flashback, Nick describes her incurable dishonesty and traces it to an unwillingness to be at a disadvantage, connecting it to the demands of "her hard, jaunty body."

Three observations follow. The comparison Nick reaches for is military and masculine, and it is offered admiringly rather than as criticism. The physical vocabulary throughout is of hardness, uprightness, and control, which are the terms the period reserved for men. And Jordan's occupation, professional golf, places her in public competition, which the era had only recently permitted.

The lens reads Jordan as evidence that scripts change, and that change is registered on bodies before it is registered in arguments. Nobody in the novel debates women's roles. Jordan simply stands differently, and the standing is legible to Nick as a departure.

The judgment attached to her is the analytically interesting part. Nick declares "She was incurably dishonest" and then immediately excuses it: "Dishonesty in a woman is a thing you never blame deeply." That sentence is a norm made audible. Nick is applying a different standard to a woman, announcing that he is doing so, and treating the double standard as a settled matter requiring no defense. An essay that quotes Jordan's dishonesty without quoting Nick's exemption has taken the narrator's framing for a fact.

### Tom in chapter one

Tom Buchanan is introduced through his body more thoroughly than any other character. Nick describes a hard mouth, a supercilious manner, arrogant eyes, an enormous power in the shoulders, and concludes that "It was a body capable of enormous leverage" and calls it "a cruel body."

The lens reads this as the physical form of a masculine script, presented as an inventory. What makes the scene analytically rich is what happens a few pages later. Daisy, showing a bruised knuckle, blames Tom and calls him "a brute of a man, a great, big, hulking physical specimen," and Tom objects: "I hate that word 'hulking,'" he says, even in joking. Daisy repeats it.

The exchange is short and it does more work than most of the novel's speeches. Tom's masculinity is his primary asset, and a single adjective threatens it, because hulking converts strength into clumsiness and moves him from the top of the hierarchy toward the bottom. He is not offended by the accusation of brutality. He is offended by the word that makes the brutality inelegant. The lens calls this norm-policing running in reverse: the person whose position the script protects is objecting to a description that would reposition him.

The scene also establishes the pairing that runs through the whole novel. Daisy's power is verbal and social and operates by naming. Tom's is physical and operates by force. Neither is presented as natural, and both are shown being deployed within thirty pages of the opening.

### Myrtle in chapter two

The apartment sequence is the novel's most explicit demonstration of gender as costume, and it is unusually literal.

Myrtle changes clothes twice in the chapter. When Nick first sees her at the garage she wears a spotted dress of dark blue crepe de chine and carries "an immediately perceptible vitality about her as if the nerves of her body were continually smouldering." At the apartment she has changed into an elaborate afternoon dress of cream-colored chiffon, and Nick records the effect precisely: "with the influence of the dress her personality had also undergone a change." He continues that "The intense vitality that had been so remarkable in the garage was converted into impressive hauteur."

The lens could not ask for a cleaner text. A change of costume produces a change of personality, and the narrator says so in those words. Myrtle is performing a different class and a different femininity simultaneously, and the two are inseparable: the hauteur is a class marker delivered through a gendered vocabulary of gesture, voice, and laughter.

The sequence ends in enforcement. Myrtle begins chanting Daisy's name, asserting a right to speak it, and Tom breaks her nose with a short deft movement of his open hand. The escalation from correcting a word, in Daisy's case, to breaking a nose, in Myrtle's, maps the enforcement mechanism onto the class hierarchy exactly. The same norm is policed in both scenes; the instrument differs according to the status of the woman challenging it.

### How do Daisy and Tom perform gender?

Daisy performs a scripted helplessness through voice, gesture, and managed charm, which Nick registers as insincere even while it works on him. Tom performs dominance through a body the novel inventories at length and defends verbally when a single adjective threatens it. Both are shown adjusting the performance under pressure.

## Womanliness as Masquerade: Daisy Read Closely

Joan Riviere's 1929 argument deserves more attention in Gatsby criticism than it receives, because it is contemporary with the novel and it explains something the standard readings struggle with.

Riviere described women whose exaggerated femininity functioned as protective covering: having exercised a competence coded as masculine, they would perform harmlessness and charm in order to deflect the retaliation the competence might attract. The mask was not insincerity in any simple sense. It was a strategy operating below the level of deliberate choice.

Apply that to Daisy and the reading sharpens considerably. The standard verdicts on her are that she is shallow, or that she is a victim, and both flatten a character the novel takes some care with. The masquerade reading says something more precise: Daisy performs helplessness most intensely at the moments of greatest exposure, and the performance is a defense.

Test it against the text. The beautiful little fool speech is delivered immediately after she has demonstrated considerable awareness of her situation, and Nick records the smirk that follows. Her charm operates at maximum in the reunion scene in chapter five, when she is at her most compromised. At the Plaza, under direct pressure, she moves from asking Tom for self-control to the sentence Gatsby needs and then to a retraction, and the retreat is executed through a performance of being unable to bear the scene. Nick's phrase for her voice earlier in the book, that it was full of a promise she was about to break, describes a technique rather than a temperament.

The reading also handles the ending better than the alternatives. Daisy kills a woman with a car, allows Gatsby to absorb the attribution, and withdraws with Tom. A moral reading calls this cowardice. The masquerade reading calls it the script working as designed: a woman who has spent her life performing incapacity has arranged a position in which someone else is always responsible, and the arrangement pays out at the moment it is expensive.

None of this exonerates her, and an essay should say so. The point is that the lens produces a description of a mechanism rather than a verdict on a personality, and the description is more defensible. The feminist reading of the novel, which centers the constraints on women and the question of Daisy's culpability, is a related but distinct project and belongs to the [feminist reading of The Great Gatsby](/2027/08/09/feminist-reading-great-gatsby/).

## Hegemonic Masculinity: Three Men as One Hierarchy

Connell's argument that masculinity is a ranked field rather than a single quality turns the novel's men into a demonstration set.

At the top sits Tom, and the novel makes his position physical, inherited, and effortless. He has the body, the money, the polo ponies, the wife, the mistress, and the assumption that all of it is his by right. The lens notes that his masculinity is unearned in exactly the way Gatsby's is earned, which is why Gatsby's is visible as performance and Tom's is not.

Gatsby occupies the position of a challenger executing the script correctly and being rejected anyway. He has assembled every component: the fortune, the house, the war record, the education claim, the clothes, the address. He is described in the language of dedication rather than of nature, and Nick's phrase that "he had committed himself to the following of a grail" makes the project sound like a vocation. The problem is that the components are visibly assembled, and a masculinity that shows its assembly reads as counterfeit to the people at the top.

Wilson occupies the subordinated position, and the novel renders it as depletion. He is described as spiritless, anemic, faintly handsome, a man who runs down. His wife's contempt for him is the novel's most direct statement of the ranking: she tells Nick that she thought Wilson knew something about breeding and found he was not fit to lick her shoe. When Tom takes his wife, Wilson has no available response within the script, and his eventual response is to leave the script entirely.

The three positions produce the novel's plot. Gatsby challenges Tom for a woman, is defeated by criteria the challenge cannot address, and is killed by the man at the bottom of the same hierarchy, acting on information the man at the top supplied. Read as three personalities the sequence is a series of accidents. Read as a hierarchy the sequence is close to inevitable. The dedicated study of these positions belongs to the treatment of [masculinity studies and The Great Gatsby](/2027/11/29/masculinity-studies-gatsby/), which owns the facet; this article owns the constructionist method that generates it.

There is a fourth position worth noting, because students miss it. Nick is a man who does not compete. He has no fortune, no body worth describing, no mistress he keeps, and he ends the novel having withdrawn from a relationship rather than fought for one. The novel's masculine hierarchy has a spectator, and the spectator is telling the story.

## Jordan and the Era's New Scripts

Jordan Baker is the character most damaged by the standard readings, which tend to treat her as a minor love interest or as a device for delivering the Louisville backstory. The lens finds her the novel's clearest evidence that gender is historical.

Everything about her marks a script that had not existed a generation earlier. She has a career in public competition. She travels alone, stays in houses without chaperones, and drives. She smokes, drinks at parties, and conducts a relationship with Nick on terms she sets and ends. Her physical presentation borrows from a masculine vocabulary, and Nick's description reaches for a cadet rather than for any feminine comparison.

The novel's treatment of her is ambivalent in a way that is analytically useful. Nick admires the presentation and distrusts the person, and the distrust attaches to qualities that would be unremarkable in a man. Jordan is competitive, which the novel codes as hardness. She is careless, which the novel codes as dishonesty. She is unwilling to be at a disadvantage, which Nick treats as a moral defect rather than as a professional requirement. The double standard is not concealed; Nick states it and dismisses it in the same breath.

Their exchange about driving is the sharpest scene in the book for this reading. Nick tells her, "You're a rotten driver," and adds that she ought to be more careful or should not drive at all. Jordan's reply is that she is careful, then that "It takes two to make an accident," then that she relies on other people to stay out of her way. Nick asks what happens if she meets someone as careless as herself, and she says she hopes she never will, adding, "I hate careless people. That's why I like you."

Read as characterization the exchange establishes that Jordan is reckless. Read through the lens it establishes something better: Jordan has articulated the logic of a protected position out loud, and she has done it in the one arena, driving, where the era had newly permitted women to occupy it. Her carelessness is not a personal flaw the novel has invented for her. It is the same carelessness Nick will later attribute to Tom and Daisy, appearing in a woman who has recently acquired access to the machine that makes it possible.

## Nick: The Narrator's Own Position

Any application of the lens has to include the narrator, and essays that skip this step consistently score lower than essays that do not.

Nick's gender position is unusual and the novel foregrounds it. He does not compete for women, does not display his body, and describes other men's bodies with an attention the book does not extend to its women's bodies in the same register. He tells us he has "been drunk just twice in my life," positioning himself against the era's masculine display. He claims, in one of the novel's most examined sentences, "I am one of the few honest people that I have ever known," and prefaces it with the observation that "Everyone suspects himself of at least one of the cardinal virtues."

The lens makes three claims about him without needing to settle the larger question. First, Nick is the novel's principal observer of other people's gender performances, which places him structurally outside the competition he narrates. Second, he applies gendered standards openly, exempting a woman from a charge of dishonesty on the grounds of her sex, and an essay should quote that exemption rather than treat his judgments as neutral. Third, his own performance is one of restraint and withholding, which the period coded as a masculine virtue and which the novel presents as at least partly a pose, since he ends up having judged nearly everyone he claimed to reserve judgment about.

The question of whether Nick is queer-coded is a separate and substantial one that other articles in this series address directly, and gender theory does not require an answer to it. What the lens does require is that the narrator's position be treated as a position rather than as a window. The performance of gender in this novel includes the performance of describing other people's.

### Is the narrator outside the novel's gender system?

No. Nick observes other people's performances while conducting one of his own, built from restraint, sobriety, and withheld judgment, all of which the period coded as masculine virtues. He also applies openly gendered standards, most visibly when he exempts Jordan from blame for dishonesty on the grounds of her sex.

## Myrtle: Class and Gender Performed Together

Myrtle Wilson repays the lens more than any character except Daisy, because her performances are visible in a way the wealthier characters' are not.

The visibility is a function of position. Daisy's femininity was installed over eighteen years in Louisville by an apparatus of chaperonage, presentation, and courtship, and by 1922 it operates without apparent effort. Myrtle is attempting the same performance in an afternoon, with purchased materials, in a rented apartment, in front of a sister and neighbors who are also performing. The seams are everywhere, and the novel points at them.

What she buys is instructive. A dog, from a man on the street, chosen with a question about whether it is a boy. Magazines and cold cream and perfume. A gossip weekly. Later, at the apartment, an elaborate afternoon dress. The list is a shopping list for a role, and Nick presents it as such. The lens notes that the components are commodities, which connects the gender argument to the novel's account of consumption and gives an essay a natural bridge between the two.

The performance also requires a script for the husband, and Myrtle supplies it by contempt. Her account of discovering that Wilson had borrowed the suit he married her in, and her verdict that he was not fit to lick her shoe, are statements about a man failing a masculine standard. She is enforcing the hierarchy from below, which shows that norm-policing is not a privilege of the powerful.

Her death completes the reading. She runs into the road toward a car she believes contains Tom, which is the last act of a performance aimed at a man, and she is killed by a woman driving. The novel's two women collide, one performing helplessness and one performing aspiration, and the one with the protected position survives. An essay that traces Myrtle's performance from the garage through the apartment to the road has an argument with a shape, which is more than most essays on this character manage.

## Gender Theory and Feminist Criticism: The Division of Labor

Students conflate these constantly and the conflation costs marks, so the distinction is worth stating plainly.

Feminist criticism of this novel centers on women: how they are constrained, how they are represented, whether the text endorses or exposes their treatment, and what verdict to reach about Daisy's culpability and Fitzgerald's sympathies. It is an evaluative project with a political commitment, and it has produced most of the important work on the novel's women.

Gender theory is broader and, in one specific sense, cooler. It analyzes the construction of all gender positions, including masculine ones, and it treats the question of whether a text endorses an arrangement as secondary to the question of how the arrangement is built. A gender-theory essay on this novel will spend as much time on Tom as on Daisy, because Tom's masculinity is as constructed as her femininity and the novel gives more evidence about its maintenance.

The practical test for a student is what your thesis is about. If it argues that the novel constrains, silences, or punishes its women, you are writing feminist criticism, and the fuller treatment of that approach sits in the [feminist reading of The Great Gatsby](/2027/08/09/feminist-reading-great-gatsby/). If it argues that the novel presents femininity and masculinity as roles produced by a specific social arrangement, you are writing gender theory. The thematic content that both methods analyze, as distinct from either method, is surveyed in the treatment of [gender roles in The Great Gatsby](/2024/08/26/gender-roles-great-gatsby/).

The two are compatible and the strongest essays are aware of both. What weakens an essay is beginning with a constructionist thesis and drifting into a verdict on Fitzgerald's attitudes, because the drift leaves the original argument unproved and substitutes a different one that has not been set up.

## The Exchange of Daisy: Reading the Plaza Scene

Gayle Rubin's account of kinship as a system for circulating women between men produces the sharpest available reading of the novel's central confrontation, and it is worth working out in detail because most treatments of the Plaza scene concentrate on who says what.

Count the participants. Five people are in the room. The dispute concerns which of two men Daisy loves and has loved. The question is put to her repeatedly, and her answers are treated by both men as evidence in a case they are conducting between themselves. Gatsby demands she say she never loved Tom, which is a demand that she revise the record so that his claim becomes prior. Tom responds by producing evidence about Gatsby's origins, which is an argument about his standing to make a claim at all.

What the lens notices is the grammar. Tom's decisive line, that the latest fashion is "to sit back and let Mr. Nobody from Nowhere make love to your wife," addresses the room rather than Daisy and describes her in the possessive. Daisy's contribution is a plea for "a little self-control" and then a collapse into an inability to answer. She says at one point that she loved both, and neither man accepts the answer, because a system that circulates women between men has no place for a woman who declines to be assigned.

The reading gains force from what happens next. Tom, having won, sends Daisy home in Gatsby's car with Gatsby, which is a gesture of contempt that only makes sense inside the system: he is demonstrating that the assignment is so secure it can survive the appearance of a challenge. Nick records the calculation without naming it, and the drive that follows kills Myrtle.

Two cautions belong with this reading. The first is that Rubin's framework describes a structure, not a conscious plan, and an essay that presents Tom as deliberately applying a kinship theory has overreached. The second is that Daisy is not without agency in the scene; she declines to give Gatsby the sentence he needs, and the declining is a decision. The framework explains the terms of the contest, not the outcome of every move within it.

## The Louisville Apparatus: Where Daisy's Femininity Was Installed

Jordan's account of the Louisville years, delivered to Nick in chapter four, is the novel's fullest description of a gender role being manufactured, and most readings treat it as backstory for the romance rather than as evidence.

The apparatus it describes is elaborate. Daisy at eighteen is the most sought-after young woman in the city. She has a white roadster and a telephone that rings continuously with officers from the nearby army camp asking for the privilege of monopolizing her for an evening. Young men are received, evaluated, and rotated. When she is discovered attempting to travel to New York to say goodbye to a soldier shipping overseas, her family stops her, and she does not speak to them for several weeks afterward. The following June she marries a man from Chicago whose wedding party fills four private cars, and who gives her a string of pearls valued at a sum Jordan reports as though it settled something.

Read as romance the sequence explains why Gatsby lost. Read through the lens it explains something more useful: how a femininity of the kind Daisy performs in 1922 gets built. The chaperonage, the rotation of suitors, the family intervention, and the display around the wedding are all mechanisms for producing a woman whose desirability is public, whose choices are supervised, and whose eventual assignment is a matter of family judgment rather than personal preference. Nothing in the account is presented as unusual. It is simply how a young woman of that class in that city was raised.

Two moments in the sequence show the machinery running against resistance. The first is the family stopping her from traveling, which is enforcement in its plainest form. The second is the night before the wedding, when Daisy has been drinking, holds a letter, and tells Jordan to inform everyone she has changed her mind. What follows is the novel's most physically detailed description of a woman being returned to compliance: Jordan and a maid get her into a cold bath, hold her there, retrieve the letter, which comes apart in the water, and dress her. The bridesmaid and the servant, both women, perform the enforcement. By half past five the dress is on and the wedding proceeds, and Jordan reports that Daisy showed no visible disturbance afterward.

The lens draws two conclusions. The first is that the role is enforced laterally as well as from above; the women closest to Daisy administer it, using care rather than force, which makes it harder to resist and harder to name. The second is that the compliance is complete enough that it becomes invisible within hours. The performance resumes, and by the time the reader meets Daisy on a couch in East Egg four years later, the effort has vanished into manner.

This is what Butler's account of repetition looks like when a novelist dramatizes it. There is no essential Daisy behind the performance that the cold bath suppressed. There is a young woman with a letter, an apparatus that returns her to the script, and the resumption of the script until it stops looking like one. Her later wish that her daughter grow up a beautiful little fool is the same woman naming the terms of the arrangement she was returned to, and the wish is for the incuriosity that makes the arrangement bearable.

### Why does the Louisville flashback matter to a gender reading?

It shows the role being manufactured rather than merely occupied. The chaperonage, the rotation of officers, the family intervention, and the cold bath before the wedding are all mechanisms returning a young woman to a script. The enforcement is administered by other women, using care rather than force, which is what makes it hard to name.

## Gender in the Party Scenes: Display as Labor

The parties are read for excess and for Gatsby's isolation. The lens finds them a working demonstration of gendered display as an activity requiring effort.

Watch what the women at Gatsby's first party do. They arrive in groups, are absorbed into the crowd, and perform a set of behaviors the narration describes as practiced: heads placed on men's shoulders in a manner Nick registers as choreographed, laughter released in bursts, swooning backward into arms that are expected to be there. Nick's tone throughout is of a man watching a routine executed by people who know it. The most memorable figure of the evening is a woman in a yellow dress who sings and weeps at once, and whose tears carry her mascara down her face in dark channels, at which point someone remarks that she has had a quarrel with a man who claims to be her husband. The performance has broken down in public and the crowd's response is amusement.

The lens reads that breakdown as the party's most informative moment. A display that continues smoothly tells you nothing; a display that fails in front of a hundred people shows both what was being maintained and what maintaining it cost. The woman's makeup is the material evidence, and Fitzgerald puts it on her face in a way that makes the artifice run down her face.

The men's display is different and equally worked. Gatsby stands apart, does not drink, watches, and delivers the famous smile in individual encounters, which is a performance of hospitality conducted from a distance. His manner is one of controlled availability, and it is the opposite of the male behavior around him. Tom, when he attends the party in chapter six, spends the evening making his contempt visible, which is also a performance, and Daisy's dislike of the whole occasion requires no explanation because her standing permits an unexplained verdict.

There is a further point about labor. The parties are staffed. Caterers, an orchestra, servants, and a corps of workers repairing the previous night's damage appear in the descriptions as functions rather than as people. Gendered display in this novel is expensive, and the expense is paid partly in money and partly in the invisible work of a population the narration does not individuate. An essay that notices this has connected the gender argument to the novel's economics without forcing the link.

## What the Ending Does to the Performances

The last two chapters dismantle the performances one by one, and the pattern of what survives is the reading's final piece of evidence.

Myrtle's ends first and most violently. She runs into the road toward a car she believes carries Tom, which is the last act of a performance directed at a man, and she is killed by a woman driving. The two women of the novel collide, one performing aspiration and one performing helplessness, and the one with the protected position survives. Nick's description of the aftermath emphasizes the body and the dress, which returns the reader to the costume the chapter two sequence made so much of.

Gatsby's ends next, and it ends in a costume. He is in the pool on a mattress, waiting for a telephone call that does not come, having sent the servants away. The elaborate apparatus of the performance, the house, the parties, the staff, the shirts, has already been dismantled by his own instruction. What remains is a man alone in water, and Nick's speculation about what he might have thought in his last moments imagines a world where the effort has stopped meaning anything.

Wilson's ends in departure from the script entirely. The novel gives him no masculine move that would restore his position, so he takes the only action available outside it, and the action removes him from the system.

Tom's performance survives, and the survival is the point. Nick meets him on Fifth Avenue, learns that he told Wilson whose car it was, and finds him entirely convinced of his own justification. He is described in the same physical terms as at the opening, walking ahead with his hands out as if to fight off interference, and Nick's final assessment is that he and Daisy were careless people who smashed things and then retreated back into their money or their vast carelessness. The masculine position at the top of the hierarchy is intact, undamaged, and unexamined.

Daisy's survives too, and it survives by doing exactly what it was built to do. A woman who has spent her life performing incapacity has arranged a life in which someone else is responsible, and at the moment the arrangement is most expensive it pays out. She kills a woman, allows Gatsby to absorb the attribution, and leaves. The lens does not call this innocence and does not need to call it wickedness. It calls it the script executing correctly under load, which is a colder and more accurate description than either verdict.

Jordan's ending is the most interesting and the least discussed. She confronts Nick, tells him she was thrown over on the telephone, and reports that she is engaged to another man, which may or may not be true. She also throws his own charge back at him, telling him she had thought he was an honest, straightforward person and that it had been a secret pride of his. The woman Nick had exempted from moral seriousness on account of her sex delivers the novel's most direct challenge to his self-account, and he concedes that he is too old to lie to himself and call it honor. The new script gets the last word in that exchange, and the narrator does not win it.

## Limits and the Strongest Objection

The strongest objection is that the lens explains too much and therefore explains nothing. If every behavior is a performance, the concept has no contrast class, and an essay that identifies performance everywhere has produced a label rather than an argument. Butler's framework is genuinely vulnerable to this in careless hands.

The answer is to insist on the seams. A useful application does not claim that Daisy is performing; it claims that the novel repeatedly shows the performance being adjusted, and it points to the adjustments. The costume change that alters Myrtle's personality, Tom's objection to a single adjective, Daisy's smirk after the beautiful little fool speech, Jordan's shoulders thrown back: these are moments where the text itself makes maintenance visible. An essay built on those moments has evidence. An essay built on the general claim that gender is constructed has a premise.

A second objection is anachronism. Butler wrote in 1990 and Rubin in 1975, and applying their vocabulary to a 1925 novel imports categories Fitzgerald could not have used. The answer here is unusually strong. Riviere published on masquerade in 1929, four years after the novel, describing the same social world. The period was itself arguing about gender roles with considerable heat, and the novel registers the argument. The theoretical vocabulary is later; the phenomenon and the contemporary awareness of it are not.

A third objection is that the lens can flatten a novel of remarkable prose into a sociology of roles. The defense is that the best evidence for the reading is stylistic. Nick's sentence about Myrtle's personality changing with her dress is a prose effect and an analytical claim at once. The description of Jordan's cadet posture is a piece of imagery doing theoretical work. An application that quotes and analyzes at sentence level is not competing with the aesthetic reading; it is conducting one.

A fourth limit concerns Fitzgerald. The lens describes what the novel depicts and how it narrates. It does not settle what the author endorsed, and the evidence pulls both ways. Fitzgerald gives Daisy the beautiful little fool line, which is a piece of social analysis placed in a woman's mouth. He also filters the entire book through a narrator who exempts women from moral seriousness in a throwaway clause. An essay claiming the novel is a critique of gender norms is overreaching; so is one claiming it merely reproduces them. Describing the arrangement is the defensible move.

A fifth objection comes from readers who find the whole approach politically loaded. Gender theory has serious critics who dispute its account of the relationship between biology and social role, question whether performativity can bear the explanatory weight placed on it, and argue that the framework understates constraints that are not socially produced. Those arguments exist and a student who knows they exist will write more carefully. For a literary essay the practical consequence is modest: apply the method, produce the textual evidence, and let the reading stand on whether it explains the pages better than the alternatives.

## Building an Essay Around the Lens

Seven decisions determine whether the essay works.

Choose the seams over the general claim. The thesis should promise an analysis of specific moments where the novel shows a performance being maintained, not a general argument that gender is constructed. Examiners have read the general argument many times.

Give the men equal time. The single most common weakness in student essays on this topic is treating gender as a topic about women, which reproduces the assumption the lens exists to question. Tom's objection to the word hulking is worth as much analytical attention as Daisy's fool speech, and it is far less used.

Include the narrator. Nick's exemption of Jordan from blame for dishonesty is a norm stated aloud by the novel's most trusted voice, and quoting it demonstrates that you are reading the narration rather than through it.

Connect gender to class rather than separating them. Gatsby's remark that Daisy's voice is full of money is the novel's most compressed statement that the two categories are produced together, and Myrtle's costume change performs class and gender in one gesture. An essay that keeps them apart is doing more work than necessary and getting a thinner result.

Name your theorist once and then stop. One clearly explained concept, applied repeatedly, outperforms four names dropped in passing. If you use performativity, define it in a clause and then demonstrate it three times.

Anticipate the over-application objection inside the essay. One paragraph acknowledging that a framework which finds performance everywhere risks explaining nothing, followed by your account of why the seams provide genuine evidence, will disarm the strongest available counterargument.

Assemble the evidence with the text in front of you rather than from memory, since the relevant details are physical descriptions scattered across nine chapters and are easy to misremember. An eighth decision concerns tone. Essays on gender in this novel often adopt a prosecutorial register, treating the task as establishing that the text or its author is guilty of something. That register invites overstatement and is easy for an examiner to answer. The descriptive register is stronger and considerably harder to rebut: here is the script each character is executing, here are the sentences where the novel shows the execution being adjusted, here is who enforces it and by what means, and here is what it costs the people who perform it well. An argument built that way survives disagreement about politics, because every claim in it points at a page.

## The Verdict

Gender theory and The Great Gatsby produce a reading that is wider than the feminist account and more precise than a general discussion of roles. The novel does not present femininity and masculinity as facts about people. It presents them as techniques, and it keeps showing the techniques being executed, adjusted, corrected, and enforced.

The Gender Performance Table is the takeaway, and the evidence for it is unusually concrete. A dress changes and a personality changes with it, and the narrator says so in those words. A woman advises that the best thing her daughter can be is a beautiful fool, which is strategy rather than description. A man with every advantage in the novel objects to a single adjective because it would reposition him in a ranking he depends on. A young woman throws her shoulders back like a cadet and the narrator reaches for a military comparison because the era has not yet supplied a feminine one.

What the lens does not deliver is a verdict on Fitzgerald, and an essay that reaches for one will overstate whatever evidence it has. What it delivers instead is a way of reading the novel's surfaces, its costumes, postures, voices, and vocabularies, as the place where its social arrangements are actually built. Daisy's line about the beautiful little fool is the book's own summary of the method. She is not telling us what a girl is. She is telling us what a girl learns to do, and what it costs to learn it well.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is a gender-theory reading of The Great Gatsby?

It is a reading that treats femininity and masculinity in the novel as constructed roles the characters perform rather than as essences they possess. Instead of asking what Daisy is like or whether Tom is a brute, it asks what script each character is executing, where the effort of execution becomes visible, who enforces the script, and what the performance costs. Applied to Fitzgerald, the method produces an argument that the novel is unusually preoccupied with maintenance: costume changes that alter personality, a word objected to because it repositions a man in a hierarchy, a modulated voice that carries a social position inside it. The reading covers men as fully as women, which is the main thing separating it from feminist criticism, and it locates the roles historically in the renegotiation of American gender norms after 1920.

### Q: How does gender theory read gender as performed and constructed?

Through the claim, developed most influentially by Judith Butler in 1990, that gender is constituted by repeated stylized acts rather than expressing a prior inner essence. On this account there is no femininity behind Daisy's behavior that the behavior reveals; the behavior, repeated under social pressure until it appears natural, is the femininity, and the naturalness is an effect the repetition produces. The construction claim is older, going back to Simone de Beauvoir's argument in 1949 that womanhood is something a person is made into rather than born as. The novel supplies evidence for both because it keeps showing the acts being adjusted. Nick reports that Myrtle's personality changed with her dress, which is a constructionist claim stated by the narrator in plain language.

### Q: How do Daisy and Tom perform gender?

Daisy performs a scripted helplessness through voice, gesture, and managed charm. Nick describes her voice as the kind men who cared for her found difficult to forget, registers the insincerity of her fool speech, and notes the smirk that follows it, which means the performance is visible to him even while it works. Tom performs dominance through a body the novel inventories at length, describing enormous power in the shoulders and calling it a cruel body capable of enormous leverage. The proof that his is a performance rather than a nature is his objection to a single adjective: when Daisy calls him hulking he protests, because the word converts strength into clumsiness and moves him down a ranking. Both are shown adjusting under pressure, which is where the evidence lives.

### Q: How did the era script the characters' gender roles?

The novel is set in 1922 and published in 1925, placing it in the middle of a sharp renegotiation of American gender norms. The Nineteenth Amendment had been ratified in 1920, women had entered wartime employment and often stayed, and the figure the period called the flapper had made short hair, shorter skirts, public smoking, and unchaperoned socializing into visible markers of a new script. Fitzgerald helped define that type for the public and spent years complicating it. Jordan Baker embodies the new script, with a career in public competition, unchaperoned travel, and a physical presentation the narrator compares to a cadet. Daisy embodies the older one, installed over eighteen years in Louisville by an apparatus of chaperonage, presentation, and managed courtship that the chapter four flashback describes in detail.

### Q: How does gender theory differ from feminist criticism?

Feminist criticism of this novel centers on women: how they are constrained, how they are represented, and what verdict to reach about Daisy and about Fitzgerald's sympathies. It is evaluative and carries a political commitment. Gender theory is broader and treats the construction of all gender positions, including masculine ones, as its object, and it treats the question of whether a text endorses an arrangement as secondary to the question of how the arrangement is built. A gender-theory essay will spend as much time on Tom as on Daisy, because his masculinity is as constructed as her femininity and the novel supplies more evidence about its maintenance. The practical test is your thesis. If it argues the novel constrains its women, that is feminist criticism; if it argues gender is produced by a social arrangement, that is gender theory.

### Q: How does the lens denaturalize gender?

By locating the performance in specific acts, at specific moments, under specific pressures, so that what looked like a property of a person becomes a description of a technique. The most efficient demonstration in the novel is Myrtle's costume change, since Nick reports that with the influence of the dress her personality had also undergone a change and that the vitality remarkable in the garage was converted into hauteur. A quality that appeared to belong to her turns out to be attached to a garment. Once that pattern is visible it can be run against characters whose performances are less obvious. Daisy's charm becomes technique rather than temperament, Tom's dominance becomes maintenance rather than nature, and the naturalness of both is revealed as the effect of successful repetition.

### Q: What does Judith Butler mean by gender performativity?

Butler's argument, set out in a 1990 book, is that gender is performative in a specific technical sense. It is not a role played by an actor who exists prior to the role, and it is not a costume put on over a stable self. It is constituted by the repeated, stylized acts through which it is enacted, so that the appearance of a coherent inner gender is produced by the repetition rather than expressed by it. The distinction from ordinary theatrical performance matters for essays, because performativity does not imply deliberate pretense. Daisy is not consciously acting when she modulates her voice. The point is that there is no prior femininity the modulation reveals. Applying the concept well means demonstrating repetition and its effects rather than accusing a character of insincerity.

### Q: Is Jordan Baker gender-nonconforming?

She departs from the older script in ways the novel registers physically rather than argumentatively. Nick describes her as a slender, small-breasted girl with an erect carriage that she accentuates by throwing her body backward at the shoulders like a young cadet, and the comparison he reaches for is military and masculine. She has a career in public competition, travels and stays unchaperoned, drives, and conducts a relationship on terms she sets. The vocabulary attached to her throughout is of hardness and control, terms the period reserved for men. Whether that makes her nonconforming depends on which norm you measure against, since the flapper script was itself a norm by 1922. What is clear is that she embodies a position the previous generation had not made available, and that the novel notices it on her body first.

### Q: What does Daisy's beautiful little fool line say about gender scripting?

It says that the role is strategic rather than essential, which is the whole constructionist case in one sentence. Daisy reports waking from the ether, learning the baby was a girl, turning her head away and weeping, and then declaring she is glad and hopes the child will be a fool because that is the best thing a girl can be. The last clause is advice about survival within an arrangement, not a description of what girls are. She is naming the incentives of a script she has been executing successfully for years. Nick's framing sharpens it further, since he registers the insincerity of the delivery and the smirk that follows, which means the speech is itself a performance about performance. The line is the novel's own summary of the method.

### Q: How does Myrtle perform class and gender at once?

Inseparably, and the novel makes the fusion visible because her performance is recent and purchased. At the garage she wears dark blue crepe de chine and carries a vitality Nick describes as nerves continually smouldering. At the apartment she has changed into elaborate cream chiffon and, in Nick's words, her personality has changed with the dress, the garage vitality converted into hauteur. Hauteur is a class marker delivered through a gendered vocabulary of gesture, voice, and laughter. The shopping list around it is a list of props for a role: the dog bought on the street, the cold cream, the perfume, the gossip weekly. She also enforces the masculine hierarchy from below, telling Nick that Wilson borrowed the suit he married her in and was not fit to lick her shoe.

### Q: What is the difference between sex and gender in this reading?

The distinction separates biological classification from the social role built on top of it, and it is what makes the role analyzable. Simone de Beauvoir's 1949 formulation established the separation by arguing that womanhood is something a person becomes through a social process rather than something she is born as. Gayle Rubin named the machinery in 1975, describing a sex and gender system that converts biological raw material into social positions through kinship and marriage arrangements. Later theorists, Butler most prominently, questioned how cleanly the two can be separated, arguing that the biological category is itself framed by social meaning. For essay purposes the working version is sufficient: the novel's characters differ in role, expectation, and permitted behavior in ways that no biological fact accounts for.

### Q: How does gender theory read Tom's body?

As the physical form of a script presented as an inventory. Nick's introduction of Tom catalogues a hard mouth, a supercilious manner, arrogant eyes, and enormous power in the shoulders, concluding that it was a body capable of enormous leverage and calling it a cruel body. The description reads as an audit of masculine assets rather than as a portrait. What converts this from description to evidence is Tom's reaction when Daisy calls him a brute of a man, a great big hulking physical specimen. He objects to the word hulking even in joking, and she repeats it. He is not offended by the charge of brutality, which suits him; he is offended by a word that makes the strength clumsy and moves him down a hierarchy. A body that had to be defended verbally is not a natural fact.

### Q: Does the novel critique or reproduce gender norms?

It does both, and an essay claiming either alone will overstate its evidence. Fitzgerald gives Daisy the beautiful little fool line, which is a piece of social analysis placed in a woman's mouth, and he shows Tom's masculinity requiring constant and undignified defense. He also filters the entire book through a narrator who exempts a woman from moral seriousness in a throwaway clause, telling us that dishonesty in a woman is a thing you never blame deeply. The novel notices the arrangement and narrates from inside it. The defensible position for an essay is descriptive: here is how the arrangement works in this book, here are the moments the text makes it visible, and here is the evidence pulling each way on the question of endorsement.

### Q: How do I write a gender-theory essay about the novel?

Build the thesis on specific seams rather than on the general claim that gender is constructed, since examiners have seen the general claim many times. Promise an analysis of moments where the novel shows a performance being maintained: the costume change that alters Myrtle's personality, Tom's objection to a single adjective, Daisy's smirk after the fool speech, Jordan's cadet posture. Give the men equal time, because treating gender as a topic about women reproduces the assumption the lens exists to question. Include the narrator, quoting Nick's exemption of Jordan from blame. Connect gender to class rather than separating them, using Gatsby's remark that Daisy's voice is full of money. Name one theorist, define one concept in a clause, and demonstrate it three times rather than dropping four names.

### Q: What passages best show gender as construction?

Four carry most of the weight. Nick's report that with the influence of the dress Myrtle's personality had also undergone a change is the most explicit constructionist statement in the book, delivered by the narrator in plain language. Daisy's beautiful little fool speech in chapter one presents the feminine role as strategy rather than essence, and the smirk Nick records afterward makes the speech itself a performance. Tom's objection to the word hulking shows a masculine position being defended verbally within thirty pages of the opening. Jordan's introduction, with the erect carriage thrown backward at the shoulders like a young cadet, shows a new script registering on a body. Quote briefly from each and analyze the mechanism rather than accumulating examples.

### Q: Does gender theory apply to a 1925 text without anachronism?

Better than most theoretical frameworks do, and the reason is a matter of dates. Butler wrote in 1990 and Rubin in 1975, so their vocabulary is later than the novel. But Joan Riviere published her account of exaggerated femininity as a protective covering in 1929, four years after the book appeared and about the same social world, which shows the phenomenon was being analyzed by contemporaries. The period itself was arguing about gender roles with considerable heat, following the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920 and the emergence of the flapper as a public type Fitzgerald helped define. The theoretical vocabulary is an import; the phenomenon and the contemporary awareness of it are not. An essay that makes this point explicitly will disarm the anachronism objection in its strongest form.

### Q: Is Nick's gender position stable in the novel?

It is a position rather than a neutral vantage, and treating it as a window is the most common weakness in essays on this topic. Nick does not compete for women, does not display his body, and describes other men with an attention the novel does not extend elsewhere in the same register. He performs restraint, telling us he has been drunk just twice in his life, and withholding, claiming to reserve judgment while judging almost everyone. Both were coded as masculine virtues in the period, and both are at least partly a pose, since the reserved judgment does not survive the book. He also applies openly gendered standards, exempting Jordan from blame for dishonesty on the grounds of her sex. Quoting that exemption shows you are reading the narration rather than through it.

### Q: How does the Plaza scene look through a gender lens?

Like a dispute conducted about a woman rather than with her. Gayle Rubin's account of kinship systems that circulate women between men supplies the framework, and the scene's grammar supplies the evidence. Tom's decisive line refers to Daisy in the possessive while addressing the room, saying the latest fashion is to sit back and let Mr. Nobody from Nowhere make love to your wife. Gatsby demands she declare she never loved Tom, which is a demand that she revise the record so his claim becomes prior. Her own contribution is a plea for a little self-control and then a collapse into being unable to answer, and when she says she loved both, neither man accepts it, because the system has no place for a woman who declines assignment. Tom then sends her home in Gatsby's car, a gesture that only reads as contempt inside that logic.

### Q: What does the lens say about Gatsby's masculinity?

That it is the novel's clearest case of a masculine script executed correctly and rejected anyway. Gatsby has assembled every component the role requires: the fortune, the house, the war record, the education claim, the wardrobe, the practiced address. Nick's phrase that he had committed himself to the following of a grail frames the project as a vocation rather than a temperament. The problem is that the components are visibly assembled, and a masculinity that shows its assembly reads as counterfeit to men whose own version arrived without effort. Tom's advantage is not that his performance is better but that it is invisible, which is what inherited position buys. Reading the two men as positions in a ranking rather than as personalities explains why money closes the economic gap and not the other one.

### Q: How does Wilson fit the novel's gender hierarchy?

At the bottom, and the novel renders the position as depletion rather than as villainy. He is described as spiritless and anemic, a man who runs down, and his wife supplies the ranking directly when she tells Nick she thought Wilson knew something about breeding and discovered he was not fit to lick her shoe. She also reports that he borrowed the suit he married her in, which is a failure to supply even the props of the role. When Tom takes his wife, Wilson has no move available within the script, since every response it offers requires resources he does not have. His eventual action, taking a gun and finding the man he believes responsible, is a departure from the script rather than a use of it, and the novel treats it as the collapse of a position rather than as an assertion of one.
