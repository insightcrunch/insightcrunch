At approximately 9:25 on the morning of October 25, 1944, Vice Admiral Takeo Kurita did the inexplicable. His Center Force had survived American submarines in Palawan Passage, survived a full day of carrier air strikes in the Sibuyan Sea that sank the super-battleship Musashi, and had passed through San Bernardino Strait during the night unopposed. His battleships and cruisers had spent two and a half hours pounding a small American task unit off Samar. The American escort carriers were in flight, their destroyer screen largely destroyed, and the road to the Leyte transports lay open. Kurita had paid an enormous price to get there, and the prize the Sho-Go plan had promised him was within reach. He ordered his force to reverse course and withdraw.

![Battle of Leyte Gulf with American escort carriers and destroyers engaging Japanese battleships off Samar - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-55.webp)

The Battle of Leyte Gulf is the largest naval battle in history by displacement, and its decision sequence is one of the most operationally tangled of the entire Pacific war. Four engagements unfolded across some 100,000 square miles of Philippine waters between October 23 and October 26, and the decisive question was never about gunnery or air power. It was about command architecture: who was responsible for guarding San Bernardino Strait, who decided it did not need guarding, and what happened when the Japanese sailed through it. This article reconstructs that decision sequence, and it takes a position. The near-disaster off Samar was produced by a specific, identifiable gap in Allied command architecture, a gap that Admiral William Halsey's October 24 decision widened into a chasm. The Japanese, meanwhile, produced a plan that was brilliant in conception and self-defeating in execution, because their own command architecture could not coordinate the forces it sent into the fight.

## The Command Split That Made the Battle Possible

The strategic setup for Leyte began long before October 1944, in the rivalry between the two American theaters that had been fighting the Pacific war on parallel tracks. General Douglas MacArthur commanded the Southwest Pacific Area and had been advancing northwest through New Guinea toward the Philippines, bound by his pledge to return after the archipelago's fall in 1942. Admiral Chester Nimitz commanded the Central Pacific theater and had been driving west through the Gilbert, Marshall, Mariana, and Palau island groups in a campaign that had destroyed Japanese naval air power and brought American bombers within range of the Japanese home islands. The attritional pattern of that advance had been established two years earlier in the Solomons, where [the Marine landing on Guadalcanal](/2006/05/08/guadalcanal-august-1942-marine-landing/) began the long education in what contested Pacific landings would cost. Each commander had a theory of how to win the war. MacArthur wanted the Philippines back. Nimitz's planners, and much of the Navy's leadership in Washington, preferred to bypass the Philippines entirely and strike at Formosa or the Chinese coast. The Joint Chiefs of Staff resolved the competition in 1944 by committing to the Philippine invasion with both theaters coordinating, and Leyte, the large island in the central Philippines, was selected as the initial invasion target. The decision was political as much as strategic, and MacArthur's influence was visible in it, but it was also operationally sound. Leyte offered airfields and anchorages from which the subsequent invasion of Luzon could be supported.

The landings began on October 20, 1944, under MacArthur's overall command. The amphibious force was covered by two American fleets with two different masters. Vice Admiral Thomas Kinkaid's 7th Fleet, part of MacArthur's Southwest Pacific command, provided the amphibious lift and close fire support. Admiral William Halsey's 3rd Fleet, part of Nimitz's Central Pacific command, provided the fleet carrier air cover and the fleet defense against any Japanese naval reaction. On paper this division of labor was clean. In practice it contained a structural flaw that would nearly prove catastrophic. Halsey reported to Nimitz. Kinkaid reported to MacArthur. No single naval commander held authority over both fleets supporting the Leyte invasion. The two fleets were expected to coordinate as equals, which meant, in the absence of explicit direction, that each commander would interpret his mission according to his own doctrine and his own reading of the situation. Allied committee architecture in the Pacific had produced extraordinary results through 1944, but at Leyte it had a hole at the center, and the Japanese had designed their entire plan to exploit it.

MacArthur went ashore at Leyte on October 20 in the famous wading photograph, and the invasion proceeded against lighter opposition than expected. The Japanese garrison on Leyte was smaller than American intelligence had estimated, and the beachhead was established quickly. But the Japanese Combined Fleet, under Admiral Soemu Toyoda, had been waiting for exactly this moment. American intelligence knew that the Japanese navy intended to fight a decisive battle for the Philippines. What American commanders did not know was how the Japanese intended to fight it, or that their plan had been built around a deliberate sacrifice.

The landings themselves, on October 20, went forward with a confidence that the subsequent naval battle would test. MacArthur's famous walk through the surf to the beach, staged for the cameras but genuine in its symbolism, announced the return he had promised. The troops ashore established their beachhead against opposition lighter than expected, and the amphibious operation proceeded with the professionalism the 7th Fleet had developed across two years of landings. Kinkaid's force handled the complex choreography of putting tens of thousands of men ashore with their equipment, and the initial phases gave no hint of the naval crisis approaching. The transports sat in Leyte Gulf, unloaded their cargoes, and waited, trusting that the fleet cover would hold. That trust was the product of the divided command, and it was misplaced in exactly the way the structure made possible.

MacArthur's presence ashore added a further complication to the command picture. The theater commander was forward, focused on the ground battle, and largely detached from the naval decision-making. Nimitz was at Pearl Harbor, thousands of miles from the action, receiving delayed and fragmentary reports. The two fleet commanders, Halsey and Kinkaid, were thus operating with minimal supervision at the decisive moment, each interpreting his orders according to his own lights. It was the worst possible configuration for the situation that developed: maximum discretion at the point of decision, minimum coordination across the seam where the Japanese planned to strike.

## The Decision for the Philippines

The choice of Leyte as the invasion target was itself a decision with a contested history, and understanding it clarifies why the battle took the shape it did. Through the first half of 1944, the American strategic debate over the Pacific had two fully developed alternatives. Admiral Ernest King, the Chief of Naval Operations, favored bypassing the Philippines entirely and striking at Formosa, which would cut Japanese communications with the south more cleanly and position American forces for the final approach to Japan. King's logic was cold and naval: the Philippines were a large landmass requiring a major ground commitment, while Formosa offered the strategic leverage without the entanglement. MacArthur's logic was political, personal, and, in the end, persuasive. He argued that American honor required the liberation of the Philippines, that bypassing them would abandon millions of Filipinos and thousands of American prisoners to Japanese retaliation, and that the archipelago's airfields and anchorages were necessary for the war's final phase. Nimitz, characteristically, held the middle ground, willing to support either course but concerned about the logistics of each.

The Joint Chiefs settled the matter in the summer of 1944 by committing to the Philippines, and the decision reflected more than MacArthur's advocacy. By that point the Central Pacific drive had brought American forces to the Palaus, close enough to the Philippines to make the operation feasible, and intelligence indicated that Japanese air power in the Philippines had been badly damaged by Halsey's carrier strikes in September. The Luzon-first versus Leyte-first question was resolved in favor of Leyte as the initial lodgment, a large island in the central Philippines with airfields that could support the subsequent invasion of Luzon. What the decision did not resolve, and what no one at the time fully appreciated, was the command question. The operation would be MacArthur's, but the fleet cover would come from Nimitz's theater, and the seam between the two commands would run directly through the waters where the Japanese intended to fight.

American intelligence in October 1944 presented a paradox that shaped every subsequent decision. ULTRA intercepts and submarine reconnaissance had given the Americans a remarkably clear picture of Japanese intentions: the Combined Fleet was coming, and it was coming in force. What intelligence could not provide was the Japanese plan's internal logic. The Americans knew Kurita's force existed and was moving. They did not know that Ozawa's carriers were empty, because nothing in American experience suggested that a navy would send carriers into battle as a deliberate sacrifice. The possibility lay outside anything the American doctrinal frame could contain. Halsey's staff evaluated Ozawa's force as a genuine carrier threat because that was the only category their doctrine contained. The intelligence was accurate about positions and compositions. It was blind to intentions, and intentions were the entire battle.

The Japanese side of the intelligence picture was worse. Toyoda's staff knew the Americans were landing at Leyte, but they had no reliable picture of American dispositions, no sense of the divided command, and no way to coordinate their converging forces in real time. Nishimura sailed into Surigao Strait without knowing Kurita's exact position or timing. Kurita fought at Samar without knowing whether Halsey's battleships were returning. Ozawa performed his decoy mission perfectly and had no way to know whether it had worked until long after it no longer mattered. Sho-Go was a plan that required its executors to act on faith, and faith, as the battle demonstrated, is a poor substitute for communications.

## The Sho-Go Gamble

Toyoda's plan was called Sho-Go, meaning Victory Operation, and it existed in four variants corresponding to the four places the Americans might invade: Sho-1 for the Philippines, Sho-2 for the Ryukyu Islands, Sho-3 for the Kuriles, Sho-4 for Hokkaido. When the Leyte landings began, Sho-1 was activated on October 18, two days before the first American troops went ashore. The plan was, by any honest reading, an act of desperation dressed in the language of decision. The Japanese carrier force had been destroyed as an offensive weapon at the Philippine Sea in June, in what American pilots called the Great Marianas Turkey Shoot. What remained of Japanese naval aviation was a hollow shell. Sho-Go accepted that reality and built it into the plan's central feature.

Vice Admiral Jisaburo Ozawa's Northern Force, comprising four carriers including the Zuikaku, the last surviving veteran of the Pearl Harbor attack force, would sail south from Japan toward the waters east of Luzon as a decoy. The carriers carried almost no aircraft. Their purpose was not to fight but to be seen, to radiate radio traffic, to present themselves as the long-awaited Japanese fleet concentration that American doctrine demanded be destroyed. The hope was that Halsey, whose standing orders from Nimitz authorized him to pursue the Japanese fleet if encountered, would take the bait and chase north with the 3rd Fleet's carriers and fast battleships. With Halsey's fleet drawn away from Leyte, two surface forces would converge on the invasion anchorage. Vice Admiral Takeo Kurita's Center Force, the most powerful surface fleet Japan had ever assembled, with five battleships including the super-battleships Yamato and Musashi, ten cruisers, and fifteen destroyers, would approach from the north through San Bernardino Strait. Vice Admiral Shoji Nishimura's Southern Force, with two older battleships, the Yamashiro and the Fuso, would approach from the south through Surigao Strait, joined by a supplementary force under Vice Admiral Kiyohide Shima. The two forces would fall on the American transports at Leyte and destroy the invasion. The plan accepted near-total Japanese naval loss as the price. If the transports were destroyed, the invasion would collapse, and Japan would gain months, perhaps a year, of breathing room.

The plan's audacity is worth pausing over, because it shaped everything that followed. Sho-Go was not a plan for victory in any conventional sense. It was a plan for a trade: the remaining Japanese fleet in exchange for the Leyte invasion. Toyoda and his staff understood that Japan could no longer win a fleet engagement against American industrial power. What they could do was aim at the soft underbelly of the American operation, the transports and supply ships that made the invasion possible. The transports were the true objective, the one target whose destruction would translate directly into strategic effect. Every other element of the plan, the decoy, the converging surface forces, the acceptance of annihilation, served that single purpose. The plan required precise timing, real-time coordination between forces that would be hundreds of miles apart, and a willingness on the part of subordinate commanders to sail into near-certain destruction. It got the sacrifice. It did not get the coordination.

## The Intelligence War

Beneath the visible battle ran an invisible one, and its outcomes shaped the visible one at every turn. American signals intelligence, the ULTRA program that had broken Japanese naval codes, provided the strategic warning that the Combined Fleet was coming. The intercepts told Nimitz and MacArthur that Sho-1 had been activated, that Kurita's force was moving, and that Ozawa's carriers were at sea. What ULTRA could not tell them was the plan's internal logic, because the critical fact, that Ozawa's carriers were empty, was never transmitted in a form the Americans intercepted, or if it was, it was never recognized for what it meant. Intelligence gave the Americans the dots. Doctrine prevented them from connecting the dots correctly.

The submarine campaign provided the tactical intelligence that mattered most. American submarines maintained patrol lines across the Japanese approach routes, and it was the Darter and Dace that provided the first hard contact with Kurita's force. But submarine reconnaissance had limits. Once Kurita passed through Palawan Passage, continuous tracking was impossible, and the critical question of his intentions after the Sibuyan Sea strikes had to be answered by inference. The inference the Americans drew, that he was retiring, was reasonable and wrong. The Japanese, for their part, operated in near-total intelligence darkness. Their reconnaissance was inadequate, their communications were vulnerable, and their commanders made decisions based on fragmentary and often outdated information. Kurita at Samar did not know Oldendorf's battleships were available. Nishimura at Surigao did not know Kurita had been delayed. Ozawa did not know whether his decoy had worked. The battle was fought, on the Japanese side, by commanders who could not see the battlefield.

## Kurita and Nishimura: The Commanders Who Sailed

The Japanese commanders at Leyte Gulf are often treated as instruments of Toyoda's plan, but their individual characters shaped the battle's outcome as much as any order. Vice Admiral Takeo Kurita was fifty-five, a torpedo specialist by training who had risen through the surface fleet. He was known as a careful, even cautious officer, respected by his subordinates but not regarded as brilliant. His caution had served him well in a navy that prized aggression sometimes to the point of recklessness. At Leyte Gulf, that same caution produced both his survival through two days of attacks and his withdrawal at the moment of decision. A more aggressive commander might have pressed on to the transports and died achieving the plan's purpose. A less resolute one might have turned back in the Sibuyan Sea. Kurita did neither, and the ambiguity of his conduct reflects the ambiguity of his character.

Kurita's experience at Palawan Passage, pulled from the sea after his flagship sank beneath him, cannot be discounted in any psychological accounting. He began the operation by nearly drowning. He continued it by watching the Musashi die. By the morning of October 25, he had been under threat of death for two days, his staff was exhausted, and his information was bad. None of this excuses the withdrawal in terms of the mission, but all of it explains the withdrawal in terms of the man. Command decisions are made by human beings under physical and psychological stress, and the reconstruction that ignores the stress misses the decision.

Vice Admiral Shoji Nishimura presents the opposite case. Where Kurita hesitated at the decisive moment, Nishimura never hesitated at all. His advance up Surigao Strait into overwhelming force was, by any tactical measure, hopeless, and he knew it. He went anyway, maintaining radio silence and pressing forward with a discipline that his enemies admired. Nishimura's conduct raises the question of what the Japanese naval ethos demanded and what it cost. The same culture that produced Nishimura's magnificent futility produced Kurita's withdrawal, because a system that demands death rather than adaptation gets both, unpredictably. Toyoda's plan required commanders who would spend their forces completely. It got one who would and one who would not, and the difference decided the battle.

## The Machinery of Sho-Go: Fuel, Orders, and Silence

Sho-Go's boldness on paper concealed desperate material constraints that shaped every Japanese decision. By October 1944 the Combined Fleet was critically short of fuel oil, so short that Kurita's Center Force had to refuel at Brunei with unrefined Tarakan crude that damaged boiler tubes and reduced efficiency. The fleet that sailed for Leyte Gulf was operating on the dregs of Japan's oil reserves, and there would be no second sortie. This was a one-way mission in logistical terms before it was one in tactical terms. Toyoda's staff understood that the fleet, win or lose, would not survive the battle as a fleet. The plan's acceptance of near-total loss was not merely bravado. It was an acknowledgment that the ships were already, in any meaningful strategic sense, lost.

The selection of Ozawa for the decoy mission reflected both his seniority and the expendability of his command. Ozawa was one of the Imperial Navy's most capable officers, a carrier specialist who understood exactly what was being asked of him. His orders were explicit: he was to approach from the north, make his presence known through radio traffic and reconnaissance exposure, and draw the American carrier forces away from Leyte. He was not expected to survive. The four carriers under his command, the Zuikaku, Zuiho, Chitose, and Chiyoda, carried between them barely a hundred aircraft, most flown by inadequately trained pilots. The Zuikaku's presence gave the force its symbolic weight. As the last surviving carrier of the six that had attacked Pearl Harbor, her name alone was enough to guarantee American attention. Toyoda was spending the Pearl Harbor legacy as bait, and the expenditure was calculated.

Perhaps the most revealing feature of Sho-Go's planning was who was not consulted. The Imperial Japanese Army, which had the larger stake in the Philippines garrison, was not informed of the naval plan in detail. Inter-service coordination, never a Japanese strength, was effectively absent. The Army's own plans for the defense of Leyte proceeded on the assumption that the Navy would do something useful, without clarity on what that something was. Within the Navy itself, coordination between the converging forces was minimal. Kurita and Nishimura had no direct communication link during the operation and no common operational picture. Nishimura, the junior, was effectively ordered to sail into Surigao Strait on a timetable that assumed Kurita's simultaneous arrival from the north, an assumption that the delays in the Sibuyan Sea had already invalidated. He went anyway, with the fatalism that the plan demanded and that Japanese naval culture honored.

## Palawan Passage and the Sibuyan Sea

The first blow of the battle was struck not by carriers or battleships but by submarines, and the circumstances illustrate how much of Leyte Gulf was decided by forces operating at the margins of the main plan. On October 23, as Kurita's Center Force steamed through the narrow waters of Palawan Passage on its way toward San Bernardino Strait, the American submarines Darter and Dace found it in the darkness. The two boats were on routine patrol, positioned by the logic of chokepoint coverage rather than by specific intelligence of Kurita's movement. Their contact was a matter of professional competence meeting opportunity. The Darter's torpedoes struck the heavy cruiser Atago, Kurita's flagship, and the cruiser went down with heavy loss of life. The Dace sank the heavy cruiser Maya. A third cruiser, the Takao, was badly damaged and forced to withdraw. Kurita himself was pulled from the water and transferred his flag to the Yamato. The Center Force pressed on, diminished but still enormously powerful, and American intelligence now had confirmation that a major Japanese surface force was approaching. The information went to both Halsey and Kinkaid. What each man did with it would define the battle.

On October 24, Halsey's Task Force 38, the fast carrier task force that was the striking arm of the 3rd Fleet, found Kurita in the Sibuyan Sea and attacked with wave after wave of carrier aircraft. The air strikes sank the Musashi, the sister ship of the Yamato and one of the two largest battleships ever built, with the loss of approximately 1,023 of her crew. It was the largest battleship sunk to that point in the war, and its destruction was a genuine achievement of American naval air power. Other ships of the Center Force were damaged. Under the pounding, Kurita did something that would be argued about for decades: at approximately 3:00 p.m., he reversed course and steamed westward, away from San Bernardino Strait. To Halsey's pilots and to Halsey's staff, it looked as though the Center Force had been turned back. The airmen reported the Japanese in retreat. Halsey, already receiving reports of Ozawa's carriers to the north, made his decision.

### Why did Halsey chase Ozawa's carriers north instead of guarding San Bernardino Strait?

Halsey held Nimitz's standing authority to pursue the Japanese fleet, and Pacific Fleet doctrine since Pearl Harbor made destroying the enemy fleet the supreme priority. Believing Kurita's Center Force crippled and retiring, he took Task Force 38 north at 8:00 p.m. on October 24. The judgment was doctrinally consistent and operationally catastrophic.

What Halsey did not know, and what his staff's reporting had obscured, was that Kurita's westward turn was temporary. After dark on October 24, Kurita reversed course again and steamed eastward toward San Bernardino Strait. The Center Force, minus the Musashi and with several ships damaged, still comprised four battleships including the Yamato, six cruisers, and eleven destroyers. It was more than enough to annihilate the light forces guarding the strait, because there were no light forces guarding the strait. Halsey had taken everything north. San Bernardino Strait, the northern entrance to the waters off Leyte, stood open.

The question of Task Force 34 sits at the center of the controversy and deserves precise treatment. Task Force 34 was to have been a surface action group built around the 3rd Fleet's fast battleships, formed for the specific purpose of guarding San Bernardino Strait against a Japanese surface sortie. Halsey had signaled its formation as a contingency, which led Kinkaid and others to believe that a battleship force would remain behind when the carriers went north. But Halsey never actually formed it. He took the fast battleships north with him, reasoning that they would be needed if Ozawa's force turned out to include battleships, and that a surface engagement with the Japanese carriers' escorts was possible. Kinkaid, reading Halsey's signals, believed San Bernardino Strait was guarded. It was not. The gap between what Halsey signaled and what Halsey did is the single most consequential communications failure of the battle, and it was entirely a product of the divided command. Had one naval commander owned both fleets, the question of who guarded the strait would have had one answer. With two commanders and no superior to adjudicate, it had two answers, and the wrong one prevailed.

## The Killing of the Musashi

The destruction of the Musashi in the Sibuyan Sea deserves detailed treatment, because it was both a tactical masterpiece and a strategic misdirection. Through October 24, Task Force 38 launched five major air strikes against Kurita's force, committing hundreds of aircraft. The Japanese anti-aircraft fire was intense but uncoordinated, and the American torpedo bombers pressed their attacks to ranges that would have been suicidal against a properly screened force. The Musashi, as the largest target, drew the concentrated attention of the strikes. She was hit, by the most careful postwar accounting, by approximately nineteen torpedoes and seventeen bombs, a punishment no warship ever built could have survived. Her damage control parties fought with extraordinary discipline, counterflooding to keep her upright long after lesser ships would have capsized, and she remained afloat for hours after receiving mortal damage. When she finally rolled over and sank in the evening, approximately 1,023 of her crew went down with her.

The tactical lesson the Americans drew was that Kurita's force had been broken. The pilots' after-action reports described a fleet in disarray, ships burning, the formation scattered. What the reports could not convey was Kurita's intent, and intent was everything. A fleet that has lost its second most powerful unit and suffered damage to several others is not necessarily a fleet that has abandoned its mission. Kurita's 3:00 p.m. westward turn was a damage-control maneuver as much as a retreat, a chance to reorganize, fight fires, and assess. His staff's decision to reverse eastward after dark reflected a commander who had never abandoned the mission, only paused it. Halsey's staff, reading the pilot reports without access to Kurita's mind, made the natural inference and the wrong one. The Musashi's death was real and total. The inference drawn from it was the error.

There is a further dimension to the Sibuyan Sea action that is often underplayed. The air strikes, for all their success against the Musashi, failed to stop Kurita. Four battleships, six cruisers, and eleven destroyers continued toward San Bernardino Strait with their fighting power largely intact. Naval air power had proven it could kill even the largest battleship ever built, but it had not proven it could stop a determined surface force from reaching its objective. That limitation would matter enormously the next morning off Samar, when the only thing between Kurita and the transports was a force that air power had already bypassed.

## Surigao Strait: The Last Battleship Duel

While Kurita was being pounded in the Sibuyan Sea and Halsey was turning north, the southern pincer of Sho-Go was sailing into a trap. Nishimura's Southern Force, with the battleships Yamashiro and Fuso, a heavy cruiser, and four destroyers, steamed up Surigao Strait in the early hours of October 25, intending to break into Leyte Gulf from the south at dawn. The force was, by the standards of 1944 naval warfare, an anachronism: two aging battleships without air cover, without modern radar fire control, steaming into waters controlled by an enemy who knew they were coming. Nishimura's orders gave him no alternative. The plan required a southern pincer, and he was the southern pincer. What the plan did not give him was any awareness that Kurita had been delayed, that coordination had broken down, or that his sacrifice would serve no purpose beyond the demonstration of resolve. Waiting for him was Rear Admiral Jesse Oldendorf's bombardment force from Kinkaid's 7th Fleet: six battleships, most of them veterans of Pearl Harbor raised from the mud and rebuilt, plus cruisers, destroyers, and torpedo boats. Oldendorf deployed his battleships across the northern end of the strait in the classic naval maneuver called crossing the T, in which a line of ships steams across the head of an enemy column and brings every gun to bear while the enemy can reply only with forward turrets. It was the maneuver every naval officer had studied since Tsushima, and Surigao Strait was its last great execution.

The battle was fought in darkness, illuminated by star shells and radar-directed gunfire, and it was a slaughter. Nishimura's force was hit first by torpedo boats, then by destroyer torpedo attacks, then by the battleship line. The Fuso broke in two and sank. The Yamashiro was pounded by American battleship gunfire and went down with Nishimura aboard. Shima's supplementary force arrived late, saw the disaster unfolding, and withdrew after a confused collision with one of Nishimura's own retreating destroyers. By dawn the Southern Force had effectively ceased to exist. Surigao Strait stands as the last major battleship-versus-battleship engagement in naval history. The battleship era, which had begun with the dreadnoughts of the early twentieth century, ended in the dark waters of a Philippine strait, killed by radar, torpedoes, and air power. Oldendorf's victory was complete and professionally executed, and it is worth noting what it did not do: it did not affect the northern approach at all. The southern pincer was destroyed. The northern pincer was sailing through an unguarded strait.

### What was the Sho-Go plan and why did Japan sacrifice its carriers?

Sho-Go was Admiral Toyoda's four-variant plan for action against the next American invasion, with Sho-1 covering the Philippines. Its core was a trade: Ozawa's four nearly aircraft-less carriers would decoy Halsey's 3rd Fleet north while Kurita's Center Force and Nishimura's Southern Force converged on the Leyte transports. Japan accepted the certain loss of its fleet for the invasion's destruction.

## Annihilation in the Dark

The destruction of Nishimura's force at Surigao Strait was methodical in a way that makes it easy to overlook the human dimension on both sides. Nishimura knew, or should have known, that he was sailing into superior forces. His two battleships, the Yamashiro and the Fuso, were veterans of an earlier era, slower and less well protected than the American battleships waiting for him. His destroyer screen was thin. He had no air cover. Yet he pressed up the strait in the darkness with a determination that his American opponents, to their credit, recognized and honored. There was no confusion in Nishimura's conduct, no hesitation, no attempt to preserve his force. He had been ordered to break into Leyte Gulf, and he spent his command completely in the attempt. The Japanese naval tradition that produced such conduct was the same tradition that produced the plan's fatal rigidity.

Oldendorf's deployment deserves recognition as a professional achievement of the highest order. His six battleships, the California, Maryland, Mississippi, Pennsylvania, Tennessee, and West Virginia, were arrayed across the strait's northern exit with cruisers on the flanks. The West Virginia's radar-directed fire control opened the battleship phase of the action with salvos fired in darkness at ranges beyond what the Japanese could effectively answer. The battle was over, for practical purposes, within minutes of the battleship line opening fire. But Oldendorf's professionalism extended beyond gunnery. He had positioned torpedo boats and destroyers down the strait to bleed Nishimura's force before it reached the battle line, and the destroyer torpedo attacks sank or crippled several Japanese ships before the big guns spoke. It was a complete combined-arms night action, executed flawlessly.

Shima's supplementary force, arriving after Nishimura's destruction, provides the battle's grim coda. Shima's flagship, the heavy cruiser Nachi, collided in the darkness with the Mogami, one of Nishimura's cruisers that was already sinking, a collision that symbolized the plan's coordination failure in miniature. Shima, seeing the scale of the disaster, ordered a withdrawal. His force launched torpedoes at long range, none of which hit, and retired south. The southern pincer of Sho-Go had been annihilated at the cost of negligible American losses. Oldendorf's victory was total, and it changed nothing about the northern approach.

## Cape Engano: Halsey Takes the Bait

On October 25, Halsey's Task Force 38 caught Ozawa's Northern Force off Cape Engano at the northeastern tip of Luzon and destroyed it. The contact had been developed through the night by search aircraft, and Mitscher's carriers launched their strikes at dawn with the methodical efficiency of a force that had been killing Japanese ships for two years. The Japanese carriers, with their handful of aircraft, could offer only token resistance. Their escorts fought gamely but hopelessly. By afternoon it was over. All four Japanese carriers, the Zuikaku, Zuiho, Chitose, and Chiyoda, were sunk along with their escorts. The Zuikaku's sinking carried a symbolism no American sailor missed: the last carrier of the Pearl Harbor striking force was gone, and with her the last pretense that Japan remained a carrier power. It was a tactically complete victory and strategically almost meaningless. The carriers had almost no aircraft. Their destruction removed nothing from the Japanese order of battle that mattered, because Japanese naval aviation was already gone. What the victory cost was time and position. While Halsey's carriers were sinking empty hulls to the north, Kurita's Center Force emerged from San Bernardino Strait at dawn and fell upon the most vulnerable American force in Philippine waters.

The decoy had worked exactly as Toyoda designed it. Ozawa's orders had been explicit: his force existed to be attacked. He was to make enough radio noise to be detected, to present himself as a fleet concentration worthy of Halsey's full attention, and to accept destruction. Every part of that mission succeeded. The uncomfortable truth for the American side is that Halsey's decision was not foolish within the framework he inhabited. It was the framework that failed.

That framework deserves a final examination, because it is the mechanism by which the error reproduced itself across the staff. Halsey's decision on the afternoon of October 24 was the single most consequential American command decision of the Pacific war, and it was made in a matter of hours under the pressure of incomplete information and institutional momentum. Through the day, search aircraft had reported Ozawa's carriers to the north, and the reports accumulated into a picture of a major Japanese carrier force within striking distance. Halsey's staff, operating within the carrier-centric doctrine of the Pacific Fleet, treated the carriers as the primary threat and the primary target. The alternative interpretation, that the carriers were a deliberate decoy, was never seriously entertained, because it required believing something no American officer in 1944 believed: that an enemy would send carriers into battle without aircraft. The staff's failure was not one of intelligence but of imagination. The possibility that would have explained everything was the one possibility their doctrine excluded. Halsey was reasoning correctly within the terms his doctrine gave him. Since Pearl Harbor, the Pacific Fleet's standing imperative had been to seek out and destroy the Japanese fleet. Nimitz's orders to Halsey explicitly authorized pursuit if a major Japanese force was encountered. Ozawa's carriers, spotted by search aircraft, looked like the decisive target the entire Central Pacific campaign had been building toward. Leaving them to escape would have meant, in Halsey's reading, allowing the last Japanese carrier force to survive and threaten future operations. The doctrine said chase. The mission said guard. Halsey's orders did not reconcile the two, and he chose the doctrine.

## The Empty Victory

The Battle of Cape Engano on October 25 was, in tactical terms, a complete American victory. Task Force 38's aircraft sank all four of Ozawa's carriers along with a light cruiser and destroyers of the screen. The Zuikaku, the last Pearl Harbor carrier, went down with her flag still flying, and with her died the last organized remnant of the naval air arm that had begun the Pacific war. Vice Admiral Marc Mitscher, commanding Task Force 38 under Halsey, conducted the strikes with the professionalism that had characterized American carrier operations since the Philippine Sea. There was nothing wrong with the execution. Everything wrong was in the decision to be there.

The cost of Cape Engano must be measured not in ships lost but in position surrendered. While Halsey's carriers were sinking empty hulls to the north, the decisive action of the battle was unfolding off Samar to the south, where Kurita's battleships were falling on Taffy 3. Every hour Halsey spent destroying Ozawa was an hour Kurita had to complete the mission Sho-Go had assigned him. The irony is exact and painful: Ozawa's mission was to be destroyed, and Halsey obliged him at the cost of nearly losing the invasion. A victory that serves the enemy's plan is a defeat wearing a victory's uniform, and Cape Engano is the clearest case in the Pacific war.

It is worth noting what Halsey believed he was accomplishing. In his mind, shaped by three years of Pacific Fleet doctrine, he was completing the destruction of Japanese naval power. The carriers were the prize. The battleships at San Bernardino were, in his assessment, a spent force retiring from the Sibuyan Sea. He was wrong about the second judgment, and the wrongness flowed from the pilot reports, from the absence of any staff challenge to the consensus, and from the lack of any superior or peer with the authority to demand a second look. Mitscher, his subordinate, reportedly had doubts about abandoning the strait, but the command relationship did not invite dissent and Mitscher did not force the issue. The architecture of the 3rd Fleet concentrated decision authority in Halsey alone, and Halsey alone decided.

## The Halsey Controversy

The controversy over Halsey's October 24 decision has generated more historical writing than any other American naval command decision of the Pacific war, and the debate is not really about whether Halsey made a mistake. It is about what kind of mistake it was, and what it reveals. The facts are not in dispute. At approximately 8:00 p.m. on October 24, Halsey took Task Force 38 north in pursuit of Ozawa. He did not detach Task Force 34, the fast battleship group, to guard San Bernardino Strait, despite having signaled its formation in a way that led Kinkaid to believe the strait was covered. He did not inform Kinkaid that the strait was open. Through the night and into the morning of October 25, as Kurita's battleships steamed toward Leyte Gulf, Halsey remained committed to the north.

The famous "Where is Task Force 34? The world wonders" message arrived on the morning of October 25, sent by Nimitz from Pearl Harbor. Nimitz, receiving fragmentary reports, wanted to know where the battleship force was. The message as composed was a straightforward operational query. But Navy communications procedure at the time required padding phrases at the beginning and end of encrypted messages to confuse enemy cryptanalysis, and the padding phrase selected for this message was "the world wonders." When the message was decoded aboard Halsey's flagship, the padding was not clearly separated from the text. Halsey read "Where is Task Force 34? The world wonders" as a sarcastic rebuke from his superior, and his reaction, by multiple accounts, was fury. He threw his cap to the deck. It took him over an hour to turn part of his force south, and by then it was far too late to intervene off Samar. The battleships he eventually detached arrived after Kurita had already withdrawn.

The question of responsibility extends beyond Halsey, and an honest reconstruction must follow it upward. Nimitz's orders to Halsey contained the fatal ambiguity. They authorized pursuit of the Japanese fleet and charged Halsey with covering the Leyte operation, without establishing which imperative took precedence if they conflicted. Nimitz, as the superior who wrote the orders, bears responsibility for the contradiction. His defenders note that he could not have anticipated the specific situation that developed, and that operational orders necessarily leave discretion to the commander on the spot. His critics note that the divided command was his design as much as MacArthur's, and that a unified naval command would have made the ambiguity impossible. Admiral King's role is more distant but real: as Chief of Naval Operations, King had championed the aggressive doctrine that made Halsey's decision feel mandatory rather than discretionary. The culture of the Pacific Fleet in 1944 did not merely permit Halsey's chase. It demanded it.

There is also the matter of what happened after. Halsey was not relieved. He was not formally censured. He went on to command at Okinawa and to accept the Japanese surrender in Tokyo Bay, his reputation intact with the public even as naval professionals debated his Leyte performance. Nimitz protected him, in part from institutional loyalty and in part because a public reckoning would have exposed the command failures above Halsey. The contrast with the treatment of less well-connected officers who made smaller errors is stark, and it is part of why the controversy endures. Leyte Gulf raised questions about accountability in high command that the Navy preferred not to answer, and the questions have outlived all the participants.

### What did Nimitz's "The world wonders" message actually say?

Nimitz's October 25 message asked where Task Force 34, the battleship group Halsey had signaled, actually was. Navy encryption required meaningless padding around message text, and the padding chosen was "the world wonders." Poorly separated in decoding, it read as sarcasm. Halsey took it as a rebuke, raged for over an hour, and turned south too late for Samar.

The scholarly consensus on Halsey has moved, over the decades, toward a mixed assessment that the brief mandates and that the evidence supports. Morison's foundational account in volume twelve of his History of United States Naval Operations in World War II treated Halsey's decision as a serious error while placing it in the context of the divided command. Thomas Cutler's comprehensive operational reconstruction, The Battle of Leyte Gulf, dissected the decision sequence in detail and found Halsey's pursuit defensible in narrow doctrinal terms but indefensible in terms of his responsibility to the Leyte operation. H. P. Willmott's analytical overview reached similar ground. The consensus position is that Halsey's decision was defensible within the framework of Pacific Fleet seek-and-destroy doctrine but violated his broader operational responsibility to protect the invasion, and that his reputation suffered damage from which neither Nimitz nor MacArthur, the architects of the divided command, ever suffered. That last point matters, and the broader reassessment of Halsey's career, with Leyte Gulf as its central episode, is taken up in [the Halsey reassessment](/2005/05/21/halsey-reassessed-leyte-gulf/). The command structure that made Halsey's error possible was designed above him, and the men who designed it escaped the historical bill.

## The Night the Strait Stood Open

The timeline of the night of October 24 to 25 repays close attention, because it shows how many opportunities existed to correct the error and how the command structure foreclosed each one. At approximately 8:00 p.m. on October 24, Halsey turned Task Force 38 north. Kinkaid, commanding the 7th Fleet off Leyte, received Halsey's movement reports and, crucially, interpreted the earlier signals about Task Force 34 to mean that battleships were guarding San Bernardino Strait. Kinkaid's dispositions for the night reflected that belief. His battleships under Oldendorf were committed to Surigao Strait. His escort carrier groups, the Taffies, were positioned for ground support. Nothing covered the northern approach, because Kinkaid believed something did.

Through the night, Kurita's Center Force steamed eastward through San Bernardino Strait unopposed and unobserved. American submarines that might have reported the movement were not positioned to do so. Search aircraft were committed elsewhere. The strait that the entire Japanese plan depended on being open was open, and no American commander knew it. At approximately 3:00 a.m. on October 25, Kinkaid began receiving reports that suggested all was not well, but the picture remained confused. It was not until dawn, when Taffy 3's lookouts sighted the pagoda masts, that the reality became undeniable. By then Kurita's battleships were already firing.

Kinkaid's famous plain-language distress calls on the morning of October 25, pleading for battleship support, reached Halsey while Task Force 38 was engaged at Cape Engano. Halsey's response was delayed by the same command dynamics that had produced the error. He was committed to the destruction of Ozawa, his staff was focused north, and the idea that the situation to the south was desperate took time to penetrate. When Nimitz's "Where is Task Force 34" message arrived, it added fury to confusion. Halsey eventually detached Vice Admiral Willis Lee's fast battleships southward, but they arrived hours after Kurita had withdrawn. The battleships that should have guarded the strait arrived in time to guard nothing.

## Samar: The Stand of Taffy 3

At approximately 6:45 on the morning of October 25, lookouts on the American escort carriers off Samar sighted pagoda masts on the horizon. It took a moment for the recognition to land. Those were Japanese battleships, and they were coming through San Bernardino Strait, the strait that was supposed to be guarded. Task Unit 77.4.3, known by its voice call sign Taffy 3, was under the command of Rear Admiral Clifton Sprague, and it was not a fighting fleet in any sense that would have mattered against what was approaching. The Taffy designation covered three escort carrier groups operating off the Leyte beachheads, numbered Taffy 1, Taffy 2, and Taffy 3 from south to north. Taffy 3 was the northernmost, closest to San Bernardino Strait, and therefore the first in the path of whatever came through. Its position was a matter of routine disposition, not tactical foresight. No one had placed Taffy 3 as a guard force, because no one believed a guard force was needed. The strait was supposed to be covered by battleships that were not there.

Sprague himself was an aviation officer, a pilot who had commanded carriers through the Central Pacific campaign. He understood immediately what the pagoda masts meant, because there was only one thing they could mean. His response in the minutes that followed was the product of training and temperament: he did not waste time on disbelief, he did not wait for confirmation from higher authority, and he did not attempt to preserve his force. He ordered the carriers to make smoke, turn east, and launch everything, and he ordered the destroyers to attack. It was the only possible response and very nearly a futile one. That it proved sufficient is a measure of his subordinates' execution rather than of the plan, because there was no plan for what happened next. Taffy 3 comprised six escort carriers, small slow ships built on merchant hulls for close air support of the invasion, each roughly one hundredth the displacement of the Yamato, plus three destroyers and four destroyer escorts. Its aircraft were armed for ground support, not anti-ship strikes. Its mission was to cover the troops ashore. It was utterly unprepared for a battleship engagement, and it was about to fight one.

What followed over the next two and a half hours is one of the most celebrated actions in American naval history, and the celebration is deserved. Sprague ordered his carriers to run for a rain squall and cloud cover to the east while launching every available aircraft, and he ordered his destroyers to attack. The destroyers USS Johnston under Commander Ernest Evans, USS Hoel, and the destroyer escort USS Samuel B. Roberts under Lieutenant Commander Robert Copeland charged directly at Kurita's battleships and cruisers, launching torpedoes and firing their five-inch guns at targets they could not hope to damage with gunfire alone. The torpedo attacks forced Kurita's battleships into evasive maneuvers that disrupted their gunnery and bought time. The Johnston, the Hoel, and the Samuel B. Roberts were all sunk in the action, and their captains and crews paid the full price of the charge. Evans, who had told his crew before the battle that he intended to go in harm's way, went down with his ship. The escort carrier Gambier Bay, hit by Yamato's eighteen-inch shells, became the only American carrier of the war sunk by surface gunfire.

The American aircraft, meanwhile, attacked with whatever they carried, including depth charges and in some cases nothing at all, making dry runs to harass the Japanese. Kurita's force, which should have annihilated Taffy 3 in minutes, found itself fighting a chaotic, confusing action against an enemy that refused to behave like a beaten force. Three Japanese cruisers were sunk in the fighting: the Chokai, the Chikuma, and the Suzuya. Others were damaged. And then, at approximately 9:25 a.m., with Taffy 3 broken and the road to the Leyte transports open, Kurita ordered a general withdrawal.

### Why did Kurita withdraw at 9:25 a.m. when the transports lay open?

No consensus exists, and Kurita's postwar accounts were inconsistent. Leading interpretations include exhaustion after two days under attack, confusion about his enemy, belief he faced fleet carriers rather than escort carriers, and fear that Halsey's battleships would return and trap him. Several likely operated together. The withdrawal was his alone, made without Toyoda's orders, and it saved the invasion.

Kurita's decision has been examined from every angle, and each examination reveals something about the nature of command under extreme stress. He had been under attack, nearly continuously, for two days. His flagship had been sunk beneath him at Palawan Passage. He had watched the Musashi, one of the two great symbols of Japanese naval power, die under air attack in the Sibuyan Sea. His force had reversed course twice in twenty-four hours. At Samar, his staff's reporting was confused. Japanese lookouts, seeing the determined air attacks and the aggressive destroyer charges, reported that they were engaging fleet carriers and cruisers, not escort carriers and destroyer escorts. The distinction mattered enormously. If Kurita believed he was fighting Halsey's main fleet units, then pressing on toward Leyte meant sailing deeper into a trap, with Halsey's battleships potentially returning from the north to cut off his retreat through San Bernardino Strait. The fear was not irrational. It was wrong, but it was not irrational, and that is an important distinction for any honest reconstruction.

There is also the matter of what Kurita's force had already endured and what it would have faced. The Center Force had lost the Musashi, had three cruisers sunk or sinking at Samar, and had expended enormous quantities of ammunition. The destroyer torpedo attacks had scattered his formation. Continuing meant steaming into Leyte Gulf against Oldendorf's battleships, which had just annihilated Nishimura at Surigao Strait and were positioned to do the same to anyone who entered from the north. Kurita could not have known Oldendorf's exact disposition, but he knew that American battleships existed in those waters and that his force was in no condition for another surface action. His withdrawal, whatever its precise motives, was the decision of a commander who had reached the limit of what his force could absorb. Toyoda's plan had demanded that Kurita accept the destruction of his fleet in exchange for the transports. Kurita, at the decisive moment, declined the trade. The Japanese never got another chance to make it.

## Two Hours That Saved an Invasion

The Battle off Samar lasted approximately two and a half hours, from the first sighting at around 6:45 a.m. to Kurita's withdrawal order at 9:25 a.m., and its details repay the attention that James Hornfischer's 2004 account first gave them at full scale. Sprague's handling of the escort carriers was a masterclass in fighting a hopeless action. He ordered a turn to the east into a rain squall, using weather as armor, and he launched every aircraft he had with orders to attack with whatever they carried. Pilots took off with depth charges, with rockets intended for ground targets, and in some cases with no ordnance at all, making dry runs that forced Japanese anti-aircraft gunners to divide their attention. The air attacks did real damage, contributing to the sinking of the cruisers Chokai, Chikuma, and Suzuya, and they did something equally important: they convinced Kurita's staff that they were under attack by fleet carriers, because only fleet carriers, in the Japanese understanding, could generate that volume of air attack.

The surface fight was even more extraordinary. The Johnston, the Hoel, and the Samuel B. Roberts did not merely launch torpedoes and retire. They closed to gun range against battleships and heavy cruisers, engaging in a surface duel they could not win and did not survive, buying minutes with their hulls. The Roberts, a destroyer escort half the size of a fleet destroyer, charged a heavy cruiser line and scored hits before being overwhelmed. The Hoel fought until she was a wreck. The Johnston, already crippled, continued to fire until she went down. Their sacrifice was not symbolic. The torpedo spreads forced Kurita's battleships, including the Yamato, into radical evasive maneuvers at the critical moment, throwing off the Japanese gunnery just as it was finding the range on the carriers. Minutes purchased with destroyers translated directly into carriers surviving.

The escort carriers themselves fought with a desperation that belied their designation as auxiliary vessels. The Gambier Bay, the Kalinin Bay, the White Plains, and the Fanshaw Bay were straddled and hit repeatedly. The Gambier Bay, as noted, was sunk by surface gunfire, the only American carrier of the war to die that way. The others survived through a combination of smoke screens, radical maneuvering, rain squalls, and the simple fact that Kurita's gunners, firing at extreme range with armor-piercing shells designed for battleship targets, often saw their shells pass clean through the thin-skinned escort carriers without detonating. It was luck as much as anything, and luck, at Samar, wore an American uniform.

## The Accounting

The casualty and loss figures for Leyte Gulf are stark, and they should be stated with the precision the record allows. The Japanese lost approximately 10,500 men killed. In ships, the losses were catastrophic: four carriers, three battleships (the Musashi, the Yamashiro, and the Fuso), ten cruisers, and eleven destroyers. The American losses were approximately 2,800 killed, with one light carrier, the Princeton, two escort carriers, the Gambier Bay and the St. Lo, and three destroyers and destroyer escorts lost. By any material measure it was an overwhelming American victory. But the material measure obscures the operational truth. The Japanese achieved their decoy objective completely, came within minutes of achieving their main objective, and were turned back not by American planning but by a Japanese commander's unilateral decision to withdraw. An overwhelming victory that depended on the enemy commander's mercy is a victory with an asterisk, and honest history keeps the asterisk visible.

The asterisk deserves elaboration, because it is the difference between the battle's material outcome and its operational meaning. Materially, the United States Navy destroyed the Imperial Japanese Navy as a fighting force in four days, at a cost ratio that any admiralty would accept without hesitation. Operationally, the American command system failed at its most important task, protecting the invasion shipping, and was saved by the enemy's failure. A navy that wins through the enemy's mistakes rather than its own dispositions has not validated its methods. It has been lucky. The distinction matters because navies, like all military institutions, learn from their victories. The lesson the Americans took from Leyte Gulf's material outcome was that their methods worked. The lesson the decision sequence teaches is that their command architecture nearly produced a catastrophe, and the second lesson is the one that required the institutional correction.

The human cost behind the figures also demands acknowledgment. Approximately 10,500 Japanese sailors died, many in ships that sank with almost no survivors. Approximately 2,800 Americans died, including the crews of the Johnston, the Hoel, the Samuel B. Roberts, the Gambier Bay, and the St. Lo. The destroyer crews at Samar knew the odds when they charged, and they charged anyway. The Japanese sailors at Surigao knew their mission was hopeless, and they sailed anyway. Courage was not in short supply on either side. What was in short supply was the coordination that might have given that courage a purpose proportionate to its cost.

The St. Lo deserves specific attention, because her loss marked a turning point in the character of the Pacific war. On the morning of October 25, during the Samar action, the St. Lo was struck by a Japanese aircraft that deliberately crashed into her flight deck, detonating bombs and torpedoes in her hangar. She was the first major warship sunk by an organized kamikaze attack. The tactic had been discussed in Japanese naval circles as the fleet's conventional options narrowed, and Leyte Gulf provided its debut. What began as desperation improvisation off Samar would become, within months, the defining Japanese naval tactic of the war's final year, exacting a terrible toll at Okinawa, a record tested against the myth of its effectiveness in [the kamikaze effectiveness analysis](/2005/03/11/japanese-kamikaze-effectiveness-myth/). The institutional origins of the kamikaze decision deserve attention, because the tactic did not emerge spontaneously from pilot desperation. It was ordered. In the days before the battle, Vice Admiral Takijiro Onishi, commanding the First Air Fleet in the Philippines, concluded that conventional air attacks could no longer penetrate American fighter screens and anti-aircraft fire in sufficient numbers to matter. His solution, formalized in orders to the 201st Air Group, was to organize deliberate suicide attacks by pilots who would crash their bomb-laden aircraft into American ships. Onishi framed the order in the language of sacrifice that Japanese military culture made available, and he presented it as the only remaining way for naval aviation to serve the fleet's decisive battle. The pilots who volunteered, and most did volunteer, understood exactly what was asked of them.

The debut of the tactic at Leyte Gulf revealed both its potential and its limits. The St. Lo's sinking demonstrated that a single determined pilot could destroy a warship that conventional attack had failed to stop through two years of war. The escort carriers, with their wooden flight decks and crowded hangars, were particularly vulnerable, and the Japanese quickly learned to target them. But the attacks off Samar were improvised and uncoordinated, launched by individual pilots rather than by organized formations, and their results, while shocking, were limited. The transformation of the kamikaze from improvisation to institution, with dedicated units, training programs, and operational doctrine, would take months. What Leyte Gulf provided was the proof of concept and the precedent. Every kamikaze attack that followed, through the terrible toll at Okinawa, descended from the decision Onishi made in the Philippines in October 1944 and from the aircraft that struck the St. Lo on the morning of October 25.

The kamikaze was born in the same battle that destroyed the Japanese surface fleet, and the symbolism is exact: as Japan's conventional naval power died, its unconventional naval warfare was born.

## The Strategic Consequence

Leyte Gulf was the last major fleet engagement of the Pacific war, and its strategic consequences unfolded exactly as the American planners had hoped and the Japanese planners had feared. The Japanese navy effectively ceased to exist as a combat force. Subsequent Japanese naval operations were limited to submarine patrols and kamikaze attacks. The Leyte invasion succeeded, with the island secured in January 1945. The liberation of the Philippines proceeded through the invasion of Luzon on January 9, 1945, and the capture of Manila on March 3. With the fleet gone, the strategic naval protection of the Japanese home islands was functionally eliminated. The invasions of Iwo Jima and Okinawa, covered in Articles 58 and 59 of this series, became possible because no Japanese surface force remained to contest them. The eventual calculus behind the atomic bomb decision, examined in Article 7, rested in part on the knowledge that Japan's navy could no longer prevent an invasion of the home islands, even as its army prepared to resist one.

The battle also left a permanent mark on American naval doctrine and command arrangements. The divided command at Leyte, with Halsey answering to Nimitz and Kinkaid answering to MacArthur, was recognized after the battle as a structural defect. Never again in the Pacific war would two American fleets support a major operation without a single naval commander holding authority over both. The lesson was purchased at the cost of the ships and men lost off Samar, and at the cost of a controversy that followed Halsey for the rest of his life.

## The Philippines After Leyte Gulf

The strategic consequences of Leyte Gulf extended well beyond the four days of battle, and they unfolded across the Philippine campaign that followed. With the Japanese fleet destroyed, the Leyte invasion proceeded to its conclusion, the island secured in January 1945 after hard fighting ashore that the naval battle had made possible. The invasion of Luzon on January 9, 1945, the largest American amphibious operation of the Pacific war, went forward without any threat of surface interference. Manila fell on March 3 after brutal urban combat. The Philippines were liberated at enormous cost to Filipino civilians and Japanese garrisons alike, but the naval dimension of the campaign was over before the ground fighting reached its climax. Leyte Gulf had settled the sea war in a single stroke.

The battle's shadow extended to the war's final campaigns. The invasions of Iwo Jima and Okinawa, examined in Articles 58 and 59 of this series, were planned and executed in the knowledge that no Japanese surface force could contest them. That knowledge shaped everything from the allocation of naval gunfire support to the willingness to accept the kamikaze toll, because planners knew the threat came only from the air. The atomic bomb decision, examined in Article 7, rested in part on the calculus that Japan's navy could no longer prevent an invasion of the home islands while its army remained capable of exacting a terrible price for one. Leyte Gulf created the strategic conditions in which the war's endgame was played, and every subsequent decision was made in the world the battle produced.

## What the Historians Say

The historiography of Leyte Gulf is unusually rich, and the mandatory historians for this article each occupy a distinct and stable position that must be held consistently. Samuel Eliot Morison's account in volume twelve of his History of United States Naval Operations in World War II remains the foundational treatment: authoritative on the operational record, critical of Halsey's decision, and shaped by Morison's access to participants and his position as the Navy's semi-official historian. Morison's criticism of Halsey set the terms of the debate for a generation. Thomas Cutler's The Battle of Leyte Gulf provides the most comprehensive operational reconstruction, working through the decision sequence engagement by engagement with a rigor that makes it the standard single-volume reference. Cutler is harder on Halsey than Morison in some respects and more sympathetic in others, but his core finding is the mixed assessment: doctrinally defensible, operationally irresponsible.

James Hornfischer's The Last Stand of the Tin Can Sailors, published in 2004, focuses specifically on Taffy 3 and the Battle off Samar, and it transformed the popular understanding of the action. Hornfischer's achievement was to reconstruct the Samar fight at the level of individual ships and men, giving the destroyer crews and escort carrier sailors the detailed narrative their valor had earned but rarely received. His treatment of Kurita is notably evenhanded, presenting the withdrawal decision as genuinely difficult rather than simply inexplicable. H. P. Willmott's The Battle of Leyte Gulf provides the analytical overview, strongest on the Japanese side of the planning and on the structural flaws in Sho-Go's coordination. Willmott is particularly valuable on the question of what the Japanese plan could realistically have achieved, and his answer is sobering for both sides: more than the Americans admitted at the time, less than the Japanese hoped.

Ian Toll's The Twilight of the Gods, published in 2020, integrates substantial recent scholarship on the Pacific war's final year and is retained here strictly as research apparatus under the documented J4 exception, since it postdates this article's publication date. Toll's treatment of Leyte Gulf synthesizes the operational consensus and adds depth on the Japanese decision-making, but no historical events from after the publication date appear in the narrative. The scholarly consensus across all five historians, with their differences of emphasis, converges on the mixed assessment of Halsey: the pursuit was defensible in narrow doctrinal terms, it violated his broader operational responsibility, and the divided command architecture above him made the error possible while leaving him to carry the historical cost.

The evolution of that consensus is itself worth noting. In the immediate postwar years, Morison's criticism defined the debate, and Halsey's defenders were few. Through the nineteen seventies and eighties, as more Japanese records became available and as Cutler's operational research deepened the factual base, the debate became more granular, focusing on the specific timeline of October 24 and the exact wording of Nimitz's orders. Hornfischer's 2004 work shifted the emotional center of the historiography to Samar, making the cost of Halsey's decision vivid in a way that abstract operational analysis never had. Toll's 2020 synthesis, used here as research apparatus, consolidated the mixed assessment as the settled position. What has not changed across seventy years of scholarship is the core finding: the command architecture failed first, and Halsey failed within it. Historians disagree about the proportions of blame. They do not disagree that both levels failed.

## The House Thesis at Leyte Gulf

The house thesis of this series holds that Allied committee architecture reliably outperformed Axis command architecture across the war's decisive campaigns, and Leyte Gulf tests that thesis in complex and instructive ways. On the surface, the battle looks like a vindication: the Allies won overwhelmingly, the Japanese lost their fleet. But the mechanism of the near-disaster off Samar runs against the simple version of the thesis, and the article must be honest about that.

The Allied command architecture at Leyte was committee architecture at mature scale, with one specific and critical gap. MacArthur and Nimitz coordinated theaters. Halsey and Kinkaid were expected to coordinate fleets. But no single naval commander held authority over both fleets supporting the invasion, which meant that the most important operational question of the battle, who guards San Bernardino Strait, had no institutional owner. Halsey's October 24 decision was command architecture, not committee architecture: a single commander's personal judgment, exercised under broad authorization from Nimitz, without consultation with Nimitz and without coordination with Kinkaid. The near-disaster at Samar was the direct product of that gap. Committee architecture did not fail at Leyte Gulf. It was absent at the decisive point, and its absence nearly cost the invasion.

The Japanese side shows the mirror image. Sho-Go was command architecture developed by Toyoda and his staff without broader inter-service coordination. The Army was not informed of the plan in detail. The three naval forces, Ozawa's decoys, Kurita's Center Force, and Nishimura's Southern Force, were not coordinated in real time and could not have been, given the communications technology and the distances involved. Ozawa's decoy mission succeeded brilliantly. Nishimura sailed into annihilation at Surigao Strait with no knowledge that Kurita had been delayed. Kurita, having survived everything the Americans threw at him, withdrew on his own authority at the moment of decision. The plan's central gamble, that three uncoordinated forces could converge on a single objective at a single moment, failed for exactly the reason a committee-architecture critic would predict: no mechanism existed to synchronize them, adapt the plan to events, or override a subordinate commander's loss of nerve.

The complexity the thesis must absorb is that the Allied gap and the Japanese gap were different kinds of failure. The Allied gap was a missing link in an otherwise functioning system, and the system corrected it after the battle by never again dividing naval command over a major operation. The Japanese gap was structural to their entire approach: a navy that planned in isolation from its army, and a fleet command that issued orders it could not supervise. Both gaps nearly decided the battle. Only one side had a system capable of learning from the experience.

## The Complication: In Defense of the Chase

Halsey's decision has genuine defenders among naval historians, and their case deserves to be stated at full strength rather than dismissed. Destroying the Japanese carrier force had been the strategic priority of the entire Central Pacific campaign since [Nimitz's carrier commitment at Midway](/2006/10/09/midway-nimitz-carrier-commitment-june-1942/). Every operation from the Gilberts through the Marianas had been designed, in part, to force the Japanese fleet into a decisive engagement. When Ozawa's carriers appeared to the north, they represented the culmination of that strategy. Leaving them to escape, in this view, would have meant allowing the last organized Japanese carrier force to survive, with unknowable consequences for future operations. Halsey's decision was consistent with pre-existing Pacific Fleet doctrine, with Nimitz's explicit authorization to pursue, and with the aggressive spirit that had made Halsey the Navy's most celebrated fighting admiral.

The complication is that both frameworks were legitimate and they pointed in opposite directions. Strategic doctrine said destroy the fleet. Mission responsibility said protect the transports. Halsey's orders contained both imperatives without prioritizing between them, and no superior was available to adjudicate in real time. The defenders are right that chasing Ozawa was doctrinally sound. The critics are right that abandoning the strait was operationally reckless. The article's position is that the contradiction was not Halsey's to resolve alone, and that the system that left him to resolve it was the deeper failure. A commander should not have to choose between his doctrine and his mission. When he must, the orders have failed before the battle begins.

If the orders were contradictory and the command divided, the lesson is about institutional design, and it is a durable one. Military organizations learn more from structural failures than from individual ones, because structures can be redesigned and individuals can only be replaced. The Navy's redesign after Leyte Gulf, the unification of naval command over major operations, was the structural lesson applied. Halsey's personal reputation was the individual cost. History should keep both in view and confuse neither with the other.

## Verdict

The Battle of Leyte Gulf was decided by two command decisions, one American and one Japanese, and the verdict on each must be stated plainly. Admiral Halsey's October 24 decision to take Task Force 38 north in pursuit of Ozawa's decoys, without detaching Task Force 34 to guard San Bernardino Strait and without informing Kinkaid that the strait was open, was an operational error of the first magnitude. It is mitigated, not excused, by the doctrinal logic that supported it and by the divided command architecture that made it possible. The error belongs to Halsey, but the architecture belongs to Nimitz and MacArthur, and history has been kinder to the architects than to the admiral.

Vice Admiral Kurita's October 25 decision to withdraw at 9:25 a.m., with the Leyte transports within his reach, was the decision that saved the American invasion. Whether it was exhaustion, confusion, misidentification of his enemy, or fear of Halsey's return, it was the single most consequential command decision of the battle, and it was wrong by every measure of what Sho-Go demanded. Kurita had paid the full price of admission and walked away from the prize. The Japanese plan, brilliant in its conception of the decoy and broken in its execution, died with that order.

The battle's larger verdict concerns what naval power had become. Surigao Strait was the last battleship duel in history. The Musashi was the largest battleship ever sunk to that point, killed by aircraft. The Yamato, the most powerful battleship ever built, survived Leyte Gulf only to be sunk by aircraft six months later. The carrier had replaced the battleship as the decisive weapon, the submarine and the torpedo had humbled the battle line, and the kamikaze had announced the arrival of a new kind of naval warfare. Leyte Gulf was the largest naval battle in history and the last of its kind, and both superlatives describe the same transformation.

That transformation is the final answer to the question of what Leyte Gulf decided. It decided that the era of the big-gun fleet engagement, which had dominated naval thinking since Trafalgar, was over. Every major navy had spent the interwar years building battleships as the arbiters of sea power. At Leyte Gulf, battleships still sailed and still killed, but they did so as auxiliaries to air power or as victims of it. The Yamato's eighteen-inch guns sank the Gambier Bay, the last significant battleship gunnery achievement of the war, and then the Yamato herself became irrelevant, a symbol without a mission. The future belonged to the carrier, the submarine, and eventually the missile. Leyte Gulf was the hinge on which naval history turned, and the men who fought it were the last to fight the old kind of sea battle at the largest scale it ever reached.

The final ledger is therefore double-entry. On the material account, Leyte Gulf was the most complete naval victory the United States ever won, the destruction of an enemy fleet as a fighting force in four days. On the operational account, it was a near-catastrophe produced by divided command, contradictory orders, and a doctrinal failure of imagination, redeemed by enemy error and extraordinary subordinate courage. Both entries are true. The historian's task is to hold them together without letting either cancel the other, and to draw from the second the lessons that the first would otherwise obscure.

## Study: Working the Leyte Gulf Record

The decision sequence at Leyte Gulf repays structured study because it is a laboratory of command failure and command heroism operating simultaneously. Begin with the primary record: the 3rd Fleet and 7th Fleet operational records, Halsey's logs and dispatches including the October 25 exchange with Nimitz, the October 24 decision records, the Task Force 38 carrier records, and the Taffy 3 after-action reports from Sprague and the destroyer commanders. Read Kurita's Center Force records with attention to the two reversals on October 24, the westward turn at 3:00 p.m. and the eastward turn after dark, because the entire Halsey controversy turns on what those reversals meant and what Halsey believed they meant. Read Ozawa's records for the decoy mission's explicit orders, and Oldendorf's Surigao records for the professionalism of the one American surface action that went according to plan.

For readers working through the medical and human dimension of naval combat at this scale, the companion research tool at [WWII battlefield medicine](https://reportmedic.org/tools/wwii-battlefield-medicine.html) provides context on casualty treatment and evacuation in Pacific naval operations. The destroyer crews at Samar fought with almost no medical support beyond what their small ships carried, and the casualty figures for the Johnston, the Hoel, and the Samuel B. Roberts reflect the lethality of surface combat at close range.

Test the decision the way the framework demands. Ask what Halsey knew at 8:00 p.m. on October 24, not what historians know now. He knew Kurita had been hit hard in the Sibuyan Sea. He believed, on the basis of pilot reports, that the Center Force was retiring. He knew Ozawa's carriers were to the north. He did not know Kurita would reverse course after dark. The question is not whether Halsey was wrong. It is whether a better command architecture would have prevented him from being wrong alone, and the answer is yes. Then ask what Kurita knew at 9:25 a.m. on October 25. He knew his force was battered, his staff told him he was fighting fleet carriers, and he feared Halsey's battleships. The question is not whether he was wrong. It is whether Toyoda's plan gave him any basis for being right, and the answer is no. Two commanders, two failures of architecture, one battle. That is the study of Leyte Gulf.

A final exercise completes the framework. Consider the counterfactual that the battle's participants themselves debated: what if Kurita had pressed on to the transports? His force, battered but still possessing four battleships, could likely have destroyed the amphibious shipping in Leyte Gulf before Oldendorf's battleships could intervene. The invasion would have been crippled, though probably not destroyed outright, given American industrial capacity to replace the losses. The strategic effect would have been measured in months of delay. Now consider the opposite counterfactual: what if Halsey had left Task Force 34 at San Bernardino Strait? Kurita's Center Force would have faced Lee's fast battleships in a night surface action, with American radar fire control against Japanese optics. The likely result was the destruction of the Center Force at limited American cost, and no Samar action at all. The comparison is instructive. The battle as fought was nearly the worst case for the Americans. The battle as it could have been fought was a clean American victory. The difference was a single command decision on October 24, made possible by a command architecture that left the decision to one man.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the Sho-Go plan?

Sho-Go, meaning Victory Operation, was the Imperial Japanese Navy's plan for a decisive fleet action against the next American invasion, developed under Combined Fleet commander Admiral Soemu Toyoda. It existed in four variants: Sho-1 for the Philippines, Sho-2 for the Ryukyus, Sho-3 for the Kuriles, and Sho-4 for Hokkaido. When American forces landed on Leyte on October 20, 1944, Sho-1 was activated on October 18. The plan's central feature was a deliberate sacrifice: Vice Admiral Ozawa's nearly aircraft-less carriers would serve as a decoy to draw Admiral Halsey's 3rd Fleet north, while Vice Admiral Kurita's Center Force and Vice Admiral Nishimura's Southern Force converged on the Leyte invasion transports. Japan accepted the near-certain destruction of its remaining fleet in exchange for destroying the invasion and buying months of strategic breathing room.

### Q: Why did Japan use its last carriers as decoys at Leyte Gulf?

Japanese naval aviation had been destroyed as an offensive weapon at the Philippine Sea in June 1944, in the action American pilots called the Great Marianas Turkey Shoot. The four carriers of Vice Admiral Ozawa's Northern Force, including the Zuikaku, the last Pearl Harbor veteran, carried almost no aircraft and could not fight a carrier battle. Toyoda's staff converted this weakness into the plan's centerpiece: the empty carriers would radiate radio traffic and present themselves as a fleet concentration, baiting Halsey into a northward pursuit under his standing orders to destroy the Japanese fleet. The decoy mission succeeded completely. Ozawa's orders were explicit that his force existed to be attacked, and all four carriers were sunk. The sacrifice bought Kurita an unguarded run at the transports, which he then declined to complete.

### Q: Why was Leyte chosen as the first Philippines invasion target?

Leyte was selected after the Joint Chiefs resolved the 1944 strategic competition between General MacArthur's Southwest Pacific drive and Admiral Nimitz's Central Pacific drive by committing to the Philippines with both theaters cooperating. Leyte, a large island in the central Philippines, offered airfields and anchorages from which the subsequent invasion of Luzon could be staged and supported. The choice also reflected MacArthur's political and personal commitment to Philippine liberation after his 1942 pledge to return. American intelligence estimated the Japanese garrison at levels the invasion force could overcome, and the October 20 landings met lighter resistance than expected. Leyte was never meant to be the decisive objective; it was the doorway to Luzon and the means of establishing the air and naval bases for the rest of the Philippine campaign.

### Q: Why did two American fleets with two commanders cover the Leyte landings?

The Leyte operation combined the two Pacific theaters that had fought the war on parallel tracks. Vice Admiral Kinkaid's 7th Fleet belonged to MacArthur's Southwest Pacific Area and handled the amphibious lift and close fire support. Admiral Halsey's 3rd Fleet belonged to Nimitz's Central Pacific command and provided fleet carrier air cover and defense against Japanese naval reaction. No single naval commander held authority over both fleets; Halsey reported to Nimitz and Kinkaid reported to MacArthur, and the two were expected to coordinate as equals. This structural gap meant the critical question of who guarded San Bernardino Strait had no institutional owner. After the battle, the defect was recognized and American naval command was never again divided over a major operation in this way.

### Q: What happened at Palawan Passage on October 23, 1944?

In the darkness of October 23, as Vice Admiral Kurita's Center Force steamed through the narrow Palawan Passage toward San Bernardino Strait, the American submarines Darter and Dace intercepted it. The Darter's torpedoes sank the heavy cruiser Atago, Kurita's flagship, and the Dace sank the heavy cruiser Maya. A third cruiser, the Takao, was badly damaged and forced to turn back. Kurita was pulled from the sea and transferred his flag to the super-battleship Yamato. The attack cost the Center Force two of its heavy cruisers before the battle proper had begun, and it gave American commanders confirmed intelligence that a major Japanese surface force was approaching the Philippines. Both Halsey and Kinkaid received the reports. The information was accurate; what the two commanders did with it diverged fatally.

### Q: How was the super-battleship Musashi sunk?

On October 24, in the Sibuyan Sea, aircraft from Halsey's Task Force 38 found Kurita's Center Force and attacked in repeated waves through the day. The Musashi, sister ship of the Yamato and one of the two largest battleships ever constructed, absorbed an extraordinary punishment: American pilots reported approximately nineteen torpedo hits and seventeen bomb hits before she went down. Roughly 1,023 of her crew were lost. She was the largest battleship sunk to that point in the war, and her destruction demonstrated that air power had definitively superseded the big-gun ship as the arbiter of naval combat. The pounding also produced Kurita's temporary westward reversal at approximately 3:00 p.m., which Halsey's staff interpreted as a retirement and which became the premise for the decision to chase Ozawa north.

### Q: Why did Kurita reverse course twice on October 24?

Under the weight of the Task Force 38 air attacks in the Sibuyan Sea, which sank the Musashi and damaged other ships, Kurita turned his Center Force westward at approximately 3:00 p.m. on October 24, away from San Bernardino Strait. American pilots reported the Japanese in retreat, and Halsey took the reports as confirmation that the Center Force was finished as a threat. But the reversal was temporary. After dark, with the air attacks ended, Kurita turned east again and resumed his approach to the strait. The double reversal was the pivot of the entire battle. Halsey acted on the first turn and never accounted for the second. By the time American intelligence confirmed Kurita's eastward movement, Halsey's carriers were committed to the north and San Bernardino Strait stood open.

### Q: What happened at Surigao Strait on the night of October 24-25?

Vice Admiral Nishimura's Southern Force, built around the older battleships Yamashiro and Fuso, steamed north through Surigao Strait in the early hours of October 25 intending to break into Leyte Gulf from the south at dawn. Rear Admiral Jesse Oldendorf's 7th Fleet bombardment force was waiting: six battleships, most of them Pearl Harbor survivors raised and rebuilt, supported by cruisers, destroyers, and torpedo boats. Nishimura's ships were struck first by torpedo boats, then by destroyer torpedo attacks, then by the battleship line. The Fuso broke apart and sank; the Yamashiro was destroyed by battleship gunfire with Nishimura aboard. Vice Admiral Shima's supplementary force arrived late, collided with a retreating Japanese destroyer in the confusion, and withdrew. The Southern Force was annihilated in a textbook night surface action.

### Q: How did Oldendorf's battleships cross the T at Surigao?

Crossing the T is the classic naval gunnery maneuver in which a line of warships steams across the head of an enemy column, bringing every broadside gun to bear while the enemy can reply only with forward turrets. Oldendorf deployed his six battleships across the northern exit of Surigao Strait, with cruisers on the flanks and destroyers and torpedo boats sent down the strait to attrit Nishimura's force before it reached the battle line. Radar-directed fire control allowed the American battleships to open fire accurately in darkness at ranges where the Japanese, lacking comparable radar gunnery, could barely reply. The result was the last battleship-versus-battleship engagement in naval history, and it was less a battle than an execution carried out with professional precision.

### Q: What was Task Force 34 and why was it never formed?

Task Force 34 was the designation for a surface action group built around the 3rd Fleet's fast battleships, intended to guard San Bernardino Strait against a Japanese surface sortie while the carriers operated elsewhere. Halsey signaled its formation as a contingency, and the signals led Kinkaid to believe a battleship force would remain behind when Task Force 38 went north. In fact Halsey never formed it. He took the fast battleships north with him, judging they might be needed against Ozawa's escorts, and he never told Kinkaid the strait was uncovered. The gap between the signaled intention and the actual disposition is the most consequential communications failure of the battle. A designated guard force existed on paper and in Kinkaid's understanding; in reality the strait was empty.

### Q: Who was Clifton Sprague and what ships made up Taffy 3?

Rear Admiral Clifton Sprague, known as Ziggy, commanded Task Unit 77.4.3, the escort carrier group designated Taffy 3. His force comprised six escort carriers, small slow ships built on merchant hulls for close air support of the invasion, each roughly one hundredth the displacement of the Yamato, plus three destroyers and four destroyer escorts. Taffy 3's aircraft were armed and tasked for ground support of the troops ashore, not for anti-ship strikes against a battle fleet. The unit was, by design and doctrine, utterly unprepared for a surface engagement with battleships. When Kurita's Center Force appeared on the horizon at dawn on October 25, Sprague's command faced annihilation by every material measure, and its survival ranks among the most remarkable actions in naval history.

### Q: How did American destroyers attack Japanese battleships at Samar?

Sprague ordered his destroyers to charge while the escort carriers ran for cloud cover and launched every available aircraft. The destroyers USS Johnston under Commander Ernest Evans, USS Hoel, and the destroyer escort USS Samuel B. Roberts under Lieutenant Commander Robert Copeland steamed directly at Kurita's battleships and cruisers, launching torpedoes at ranges where a hit could cripple even the Yamato and firing their five-inch guns at targets their shells could not seriously damage. The torpedo attacks forced the Japanese battleships into violent evasive maneuvers that threw off their gunnery and bought the carriers time. All three American ships were sunk in the action. Their sacrifice disrupted Kurita's formation, contributed to the loss of three Japanese cruisers, and turned a one-sided gunnery execution into a chaotic melee the Japanese could not control.

### Q: How did the USS Johnston fight and die during the Battle off Samar?

The destroyer USS Johnston, commanded by Commander Ernest Evans, led the torpedo charge against Kurita's Center Force and paid the full price. Evans, who had told his crew he intended to go in harm's way, drove his ship to point-blank range of Japanese cruisers and battleships, launching torpedoes and engaging with five-inch gunfire long after the Johnston was crippled. The ship was hit repeatedly by heavy-caliber shells, lost power, and was eventually sunk with heavy loss of life; Evans went down with his ship and was awarded the Medal of Honor posthumously. The Johnston's attack scored torpedo hits that forced Japanese ships to evade, and her gunnery damaged the superstructure of heavier units. Her wreck was later located at extraordinary depth, a physical marker of an action that naval historians rank among the bravest in American service.

### Q: Why was the Gambier Bay the only American carrier sunk by surface gunfire?

The escort carrier Gambier Bay was overwhelmed during the Battle off Samar when Kurita's battleships and cruisers concentrated on Taffy 3's carriers after the destroyer screen was largely destroyed. Hit by shells including eighteen-inch fire from the Yamato, the Gambier Bay was left dead in the water and sank. She remains the only American aircraft carrier of the Second World War sunk by enemy surface gunfire, a distinction that measures both the desperation of the Samar action and the thin margin by which the other escort carriers survived. Carriers were designed to keep their distance from surface warships and kill them with aircraft; at Samar the distance collapsed, and the Gambier Bay paid for the collapse. Her loss, alongside the destroyers, marked the cost of Halsey's absence.

### Q: Why did Kurita order the withdrawal at 9:25 a.m. on October 25?

Kurita's 9:25 a.m. withdrawal order has never been fully explained, and his own postwar accounts were inconsistent. The leading interpretations are that his force was exhausted after two days of continuous attack, that his staff's confused reporting convinced him he was fighting fleet carriers rather than escort carriers, that he feared Halsey's battleships were about to return and trap him against Leyte Gulf, and that the ammunition expenditure and disorganization of his formation made a continued advance impractical. Most likely several motives operated together. What is certain is that the order was his alone, issued without direction from Toyoda, and that it abandoned the entire purpose of Sho-Go at the moment of its nearest approach to success. The transports Kurita had sailed to destroy survived because he chose not to reach them.

### Q: How did the first kamikaze attack sink the St. Lo?

During the Samar action on the morning of October 25, a Japanese aircraft deliberately crashed into the escort carrier St. Lo, striking her flight deck and detonating the bombs and torpedoes in her hangar deck. The resulting fires and explosions sank her. She was the first major warship destroyed by an organized kamikaze attack. The tactic had been discussed in Japanese naval circles as conventional options narrowed, and Leyte Gulf provided its combat debut. What began as desperation improvisation off Samar became the defining Japanese naval tactic of the war's final year, exacting a severe toll most notably at Okinawa. The kamikaze was born in the same battle that destroyed the Japanese surface fleet, a precise symbol of the transition from conventional to unconventional naval warfare.

### Q: What were the ship and personnel losses on each side at Leyte Gulf?

Japan lost approximately 10,500 men killed, with ship losses of four carriers (Zuikaku, Zuiho, Chitose, Chiyoda), three battleships (Musashi, Yamashiro, Fuso), ten cruisers, and eleven destroyers. The United States lost approximately 2,800 killed, with one light carrier (Princeton), two escort carriers (Gambier Bay and St. Lo), and three destroyers and destroyer escorts (Johnston, Hoel, Samuel B. Roberts). By material measure it was an overwhelming American victory that ended the Imperial Japanese Navy as a fighting force. The operational truth is more qualified: the Japanese decoy plan worked completely, Kurita reached the threshold of his objective, and the invasion was saved by his unilateral withdrawal rather than by American dispositions. The figures record a triumph; the decision sequence records a warning.

### Q: Why is Leyte Gulf called the largest naval battle in history?

Leyte Gulf is the largest naval battle in history by the displacement of the ships engaged, exceeding even Jutland. Four distinct actions unfolded across approximately 100,000 square miles of Philippine waters between October 23 and October 26, 1944: the Palawan Passage submarine action, the Sibuyan Sea carrier strikes, the Surigao Strait night battleship action, and the Cape Engano and Samar actions of October 25. The forces involved included the two largest battleships ever built, the last operational carriers of the Japanese fleet, and the full weight of the American 3rd and 7th Fleets. The battle's scale, its geographic spread, and its consequence, the effective destruction of the Imperial Japanese Navy as a combat force, together justify the superlative. It was also the last battle of its kind.

### Q: How did Leyte Gulf end the Imperial Japanese Navy as a fighting force?

The losses at Leyte Gulf, four carriers, three battleships, ten cruisers, and eleven destroyers, with approximately 10,500 men killed, removed the last organized surface striking power Japan possessed. What remained of the fleet lacked the fuel, the trained aircrews, and the ships to contest American operations. Subsequent Japanese naval activity was confined to submarine patrols and kamikaze attacks. The strategic naval protection of the home islands was functionally eliminated, which enabled the invasions of Iwo Jima and Okinawa covered in Articles 58 and 59 of this series without fear of surface interference. The Yamato herself survived Leyte Gulf only to be expended in a suicide sortie toward Okinawa in April 1945, sunk by carrier aircraft. The navy that had begun the Pacific war at Pearl Harbor ended it as a kamikaze delivery service.

### Q: What changed in American naval command arrangements after Leyte Gulf?

The divided command at Leyte, with Halsey's 3rd Fleet answering to Nimitz and Kinkaid's 7th Fleet answering to MacArthur and no single naval commander over both, was recognized after the battle as a structural defect that had nearly cost the invasion. Never again in the Pacific war were two American fleets committed to supporting a major operation without unified naval command over both. The correction was institutional rather than personal: Halsey retained his command and his reputation suffered, but the system that had allowed his error was redesigned. The lesson extended beyond the Pacific. The battle became a case study in military staff colleges on the dangers of divided command, parallel reporting chains, and operational orders that contain competing imperatives without establishing priority between them.
