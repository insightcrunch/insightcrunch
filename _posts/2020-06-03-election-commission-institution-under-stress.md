The Election Commission of India enters most textbooks as a piece of constitutional machinery, a body that prepares rolls, notifies schedules, counts ballots and declares results, and the description is accurate in the way a description of a bridge as concrete and steel is accurate. It leaves out the loads. The claim defended in this article is that the Election Commission is an institution under continuous structural-political stress, running from the founding of the republic through every decade since, and that electoral institutions are political objects rather than neutral procedural infrastructure. The stress is not an episode. It is a condition built into the constitutional text of 1950, reproduced by the way the institution is staffed and financed, and sharpened by the fact that every decision the Commission takes redistributes advantage among the parties it regulates. To read the Commission as machinery is to miss the politics that the machinery carries, and the post-1950 record, from Sukumar Sen's improvisation of 1951 to the appointment legislation of 2023, is legible as a single long negotiation between a powerful constitutional mandate and the pressures that mandate attracts.

The argument proceeds through a framework of three components, each grounded in named texts and dated episodes rather than in general complaint. The first component is constitutional and statutory under-specification: Article 324 of the Constitution grants the Commission superintendence, direction and control over elections in a handful of clauses, and leaves nearly every operational question to later statutes, to rules, and to the Commission's own discretion. The second component is executive-architectural dependence: the Commission draws its budget through the Union government, receives its commissioners through appointments the executive long controlled outright, borrows the district administration and the police of the states to run polling, and relies on public sector manufacturers for its voting machines. The third component is the political-strategic stake: because schedules, roll revisions, model code rulings and symbol orders change electoral outcomes, political actors press upon the Commission constantly, and the pressure intensifies at the moments of highest consequence. Each component names a mechanism. Together they explain why the institution has been contested in every era, under governments of every complexion, and why the contestation takes recognisable forms that recur across seventy years.

This reading has a rival, and the rival deserves its strongest statement. The institutional-procedural account, carried in the standard public administration literature and in the Commission's own self-description, treats the Election Commission as a constitutional functionary whose authority is adequately described by listing its powers: Article 324, the Representation of the People Acts of 1950 and 1951, the rules framed under them, and a body of Supreme Court interpretation. On that account, controversy around the Commission is episodic, a matter of individual commissioners, errant governments or unusual elections, and the institution itself is a stable piece of infrastructure that delivers elections the way a railway delivers trains. The adjudication offered here accepts the procedural account's facts and disputes its sufficiency. A list of powers cannot explain why the same office produced Sukumar Sen's quiet improvisation, T. N. Seshan's open warfare with two governments, and the dissent notes of a commissioner in 2019. The institution-under-stress reading can, because it locates the drama not in personalities but in structure: a vast mandate written in few words, held by appointees of the executive the mandate polices, exercised over contests whose outcomes decide who governs.

The long arc of the pillar that this article opens is organised around four passages of that negotiation, each of which a later article in the pillar examines in full. The founding passage belongs to Sukumar Sen, the first Chief Election Commissioner, who between March 1950 and the general election of 1951 and 1952 built an electoral administration for 173 million electors out of a constitutional paragraph and a borrowed bureaucracy. The assertive passage belongs to T. N. Seshan, Chief Election Commissioner from December 1990 to December 1996, who converted the Commission's dormant discretion into an instrument of enforcement and forced the republic to argue about what the institution was for. The technological passage runs from the first experimental electronic voting machines of 1982 through the nationwide machine election of 2004 to the paper trail litigation that reached the Supreme Court in 2013. The contested passage runs through the post-2014 arguments over appointments, schedules, code enforcement and dissent within the Commission itself. A hub article's duty is to set the frame those articles inhabit, and the frame here is that the four passages are one story: an institution designed with deliberate breadth, and stressed, continuously and structurally, by everything that breadth invites.

Two scholarly anchors orient the argument across that arc. M. S. Gill, who served as Chief Election Commissioner from 1996 to 2001 and so wrote as a practitioner as well as an analyst, set out the pre-Seshan analytical treatment in Electoral System: A Suggested Approach of 1976, reasoning about electoral administration as a designed system whose parts could be reformed by argument. David Gilmartin's essays on Indian electoral institutions supply the historian's caution that elections in India have never been mere procedure, that the act of counting heads one by one remade the meaning of political community in a society organised by hierarchy. E. Sridharan's essays on the post-2014 period give the stress reading its most careful contemporary form, tracing how appointment conventions, enforcement asymmetries and public argument have tested the institution's accumulated authority. The three writers disagree with one another in period and in temperament, and the article returns to each at the point where their evidence bears on the framework. Where the sources disagree about characterisation, the disagreement is stated and attributed. Where the record is documentary, the dates, the texts and the votes, it is stated plainly.

## Article 324 and the Election Commission: A Vast Mandate in Few Words

The constitutional text must be read closely, because the whole argument grows out of its proportions. Part XV of the Constitution, running from Article 324 to Article 329, governs elections. Article 324, clause 1, vests the superintendence, direction and control of the preparation of electoral rolls for, and the conduct of, all elections to Parliament, to the legislature of every state, and to the offices of President and Vice-President in an Election Commission. Three verbs of command, superintendence, direction and control, are attached to the entire field of election administration, and no clause of the article enumerates what those verbs permit or forbid in practice. Clause 2 provides that the Commission shall consist of the Chief Election Commissioner and such number of other Election Commissioners, if any, as the President may from time to time fix, with appointments made by the President subject to any law Parliament enacts. Clause 3 makes the Chief Election Commissioner the chairman when other commissioners are appointed. Clause 4 subjects regional commissioners to presidential appointment after consultation with the Commission. Clause 5 carries the tenure bargain at the heart of this article, and clause 6 obliges the President and the state governors to make available to the Commission such staff as it requests for the discharge of its functions.

The Constituent Assembly debated what became Article 324 on 15 and 16 June 1949, when the provision stood as draft Article 289, and the debate records the founders choosing breadth with open eyes. B. R. Ambedkar, moving the provision, explained that the Assembly had decided to make election machinery independent of the executive of the day, centralising it in a single commission rather than leaving elections to provincial governments whose partisan control the members distrusted. K. M. Munshi and other members pressed the opposite anxiety, that centralisation concentrated too much in one office, and amendments sought to distribute the authority or to hedge the appointment power with legislative confirmation. Ambedkar's defence rested on a fear the Assembly broadly shared, formed in the provincial politics of the 1937 and 1946 elections, that a government conducting elections to judge itself would bend the rolls and the machinery. The founding settlement, examined in [the founding-period long-arc context](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/), thus deliberately traded specification for independence: the article says who holds the power and how the holder is protected, and it declines to say, in any operational detail, what the holder may do with it.

Clause 5 repays the closest reading of all, because its asymmetry is the textual seed of the dependence component. The Chief Election Commissioner, the clause provides, shall not be removed from office except in like manner and on the like grounds as a judge of the Supreme Court, which is to say by an address of each House of Parliament supported by special majorities after an inquiry into proved misbehaviour or incapacity. The conditions of service of the Chief Election Commissioner cannot be varied to his disadvantage after appointment. An Election Commissioner or a regional commissioner, by contrast, shall not be removed except on the recommendation of the Chief Election Commissioner. The design is a hierarchy of protection inside a single institution: the head is guarded by the impeachment-grade process, the members are guarded only by the head, and neither member nor head is guarded by any constitutional rule about how they arrive. Appointment is left to clause 2, to the President acting on ministerial advice, subject to a parliamentary law that Parliament did not enact for the seventy-three years between 1950 and 2023. The text therefore combines the strongest available tenure protection at the top with complete openness at the point of entry, and every appointment controversy of the later record is an argument conducted inside that combination.

### What does Article 324(5) provide on removal of commissioners?

Article 324, clause 5, provides that the Chief Election Commissioner can be removed only in like manner and on like grounds as a Supreme Court judge, by parliamentary address after proved misbehaviour or incapacity, while an Election Commissioner or regional commissioner can be removed only on the recommendation of the Chief Election Commissioner.

The conditions-of-service legislation illustrates how the openness at entry persisted through statutory layers. Parliament regulated the commissioners' salaries and terms piecemeal, and the Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners (Conditions of Service) Act of 1991 fixed tenure at six years or the age of sixty-five, whichever came earlier, a settlement that endured until the appointment statute of 2023 recast the whole arrangement. Between 1950 and 1993 the Commission was in practice a single-member body, the Chief Election Commissioner alone, interrupted by two brief experiments: the appointment of two additional commissioners in January 1990, reversed within days by the incoming government, and the durable conversion to a three-member Commission on 1 October 1993, when the government of P. V. Narasimha Rao appointed M. S. Gill and G. V. G. Krishnamurty as Election Commissioners alongside T. N. Seshan. The text of clause 2 had permitted both moves all along, because the number of commissioners was itself left open. Under-specification, in this institution, reaches even the question of how many people the institution is.

The surrounding articles complete the architecture and its limits. Article 325 commands a single general electoral roll for every constituency and bars exclusion on grounds only of religion, race, caste or sex. Article 326 establishes adult suffrage, elections on the basis of the vote of every citizen of eighteen years and above, a threshold lowered from twenty-one by the Sixty-first Amendment of 1988. Article 327 gives Parliament power to legislate on elections to Parliament and the state legislatures, and Article 328 gives the state legislatures a parallel power subject to Parliament's. Article 329 bars judicial interference in electoral matters except through the election petition route the law provides. The scheme is a constitution that fixes the franchise, the custodian and the immunities, and then hands the entire working content of elections to ordinary legislation and to the custodian's discretion. A reader who asks where the rules of Indian elections live will find them in the Representation of the People Acts, in the rules and handbooks the Commission issues, and in the Commission's accumulated practice, and only their frame in the Constitution itself. That distribution of content is the first component of the stress framework, and it is a design fact, not an accusation.

## Under-Specification: The First Component of the Stress Framework

The statutory layer filled part of the constitutional silence, and measuring how much it left open is the way to state the first component precisely. The Representation of the People Act 1950 settled the allocation of seats, the delimitation machinery of its day, the preparation of electoral rolls and the qualifications of electors. The Representation of the People Act 1951 settled the conduct of elections: notifications, nominations, polling, counting, the declaration of results, the corrupt practices and electoral offences that void an election, and the election petition process through which disputes reach the high courts. Together the two Acts run to hundreds of sections, and they create the impression of a complete code. The impression dissolves at the points where the Acts speak in standards rather than rules, or fall silent and leave the field to Article 324. The Commission fixes schedules within broad statutory windows. The Commission superintends roll revision whose intensity and method it designs. The Commission enforces a Model Code of Conduct that appears in no statute at all. The Commission allots symbols under the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order of 1968, an instrument of its own making whose authority the Supreme Court upheld in Sadiq Ali v Election Commission in 1971 on the strength of Article 324 itself.

The Model Code is the cleanest illustration of the component, because its power is real and its legal footing is pure discretion. A code of campaign conduct was first adopted for the Kerala assembly election of 1960 and generalised for the general election of 1962, growing by accretion into the detailed instrument that governs ministerial conduct, campaign speech, processions, polling day behaviour and the use of official machinery. The Commission has repeatedly told Parliament and the courts that the code rests on the moral authority of consensus among parties rather than on statutory sanction, and the arrangement survived because it worked quickly: a code complaint can be answered in hours, where a prosecution takes years. The Goswami Committee on Electoral Reforms, whose report of 1990 examined the post-1989 conditions of electoral politics, discussed giving the code statutory backing and the proposal travelled through later reform documents without enactment. The code therefore stands as governing law in effect and as Commission practice in form, enforceable by censure, by postponement of a rally, by transfer of officers, by the Commission's power over the schedule itself. Every enforcement action taken under it can be, and routinely is, attacked from two directions at once: by the party restrained, as discretion without legal warrant, and by the party injured, as discretion exercised too late or too softly. Under-specification does not merely permit such attacks. It guarantees them, in both directions, at every election.

The Supreme Court converted the silence of the text into doctrine in Mohinder Singh Gill v Chief Election Commissioner, decided in December 1977 and reported in 1978, and the judgment remains the charter of the Commission's discretionary power. The case arose from the Firozpur parliamentary constituency, where violence and disorder at counting in the 1977 general election led the Commission to order a repoll across the constituency, and the displaced candidate, Mohinder Singh Gill, challenged the order. The Constitution Bench held that Article 324 is a reservoir of power, in the phrase the bar and the bench have repeated since, a plenary grant of the entire field of election conduct in areas left unoccupied by legislation, wide enough to sustain the repoll order but bounded by the requirement that the Commission act fairly, within the law that does exist, and in obedience to the principles of natural justice where its orders injure a candidate. Justice V. R. Krishna Iyer's opinion framed the Commission as a high constitutional functionary whose independence the courts must guard and whose orders the courts must nevertheless keep inside legality. The holding matters for the stress framework because it ratifies the structural position: the Commission's discretion is not a defect of drafting but a constitutional allocation, confirmed by the Court at the first great test, and discretion so confirmed carries its contestability with it. A power that exists because the text is silent will be argued over every time it is used, since each use writes precedent in public, under partisan scrutiny, with the next election already in view.

Discretion over the calendar shows the same mechanism in its most sensitive form. The Commission announces election schedules, decides the number of phases, and staggers polling across weeks for reasons of force availability and administrative reach, and each such decision moves campaign momentum, spending rhythms and the exposure of ministers to the Model Code's restraints. The seven-phase general election of 2019, running from 11 April to 19 May for a House of 543 seats and an electorate of roughly 911 million, was defended by the Commission on logistical and security grounds and attacked by opposition parties for its length and for the distribution of phases across states. The Commission's scheduling power had been litigated as early as the reference on the Gujarat assembly elections of 2002, where the Supreme Court, answering the President's reference in Special Reference No. 1 of 2002, held that the Commission's Article 324 power over timing operates within the constitutional expectation that a new assembly be constituted within six months of the last sitting of its predecessor. The Gujarat episode placed the schedule itself at the centre of a constitutional confrontation between the Commission under J. M. Lyngdoh and the Union government, and the confrontation ended with the Court drawing one boundary line and the Commission retaining the territory inside it. Schedules have been argued over before and since, and the argument is structural: the calendar is too consequential to be neutral, and the text gives it to a discretion that no rule can fully discipline.

### Why does the Model Code of Conduct lack statutory force?

The Model Code began as a consensual instrument, first adopted for Kerala in 1960 and generalised for the 1962 general election, and Parliament never enacted it into law, leaving enforcement to the Commission's Article 324 authority, to party consensus and to the speed of censure over prosecution.

Roll preparation, the least visible of the Commission's fields, exhibits the component in its administrative register. The Representation of the People Act 1950 assigns the rolls to electoral registration officers working under the Commission's superintendence, and the Commission designs the revision cycles, the summary and intensive revisions, the house-to-house verification drives, the forms and the claims-and-objections process. Decisions inside that design decide who votes: the special intensive revision of Bihar's rolls conducted in 2025, outside the strict frame of this article's period but continuous with its argument, became an all-India controversy over documentation and deletion within weeks of its announcement, and the controversy followed the familiar channels, party delegations to Nirvachan Sadan, petitions to the Supreme Court, and public argument about whether the Commission's undoubted power over rolls was being exercised on a timetable and with a documentary burden that the statute nowhere specified. The Bihar revision is invoked here only as the pattern's most legible recent form. From the first revision drives of the 1950s to the digitised rolls of the 2000s, the Commission has designed the gateway to the franchise inside a statutory frame that grants the gateway and omits the gate's dimensions, and the omissions are where the politics collects.

## Dependence: Staff, Money and Appointments Supplied by the Regulated

The second component begins from an organisational fact that no dignity of constitutional status can dissolve: the Election Commission of India is a small secretariat in New Delhi that conducts the largest recurring administrative operation on earth by borrowing other people's staff. Article 324, clause 6, obliges the President and the governors to make available such staff as the Commission requests, and the obligation is the constitutional recognition of the dependence. In practice the Commission's field machinery is the district administration of the states: district magistrates serve as district election officers, sub-divisional officers and tehsildars as returning and assistant returning officers, schoolteachers and clerks as polling personnel, and the police of the state and the central armed police forces as the security of the poll. The chief electoral officer of each state is a serving state civil servant seconded to the Commission's discipline for election purposes. The Commission can demand transfers, and it does, moving collectors and superintendents of police whose neutrality it doubts, and every transfer order is a small public judgment on an officer's integrity delivered by a body that does not employ him. The friction this generates is not incidental to the design. It is the design, operating.

Money follows the same channel with a distinction that reformers have pressed for decades. The Commission's own secretariat expenditure is charged on the Consolidated Fund of India, which places it beyond the annual vote, but the vast operational cost of elections is borne through government budgets, the Union's for parliamentary elections and the states' for assembly elections, administered through the very executive machinery the Commission directs. Successive reform bodies, from the Goswami Committee onward, recommended that the Commission's full expenditure be charged on the Consolidated Fund and that the Commission receive an independent secretariat on the model of the higher judiciary's registries or the Comptroller and Auditor General's audit service, and the recommendations record the diagnosis precisely: an institution that must annually negotiate its means with the government of the day carries a dependence that no tenure clause cures. The Law Commission's 255th Report on electoral reforms, submitted in March 2015, repeated the recommendation of an independent secretariat and charged expenditure, and the repetition across a quarter century of reports is itself the evidence that the dependence is structural rather than episodic.

Appointments are the component's sharpest edge, and they carry the longest documentary trail. From 1950 to 2023 no statute governed the selection of the Chief Election Commissioner or the Election Commissioners; the President appointed on the advice of the Council of Ministers, which is to say the Prime Minister of the day chose the referees of the elections his party would contest. The conventions of the system softened the fact without removing it: appointees were overwhelmingly senior civil servants, frequently former cabinet secretaries or home secretaries, and several governments appointed officers whose service records were known quantities to the appointing executive. The institutionalist defence of this arrangement, stated by successive governments in Parliament and echoed by commentators who worked inside the system, is that it functioned: appointees of every government grew into the office's independence, the impeachment-grade tenure protected them after arrival, and the record from Sen through Seshan to Gill shows officials confronting the governments that named them. The erosion thesis, stated by E. Sridharan in his post-2014 essays and by the petitioners in the appointment litigation, answers that a selection system resting on the restraint of the selector is not a system but a hope, and that the hope is least reliable precisely when the stakes of a single appointment are highest. The adjudication between those positions occupies a later section of this article, after the judicial record has been set out, because the courts have entered the appointment field twice, once by direction and once by review of the statute that answered the direction.

The staffing dependence has a technological annex that grew across the pillar's period. Electronic voting machines were designed and manufactured not by the Commission but by two public sector undertakings, Bharat Electronics Limited and the Electronics Corporation of India Limited, both answerable to ministries of the Union government, and the machines reached polling stations through state administrations. When machine politics became machine controversy after 2004, the Commission found its technical credibility dependent on the assurances of manufacturers within the executive's own corporate family, a dependence it managed by opening machines to technical committees, academic reviewers and challenge procedures, none of which altered the underlying channel. The technological passage of the pillar, which runs from the Parur experiment of 1982 to the paper audit trail, is treated in its own later article in the pillar; its place here is to register that the dependence component is not confined to men and money. It extends to the instruments of the vote itself.

### How does the Election Commission obtain its polling staff?

Article 324, clause 6, requires the President and state governors to place staff at the Commission's disposal, and in practice polling is run by borrowed district officials, teachers and clerks under returning officers drawn from the state administrations, with security from state police and central forces.

One further channel deserves naming because it is the quietest: the Commission's dependence on the state governments for the ordinary peace of the constituencies it must poll. The Commission can requisition forces, stagger phases and transfer officers, but it cannot garrison a state, and the conduct of an election in a region of insurgency or communal tension rests on the cooperation of a government that is itself a contestant. The Kashmir elections of 1996, conducted under President's rule with a massive deployment after a nine-year gap in assembly polling, and the assembly elections held across decades in the states of the Northeast under conditions examined in [the Northeast's distinct political settlement](/2020/02/08/northeast-distinct-political-settlement/), mark the outer edge of the pattern. Inside that edge, the routine dependence is so familiar that commentary treats it as nature. The framework insists on calling it by its analytical name: the Commission's operational sovereignty is real at the level of direction and borrowed at the level of execution, and the gap between direction and execution is one of the places where stress accumulates, because every failure of execution attaches to the Commission's name while every instrument of execution answers to another master.

## Stakes: Why Every Decision Attracts Pressure

The third component explains the temperature of the institution's life. Most constitutional bodies decide questions whose consequences are confined to the parties before them, but the Commission's decisions allocate the chances of power itself. A schedule that places a state's polling before or after a harvest, a festival or a monsoon alters turnout and organisation. A roll revision that adds or deletes names in lakhs alters constituencies. A symbol order that awards a party's reserved symbol to one faction and freezes it against the other can decide which faction inherits a statewide organisation, as happened in the disputes over the AIADMK's two leaves symbol in 2017 and 2019, over the Shiv Sena's bow and arrow in 2022 and 2023, and over the Nationalist Congress Party's clock in 2024, each order issued under paragraph 15 of the Symbols Order on the Commission's reading of a party's constitution and its legislative and organisational wings. A model code ruling that cancels a rally, bars a speaker or advances a transfer changes a campaign in the forty-eight hours when change is irreversible. The political-strategic stake is therefore not an external pressure brought to the institution from outside politics. It is generated by the decisions themselves, and it scales with the equality of the contest: the closer the election, the greater the value of every marginal ruling, and the more certainly the ruling will be litigated in public before it is litigated in court.

Party organisation inside India converts that stake into sustained, professionalised pressure. Every significant party maintains an election cell, a legal cell and a standing practice of representation before the Commission, filing complaints against opponents' speeches, advertisements, expenditure and officers, and demanding the same enforcement against rivals that it resists for itself. The Commission's hearing rooms at Nirvachan Sadan receive delegations weekly in election season, and the delegations arrive with the expectation, cultivated over decades, that access is part of the process. The arrangement has a democratic defence: the regulated are entitled to be heard, and a Commission sealed against representation would forfeit information and legitimacy together. It has an equally evident cost, which is that the institution's working day is structured by the strategic needs of its regulatees. M. S. Gill, reflecting on the system he administered, treated the management of party pressure as the commissioner's core craft, a matter of absorbing representation without surrendering judgment. His 1976 treatment of electoral design had already located the administrator inside politics rather than above it, and the later memoir literature of the office, including T. N. Seshan's account of his tenure and Ashok Lavasa's reflections after his resignation in 2020, confirms the same working reality from opposed temperaments.

Expenditure enforcement exhibits the stake component in its most quantified form. The Commission polices candidate expenditure ceilings fixed by rule, Rs 95 lakh for a Lok Sabha constituency in the larger states after the 2022 revision, through expenditure observers deputed from the revenue services, shadow registers, video surveillance teams and seizure drives that in the 2019 general election intercepted cash, liquor, narcotics and inducements valued in the thousands of crores. Every seizure is a finding against a campaign, every observer's report a potential ground of petition, and the ceiling itself is the product of the statute and rules the Commission administers but does not write. The analytics of a campaign, meanwhile, run through data the Commission publishes and the parties mine: the electoral rolls in electronic form, the turnout figures booth by booth, the affidavit disclosures of candidates' assets and criminal antecedents that the Supreme Court directed the Commission to collect in its judgments of 2002 and 2003 in the Association for Democratic Reforms and People's Union for Civil Liberties cases. Citizens and campaigners who work with those disclosures handle public documents produced at national scale, and the document-handling discipline they require is the same discipline described in [this guide to viewing Office files such as spreadsheets and documents without dedicated software](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), applied here to the archive of Indian democracy itself. The Commission is, among its other identities, one of the republic's largest publishers of politically consequential data, and that identity too draws pressure, since the timing, format and granularity of disclosure are decisions with campaign effects.

The three components interlock, and the interlocking is what makes the stress structural rather than a sum of difficulties. Under-specification means that consequential decisions rest on discretion. Dependence means that the discretion is exercised by appointees of, and with the borrowed instruments of, the governments the decisions affect. Stakes mean that every exercise of the discretion is worth contesting, and the contest is carried on by organisations with permanent staffs and long memories. Remove any component and the others quieten: a fully specified code would narrow the argument to interpretation, an independently staffed and selected commission would narrow it to error, and low-stakes decisions would not be argued at all. The Indian design retains all three, deliberately at the founding and by inertia since, and the retention is the article's central object. ## The Founding Passage: Sukumar Sen and the Election of 1951 and 1952

The institution's first decade is the control case for the framework, because it shows the stress operating at low temperature under conditions of maximum improvisation. Sukumar Sen, a member of the Indian Civil Service who had served as chief secretary of West Bengal, took office as the first Chief Election Commissioner on 21 March 1950, and the Commission he inherited consisted of a constitutional article, a small secretariat and a country in which no general electoral roll on adult suffrage had ever existed. The task set for him was the preparation of rolls for an electorate that the Commission itself estimated, on the 1941 census projected forward, at about 173 million persons, the great majority unable to read the forms on which their names were to be entered, in a territory still absorbing the displacements of Partition. The Representation of the People Act 1950 supplied the roll's legal skeleton, the Act of 1951 followed for the conduct of the poll, and everything between skeleton and event, the enumeration drives, the symbols for candidates of an illiterate electorate, the ballot boxes for each candidate in each booth, the training of over a million polling personnel, was Sen's design executed through the borrowed district administrations of the provinces. The founding passage is treated in full in [the post-1951 first general election context](/2019/01/30/first-general-election-1951-1952-india/), and its stress features can be summarised here.

Two episodes from the founding display the components in embryo. The first is the controversy over the names of married women on the draft rolls. Enumeration practice, following household convention, entered lakhs of women under their husbands' names or as someone's wife or widow, and Sen ruled that a woman's entry must carry her own name, ordering revisions that struck off entries recorded in relational form until corrected, a decision that administrators protested on grounds of feasibility and that the Commission defended as the franchise's meaning: the vote belonged to a citizen, not to a household. The ruling was pure Article 324 discretion, standardless in the statutes, contested by the machinery charged with executing it, and directed against the social grain of the country the Commission served. It is the Model Code pattern thirty years early, an under-specified power used to settle a question the political system had not settled for itself. The second episode is the refusal, in the same founding years, of demands from quarters of the Congress organisation for scheduling and administrative arrangements convenient to the ruling party, refusals that Sen recorded without drama and that established the office's first convention: the Commissioner answers representations and then decides against the government when the record requires it. Jawaharlal Nehru's government, for its part, accepted the refusals, and the acceptance also became precedent. The founding settlement's stress was borne, in the 1950s, by conventions of restraint on both sides, and the framework's question for every later decade is what happens to the stress when the conventions thin.

Sen's own account, in his reports on the first and second general elections, is the under-cited professional literature of the institution, and its tone is part of the evidence. The reports enumerate problems of transport, of ballot paper printing, of simultaneous elections to Parliament and the assemblies doubling the ballot, and they record the Commission's decisions in the flat prose of an administrator who expected the system to be argued about and built his record accordingly. The first general election polled between 25 October 1951 and 21 February 1952 across 489 Lok Sabha seats, with turnout near 46 percent of the enrolled electorate, and the second, in 1957, repeated the operation at greater speed. David Gilmartin's essays on the electoral founding supply the reading that dignifies these logistics: in a society where political standing had been allocated by caste, community and property, the act of enrolling every adult as an equal unit of count was itself a transformation of the political object, and the Commission was the instrument of the transformation. On Gilmartin's reading the institution was political from its first day, not because it took sides, but because its procedure embodied a claim about who the people were. The institution-under-stress framework absorbs that reading without remainder: a body whose routine operations perform a contested theory of the polity will be a political object whatever its intentions, and Sen's decade shows the stress at its most constructive, the pressure of a new design working against an old society.

What the founding decade did not test was the appointment channel, since Sen was the unanimous choice of a founding government and his successor K. V. K. Sundaram, who served from 1958 to 1967, inherited a settled office. The stress of the 1960s and 1970s gathered elsewhere, in the Commission's relations with state governments over rolls and schedules and, decisively, in the Emergency of 1975 to 1977, when the electoral calendar itself was suspended by the postponement of the general election due in 1976. The Commission of the Emergency years, under Chief Election Commissioner T. Swaminathan, administered an order in which elections were deferred by constitutional amendment and the Lok Sabha's term extended, and its formal position, that it conducts elections when they are called under law, was both legally exact and institutionally costly, since the deferral taught every political actor that the calendar could be reached by reaching the law. The restoration election of March 1977, conducted by Chief Election Commissioner K. V. K. Sundaram's successor Swaminathan in the Emergency's immediate aftermath under a caretaker dispensation, returned the institution to its rhythm, and the verdict it declared, examined in the account of [the 1977 Janata verdict](/2019/04/28/1977-janata-verdict-explained/), stands among the most consequential counts in the republic's history. The Emergency remains the limiting case in the framework's vocabulary: the stress did not break the institution, but the episode disclosed that the institution's continuity ran through law and convention, both of which a determined executive with a parliamentary majority could bend. The lesson was filed by every later commissioner, and T. N. Seshan, who had watched the system from the cabinet secretary's chair, built his tenure on refusing to let it be forgotten.

## The Assertive Passage: The Goswami Diagnosis and the Seshan Tenure

By the late 1980s the stresses the framework names had accumulated into a public diagnosis, and the diagnosis has an official text. The government of V. P. Singh, formed in December 1989, constituted a committee on electoral reforms under the law minister Dinesh Goswami, drawing members from the parties of the National Front and its supporters, and the Goswami Committee's report of 1990 is the under-cited primary source of this article because it records the institutional-political stress conditions of the post-1989 moment from inside the political system itself. The committee recommended, among its sixty-odd proposals, that the Election Commission be made a multi-member body, that commissioners be appointed after consultation extending beyond the executive, that the Model Code receive a firmer legal footing, that state funding of elections be examined, that the Commission's expenditure be charged on the Consolidated Fund, and that the anti-defection and expenditure regimes be tightened. Read as a set, the recommendations are a map of the three components drawn by parliamentarians: multi-member composition and consultative appointment against the dependence component, a statutory code against under-specification, and expenditure reform against the stakes that money multiplies. The committee's immediate harvest was thin; the durable conversion of the Commission to three members came only in October 1993, and then as an instrument in a conflict rather than as a reform, which is itself the most Goswami-like outcome imaginable. But the report fixed the vocabulary in which the next decade argued, and the Seshan tenure is unintelligible without it.

Tirunellai Narayana Iyer Seshan, a Tamil Nadu cadre officer of the Indian Administrative Service who had served as cabinet secretary, took office as the tenth Chief Election Commissioner on 12 December 1990, and the tenure that followed, to 11 December 1996, is the pillar's second passage and the subject of its own later article. Its outline belongs here because Seshan is the framework's most instructive case: a commissioner who read Article 324 as Mohinder Singh Gill had licensed it to be read, as a reservoir, and who set out to demonstrate, decision by decision, how much of the electoral system's decay was attributable not to missing law but to unused power. He enforced the Model Code against sitting chief ministers, countermanded and postponed polls where violence or booth capturing had destroyed the conditions of a free election, insisted on photo identity cards for electors over the objections of governments that pleaded cost, deployed central forces and observers at scales his predecessors had treated as exceptional, and converted the Commission's press conferences into a running audit of the political class. The election to the Bihar assembly and the repeated postponements of polling in Punjab and in Jammu and Kashmir placed the calendar power at the centre of national argument, and governments of V. P. Singh's successors, Chandra Shekhar, P. V. Narasimha Rao and the United Front, each in turn explored the law and the ordinances available to restrain him.

The restraint arrived through the dependence channel, exactly as the framework predicts. In October 1993 the Rao government promulgated an ordinance, later enacted, fixing the Commission at three members and appointed Gill and Krishnamurty, on the reasoning, defended publicly, that a multi-member body was the Goswami recommendation and a corrective to single-member autocracy. Seshan challenged the move and the service conditions it set, and the litigation reached the Supreme Court as T. N. Seshan v Union of India, decided in July 1995. The Constitution Bench upheld the multi-member conversion and the ordinance, held that the Chief Election Commissioner is not a superior authority over the Election Commissioners but the chairman of an equal body, whose business the Commission must transact collectively, and rejected Seshan's contention that the appointees were constitutionally unfit for their offices. The judgment is the second great charter of the institution, and its stress significance is double. It confirmed that the composition of the Commission is itself an instrument that governments can reach, since clause 2 leaves the number open. And it redescribed the Commission's internal constitution, distributing the reservoir of Article 324 among three equals, so that the dissent and majority mechanics of a multi-member body, dormant since 1993, became part of the design within which all later controversies, including those of 2019, would be fought.

### What did the Supreme Court hold in T. N. Seshan v Union of India?

The Constitution Bench held in July 1995 that converting the Commission to three members was constitutional, that the Chief Election Commissioner is chairman among equals rather than a superior over the Election Commissioners, and that the Commission's business must be transacted collectively by the body.

Seshan's own retrospective defence, stated in his memoir and interviews, is that assertiveness was the office's duty performed at last, and that the political class's discomfort measured his fidelity. The institutionalist criticism, stated by commentators who admired the enforcement and feared its form, is that a design in which the institution's authority depends on one officeholder's appetite teaches the political system to solve the problem at the appointment stage, by choosing quieter referees. Both readings capture part of the record, and the framework adjudicates between them by shifting the unit of analysis: the Seshan tenure is best read neither as heroism nor as aberration but as a stress test that the institution administered to itself, revealing which of its powers were real, the code, the calendar, the transfer, the countermand, and which of its supports were not, the appointment convention, the single-member form, the expectation that discretion would stay sheathed. The demonstration could not be unlearned by either side. Governments after 1996 selected commissioners with the Seshan possibility in view, and commissioners after 1996 worked in an office whose demonstrated powers every party had catalogued. The assertive passage thus raised the stakes component permanently, because the value of influencing an institution is a function of what the institution has shown it can do.

## The Courts and the Commission: Gill, Disclosure and the Appointment Cases

The judicial record is where the framework's components receive their legal adjudication, and it falls into three movements: the expansion of the Commission's discretion, the expansion of the voter's rights against the Commission's silence, and the contest over the appointment door. The first movement is Mohinder Singh Gill, already discussed for its reservoir doctrine, and its sequel in the schedule reference of 2002, where the Court balanced the Commission's calendar power against the six-month expectation for a new assembly. The through line of the first movement is judicial protection of the institution's field: the courts treated Article 324 as a deliberate allocation and policed only its edges, requiring fairness, fidelity to statute and obedience to natural justice, while refusing to convert the Commission into a tribunal whose every order awaited judicial pre-clearance. Article 329's bar on mid-process interference, applied in N. P. Ponnuswami v Returning Officer in 1952, the first year of the republic's elections, completes the protection: the electoral process, once set in motion, runs to its declaration before the courts entertain challenge, and then by election petition alone. The stress consequence is subtle but real. Judicial abstinence during the process concentrates all conflict onto the Commission itself in the weeks when its decisions are irreversible, which is precisely when the stakes component peaks.

The second movement reversed the direction of judicial energy. In Union of India v Association for Democratic Reforms, decided in May 2002, the Supreme Court held that the voter's right to know the antecedents of candidates flows from the freedom of expression under Article 19(1)(a), and directed the Commission to require affidavits disclosing criminal convictions and pending charges, assets, liabilities and educational qualifications. When Parliament responded with an amendment designed to narrow the disclosure, the Court in People's Union for Civil Liberties v Union of India in 2003 struck the narrowing down and restored the fuller regime. In 2013 the same logic produced the direction, in People's Union for Civil Liberties v Union of India, that ballot units carry a none of the above option, so that a voter could record rejection without forfeiting secrecy or the vote itself. The Commission implemented each direction, building the affidavit archive and the NOTA button into the process it administered, and the sequence matters to the framework because it shows the Commission's field being written by a third author. Neither Parliament nor the Commission designed the disclosure regime; the Court did, on the Commission's machinery, and the Commission's compliance made it one of the largest transparency publishers in the constitutional order. Under-specification, the first component, is usually told as the Commission's freedom. The disclosure cases complete the picture: a field left open by the text can be filled by the judiciary as well as by the Commission, and every filling rearranges the stresses on the institution that must operate the result.

The third movement concerns the door through which commissioners enter, and it is the movement that carried the dependence component into constitutional litigation. Petitions filed from 2015 onward, organised as Anoop Baranwal v Union of India, asked the Supreme Court to fill the seventy-year vacancy left by clause 2's phrase subject to any law made by Parliament, arguing that executive-only appointment of the election referee violated the independence the Constituent Assembly had designed. On 2 March 2023 a Constitution Bench agreed in part: reading Article 324 with the Assembly's debates and the Goswami Committee's recommendations in view, the Court directed that, until Parliament enacted a law, appointments of the Chief Election Commissioner and Election Commissioners be made by the President on the advice of a committee of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha and the Chief Justice of India. The direction governed the appointment field for a matter of months. Parliament enacted the Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act in December 2023, constituting a selection committee of the Prime Minister as chair, the Leader of the Opposition and a Union cabinet minister nominated by the Prime Minister, advised by a search committee, and setting the commissioners' salary and conditions by reference to the cabinet secretary rather than to a Supreme Court judge as the earlier settlement had equated them. Challenges to the Act were filed immediately, interim stays were declined, and the appointments of March 2024, which filled two vacancies on the eve of the general election, proceeded under the new statute while its validity remained under challenge.

The adjudication of the appointment question must state every position at its strength, because the pillar's stance charter requires it and because the argument is genuinely balanced on the texts. The government's defence of the 2023 Act, stated in Parliament by the law minister during the bill's passage, is that Article 324(2) expressly reserves appointment to a law of Parliament, that the Constitution Bench's committee was an interim arrangement until Parliament acted, and that a committee chaired by the Prime Minister with the Leader of the Opposition as a member satisfies democratic accountability while restoring the legislative role the text assigns. The critics' answer, stated by the petitioners and by E. Sridharan among other scholars, is that a selection committee in which the executive holds a structural majority of two to one reproduces executive appointment in committee form, that the substitution of a nominated cabinet minister for the Chief Justice removes the only member with no stake in the electoral outcome, and that equating the commissioners' service conditions with the cabinet secretary's recasts constitutional officers as senior civil servants. The institutionalist middle position, represented in the Law Commission's 255th Report of 2015 and in the Goswami recommendations a generation earlier, proposed a broader collegium including the Chief Justice precisely to place appointment beyond single-party control, and proposed charging the Commission's expenditure and giving it an independent secretariat in the same breath, treating selection, money and staffing as one dependence problem. The honest summary is that the Constitution's own design, breadth in clause 1 and openness in clause 2, guarantees that the appointment argument recurs whenever the political temperature rises, and that the events of 2023 settled the argument's venue, in Parliament and before the Court, without settling the argument.

### What did Anoop Baranwal v Union of India direct in March 2023?

The Constitution Bench directed on 2 March 2023 that, until Parliament legislated, the President appoint the Chief Election Commissioner and Election Commissioners on the advice of a committee of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha and the Chief Justice of India.

The Commission's defenders inside this debate make one further point that the framework must register at full strength, because it is the strongest form of the complication the brief assigns. The institution under the allegedly dependent design has, across seven decades, repeatedly ruled against the governments that appointed its members: it postponed their elections, transferred their officers, censured their ministers, countermanded their polls, and in 2019 carried the public dissent of one of its members against the majority's code decisions. Ashok Lavasa, appointed an Election Commissioner in January 2018, recorded dissent notes in the Commission's orders of April and May 2019 concerning complaints against speeches by the Prime Minister and other campaigners, and asked that dissent in code matters be recorded in the final orders; the majority held that such orders were not quasi-judicial and need not carry dissent. Lavasa resigned in August 2020 to take up a vice-presidency of the Asian Development Bank, and the episode entered the record as evidence available to both readings: to the erosion thesis, proof that the multi-member mechanics of 1995 could be used to isolate internal disagreement; to the institutionalist defence, proof that the disagreement occurred, was recorded, was argued in public and left the institution standing. The framework does not require the reader to choose a moral for the episode before examining it. It requires the episode to be classified, and its classification is exact: the stakes component, operating through the internal constitution that the dependence and under-specification components had built.

## The Technological Passage and the Post-2014 Controversies

The machine question carried the stress into a register the founders could not have imagined, and it begins in a single constituency. Electronic voting machines were used experimentally in fifty polling stations of the Parur assembly constituency in Kerala in the by-election of May 1982, and the experiment's defeated candidate challenged the result; in A. C. Jose v Sivan Pillai in 1984 the Supreme Court held that the Representation of the People Act 1951 and the Conduct of Elections Rules, which spoke of ballot papers and ballot boxes, did not authorise machines, and that the Commission's Article 324 power could not override the statutory scheme. Parliament answered with section 61A, inserted into the Act in 1989, empowering the Commission to specify constituencies where voting would be by machine. Machines returned in state elections from the late 1990s, and the general election of 2004 was the first conducted entirely on electronic voting machines across 543 constituencies and an electorate of some 671 million. The technological passage receives its full treatment later in the pillar; its stress anatomy is what belongs here.

The machine controversy's anatomy repeats the three components with unusual clarity. Under-specification: the security, custody and audit protocols of a machine election were written by the Commission and its technical committees rather than by any statute, so every protocol choice, from the randomisation of units to the mock poll to the counting-day procedure, stood open to challenge as discretion. Dependence: the machines were manufactured by Bharat Electronics and the Electronics Corporation of India, public sector companies within the Union government's industrial family, so the institution's assurance of its instruments' integrity rested on the word of suppliers its regulator did not control. Stakes: because a general allegation against machines impugns every result they record, machine criticism became, after the results of 2009, 2014 and 2019, the vehicle of defeated parties' wider arguments about the process, and the Commission answered with public challenge procedures, most fully in the open hackathon invitation of June 2017, which no registered party ultimately attempted on the Commission's terms. The paper trail litigation converted controversy into design: in Subramanian Swamy v Election Commission in October 2013 the Supreme Court held that a verifiable paper audit trail was indispensable to free and fair elections and directed its phased introduction, and voter verifiable paper audit trail units accompanied machines universally from the general election of 2019, with slips counted mandatorily in a sample whose size, five polling stations per assembly segment after the Court's direction of April 2019, was itself fixed by litigation against the Commission's smaller initial sample. The sample size argument is the technological passage in miniature: a number nowhere written in any text, consequential beyond measure, and settled by the interaction of commission discretion and judicial review under partisan observation.

The post-2014 controversies, which the pillar's closing article examines as a set, are gathered here only to be placed inside the framework, and the placement is the hub's essential work. The controversies of the period are heterogeneous in substance. They include the timing and phasing of state elections, argued over in Gujarat and Himachal Pradesh in 2017 when the Commission announced the two schedules on different days; the enforcement of the Model Code in the 2019 general election, including the Lavasa dissents and the Commission's unprecedented orders under Article 324 barring named campaigners from the trail for periods of forty-eight to seventy-two hours, an exercise of the reservoir power against speech that the Supreme Court declined to disturb; the office of profit reference concerning twenty Delhi legislators, in which the President, acting on the Commission's opinion in January 2018, disqualified members whose disqualification the Delhi High Court later set aside on natural justice grounds, returning the matter to the Commission; the scheduling and conduct questions of the pandemic-era elections of 2020 and 2021, when the Madras High Court observed in April 2021, in terms it later softened in the written order, that the Commission bore responsibility for the second wave's spread through its conduct of campaigning; and the appointment sequence of 2023 and 2024 that the preceding section set out. No single theory of decline or of resilience organises this material honestly, and the framework does not impose one. What the framework claims is narrower and stronger: that each controversy is an instance of a mechanism named in 1950's design and visible in every decade since, and that the post-2014 period differs from its predecessors in the density and simultaneity of the mechanisms' activation, not in their kind. The reading of the period as exceptional aberration and the reading of the period as terminal erosion both flatten the record. The record shows an institution whose stresses are continuous, whose activation varies with the political weather, and whose post-2014 weather has been severe.

That severity has a context the pillar keeps in view, and the context is the wider argument about constitutional institutions in the Modi era, the renegotiation of inherited settlements that [the post-2014 PMO architecture context](/2019/08/11/pmo-centralisation-question-india/) examines from the executive's side. The Commission does not stand alone among the bodies whose appointment conventions, tenures and public authority have been argued over since 2014, and a reader who places its controversies beside those of the higher judiciary's appointments, the information commissions and the investigative agencies will see a family resemblance that the framework helps to specify. The resemblance is not identity. The Commission's tenure protection is constitutional where others' is statutory, its field is the most public of all, and its decisions are tested at a frequency no other institution endures, since every general election is a referendum on the referee conducted by the players. A contested-settlement analysis, of the kind applied to [the contested-settlement framework](/2020/04/09/indian-secularism-contested-settlement/) in the pillar on religion and politics, treats such institutions as settlements under permanent renegotiation, and the Commission fits the type exactly: its settlement was struck in 1949 and 1950, and every decade since has renegotiated some clause of it, in committee rooms, in courtrooms and on the campaign trail.

## The Adjudication: What the Stress Reading Explains

The named disagreement this article must adjudicate can be stated in its mature form. The institutional-procedural framing holds that the Election Commission is adequately understood as the sum of its legal instruments, that its history is the history of those instruments' application, and that controversy attaches to episodes and persons rather than to the design. The institution-under-stress reading holds that the design itself, the triad of under-specification, dependence and stakes, is the analytically prior object, that the instruments are unintelligible except as moves within it, and that the episodes are the design becoming visible. The defence of the second reading rests on comparative explanatory power across four tests. First, persistence: the procedural framing must treat controversy in the 1950s, the 1970s, the 1990s and the 2010s as a run of unrelated accidents, where the stress reading predicts recurrence from structure and finds it. Second, symmetry: governments and oppositions of every party have occupied both sides of Commission controversies within a few years of one another, attacking the referee they lately praised, and the stress reading explains the rotation by the stake attached to decisions, where a partisan theory of the institution must posit conversions of character in the same men. Third, the appointment cycle: the procedural framing cannot explain why selection of commissioners became a constitutional case in 2023 after seventy years of quiet, where the stress reading locates the change in the rising value of the office's demonstrated powers, the Seshan demonstration chief among them. Fourth, the internal record: the Commission's own reform proposals, from its annual reports to its evidence before committees, return obsessively to charged expenditure, an independent secretariat and appointment reform, which is to say the institution describes its own condition in the framework's vocabulary whenever it speaks professionally.

The strongest counter to the stress reading is the complication the brief directs this article to meet head on, and it is a formidable one. An institution under continuous stress might be expected, over seventy years, to fail, and the Election Commission has not failed. It has delivered eighteen general elections to the Lok Sabha through the election of 2024 and, on the Commission's own tally, some four hundred elections to state assemblies, transferring power repeatedly between rival parties and coalitions, including the transfers of 1977, 1989, 2004 and 2014 in which the incumbent formation lost and left office on the Commission's count. No election in the republic's history has been annulled wholesale, no result has been refused implementation by the armed services or the civil administration, and the losers of Indian elections, with vanishingly few exceptions, have vacated office on schedule. A reading of Indian politics that begins from outcomes would call this institution a triumph, and the triumph is real. The adjudication turns on a distinction the framework draws with care, between institutional collapse and structural-political stress. Collapse is a result: the cessation of function, the refusal of outcomes, the capture so complete that the form itself is abandoned. Stress is a condition: the continuous expenditure of authority to maintain function under pressures that the design itself generates. The post-1950 trajectory shows stress without collapse, and indeed shows the two related, because the authority the institution spends in each controversy was accumulated by its previous survivals. Seshan could spend the office's prestige because Sen had banked it. The commissioners of 2019 could draw on Seshan's demonstration. An institution can be simultaneously successful and stressed, as a bridge can be simultaneously standing and loaded, and the analytical error of the procedural framing is to infer from the standing that the loads are imaginary.

There is a second, quieter counter that also deserves its answer. It holds that stress language imports a pathology into what is simply democratic politics working as intended: parties argue with referees in every democracy, courts supervise, newspapers investigate, and the Indian Commission's controversies differ from those of the American Federal Election Commission or the British Electoral Commission in scale rather than in nature. The comparison is instructive and the framework accepts its discipline. What distinguishes the Indian case, on the evidence assembled here, is the concentration of the three components in one body. The American commission is a statutory regulator of campaign finance that does not conduct elections, which are run by the states. The British commission regulates and advises while returning officers drawn from local government conduct the poll. The Indian Commission conducts, on a continental scale, with borrowed staff, under a two-hundred-word constitutional grant, through commissioners chosen until 2023 by the executive alone. Comparative study, of the kind David Gilmartin's historical essays model, disciplines the stress reading into precision: the claim is not that India argues about elections, which every democracy does, but that the Indian design concentrates the argument onto a single institution with unusual completeness, and that the concentration, visible in the Goswami report's map of grievances as much as in the litigation of 2023, is the political object the pillar studies.

The framework's yield for the articles that follow can be stated as a set of standing questions, and a hub article earns its place by the quality of the questions it bequeaths. To the founding article: how did Sen's improvisation convert the text's silences into conventions, and which of those conventions were personal to him. To the Seshan article: which of the demonstrated powers survived their demonstrator, and what did the 1995 judgment's collegial redesign do to the office's subsequent behaviour. To the machine article: whether the paper trail settlement of 2013 to 2019 closed the technological controversy or merely relocated discretion to the audit sample. To the closing article of the pillar: whether the post-2014 activation of all three components at once marks a change in the institution's condition or only in its weather. The hub does not answer those questions in advance, since pre-empting the articles that own them would defeat the pillar's design, but it fixes the terms in which their answers will count as answers. Under-specification, dependence and stakes are the pillar's measures. An episode that cannot be stated in those terms has not yet been analysed, and a claim about the institution that ignores them has not yet met the record.

## The Reform Record: Committees That Mapped the Stress

The framework does not lack for official corroboration, because the Indian state has spent fifty years commissioning maps of the territory the framework describes. The Tarkunde Committee, appointed in 1974 by Jayaprakash Narayan's Citizens for Democracy and reporting in 1975 under the chairmanship of Justice V. M. Tarkunde, was the first systematic audit of the electoral system from outside government, and its recommendations, on election expenses, on the appointment of commissioners through a wider consultative process, and on the Commission's insulation from the executive, read as an early draft of the dependence critique written while the Emergency's author was still in office. The committee's unofficial status limited its immediate effect, but its categories entered the reform vocabulary permanently, and the Janata government of 1977 carried several of its concerns into official discussion. The pattern set by Tarkunde repeats through the sequence: diagnosis from a committee, selective harvest by a government, return of the unharvested residue to the next committee's agenda. The residue is the interesting stratum, because the recommendations that no government enacts, across five decades and every party's tenure, mark the points where the design's beneficiaries judge the design's costs acceptable.

The Goswami Committee of 1990, already examined as the Seshan era's diagnostic text, was followed by the Indrajit Gupta Committee on State Funding of Elections, constituted in 1998 and reporting the same year, which recommended partial state funding in kind, free airtime and supplies through public channels, on the ground that the money stake had outgrown private regulation. The Law Commission's 170th Report on Reform of the Electoral Laws, submitted in May 1999 under Justice B. P. Jeevan Reddy's chairmanship, proposed disqualification reform, the regulation of party finances and internal party democracy, and returned to appointment and tenure protections for commissioners. The National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution, chaired by Justice M. N. Venkatachaliah and reporting in March 2002, recommended that the Chief Election Commissioner and Election Commissioners be appointed on the recommendation of a committee including the Prime Minister, the leaders of the opposition in both Houses and other constitutional figures, a collegium proposal that anticipated the Anoop Baranwal direction by twenty-one years and shared its fate of deferral. The Commission itself added its own voice to the sequence, most fully in its compendium of proposed electoral reforms of 2016, which gathered proposals it had pressed since 1998: decriminalisation measures, expenditure and affidavit enforcement, charged expenditure for the Commission, and an independent secretariat. The Second Administrative Reforms Commission's report on ethics in governance of 2007 endorsed a collegium for appointments to constitutional bodies in the same family of reasoning.

Read consecutively, the reform record performs an unintended service for the stress framework: it demonstrates that the diagnosis is not a partisan artefact of any period. A committee of the Janata opposition's civil society, a committee of the National Front government, a committee of the BJP-led coalition's Parliament, a commission under a Congress-era Law Commission chairman and a review commission appointed by the Vajpayee government converge, across twenty-seven years, on the same short list of structural anxieties, appointment insulation, financial dependence, expenditure control and the legal footing of the Commission's instruments. Convergence of that kind, among bodies appointed by rivals and staffed by establishment figures, is the strongest available evidence that the stress is a property of the institution rather than of the commentary about it. The same record disciplines the erosion thesis from the other side: if the structural defects were identified, in nearly their present terms, by 1975 and again by 1990, then the post-2014 controversies cannot be narrated as the discovery of the defects, and any account of the period must explain activation and intensity rather than origin. The framework's vocabulary was built for exactly that explanation.

The unharvested residue deserves itemised attention because its persistence is data. Charged expenditure for the whole of the Commission's operations, recommended by Goswami in 1990 and by the Law Commission in 2015, remains unenacted; the Commission's secretariat costs are charged, but the operational budget of elections flows through voted grants and state budgets. An independent secretariat, on the analogy of the audit department, remains unenacted; the Commission's officers are drawn from the civil services and return to them, and its senior staff rotate on deputation. A statutory footing for the Model Code remains unenacted; the code governs, as it has since 1962, by consensus and Article 324. Appointment by collegium arrived only by judicial direction in 2023 and was replaced within the year by the statutory committee the critics describe as executive-weighted. Four recommendations, four decades, four governments of opposed complexion, and the residue is intact. A political scientist reading only the reform record, without a single newspaper, would conclude that the Indian political system finds the stressed design useful: every governing formation values the discretion the design leaves within reach, and values it more in office than it feared it in opposition. That conclusion, unflattering to all parties equally, is the framework's evenhandedness test passed by the evidence itself.

## Federal Reach: State Elections, Local Bodies and the Simultaneous Poll Question

The Commission's jurisdiction has a boundary that the stress framework must mark, because the boundary locates part of the institution's politics. Article 324 covers elections to Parliament, the state legislatures and the offices of President and Vice-President. Elections to panchayats and municipalities stand outside it, assigned by the Seventy-third and Seventy-fourth Amendments of 1992 to State Election Commissions under Articles 243K and 243ZA, bodies appointed by governors and chronically weaker in tenure, staffing and public standing than their national counterpart. The contrast is a natural experiment in institutional design. The state commissions replicate the national design's features, a single commissioner, executive appointment, borrowed staff, in state after state, and their record, of litigation over delayed local elections, of commissioners removed or curtailed by state governments, and of Supreme Court interventions to compel timely polls, displays the same stress components operating without the national institution's accumulated authority to absorb them. The Maharashtra and West Bengal local election disputes of the 2010s and 2020s, and the Andhra Pradesh episode of 2020 in which the state government removed its election commissioner by ordinance in mid-process and the courts restored him, show what the framework predicts for the design at lower altitude: the stress is constant, the ballast smaller. The national Commission's relative success is therefore not evidence against the framework. It is evidence about the variable the framework calls accumulated authority, and about how much of the national record is owed to the office's history rather than to its text.

Within its own jurisdiction, the Commission's federal dealings generate a distinct stress, because the assemblies and governments it must poll are run by parties that may be in opposition at the centre, and its borrowed machinery belongs to them. The Commission's authority over state officers during elections is functional and temporary, asserted through deputation discipline and transfer orders, and state governments have tested its edges from the 1950s onward, most visibly where a state government's survival itself depends on the poll. The simultaneous elections question raises this federal stress to the level of constitutional design. From 1951 to 1967 the republic voted for Parliament and the assemblies together, and the cycle broke when premature dissolutions, beginning with the assemblies dismissed and re-elected out of phase after 1967 and the Lok Sabha's early dissolution in 1970, scattered the calendars. Proposals to restore simultaneity, examined by the Law Commission in a draft report of 2018, endorsed in principle by the NITI Aayog's discussion papers, and taken up by the high-level committee chaired by former President Ram Nath Kovind, which reported in March 2024 in favour of a phased return to simultaneous polls with constitutional amendments, would assign the Commission a single recurring national operation of unprecedented concentration. The proposal's supporters, including the Kovind committee, argue administrative economy and the release of governance from perpetual campaign mode. Its critics, including opposition-ruled state governments and federalism scholars, argue that simultaneity subordinates state electoral rhythms to the national calendar and lengthens or shortens assembly terms by constitutional engineering. For the framework, the debate is a stress calculation conducted in public: simultaneity would reduce the frequency of the Commission's exposure while multiplying the stakes of each exposure to the maximum the system can generate, and would deepen the dependence component, since an operation of that scale can be mounted only in total reliance on the Union's and the states' combined machinery. The pillar records the debate as the largest pending question of the institution's design, and leaves its merits to the evidence assembled across the pillar's articles.

The Vice-Presidential and Presidential elections, small electorates of legislators conducted by the Commission through returning officers drawn from the parliamentary secretariat, close the jurisdictional survey with a study in contrast. These elections have produced their own controversies, the invalid votes dispute of the 1969 presidential election among them, which reached the Supreme Court in Shiv Kirpal Singh v V. V. Giri in 1970, but they operate at low stress because their electorates are few, professional and observable. The gradient is the lesson. Stress in this institution scales with the mass of the electorate, the equality of the contest and the consequence of the outcome, and the Commission's daily reality is the maximum of all three. No other constitutional body in India is required, on a five-year rhythm, to perform its function perfectly in public before an audience of nine hundred million, knowing that a visible failure in one district can be generalised into a national argument by nightfall. The institution-under-stress reading is, at bottom, the refusal to treat that requirement as a routine administrative fact. It is the republic's most exposed office doing the republic's most consequential work with the republic's most contested instruments, and the wonder the record supports is not that the Commission is argued about. It is that the argument has so reliably ended, eighteen times at the national level, with the transfer of power.

## Registration, Symbols and the Party System the Commission Governs

A further field of the Commission's authority illustrates all three components operating at low visibility and high consequence: its governance of political parties as registered associations. Section 29A of the Representation of the People Act 1951, inserted in 1989, requires associations seeking registration as parties to swear fidelity to the Constitution and to the principles of socialism, secularism and democracy, and empowers the Commission to register them, with the privileges that flow from registration: a reserved or free symbol, recognition stages as state and national parties under the Symbols Order, broadcast time on public media and access to electoral rolls. The Commission's power to deregister is, by the Supreme Court's reading in Indian National Congress (I) v Institute of Social Welfare in 2002, confined to narrow grounds: registration obtained by fraud, an association's own declaration that it has ceased to adhere to its constitutional undertaking, or illegality of the association itself. The result is a register carrying thousands of parties, the great majority of which contest rarely or never, and a Commission that can enrol but cannot prune. The anomaly is under-specification in reverse: here the statute speaks and the courts construe it narrowly, and the Commission's public complaints about shell parties and tax-exempt non-contestants, pressed in its reform proposals of 2016 among other documents, have not moved Parliament to widen the power. The episode refines the framework by showing that the institution's stress is not a simple function of too much discretion. It is a function of the fit between discretion and task, and the fit fails in both directions.

Symbol adjudication, by contrast, is discretion at full stretch, and its case law is the pillar's richest seam after the appointment cases. When a recognised party splits, paragraph 15 of the Symbols Order empowers the Commission to decide, after hearing the factions, which of them is the party for the purposes of the reserved symbol, applying the tests the Supreme Court laid down in Sadiq Ali v Election Commission in 1971: the party's constitution and its aims, the majority in the organisational wing, and the majority in the legislative wing, weighted as the circumstances require. The Commission's orders in these disputes are quasi-judicial acts performed under maximum political exposure, since the symbol is frequently the single asset that carries a party's identity to an electorate among whom the name alone does not travel. The Congress split of 1969 produced the lineage of the doctrine, as the Commission recognised Indira Gandhi's faction and the symbol question travelled to the courts. The Samajwadi Party dispute of January 2017 required the Commission to decide between Mulayam Singh Yadav and Akhilesh Yadav on affidavits of legislators and party delegates counted in thousands, on a timetable set by the Uttar Pradesh election the Commission itself had scheduled. The AIADMK disputes of 2017, the Shiv Sena dispute decided in February 2023, and the Nationalist Congress Party dispute decided in February 2024 each placed the Commission in the position of certifying the authentic continuation of a party whose factions were simultaneously contesting the state. Critics of particular orders, in each case the losing faction and its scholarly supporters, charged the Commission with overweighting the legislative wing, where a ruling dispensation's pull is strongest, against the organisational wing. The Commission's stated doctrine, repeated across the orders, is that the tests are applied to the evidence of support as the party's own constitution structures it. The framework's classification of the field is exact whatever view a reader takes of any order: a 1968 executive instrument, sustained by a 1971 judgment, assigns the identity of India's parties to a body appointed under clause 2, and every split in a major party therefore routes a question of political inheritance through the dependence and under-specification components together.

Recognition itself, the grading of parties into national and state status by vote and seat thresholds under the Symbols Order as amended, most substantially in 2000 and 2016, extends the Commission's governance into the party system's architecture. Recognition carries the reserved symbol, the broadcast entitlement and the star campaigner privileges that structure a campaign's economics, and its loss, as the reviews of 2019 and 2023 showed when established parties faced the withdrawal of national status, is a public demotion administered by the referee. The Trinamool Congress, the Nationalist Congress Party and the Communist Party of India lost national party status in the Commission's review announced in April 2023, while the Aam Aadmi Party gained it, and the decisions, taken on the Order's published criteria across two election cycles, were accepted with little of the controversy that attends the Commission's other acts. The quiet around a mechanical decision is itself evidence for the framework: where the rule is specified, published and applied by arithmetic, the stress falls away. The institution's contested life is lived in the discretionary fields, and the recognition reviews show by contrast what the discretionary fields cost.

## How the Institution Renews Its Authority

A stress framework that could not explain recovery would be a decline narrative in disguise, and the record contains recoveries substantial enough to require their own account. The Commission's authority has been renewed, across its history, by three recurring methods. The first is demonstration: a commissioner performs the office's powers at a scale that resets expectations, as Sen did with the founding election itself and Seshan did with enforcement, and the performance deposits authority that successors inherit even when their temperaments differ. The second is judicial alliance: at the moments of greatest exposure, the courts have repeatedly, though not invariably, underwritten the institution's field, in Ponnuswami in 1952, in Mohinder Singh Gill in 1977, in the disclosure judgments that enlarged its archive, and in the paper trail direction that settled the machine controversy's terms, and each underwriting converted a contested discretion into a supervised settlement. The third is procedural self-publication: the Commission's manuals, handbooks for returning officers, compendia of instructions and statistical reports constitute one of the most complete published records of any Indian institution's working methods, and the publicity is a deliberate technology of legitimacy, since a process described in advance in public documents can be audited against its description by every party and newspaper in the country. The statistical reports on each general election, published since Sen's foundational volumes of the 1950s, remain the primary data source on which scholars including E. Sridharan have built the quantitative study of Indian elections, and an institution that publishes the evidence of its own performance arms its critics and its defenders from the same shelf.

The renewal account also explains the institution's survival of its two gravest passages. The Emergency tested the design at the point of the calendar, and the restoration of 1977 returned the rhythm; the Seshan conflict tested it at the point of composition, and the 1995 judgment converted a confrontation into a collegial redesign that has since housed both assertive majorities and recorded dissents. In neither case did the institution emerge unchanged, and the changes are the point: stress, in this institution, is the medium through which adaptation occurs, because the under-specified text permits the adaptation that a detailed code would forbid. A constitution that had specified the machinery of 1950 in full would have fossilised the ballot paper, the single-member commission and the paper roll. The silence that generates the stress is the same silence that permitted machines, photo rolls, affidavits, paper trails and a three-member commission to be absorbed without constitutional amendment. The adjudication of this article can therefore be closed in a single sentence that the whole pillar will test: the Election Commission's stresses and its adaptability are one property of its design, read in bad weather and in good, and the institution-under-stress framework is the reading that keeps both halves of that property in view at once.

## The Frozen Map: Delimitation and the Boundaries of the Commission's Field

One adjacent institution completes the survey of the Commission's environment, because the map on which all its elections are fought was drawn by another hand and then frozen by constitutional politics. Delimitation, the drawing of constituency boundaries and the allocation of seats among states, is assigned by Articles 82 and 170 to parliamentary law, and the Delimitation Acts of 1952, 1962, 1972 and 2002 constituted commissions chaired by retired judges, with the Chief Election Commissioner or an Election Commissioner as an ex officio member, to perform it. The Forty-second Amendment of 1976 froze the allocation of Lok Sabha and assembly seats among the states on the census of 1971, and the Eighty-fourth Amendment of 2001 extended the freeze until the first census after 2026, while permitting the redrawing of boundaries within states on the census of 2001, the work the Delimitation Commission under Justice Kuldip Singh performed between 2002 and 2008. The freeze was a population-policy bargain: states that reduced their birth rates would not lose parliamentary weight to states that did not. Its consequence for the Commission is that it administers elections on a map whose interstate proportions embody the demography of 1971, and the malapportionment between states, and between the urban and rural constituencies within them, has grown with every census the freeze has skipped. When the freeze expires, the delimitation that follows will be the largest single redistribution of representation since 1976, and the Commission, as the delimitation body's permanent member and the administrator of whatever map emerges, will stand adjacent to a controversy whose authorship is Parliament's and whose administration is its own. The episode belongs in the hub because it marks the framework's outer boundary: some of the stress that attaches to the Commission's name originates in decisions the Commission does not take, and the discipline of the framework is to assign each stress to the component and the author that produced it, delimitation to the constitutional amendments that froze it, and only the administration of the frozen map to the Commission itself.


The delimitation question also exposes a quieter dependence that the framework should record. The Commission supplies the delimitation body's working data, the constituency-level rolls, polling station geography and census-aligned maps on which boundaries are drawn, and its officers staff the exercise in the states, so the frozen map's administration and the frozen map's eventual replacement both run through the institution that will be blamed for their effects. State governments and parties have learned to treat the Commission as the address for delimitation grievances that belong constitutionally to Parliament, and the Commission's consistent answer, that it administers boundaries it does not draw and allocates no seats among states, is legally complete and politically insufficient, since the body that conducts the election absorbs the argument the election generates. The pattern is the framework in its purest form: an under-specified boundary between two institutions, a dependence of the visible institution on the invisible one's decisions, and stakes high enough to guarantee that the grievance lands on the doorstep of whichever body the voter can name.
## Enrolment: The Quiet Front Line of the Institution

Beneath the controversies that reach the courts and the newspapers runs the Commission's least dramatic and most continuous operation, the maintenance of the electoral roll, and the framework reads it as the place where all three components touch the individual citizen. The roll is the franchise in documentary form: a name absent from it on polling day is a vote that does not exist, whatever the Constitution promises, and the Commission's superintendence of enrolment is therefore the point at which the institution's abstractions become a person's right. The scale defies intuition. The roll for the general election of 2019 carried roughly 911 million names, each attached to a polling station, a part number and a serial entry, maintained through continuous revision, annual summary revision and, at intervals, the intensive revisions in which enumerators verify entries door to door. The machinery of enrolment is the borrowed machinery of the dependence component in its purest form: booth level officers drawn from local government staff, schoolteachers and anganwadi workers verify the names of their own neighbours under the direction of electoral registration officers who answer, for this purpose, to the Commission.

The politics of the roll has its own dated history. The founding revision under Sukumar Sen established the principle that enrolment is an act of citizenship rather than of household membership, in the ruling on women's names already described. The lowering of the voting age from twenty-one to eighteen by the Sixty-first Amendment, passed in 1988 and effective from March 1989, added tens of millions of young electors to the roll at a stroke and converted enrolment of the young into a standing administrative campaign, later institutionalised in the Systematic Voters' Education and Electoral Participation programme the Commission launched in 2009, with its national voters' day each 25 January, the anniversary of the Commission's own founding in 1950. The computerisation of the rolls in the 1990s and 2000s, and the issue of electors' photo identity cards that Seshan had demanded as a condition of electoral integrity, changed the roll's material form without changing its legal character: it remains a list prepared by officers under superintendence, open to claims and objections, and corrected through a public process whose every stage is a small exercise of discretion. Deletions, additions, corrections of names and the shifting of entries between constituencies are decided in lakhs each year by officers the Commission directs but does not appoint, and each decision is, in the framework's terms, an under-specified act with strategic value, since a constituency's roll is the electorate itself, defined in advance of the contest that will claim to speak for it.

The controversies of enrolment therefore follow the framework's script with unusual fidelity. Parties maintain booth level agents to scrutinise draft rolls because the roll is worth scrutinising; the Commission answers with publication, with the display of draft lists, with searchable electronic rolls, and with the transparency technologies that convert suspicion into a checkable record. The special intensive revision announced for Bihar in 2025, and the litigation and public argument it generated over documentation and the burden of proof on the enrolled citizen, is the contemporary form of an argument as old as the institution: Sen's enumerators struck off entries recorded in the wrong form in 1950, and the argument then, as in 2025, was about whether the gateway to the franchise was being administered in the citizen's favour or against it. The hub records the pattern without adjudicating the pending episode, whose facts belong to the pillar's later articles, and draws the structural conclusion the pattern supports. An institution whose daily work is the definition of the electorate will be argued about by everyone who hopes to be elected by it, and the argument's persistence across seventy-five years, through paper rolls, photo rolls and digital rolls, is the quietest and strongest evidence that the stress this article describes is a property of the design and not of any decade's politics.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the Election Commission of India?

The Election Commission of India is the constitutional body established by Article 324 of the Constitution, in force from 26 January 1950, and charged with the superintendence, direction and control of elections to Parliament, to the state legislatures, and to the offices of President and Vice-President. It consists of the Chief Election Commissioner and, since October 1993, two Election Commissioners, and it operates from Nirvachan Sadan in New Delhi through the chief electoral officers of the states and the borrowed machinery of the district administrations. The Commission prepares and revises the electoral rolls, notifies and conducts polling, counts votes and declares results, enforces the Model Code of Conduct, registers political parties and allots their symbols, and advises the President and the governors on legislators' disqualification. On the reading defended across this pillar, it is best understood not as neutral procedural machinery but as a political institution whose design, a vast and briefly worded mandate combined with dependence on the executive for appointments, money and staff, places it under continuous structural stress, and whose history from Sukumar Sen onward is the record of that stress being borne, contested and renewed across eighteen general elections.

### Q: What is Article 324?

Article 324 is the founding provision of Part XV of the Constitution of India, the part governing elections, and the source of the Election Commission's authority. Its first clause vests the superintendence, direction and control of the preparation of electoral rolls and the conduct of all elections to Parliament, the state legislatures and the offices of President and Vice-President in the Election Commission. Its second clause provides that the Commission shall consist of the Chief Election Commissioner and such number of other Election Commissioners as the President may fix, appointed by the President subject to any law made by Parliament. Its fifth clause protects the Chief Election Commissioner from removal except in the manner of a Supreme Court judge, and Election Commissioners from removal except on the Chief Election Commissioner's recommendation. Its sixth clause obliges the President and the state governors to make staff available on request. The article was debated in the Constituent Assembly on 15 and 16 June 1949 as draft Article 289, with B. R. Ambedkar defending its centralisation of election machinery in an independent body.

### Q: How are election commissioners appointed?

Election commissioners are appointed by the President of India under Article 324, clause 2, which for seventy-three years operated without any governing statute, so appointments were made on the advice of the Council of Ministers, in practice by the choice of the Prime Minister of the day, and fell overwhelmingly on senior civil servants. On 2 March 2023 the Supreme Court, in Anoop Baranwal v Union of India, directed that until Parliament legislated, appointments be advised by a committee of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha and the Chief Justice of India. Commissioners hold office for six years or until the age of sixty-five, whichever is earlier. The 2023 Act was challenged in the Supreme Court on the ground that its committee structure restores executive dominance in committee form, and appointments made under it in March 2024 proceeded while the challenge remained pending.

### Q: What is the ECI's institutional-architectural-stress?

The Election Commission's institutional-architectural stress is the condition, named by the framework this article establishes for the pillar, in which the institution's design continuously generates political pressure upon it through three structural features. The second is executive-architectural dependence: the Commission's members were appointed for over seventy years by the executive alone, its operations are financed through government budgets, and its elections are run by borrowed district officials, state police, central forces and machines made by public sector companies. The third is the political-strategic stake carried by its decisions, since schedules, rolls, code rulings and symbol orders redistribute advantage among the parties the Commission regulates, giving every party a permanent incentive to press and litigate against it. Stress is not failure: the Commission has delivered eighteen general elections without collapse. It is the continuous load the architecture places on the institution's authority.

### Q: Are electoral institutions political objects?

On the reading defended in this article, yes: electoral institutions are political objects, meaning that their decisions, structures and controversies are themselves political content rather than neutral infrastructure standing outside politics. The institution's composition is politically determined, since its members are appointed through processes governments design and courts supervise, and its resources are allocated by the executives it regulates. Treating the Commission as a political object does not mean treating it as a partisan actor: the framework distinguishes an institution that operates inside politics, as every election body must, from an institution captured by one political interest, a further claim requiring its own evidence in each episode. David Gilmartin's essays support the wider point that India's electoral process has always carried substantive political meaning, since enrolling every adult as an equal unit of count remade the idea of the political community in a hierarchical society. The procedural framing, which lists the Commission's powers and stops, cannot explain why the same office has been fought over in every decade.

### Q: What does the ECI do?

The Election Commission conducts the elections through which India chooses its governments, and its work divides into continuous administration and the election operation itself. When an election is called, the Commission announces the schedule, brings the Model Code of Conduct into force, deploys observers and central forces, transfers officials whose neutrality it doubts, supervises nominations, polling in phases and counting, enforces candidate expenditure ceilings through observers and seizure operations, and declares the results that constitute the new House. It also conducts the elections of the President and the Vice-President, maintains the affidavit archive of candidates' criminal antecedents, assets and education that the Supreme Court's directions of 2002 and 2003 required, and publishes the statistical reports that form the republic's official record of its own voting. Elections to panchayats and municipalities lie outside its charge, belonging to the State Election Commissions under Articles 243K and 243ZA.

### Q: How does the Representation of the People Act work?

The Representation of the People Acts are the two statutes that convert the Constitution's electoral framework into working law, and they divide the field between them. The Act of 1950 governs the allocation of seats in Parliament and the state legislatures, the preparation and revision of electoral rolls, and the qualifications for registration as an elector. Section 61A, inserted in 1989, authorises voting by electronic machine in constituencies the Commission specifies. The Acts operate inside the space Article 324 marks out: where they speak in detail, the Commission administers them as written; where they are silent or speak in standards, the Commission's constitutional discretion fills the gap, subject to the Supreme Court's doctrine that the discretion be exercised fairly, lawfully and consistently with natural justice. Disputes over results may be raised only by election petition after the declaration, a bar on mid-process litigation upheld in N. P. Ponnuswami v Returning Officer in 1952.

### Q: What did the Goswami Committee recommend on electoral reforms?

The Goswami Committee on Electoral Reforms, constituted by the National Front government under Law Minister Dinesh Goswami and reporting in 1990, is the official diagnosis of the electoral system's stresses at the end of the 1980s, and its recommendations map closely onto the three components of the framework this article establishes. On composition and appointment, it recommended that the Election Commission become a multi-member body and that commissioners be chosen through consultation extending beyond the executive of the day, anticipating by three years the conversion of October 1993 and by three decades the appointment litigation of 2023. On the Commission's instruments, it examined giving the Model Code of Conduct a firmer legal footing and strengthening the law on election expenditure and the disqualification of candidates with criminal records. On resources, it supported charging the Commission's expenditure on the Consolidated Fund of India, a recommendation the Law Commission repeated in its 255th Report of 2015 without enactment following.

### Q: Who was Sukumar Sen and what did he build between 1950 and 1952?

Sukumar Sen was the first Chief Election Commissioner of India, a member of the Indian Civil Service and former chief secretary of West Bengal, who took office on 21 March 1950 and served until December 1958. Sen's design decisions included candidate symbols for an electorate of limited literacy, separate ballot boxes for each candidate in the first election, the training of over a million polling personnel drawn from the district administrations, and a celebrated insistence that women be enrolled under their own names rather than as wives or widows of household heads, enforced by striking off relational entries until corrected. His reports on the first two general elections remain the foundational professional literature of the institution, and he shows the design at its most constructive: the under-specified mandate first generated conventions of impartial administration that his successors inherited.

### Q: What happened during T. N. Seshan's tenure as Chief Election Commissioner?

Tirunellai Narayana Iyer Seshan, a Tamil Nadu cadre officer of the Indian Administrative Service and former cabinet secretary, served as the tenth Chief Election Commissioner from 12 December 1990 to 11 December 1996, and his tenure converted the Commission's dormant discretion into active enforcement on a scale no predecessor had attempted. Successive governments explored the means of restraint and found them in the Constitution's own openness: in October 1993 the government of P. V. Narasimha Rao converted the Commission into a three-member body by ordinance, appointing M. S. Gill and G. V. G. Krishnamurty as Election Commissioners, a move defended as the Goswami Committee's reform and attacked by Seshan as a device to outvote him. His challenge failed in the Supreme Court in 1995, in a judgment that recast the Chief Election Commissioner as chairman among equals. After Seshan, every government selected commissioners, and every commissioner acted, in knowledge of what the office had shown it could do.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide in Mohinder Singh Gill v Chief Election Commissioner?

Mohinder Singh Gill v Chief Election Commissioner, decided by a Constitution Bench in December 1977 and reported in 1978, is the foundational judgment on the scope of the Election Commission's power. The Court upheld the repoll and held that Article 324 is a plenary provision, a reservoir of power covering the entire field of election conduct in areas legislation has left unoccupied, wide enough to sustain the Commission's order. At the same time the Bench bounded the power: the Commission must act within the Representation of the People Acts where they speak, must observe natural justice where its orders injure a candidate, and must conduct itself as a high constitutional functionary whose independence the courts will guard and whose legality they will police. Justice V. R. Krishna Iyer's opinion gave the doctrine its enduring language. The judgment ratified under-specification as constitutional design: the discretion exists because the text is silent.

### Q: When did the Election Commission become a multi-member body?

The Election Commission became a durably multi-member body on 1 October 1993, when the government of Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao promulgated an ordinance fixing the Commission's strength at three and appointed M. S. Gill and G. V. G. Krishnamurty as Election Commissioners alongside the serving Chief Election Commissioner, T. N. Seshan; Parliament subsequently enacted the arrangement. The 1993 conversion was defended publicly as the implementation of the Goswami Committee's recommendation and was understood by its critics, Seshan foremost among them, as a device for outvoting an assertive chief. His challenge reached the Supreme Court, which in T. N. Seshan v Union of India in July 1995 upheld the conversion and held that the Chief Election Commissioner is the chairman of the Commission, not a superior authority over the Election Commissioners, and that the Commission's business is transacted collectively. The three-member form has governed the institution since, structuring its internal controversies, including the dissent notes recorded in 2019.

### Q: How can a Chief Election Commissioner be removed from office?

A Chief Election Commissioner can be removed only in like manner and on like grounds as a judge of the Supreme Court, under the proviso to Article 324, clause 5, the central tenure protection of the office. The grounds are thus confined to proved misbehaviour and incapacity, the process is parliamentary and deliberately laborious, and the protection extends to the rule that the Chief Election Commissioner's conditions of service cannot be varied to his disadvantage after appointment. No Chief Election Commissioner has ever been removed under this process, and its practical history is one of deterrence: governments wishing to restrain assertive commissioners, most visibly during the Seshan tenure of 1990 to 1996, worked around the protection through the Commission's composition rather than against the incumbent's tenure. Election Commissioners stand lower in the same clause: they may not be removed except on the recommendation of the Chief Election Commissioner, an asymmetry tested in the proceedings initiated against Election Commissioner Navin Chawla in 2009.

### Q: When was the none of the above option introduced on Indian ballots?

The none of the above option, known by its abbreviation NOTA, was introduced on Indian ballot units following the Supreme Court's judgment in People's Union for Civil Liberties v Union of India of 27 September 2013, in which the Court held that a voter who does not wish to support any candidate must be able to record that rejection without sacrificing the secrecy of the ballot or abstaining altogether, grounding the right in the freedom of expression under Article 19(1)(a). The option's legal effect is deliberately limited: a plurality of NOTA votes does not void the election or bar the leading candidate, who is declared elected regardless, a position the Supreme Court has declined to disturb. Its significance for this article's framework is institutional rather than arithmetic. NOTA is a feature of the ballot that neither Parliament nor the Commission designed: the judiciary wrote it into the Commission's field on the strength of the voter's rights, and the Commission delivered it within a single election cycle.

### Q: When were electronic voting machines first used in an Indian election?

Electronic voting machines were first used in an Indian election in May 1982, when the Election Commission deployed them experimentally in fifty polling stations of the Parur assembly constituency in Kerala for a by-election, making India one of the earliest democracies to trial machine voting at any scale. The experiment immediately produced the technology's founding legal crisis: the defeated candidate, A. C. Jose, challenged the result, and the Supreme Court held in A. C. Parliament repaired the legal footing by inserting section 61A into the Act in 1989, authorising the Commission to specify constituencies where voting would be by machine. Machines returned to state elections from the late 1990s, and the general election of 2004 was the first in which every Lok Sabha constituency voted electronically, across an electorate of some 671 million. The machines, manufactured by Bharat Electronics Limited and the Electronics Corporation of India Limited, record votes on a control unit without any network connection, and their custody and randomisation procedures are governed by the Commission's own protocols.

### Q: What is the voter verifiable paper audit trail and why was it added?

The voter verifiable paper audit trail, known as VVPAT, is a device attached to the electronic voting machine that prints a paper slip showing the symbol and name of the candidate for whom the machine has recorded a vote, displays the slip behind glass to the voter for a few seconds, and then drops it into a sealed box, creating a paper record that can be counted by hand to audit the machine's electronic tally. It was added because the Supreme Court, in Subramanian Swamy v Election Commission in October 2013, held that a paper trail is an indispensable requirement of free and fair elections, since a purely electronic count leaves the voter and the candidate with no independently checkable record, and directed the Commission to introduce the system in phases. The paper trail relocated the machine controversy, from the integrity of the machines to the adequacy of the audit sample.

### Q: What did the 2023 appointment Act change about selecting election commissioners?

The Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act 2023 changed the selection of India's election commissioners for the first time since the Constitution came into force. The Act replaced that interim committee with a selection committee chaired by the Prime Minister and comprising the Leader of the Opposition and a Union cabinet minister nominated by the Prime Minister, assisted by a search committee that prepares a panel of names. The Act also reset service conditions, equating salary and terms with those of the cabinet secretary rather than with a judge of the Supreme Court as the earlier settlement had provided, while retaining the six-year tenure subject to the age limit of sixty-five and the removal protections of Article 324. Defenders argued that Article 324 reserves appointment to parliamentary law. Critics, including scholars such as E. Sridharan, argued that the structure gives the executive a built-in majority.

### Q: How does the Commission decide which faction of a split party keeps its symbol?

The Commission decides symbol disputes between factions of a recognised party under paragraph 15 of the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order 1968, an instrument issued under its Article 324 authority and upheld by the Supreme Court in Sadiq Ali v Election Commission in 1971. The Commission may allot the reserved symbol to one faction, allot new symbols to both, or freeze the reserved symbol so that neither faction may use it, as in the AIADMK dispute of 2017. The orders in the Samajwadi Party dispute of January 2017, the Shiv Sena dispute of February 2023 and the Nationalist Congress Party dispute of February 2024 each certified one faction as the party, in the later cases principally on the strength of legislative majority, and each drew criticism from the losing faction that the organisational test had been underweighted. The Commission's stated position is that the tests are evidentiary, not hierarchical.

### Q: Do State Election Commissions conduct elections to state assemblies?

No. Elections to the state legislative assemblies are conducted by the Election Commission of India under Article 324, through the chief electoral officer of each state and the district machinery placed at its disposal, and the State Election Commissions have no role in them. The State Election Commissions are separate constitutional bodies created by the Seventy-third and Seventy-fourth Amendments of 1992, charged under Articles 243K and 243ZA with the superintendence, direction and control of elections to the panchayats and the municipalities respectively, and headed by a State Election Commissioner appointed by the governor. The Andhra Pradesh episode of 2020, in which a state government removed its election commissioner by ordinance in the middle of the local election process and the courts restored him, is the clearest instance in the recent record. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that state commissions possess, within their field, the same plenary character that Article 324 confers nationally.

### Q: How many general elections has the Election Commission conducted?

The Election Commission has conducted eighteen general elections to the Lok Sabha from the founding election of 1951 and 1952 through the general election of 2024, together with, on its own tally, some four hundred elections to the state legislative assemblies, beside the elections of the President and the Vice-President and the by-elections that fill casual vacancies between general polls. The scale of the operation has grown with the franchise: the first general election enrolled about 173 million electors for 489 seats, while the general election of 2019 enrolled roughly 911 million electors for 543 seats, the largest electorate ever administered, and the election of 2024 extended the roll further still. This record is the strongest form of the complication addressed in this article's adjudication: an institution can be under continuous structural-political stress and simultaneously deliver, without collapse, the most demanding recurring operation in democratic government, because stress names the load on the institution's authority, not the failure of its function.
