Health laws in India do not begin with a health Act, because at the level of the Union there is none. No statute of Parliament states what the state owes a person who falls ill, creates a general duty to provide care, or organises the health system as a whole. What exists instead is a field of narrow, subject-specific statutes written decades apart, each answering a particular scandal, technology or administrative problem: a statute for drugs, a statute for doctors, a statute for medical colleges, a statute for the hospitals that some states have agreed to register, statutes for transplants, terminations, mental illness and surrogacy, and a four-section statute from 1897 that still carries the law of epidemics. A reader who understands that single fact, that the field has no framework and was never designed as a system, understands the shape of Indian health law better than a reader who has memorised the titles of thirty Acts.

The reason for the shape is constitutional, and it is the subject of the first part of this guide. Public health is primarily a state subject, so the Union has entered the field sideways, through the entries the Constitution does give it: the professions, medical education, drugs, food standards and the prevention of disease crossing state borders. Two of the field's principal statutes were not even enacted under those entries. They were passed under Article 252, on the resolutions of a handful of state legislatures, and they apply only where a state has asked for them or later adopted them, which means that the map of Indian health law is not one map but several, overlapping at different depths in different states. The right to health, meanwhile, exists in India as a judicial construction built on the right to life, not as a legislative entitlement, and the framework statute that would have changed that has been drafted twice, in 2009 and again in 2017, and introduced in Parliament never. This guide maps the whole field: the constitutional division, the colonial skeleton, the product and professional statutes, the facility and practice statutes, the outbreak framework, the case law, the missing framework Act, and the large layer of schemes that govern much of Indian health care without being law at all. Each statute named here is treated at headline depth and signposted to the article in this series that owns it, because the purpose of a map is to show where everything is and how the pieces connect.

## The constitutional division behind health laws in India

Every structural feature of Indian health law, the patchwork, the adopting-state statutes, the dominance of professional regulation over service regulation, follows from three lists in the Seventh Schedule to the Constitution. The State List gives the states "public health and sanitation; hospitals and dispensaries" in Entry 6 of List II, and with those few words it assigns the core of the health system, the running of hospitals, the organisation of sanitation, the day-to-day work of public health administration, to the state legislatures. A state government owns and staffs the district hospitals, runs the primary health centres, employs the public health workforce and answers politically when the system fails. Parliament cannot legislate directly on that entry for the states, and the great bulk of what a citizen experiences as the health system sits inside it.

The Concurrent List then carves out the subjects on which both Parliament and the state legislatures may legislate, and it is through these entries that the Union has built most of its health law. Entry 26 of List III covers "legal, medical and other professions", which is the constitutional home of the statutes that decide who may practise medicine and under what discipline. Entry 25 covers education, expressly including medical education and universities, subject to the Union List entries on institutions of national importance and on the coordination and determination of standards in higher education, and it is the home of the medical education statutes. Entry 19 covers drugs and poisons, subject to the Union List entry on opium, and Entry 18 covers the adulteration of foodstuffs and other goods, and between them they carry the drug and food safety legislation. Entry 29 covers the prevention of the extension from one state to another of infectious or contagious diseases or pests affecting men, animals or plants, the one expressly health-specific concurrent entry, and Entry 20A, inserted by the Forty-second Amendment, covers population control and family planning. Entry 16 of the same list, a survival of colonial drafting, covers lunacy and mental deficiency, including places for their reception and treatment, and Entry 30 covers vital statistics, including the registration of births and deaths.

The Union List completes the frame with the subjects that are national by nature. Entry 28 of List I covers port quarantine, including hospitals connected with it, and Entry 81 covers inter-State migration and inter-State quarantine, so the border-facing parts of disease control were Union subjects from the start. Entry 66 gives the Union the coordination and determination of standards in institutions for higher education and research, which is why the Union's grip on medical education is firmer than its grip on medical services: the Constitution lets the Centre set the standard for the college even though the state runs the hospital attached to it. The result is the field's defining asymmetry. The Union regulates the producers of health care, the doctors, the colleges, the drugs, the food, and the states operate the system that delivers care, and any account of Indian health law that ignores the asymmetry will misread every statute in it.

### Which entries in the Seventh Schedule mention health or medicine?

The field is carried by Entry 6 of the State List for public health, sanitation, hospitals and dispensaries; by Concurrent List entries on drugs and poisons, adulteration of foodstuffs, medical education, the medical and other professions and disease crossing state borders; and by Union List entries on port quarantine, inter-State quarantine and standards in higher education.

Those entries explain, in advance, nearly every argument in the field. When the Union regulates a new medical technology, it reaches for the professions entry, the drugs entry or the education entry, because those are the doors open to it. When a state resists a central health measure, it points to Entry 6 and asks by what right Parliament is legislating on hospitals at all. When the two levels legislate on the same concurrent subject and the texts collide, Article 254 resolves the collision in favour of the central law, unless the state law was reserved for the President's consideration and received assent, in which case the state law prevails within that state until Parliament chooses to legislate over it. And when Parliament wants to legislate squarely on a state subject, as it twice did in this field, it must either persuade states to invite it in under Article 252 or wait for the exceptional routes of a Rajya Sabha resolution in the national interest under Article 249 or a proclaimed emergency under Article 250. The entries are not background. They are the operating system on which every statute described below runs, and the fuller treatment of how the three lists work sits with [the guide to the Seventh Schedule and its lists](/2018/09/15/seventh-schedule-lists-explained/), which owns that ground for the series.

One more constitutional text matters, and it is not in the Seventh Schedule at all. Article 47, among the Directive Principles of State Policy, provides that the State shall regard the raising of the level of nutrition and the standard of living of its people and the improvement of public health as among its primary duties. The Directive Principles are not enforceable by any court, so Article 47 creates no claim a patient can sue on, but it has been read into the enforceable right to life by the courts, it is quoted in the preamble of the clinical establishments statute as its constitutional mandate, and it is the text governments cite when they explain why health spending is a duty rather than a discretion. Articles 39, 41 and 42 add the health of workers, public assistance in sickness and disablement, and maternity relief to the same unenforceable but interpretively powerful layer. The Indian health constitution, in short, assigns the services to the states, the standards to the Centre, and the aspiration to a part of the Constitution that no court can directly enforce, and the statutes have been filling the space between those three decisions ever since.

Two historical decisions sharpen the picture, because both show the allocation being chosen rather than merely inherited. The first is the treatment of education. Education, like public health, began in the State List, and the Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976 moved education into the Concurrent List, where Entry 25 expressly includes medical education. Health services were not moved. The transfer of education and the non-transfer of health happened in the same amending Act, in the same Parliament, in the same year, and the contrast is the clearest evidence that the state character of health services is a maintained position of the constitutional order rather than an unnoticed survival. Proposals to repeat the operation for health, by shifting public health in whole or part to the Concurrent List, resurface after every major outbreak and are regularly discussed in the reports of bodies that review centre-state relations, and they have never commanded the agreement of the states whose consent a constitutional amendment of that kind politically requires. The second sharpening decision is fiscal. The Constitution gives the Union no general power to run state hospitals, but Article 282 permits the Union to make grants for any public purpose, and the Union has used its superior revenues to build the centrally sponsored scheme, an instrument by which central money enters a state subject against central conditions, plan approvals and cost-sharing ratios. The National Health Mission and the assurance schemes described below all travel on this fiscal bridge, and it explains how the Centre can be the dominant funder of priorities inside a field it may not legislate on directly. Competence and influence are different things in Indian health federalism: the states hold the competence, and the Centre, through the professions entries, the standards entries and the grant power, holds much of the influence.

The standards question deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is where courts have most often had to police the boundary. Where a state law on a concurrent subject and a central law on a Union standards entry overlap, the Supreme Court has consistently protected the Union's power to determine standards in higher education, reading Entry 66 of List I as a specific power that state legislation under the education entry cannot dilute. In the health field this doctrine has concrete consequences: a state may found medical colleges, fund them and administer them, but the standards of the education they deliver, the qualifications they award and the recognition of those qualifications are governed nationally, and a state rule that lowers or contradicts the national standard fails. The parallel doctrine in the professions is similar in effect. State medical councils register and discipline, but they do so inside a statutory frame whose apex body is created by Parliament, and the practitioner who moves between states carries a nationally recognised qualification into a state-kept register. The boundary is not always peaceful, and councils and commissions have litigated their respective spheres, but the direction of the case law has been steady: uniformity of standard is a Union interest the courts will defend, while the operation of services is a state domain they will not enter by interpretation.

Below the two legislative tiers sits a third, which the Constitution added in 1992 and which health lawyers ignore at their peril. The Eleventh Schedule, read with Article 243G, lists among the subjects that may be devolved to panchayats "health and sanitation, including hospitals, primary health centres and dispensaries", together with family welfare and women and child development, while the Twelfth Schedule does the corresponding work for municipalities. The devolution is permissive in form, since the schedules list what state legislatures may entrust to local bodies, and its depth varies enormously, but its direction is unmistakable in the states that acted on it: Kerala devolved institutions and staff to local governments on a large scale, so that a Kerala primary health centre is in meaningful part a panchayat institution, while in most states the local bodies remain sanitary authorities rather than service providers. The three-tier picture is therefore the complete one. The Union sets the professional, product and educational standards; the states own the service system; and the municipalities and panchayats carry the sanitary substrate, the water, waste, vector control and local facilities on which the rest silently depends. When public health fails in an Indian city, the failure is located somewhere in that stack, and a competent analysis begins by identifying which tier's law has failed, because the remedy, the forum and the responsible government differ at each level.

A word is needed on the door that does not open, because it is the first one an ingenious draftsman tries. Article 248 and Entry 97 of the Union List give Parliament the residuary power to legislate on any matter not enumerated in the State or Concurrent Lists, and a general law of public health might appear to be such a matter. It is not. Public health is enumerated, in terms, in Entry 6 of the State List, and the settled doctrine of the courts is that the residuary power is engaged only where the subject cannot be found in any of the three lists, and cannot be used to enter a field the Constitution has expressly assigned to the states. The same logic defeated attempts to locate other state subjects in the residuary entry and it would defeat a residuary public health Act: Parliament cannot do by the back door of Entry 97 what Entry 6 of List II forbids at the front. This is why the honest routes to a national framework are exactly the three the Constitution itself marks out, consent under Article 252, a Rajya Sabha resolution under Article 249 in the national interest, and constitutional amendment, and why every serious framework proposal in the field's history has been drafted in the grammar of cooperation rather than command. The constraint is not a technicality to be drafted around. It is the federal settlement itself, and a guide to this field that treated it as an inconvenience would misunderstand the field's every statute.

## The colonial skeleton under the field

The field did not begin in 1950, and its oldest working part is older than the Republic by half a century. The Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897, Act 3 of 1897, was passed in February 1897 in the emergency of the Bombay plague, and it remains the principal epidemic statute of the Union. Around it, the provinces built their own public health law: the Madras Public Health Act, 1939, and its counterparts of the same vintage in other provinces organised sanitation, vaccination, nuisance control and local health officers for the territories they governed, and several of those provincial statutes, amended and renamed, still supply the public health machinery of their successor states. That is why outbreak law in India is not uniform. The 1897 Act is a shell into which each state pours its own administrative tradition, and the state public health Acts of the colonial period are the reason two neighbouring states can face the same disease with noticeably different legal toolkits.

The professions and products layers are colonial in origin too. The Indian Medical Council Act, 1933 created a central council for medical qualifications and registration in British India, and its successor of 1956 inherited the design: a national register's standards set at the centre, the actual registers kept by provincial and later state councils. The Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 followed the report of the Drugs Enquiry Committee chaired by R. N. Chopra, which had reported in 1931 on the chaos of unregulated drug manufacture and sale, and the Act's architecture, central control of imports and standards, state control of manufacture and sale licensing, is the committee's federal compromise preserved in statute. The Pharmacy Act, 1948, the Dentists Act, 1948 and the Indian Nursing Council Act, 1947 completed the first ring of professional councils in the years around independence, each on the same template of a central council setting educational standards and state chapters keeping registers.

Independence changed the constitutional allocation but not the drafting habit. The Constitution's framers kept public health with the units, as the Government of India Act, 1935 had done before them, and the new republic legislated in the field the way the old regime had: one subject at a time, in response to a specific pressure, through whichever entry lay closest to hand. The Indian Medical Council Act, 1956, Act 102 of 1956, replaced the 1933 Act and governed medical education and registration for more than six decades. The pattern visible across the whole period is that Indian health law has always grown by accretion. No generation has sat down to design it. Each generation has added the statute its particular crisis demanded, and the guide that follows this one through the field has to be read with that in mind, because the gaps between the statutes are as much a part of the law as the statutes themselves.

The one moment at which the field nearly received a design came at the end of the colonial period, and its fate foreshadows everything after. The Health Survey and Development Committee, appointed in 1943 under the chairmanship of Joseph Bhore and reporting in 1946, recommended an integrated, tax-funded national health service for India, with salaried medical services, a pyramid of primary and secondary units, and the fusion of preventive and curative care. The Bhore blueprint was the most complete plan for Indian health ever officially produced, and it was implemented only in fragments: the primary health centre network descends from it, but the integrated statutory service did not follow, because the Constitution that arrived four years later placed the subject with the provinces and the new states built what their budgets allowed within their own statutes. Vaccination law shows the same layering in miniature. The Vaccination Act, 1880 made vaccination against smallpox compulsory in municipalities and notified areas in the nineteenth century, an early demonstration that coercive public health power in India was exercised locally, under provincial and municipal authority, long before the Republic; smallpox was eradicated and the statute faded, but the pattern it set, compulsion by local instrument under a general enabling frame, remained the Indian pattern. When the Government of India Act, 1935 distributed subjects between the federation and the provinces, public health went to the provinces, and the Constituent Assembly, working with the 1935 scheme before it, carried the distribution into the Seventh Schedule with the modifications described above. The colonial skeleton is thus not merely chronological background. The entries, the council template, the shell epidemic statute and the unbuilt blueprint are all colonial inheritances, and the independent republic's health law is best read as the continued occupation of that skeleton by new statutes.

## Products and safety: drugs, food, narcotics and the advertising of cures

The most complete central regulation in the field is not of services at all but of things: the medicines, foods and substances that enter the body. The Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940, Act 23 of 1940, regulates the import, manufacture, distribution and sale of drugs and cosmetics across India, and its machinery expresses the constitutional compromise in institutional form. Standards for drugs are set nationally, the approval of a new drug is a central function exercised through the Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation and the Drugs Controller General of India, and the licensing of manufacturing premises and of sale establishments belongs to the state licensing authorities, so that a single tablet sits under two levels of government at different points of its journey. The Act's schedules and rules classify drugs by the conditions of their sale, its prohibitions run against misbranded, adulterated and spurious drugs, and medical devices have been drawn within its definition of drugs by notification, which is how a statute written for medicines came to govern implants and instruments. The statute is the field's workhorse and its age shows in its penalties and procedures, which is why its replacement has been drafted and debated; the full analysis belongs to [the guide to the Drugs and Cosmetics Act 1940](/2015/06/15/drugs-and-cosmetics-act-1940-explained/), which owns the statute for this series.

Price is regulated on a separate track that surprises readers who assume the drugs Act does it. The ceiling prices of scheduled formulations are fixed by the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority under the Drugs (Prices Control) Order, and that order is issued not under the drugs statute but under the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, a general statute of economic control. The legal consequence is that the affordability of medicines in India rests on the proposition that a medicine is an essential commodity whose price the Union may control in the public interest, and the litigation over price control has been fought on that ground rather than on the ground of health. Around the core statute sit two narrower ones. The Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985, Act 61 of 1985, consolidates the law against illicit traffic in narcotic drugs, implements India's obligations under the international drug conventions, and operates as a prohibitory criminal statute with stringent penalties and forfeiture, deliberately kept apart from the regulatory statute so that the medicine and the contraband versions of the same molecule are governed by different logics. The Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954, Act 21 of 1954, prohibits the advertisement of remedies claiming to cure a scheduled list of conditions and to work by magic, a small statute that marks the boundary the law draws between commerce and quackery in the health market.

Food was brought into the same safety architecture later and more completely. The Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006, Act 34 of 2006, consolidated a scatter of food laws and orders, among them the Prevention of Food Adulteration Act, 1954 and the food product orders administered by different ministries, into a single statute administered by a single authority, the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India, with state food safety commissioners and licensing officers beneath it. Its constitutional footing rests on the Concurrent List entries on adulteration of foodstuffs, on drugs and poisons where relevant, and on the Union's trade and standards powers, and its design followed the international movement from punishing adulteration after the fact to prescribing science-based standards for the whole food chain. The food statute matters to a guide on health law for a structural reason: it is the clearest example in the field of Parliament replacing a patchwork with a framework, consolidating enactments, creating one regulator and covering the subject whole, which is precisely the operation that has never been performed for health services themselves. The contrast is worth holding. Where the object of regulation was a product that crosses state borders in trade, consolidation proved possible. Where the object is a hospital inside a state, it has not.

Two features of the product layer deserve emphasis because they recur across the field. The first is the division between standard-setting at the centre and enforcement in the states, which means the quality of drug and food regulation a citizen actually meets depends heavily on the inspectorate of her state, and the statutes conceal wide variation in state capacity behind a uniform national text. The second is that this layer is where the Union's presence in daily health experience is strongest: the medicine bought in any state answers to a national standard, even though the clinic where it is prescribed may answer to no national standard at all. The field regulates what goes into the patient more thoroughly than what happens to the patient, and that inversion is one of the deepest facts about it.

Three corners of the product layer repay a closer look, because each shows a different regulatory technique. Blood is the first. Blood banks and transfusion services are licensed under the drugs statute and its rules, blood being treated within that framework, and the framework was tightened under judicial pressure: in proceedings brought by Common Cause, the Supreme Court in 1996 directed the licensing and regulation of blood banks and the phasing out of professional blood donation, after which the national blood policy and the licensing regime were rebuilt around voluntary donation and mandatory testing. The episode is a recurring Indian pattern in this field, a regulatory gap inside an existing statute closed by litigation rather than by amendment. Medical devices are the second corner. For most of the statute's life only notified devices were regulated at all, drawn in as drugs by executive notification, and a dedicated Medical Devices Rules was framed in 2017 under the 1940 Act, after which the net was widened by notification to cover all medical devices in phases. A twenty-first-century industry is therefore governed by being defined into a statute written for tablets and tinctures, which works and also demonstrates the cost of the missing framework habit: each new technology must be smuggled into an old text. The third corner is the profession that handles the products. The Pharmacy Act, 1948 created the Pharmacy Council of India and the state pharmacy councils and made registration the condition of dispensing, so the sale of medicines is doubly regulated, the product by the drugs statute and the seller by the pharmacy statute, and the weakness of enforcement against unqualified dispensing is a standing illustration of the distance between the field's texts and its ground reality.

Price control deserves its mechanism to be stated once, precisely, because it is so often misattributed. The National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority is an executive body created by government resolution in 1997, and its legal power flows from the Drugs (Prices Control) Order issued under Section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, the provision by which the Union controls the production, supply and distribution of essential commodities. When manufacturers have challenged ceiling prices, the contest has been fought on the terms of the Order and the pricing policy, in litigation that reached the Supreme Court on the inclusion of essential drugs within control, rather than on any health statute. The practical consequence for the map is that the affordability of medicines, arguably the health question citizens feel most directly, is an application of commodity control law administered by the Department of Pharmaceuticals in the Ministry of Chemicals and Fertilizers, a different ministry from the one that administers the health statutes. A reader looking for the law of medicine prices in the health ministry's statute book will not find it, and that filing accident, replicated across the field, is one more reason the field needs a map rather than a shelf.

The food statute's own machinery shows what consolidation buys when it is allowed to happen. The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India frames the science-based standards through scientific panels and a scientific committee, licenses and registers food businesses through a central and state licensing structure, and works in the states through commissioners of food safety and their designated and licensing officers, while the older world of municipal food inspectors and adulteration prosecutions under the 1954 Act was wound into the new system. Two lessons from the food consolidation recur in the framework debate and should be recorded here. The first is that consolidation was possible because the subject-matter travelled: food crosses state lines in trade, the Union's commerce and standards powers were adequate to the task without any state's consent, and the states accepted a national regulator for a product in a way they have never accepted for a hospital. The second is that consolidation did not end the federal division of labour; it regularised it, leaving enforcement, prosecution and the licensing of most businesses with state officers whose capacity continues to vary, so that the same text yields different food safety in different states in the same way that the drugs statute yields different drug control. Uniformity of text is achievable in this field. Uniformity of administration is a harder and separate problem, and the framework debate goes wrong whenever it confuses the two.

Nutrition completes the products layer, and it brings with it the field's largest entitlement statute, which is usually shelved under food rather than health. The National Food Security Act, 2013, Act 20 of 2013, converted the public distribution of foodgrains and a set of nutritional supports into legal entitlements: subsidised grain for covered households, meals and take-home rations for children and for pregnant and lactating mothers through the anganwadi and school systems, and a maternity benefit for pregnant women, all resting on the same Article 47 text that names the raising of the level of nutrition a primary duty of the State. The statute matters to this guide for two reasons. It proves that Parliament can write enforceable welfare entitlements in the health borderland when it chooses the food door, since foodstuffs travel under the Concurrent and Union trade entries rather than under the State List's health entry; and it shows the characteristic Indian route to a health-adjacent right, which is through the stomach and the supply chain rather than through the hospital. A reader tracing how India legislates about the determinants of health will repeatedly find this pattern: the strongest statutory rights in the neighbourhood of health attach to grain, water and wages, and the weakest attach to medical care itself.

The environmental determinants extend the same logic one step further, and they supply the field's third and oldest modern example of consent legislation. Safe water and air are public health subjects in substance whatever their constitutional filing, and Parliament legislated for water pollution through the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974, which was itself enacted under Article 252 on the resolutions of state legislatures, water being a State List subject, and which created the central and state pollution control boards. The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 took the opposite route, resting on Article 253 and India's international commitments after the Stockholm Conference, and it carries the rules on biomedical waste generated by hospitals, so that the disposal of a hospital's needles and dressings is governed by rules under an environmental statute administered by pollution boards rather than by any health Act. The pattern is familiar enough by this point to be stated as a rule: when the Union has legislated comprehensively about a determinant of health, it has done so under a trade, environment or consent entry, never under a general health power, because no such power exists. The determinants are governed; the care is not; and the joining of the two in one legal scheme remains the unattempted work.

## Professions and education: who may practise, and who decides

If the product layer is the field's most complete central regulation, the professions layer is its most contested. Entry 26 of the Concurrent List puts the medical and other professions within the reach of both levels of government, Entry 25 does the same for medical education subject to the Union's standards entries, and Parliament used those doors to build a family of statutory councils and commissions, one for each recognised system and branch of practice. For modern medicine, the Indian Medical Council Act, 1956 created the Medical Council of India, which set standards for undergraduate and postgraduate medical education, recognised qualifications, and maintained the Indian Medical Register, while the state medical councils, creatures of state statutes, kept the registers of practitioners and exercised discipline over them. A doctor's legal existence was therefore always dual: educated to a national standard, registered and answerable in a state.

That architecture was replaced, after decades of criticism of the Council's functioning and repeated interventions by Parliament, by the National Medical Commission Act, 2019, Act 30 of 2019, which repealed the 1956 Act with effect from 25 September 2020 and substituted a nominated commission working through four autonomous boards for undergraduate education, postgraduate education, assessment and rating of colleges, and ethics and medical registration. The change was fought over precisely because it redistributed power: from an elected professional body toward an appointed one, and from the profession toward the government of the day, and the arguments remain live in the successor articles. The replacement is analysed in full in [the guide to the National Medical Commission Act 2019](/2015/07/01/national-medical-commission-act-2019/), and the comparison with the council it replaced belongs to that cluster too. What matters for the map is the pattern, because the same pattern was then applied across the health professions in a deliberate legislative sequence.

The National Commission for Indian System of Medicine Act, 2020, Act 14 of 2020, replaced the Indian Medicine Central Council Act, 1970 and governs education and practice in Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha and Sowa-Rigpa. The National Commission for Homoeopathy Act, 2020, Act 15 of 2020, replaced the Homoeopathy Central Council Act, 1973 on the same model. The National Commission for Allied and Healthcare Professions Act, 2021, Act 14 of 2021, extended statutory regulation to the large workforce of allied and healthcare professions, from laboratory and radiography technologists to physiotherapists and behavioural health workers, many of whom had practised without any statutory register at all. Nursing followed with the National Nursing and Midwifery Commission Act, 2023, Act 26 of 2023, replacing the Indian Nursing Council Act, 1947, and dentistry was moved to a commission model by a statute of 2023 replacing the Dentists Act, 1948, while pharmacy continues under the Pharmacy Act, 1948 and its council. Read together, these statutes show a legislature systematically rebuilding the professions layer on one template within a single decade: appointed commissions, autonomous boards for education and registration, national registers, and a stated goal of uniform standards, with the states retaining the registration machinery and a defined share in the commissions' composition.

Two tensions run through this layer and no reader should miss them. The first is between standard and control: everyone in the debate accepts that a medical degree in one state must mean the same thing in another, which requires a national standard-setter, but the step from setting standards to approving colleges, fixing fee bands and directing the regulator is a step from coordination toward administration, and the state governments and the profession have resisted it in those terms. The second tension is between the systems of medicine themselves. The statutes maintain parallel universes of recognised practice, modern medicine under one commission, the Indian systems under another, homoeopathy under a third, and the contested question of cross-system practice, whether a practitioner of one system may prescribe the drugs of another, has been answered by the courts largely in the negative and by state governments in both directions at different times. The law recognises many kinds of doctor and polices the borders between them, and those borders are legal constructs as much as medical ones.

The human machinery under the commission statutes deserves attention, because the registers are where the professions layer touches the individual practitioner. A doctor who qualifies in one state and wishes to practise in another deals with the state medical council of the second state, whose register is the operative legal record of entitlement to practise, and the state councils are not branches of the national body but statutory corporations under state Acts, some of them older than independence in origin, with their own elected members, registrars and disciplinary committees. Complaints against a practitioner for professional misconduct are heard in that state machinery in the first instance, with the national commission's ethics and registration board standing above it under the 2019 Act. The design produces the confusion the brief for this article flags as a live public problem: a patient wronged in a hospital may be told to go to the medical council, the consumer commission, the police or the facility's registering authority, and each direction is correct for a different slice of the wrong. The council can strike at the licence for professional misconduct but cannot award the patient compensation; the consumer commission can award compensation for deficiency in service but cannot touch the licence; the criminal law can punish the gravest negligence but is a blunt instrument for clinical error. No forum sees the whole case, and the fragmentation of remedy is a direct expression of the fragmentation of the statutes themselves.

The replacement of the elected Medical Council by the nominated Commission was the professions layer's great controversy, and it should be recorded with its holders named. The Indian Medical Association, the largest body of practitioners of modern medicine, opposed the National Medical Commission Bill through its passage, objecting to the nominated structure, to the proposed bridge provisions for practitioners of other systems, and to the breadth of the Commission's fee and exit-examination powers, and it carried its opposition into strikes and litigation. The government's case, made through the Health Ministry in Parliament and resting on a decade of reporting on the old Council's corruption and paralysis, including its supersession and government by a board of governors in its final years, was that an elected regulator had been captured by the interests it regulated and that a smaller, appointed, board-based commission would be more transparent and more accountable. The 2019 Act enacted the government's model with modifications. Which model better serves patients is a genuinely open institutional question, and the successor articles argue it on the record; the map's duty is to note that the same argument, election against nomination, professional self-government against state direction, is inscribed in every commission statute of the 2019 to 2023 sequence and is the deepest political fault line in the professions layer.

A distinct shelf of the professions and education layer holds the institutes of national importance, and it explains where the Centre's own hospitals come from. The All India Institute of Medical Sciences Act, 1956, Act 25 of 1956, created the first AIIMS as an autonomous statutory institution with degree-granting powers, resting on the Union List entries for institutions of national importance and for standards, and the model was repeated for the Postgraduate Institute at Chandigarh by a statute of 1966, for the Jawaharlal Institute at Puducherry by a statute of 2008, and for the new AIIMS established across the states in the expansion rounds, each declared an institution of national importance and each governed by its governing body under the parent pattern. These institutes are the visible tip of the Union's direct presence in service delivery: a few dozen islands of central operation in a state ocean, created one statute or one amendment at a time, exactly as the field's accretion habit predicts. Beside them stands the research establishment, of which the Indian Council of Medical Research is the head and the standing anomaly: the country's apex medical research body, founded in 1911 as a research fund association, is not a statutory corporation at all but a society sustained by government grant through the Department of Health Research, so that the knowledge infrastructure of the field, like much of its delivery infrastructure, rests on executive rather than legislative foundations. The pattern recurs so often in this guide that it deserves to be named as one of the field's laws: in Indian health, the more strategic the function, the more likely it is to have been built without a statute.

Occupational health forms a small, complete sub-field of its own, and it shows the same architecture built from the labour side. The Factories Act, 1948 contains a chapter on the health of workers, prescribing cleanliness, ventilation, drinking water, latrines and the notification of occupational diseases, enforced by state factory inspectorates under a concurrent labour entry; the Employees' State Insurance Act, 1948 adds the insurance and medical benefit system already noticed; the Mines Act and the plantations legislation carry parallel provisions for their workforces; and the consolidating labour codes of 2020, including the code on occupational safety, health and working conditions, were enacted to absorb these statutes into a unified text, with their commencement a matter of staged notification. The constitutional case law on workers' health, from the asbestos litigation onward, was built on this statutory shelf, and the occupational shelf matters to the map because it is the one place where a recognisable code of health protection, duties, inspectorates, offences, compensation, existed early and worked tolerably: proof, within the field itself, that the Indian legislature can build such a system when the subject reaches it through an entry it fully controls.

## Facilities: the hospital on the register, or off it

Between the products and the professions lies the institution where care actually happens, and it is here that the field's constitutional logic produces its strangest result: the registration and minimum-standards regulation of hospitals and clinics in India depends on where the hospital is. The Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act, 2010, Act 23 of 2010, requires clinical establishments to register, to meet prescribed minimum standards of facilities and services, to maintain records and to display their rates, and its preamble expressly invokes Article 47 and the improvement of public health as its mandate. But a hospital is a State List subject, and Parliament had no power to enact that statute for the states on its own authority. The Act was therefore passed under Article 252 of the Constitution, the consent route by which two or more state legislatures may resolve that Parliament should legislate on a state subject for them, after resolutions were passed by the legislatures of Arunachal Pradesh, Himachal Pradesh, Mizoram and Sikkim. It applied in the first instance to those four states and to the Union territories, it was brought into force in 2012, and it extends to any other state only when that state adopts it by the same kind of resolution. The consent device is a general feature of Indian federalism, and the series has traced it in the opposite direction, in the repeal of a central statute that itself rested on state consent, in [the account of the urban land ceiling repeal](/2013/12/01/urban-land-ceiling-act-repeal/), which owns the Article 252 story for the series.

Adoption has been partial in a way that maps the politics of the subject exactly. A further group of states, including Assam, Bihar, Haryana, Jharkhand, Rajasthan, Telangana, Uttar Pradesh and Uttarakhand, adopted the central Act, so it governs in a substantial part of the country. Several large states did not adopt it and instead kept or wrote their own statutes: Maharashtra continues with the Bombay Nursing Homes Registration Act, 1949, a licensing statute of the old pattern; Tamil Nadu regulates private clinical establishments under its own Act of 1997; Karnataka under the Karnataka Private Medical Establishments Act, 2007; West Bengal replaced its older legislation with the West Bengal Clinical Establishments (Registration, Regulation and Transparency) Act, 2017; and Kerala enacted the Kerala Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act, 2018, in force from 2019. Delhi retains its own nursing homes registration statute of 1953. The consequence, stated plainly, is that the question whether a private nursing home must meet statutory minimum standards, register with a public authority and display its charges has different legal answers in different states, and in the gaps between the central Act's adopting states and the states with home-grown statutes of varying strength, some establishments answer to little more than municipal trade licensing and the general law.

### Who runs a government hospital in India, the Centre or the state?

The state government runs it. Entry 6 of the State List places hospitals and dispensaries with the states, so district hospitals, primary health centres and community health centres are state institutions staffed by state services. The Centre runs only a few institutions of its own, such as the All India Institutes of Medical Sciences, and otherwise finances state systems.

The facilities layer also shows why the Article 252 finding is the article's structural discovery rather than a technicality. Article 252 legislation has a feature that ordinary central legislation lacks: once a state has consented and Parliament has legislated, the resulting Act can be amended or repealed, in its application to that state, only by Parliament, and not by the state legislature itself. A state that adopts the clinical establishments statute therefore hands away a measure of its own legislative freedom in exchange for a ready-made national template, and a state that merely dislikes parts of the template cannot rewrite them for itself. That rigidity is one honest reason adoption has stalled, alongside the resistance of private providers to registration, rate display and prescribed standards, and the reluctance of state governments to acquire a regulatory burden whose costs are local and whose credit is not. The statute's defenders answer that a floor of standards that any state can dilute for itself is not a floor at all. Both positions are really about the same question, which is whether the quality of a hospital is a matter of national citizenship or of local choice, and the Constitution, characteristically, has arranged for the question to be answered state by state.

The central Act's machinery is worth one paragraph, because the contrast between its paper completeness and its territorial incompleteness is the facilities problem in miniature. The Act creates a national council and state councils for clinical establishments, a system of provisional and then permanent registration through district registering authorities, classification of establishments by type, prescribed minimum standards for infrastructure, staffing and services, duties to maintain records and to stabilise patients in emergencies, and monetary penalties for operating unregistered. It exempts establishments of the armed forces and is designed to cover government and private establishments alike within its territory. On paper, in an adopting state, an Indian hospital is a registered, classified, standards-bound institution whose charges are displayed and whose records are inspectable. The difficulty is everything the paper cannot do: the standards must be notified and staffed by the adopting state, the district authorities must exist and act, and the states that judged the burden greater than the benefit simply stayed out, leaving their establishments to older and thinner regimes. The facility layer therefore delivers, in the states that adopted the Act and built its machinery, the closest thing in Indian health law to modern institutional regulation, and, a border away, a regime in which a nursing home's legal personality may rest on a municipal licence and the Shops and Establishments Act. Both are called health care regulation in India, and the difference between them is not drafting quality but a resolution passed, or not passed, in a state legislature.

The administrative machinery that operates all of this within a state has its own legal anatomy, and no map is complete without it. A state health department works through directorates that divide the field among themselves: a directorate of health services for curative institutions, a directorate of public health and preventive medicine for the sanitary and disease-control functions in the states that maintain the distinction, a directorate of medical education for the teaching hospitals and colleges, and mission directorates that run the centrally sponsored programmes as parallel structures with their own societies, accounts and contractual staff. At the district, the chief medical officer or civil surgeon stands at the point where every statute in this guide converges on one office: the officer who supervises the district hospital, answers for the primary health centres, receives the notifications of disease, conducts the medico-legal work, and implements the registration and inspection duties the facility and practice statutes assign. The district is the real unit of Indian health law in operation, and its officer is the real regulator most providers ever meet. The recurring proposal for a dedicated public health cadre, a specialist service of public health officers distinct from the clinical services, which the 2017 draft Bill contemplated and which a few states approximate through their public health directorates, is best understood as an attempt to give this machinery a profession of its own, since the present system staffs public health posts largely with clinicians whose training, incentives and careers lie in curative medicine. A framework statute would settle the cadre question as part of its architecture; in its absence the question is answered state by state, mostly by default, and the quality of a state's preventive system tracks the answer more closely than it tracks any text in the statute book.

One soft-law instrument belongs in this section because it operates only through the facilities layer and shows that layer's characteristic half-life. A Charter of Patients' Rights, drafted by the National Human Rights Commission, was adopted by the Union Health Ministry in 2018 and commended to the states and to the councils under the clinical establishments framework for incorporation into the conditions of registration: rights to information about one's condition and treatment, to records and reports, to emergency care, to informed consent, to confidentiality, to a second opinion, to transparency in rates, and to redressal, among others. The charter is not a statute and creates no free-standing claim; its legal force, where it has any, arrives indirectly, when a registering authority writes it into the standards a registered establishment must observe, or when a consumer commission treats its norms as evidence of the service reasonably to be expected. It is thus a characteristic artefact of the field as this guide has mapped it: a national statement of what patients are owed, promulgated by the Centre, dependent for its effect on state machinery it cannot command, and enforceable, if at all, through forums built for other purposes. The distance between the charter's text and a patient's remedy is the distance this whole article measures.

## Specific practices: the statutes written around single procedures

A third layer of the field consists of statutes each built around one practice that raised a problem general law could not handle, and these statutes are where health law most openly makes moral and social choices. The Pre-natal Diagnostic Techniques (Regulation and Prevention of Misuse) Act, 1994, Act 57 of 1994, renamed the Pre-Conception and Pre-natal Diagnostic Techniques Act after its amendment in 2003, prohibits the determination and disclosure of the sex of a foetus and regulates the clinics and machines that could be used for it, and it works by registering every ultrasound facility and treating the diagnostic encounter itself as the point of control. The Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971, Act 34 of 1971, created the conditions on which a registered medical practitioner may lawfully terminate a pregnancy, displacing the near-total prohibition of the penal code with a medicalised permission structured by gestational limits and opinions, and its amendment by Act 8 of 2021 extended the limits, introduced medical boards for specified categories and strengthened confidentiality. The depth on both statutes belongs to their own articles in this series; the map records what kind of instruments they are, statutes that license a practice by defining the exact conditions of its legality.

The Transplantation of Human Organs Act, 1994, Act 42 of 1994, extended to tissues and renamed by its amendment of 2011, is the second of the field's Article 252 statutes, and its preamble records the same constitutional route: resolutions of the legislatures of Goa, Himachal Pradesh and Maharashtra asked Parliament to legislate, the Act applied first to those states and the Union territories, it was brought into force in 1995, and other states have adopted it since, so that a statute against the commercial trade in organs also has a consent-based map. Its substance is a prohibition system: removal of organs is lawful only through the channels the Act creates, donation is confined largely to near relatives and to altruistic donors cleared by authorisation committees, brain-stem death is given a statutory definition and certification procedure, and commercial dealing is criminalised. The Mental Healthcare Act, 2017, Act 10 of 2017, works differently again, as a rights statute: it replaced the Mental Health Act, 1987, recognised a right to access mental health care, provided for advance directives and nominated representatives, created central and state mental health authorities and review boards, required insurers to treat mental illness on the same basis as physical illness, and effectively decriminalised attempted suicide by presuming severe stress and barring prosecution. The Human Immunodeficiency Virus and Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (Prevention and Control) Act, 2017, Act 16 of 2017, in force from 2018, is also a rights instrument, prohibiting discrimination against persons with HIV in employment, health care and residence, and conditioning testing and treatment on informed consent and confidentiality.

The newest members of the layer govern reproduction as a service. The Surrogacy (Regulation) Act, 2021, Act 47 of 2021, prohibits commercial surrogacy, permits only altruistic surrogacy for intending couples who meet its conditions, and creates national and state boards and a registry of clinics; the Assisted Reproductive Technology (Regulation) Act, 2021, Act 42 of 2021, regulates the clinics and banks that provide assisted reproduction, their registration, the handling of gametes and the duties owed to donors and children. Both were brought into force in January 2022. Taken together, the practice statutes reveal the legislature's preferred technique in morally charged areas: not a general code of medical law, but a bespoke statute for each practice, with its own regulator, its own register and its own offences, drafted when the practice became too visible to leave to professional ethics and the penal code. The accumulation is workable statute by statute, and incoherent as a body, because a single fertility clinic may answer to the surrogacy and assisted reproduction statutes, the diagnostic techniques statute, the clinical establishments law of its state if there is one, and the professional discipline of its doctors' councils, under four different logics at once.

Two of the practice statutes reward a closer account of their machinery, because the machinery is the statute. The diagnostic techniques Act works through appropriate authorities at state and district level and advisory committees, requires every genetic counselling centre, laboratory and ultrasound clinic to register, obliges it to maintain records of every scan of a pregnant woman, prohibits the communication of the sex of the foetus to anyone, and backs the scheme with search, seizure and suspension powers and with offences by companies and practitioners alike. Its enforcement history, run through sting operations, sealed machines and prosecutions of radiologists, shows a prohibition statute aimed at a social practice, the selection against daughters, using a medical technology as its point of application, and its critics within medicine answer that the record-keeping burden has made the statute a harassment instrument against a whole specialty while sex selection adapted around it. The transplantation Act works through authorisation committees that must approve donations outside the near-relative circle, appropriate authorities that register and inspect transplant hospitals, and, after the 2011 amendment brought tissues within the Act, a national organisation and registry architecture for organ and tissue transplantation. Its critics point to the thriving illegal market that the prohibition was meant to kill and to the long waiting lists its restrictions maintain; its defenders answer that the alternative visible in the pre-1994 market, in which the kidneys of the poor were a commodity, is precisely what a civilised statute exists to prevent. In both statutes the legislature made a values choice and built a licensing machine to hold it, and in both the machine's friction is the price of the choice, which is why these Acts generate more litigation per section than almost anything else in the field.

The termination statute has the longest shadow of the group, because it was an early Indian example of Parliament legalising by condition what the criminal law prohibited without exception. The Indian Penal Code criminalised the causing of miscarriage; the Shantilal Shah Committee, appointed in 1964 to examine the question, recommended liberalisation on health and humanitarian grounds, and the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971, brought into force in 1972, made termination lawful when a registered medical practitioner formed the opinion, in good faith and within the statutory gestational structure, that continuance would risk the woman's life or gravely injure her physical or mental health, or that the child would suffer serious abnormality, with the woman's consent as the Act's organising requirement. The 2021 amendment's extension of limits and creation of medical boards for later terminations extended that structure without changing its logic. The statute is therefore best understood as the penal code's exception organised into a system, and it belongs on the map as the clearest demonstration that Indian health law often lives inside the interstices of the general criminal law rather than in a code of its own.

The mental health and HIV statutes repay the same machinery-level attention, because they mark the field's turn from licensing to rights. The Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 builds a rights-holder's statute: a person with mental illness holds a statutory right to access mental health care and treatment from services run or funded by government, rights to community living, protection from cruel and degrading treatment, confidentiality and access to medical records; the Act provides for advance directives by which a person records treatment preferences and for nominated representatives to support decision-making; it obliges insurers to provide medical insurance for mental illness on the same basis as for physical illness; it creates central and state mental health authorities to register and oversee mental health establishments and Mental Health Review Boards to protect admissions and rights at district level; and by its provision on attempted suicide it presumes that a person who attempts suicide is under severe stress and shall not be tried or punished under the penal code, reversing a criminal-law position of more than a century. Its weakness is the weakness of the facilities layer in another form: the authorities, boards and registered establishments the Act imagines have been constituted unevenly across the states, so a rights statute of national application operates through machinery whose existence varies by state. The HIV and AIDS (Prevention and Control) Act, 2017 works in the same register for a single condition: it prohibits specified acts of discrimination in employment, education, health care, housing and insurance, requires informed consent for testing and treatment, protects confidentiality with defined exceptions, and creates ombudsmen at the state level to inquire into violations. Together with the rights chapters of the transplantation and surrogacy statutes, these Acts show the legislature learning a second technique late in the field's history: not merely to license the provider and punish the malpractice, but to endow the patient. The endowment technique is the one a framework statute would generalise, which is another way of seeing why the drafted framework bills were rights bills at their core.

## Outbreak response: the 1897 statute and the disaster framework

When disease spreads, the field reaches for its oldest tool. The Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897 is a statute of four substantive moves: when a state government is satisfied that any part of its territory is visited by or threatened with an outbreak of a dangerous epidemic disease and that the ordinary law is insufficient, it may take, or require or empower any person to take, such measures and prescribe such temporary regulations as it thinks necessary, including the inspection, detention and segregation of persons and the disinfection of places and conveyances. A parallel provision empowers the central government in relation to ships and vessels and the country as a whole, disobedience of an order under the Act is treated as an offence under the penal code's provision on disobeying a public servant's lawful order, and persons acting in good faith are protected from suit. That is nearly the whole of it. The Act defines no disease, declares no emergency, creates no authority, guarantees no treatment and compensates nobody. Its full treatment, including its amendment in 2020 to protect health care personnel and property during epidemics and to strengthen penalties, belongs to [the study of the Epidemic Diseases Act in modern use](/2015/10/15/epidemic-diseases-act-1897-india/); for the map, the point is the contrast between the size of the power it confers, which is very large, and the size of the statute, which is very small.

The modern layer over it is not a health statute at all. The Disaster Management Act, 2005, Act 53 of 2005, defines disaster widely enough to include a biological catastrophe, creates national, state and district disaster management authorities and executive committees, and empowers them to direct ministries, departments and local authorities, requisition resources and control movement in a threatened or actual disaster. When the coronavirus pandemic reached India, the two statutes were used together and in sequence: state governments invoked the epidemic statute for their regulations on closures, masking and movement, while the national lockdown orders of March 2020 and the directions that followed were issued through the disaster management machinery, with the Union Home Secretary's executive committee at the centre and the Health Ministry's powers enlarged by delegation. The arrangement worked as an improvisation and was criticised as one: scholars of public health law pointed out that a disaster statute designed for earthquakes and floods was supplying the legal basis for the largest public health intervention in the Republic's history, because the health statutes themselves contained no graded emergency framework, no statutory trigger, no defined powers matched to defined stages of an outbreak, and no rights protections drafted for the situation. The episode is the strongest modern evidence for the framework argument taken up below, and also for the federal objection to it, because the improvisation succeeded precisely by letting each state write its own epidemic regulations under a permissive central shell.

### Why do outbreak powers differ from one state to another?

Because the operative law is state law. The epidemic statute of 1897 gives its powers to state governments first, and the public health Acts organising sanitation and notification are state statutes of different vintages, some descended from colonial provincial Acts. One outbreak is thus met through different texts, officers and penalties across states, coordinated, not unified, by the Centre.

Beneath both statutes lies the older state layer already described: the public health Acts of the states, descended from provincial legislation such as the Madras Public Health Act, 1939, which organise the unglamorous permanent machinery of notification of disease, sanitation, water, burial grounds, food premises and local health officers. Epidemics are fought with the emergency statutes, but they are prevented, insofar as law prevents them, by this municipal and state layer, and its unevenness across states is a large part of why Indian outbreak outcomes vary by geography. The Registration of Births and Deaths Act, 1969, a central statute resting on the Concurrent List entry for vital statistics, completes the informational base: a state cannot manage what it does not count, and the counting of births, deaths and their causes is itself a legal system with registrars in every local area. None of these instruments was designed as part of an epidemic code. Together they are what India has instead of one.

The pandemic also exposed a second absence, the absence of any statutory law of quarantine and isolation with rights attached. The 1897 Act empowers measures; it does not say who decides that a particular person is detained, for how long, on what evidence, with what review, or at whose cost, and the disaster statute is equally general. The operational rules of the lockdown period, on home isolation, institutional quarantine, testing, travel and the sealing of premises, were written in executive orders and standard operating procedures issued and revised at speed by ministries and district administrations. Courts reviewed the margins of that edifice, on migrant workers, on testing prices, on hospital charges and on vaccination conditions, but the core powers were never located in a considered legislative text. International obligation supplied no substitute: the International Health Regulations of 2005, which India accepts and which Article 253 of the Constitution would let Parliament implement by legislation on any subject, require capacities for surveillance and response at points of entry, and India meets them administratively; no implementing public health statute translates them into domestic duties and powers. A framework statute would have been the place where emergency power and individual right were balanced in advance, by a legislature, in calm weather. Its absence meant the balance was struck by administrators in the storm, and ratified, mostly, afterward. That is the concrete sense in which the missing statute is not an aesthetic complaint about the statute book but a gap in the rule of law as it operates on citizens.

Surveillance and the border complete the outbreak layer, and both run on instruments thinner than their importance. The Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme, the national system that collects weekly reports of epidemic-prone diseases from districts and states, is an executive programme, created and sustained by government decision without a parent statute; the duty to notify a dangerous disease in the first place rests on the state public health Acts and municipal law, which is why the same fever is a notifiable event in one state and a matter of practice in another. At the border, the Union's entries on port quarantine and inter-State quarantine support the port health and airport health organisations and the health screening of international traffic, operating through rules and standing orders under the general framework of the epidemic statute and the aircraft and port health rules, and implementing administratively the International Health Regulations of 2005. Nothing in this layer is irrational, and much of it worked under extreme pressure, but its legal character should be seen clearly: the systems that watch for the next epidemic and guard the points of entry are programme and rule, not statute, and a future framework law would find in surveillance the easiest of its chapters to write, because the machinery exists and needs only the duties, data protections and inter-state obligations that executive instruments cannot impose on unwilling reporters.

One layer of the field is so familiar that readers forget it is health law at all: the penal code's chapter on offences affecting the public health, safety, convenience, decency and morals. Sections 269 to 271 of the Indian Penal Code punished the negligent and malignant acts likely to spread the infection of a disease dangerous to life, and disobedience to a quarantine rule made by government, and Sections 272 to 276 punished the adulteration of food and drink, the sale of adulterated food, and the adulteration and sale of drugs, provisions carried forward into the successor penal code of 2023. These offences are the enforcement teeth most often actually used in health emergencies, since disobedience of an epidemic order is prosecuted through the penal code's provision on disobeying a public servant rather than through the epidemic statute itself, and the food adulteration offences ran in parallel with the food safety statute for years. The penal layer also holds the offences of causing miscarriage from which the termination statute carves its exceptions, and the general provisions on negligent acts endangering life under which botched and unqualified practice is prosecuted when no specialist statute reaches it. The map is incomplete without this layer, because for the citizen the state most often appears in health matters not as a provider or a regulator but as a prosecutor, and the antiquity of the provisions, Victorian in origin like the epidemic Act, is one more proof that the criminal law was the nineteenth century's public health law and has never entirely been replaced in that office.

The states' own institutional inventions fill the remaining space in the facilities picture, and one of them became a national template without any statute changing hands. Tamil Nadu's pooled procurement of drugs for its public facilities, organised through the Tamil Nadu Medical Services Corporation created in 1994, bought quality-assured generic medicines for the public system at prices private purchasers could not approach, and the model was copied by other states and studied by the Union precisely because it solved by administration a problem, the price and quality of medicines in public hospitals, that legislation had left untouched. The example cuts both ways in the framework argument, and it should be allowed to. It shows that a state acting inside its own competence can build system-level machinery of the first importance without waiting for any national framework; and it shows that the machinery spreads by imitation and fiscal fashion rather than by legal obligation, so that whether a patient in a given state receives free quality medicines in a public hospital depends on whether her government has chosen to copy a neighbour's corporation. A framework statute is, at bottom, a device for converting the second kind of dependence into the first kind of entitlement, and the states that need the device least are the ones whose example proves it can work.

The 2020 amendment of the epidemic statute is worth its own paragraph, because it is the only amendment the Act has received in more than a century and it shows exactly what Parliament will and will not do in this field under pressure. The Epidemic Diseases (Amendment) Act, 2020 began as an ordinance promulgated in April 2020, after attacks on health workers during the pandemic's first wave, and it did three things: it defined healthcare service personnel broadly to include doctors, nurses, paramedical workers, community health workers and others deployed against the epidemic; it made acts of violence against such personnel, and damage to property including clinical establishments, cognizable and non-bailable offences punishable with imprisonment and fines, with compensation for damage recoverable from the offender; and it extended and clarified the central government's powers of inspection and detention in relation to conveyances beyond ships to buses, trains and aircraft. What the amendment did not do is as instructive as what it did. It created no framework, defined no emergency, set no trigger, conferred no entitlement to treatment and sunset none of the powers it sharpened. Confronted in a crisis with the inadequacy of its public health statute, Parliament amended the statute's teeth and left its skeleton untouched, which is the drafting history of the whole field repeated in miniature: the Indian legislature legislates at the point of pain, and the point of pain in 2020 was violence against doctors, not the absence of a public health code.

## The right to health the courts built

The Constitution contains no fundamental right to health in terms. What it contains is Article 21, the guarantee that no person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law, and a line of Supreme Court decisions that read health into life. In Parmanand Katara v. Union of India, decided in 1989, the Court held that every doctor, in government service or otherwise, has a professional obligation to extend his services with due expertise to protect life, and that no law or state action can intervene to delay the discharge of that obligation when an injured person is brought for treatment, a holding aimed at the practice of turning away emergency cases on medico-legal pretexts. In Consumer Education and Research Centre v. Union of India, decided in 1995, the Court held that the right to health and medical care is a fundamental right under Article 21 read with the Directive Principles on the health of workers, because a worker's health is integral to a meaningful life, and it issued directions on occupational health and compensation for asbestos workers. In Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of West Bengal, decided in 1996, the Court confronted a man who had fallen from a train and been turned away from a succession of government hospitals in Calcutta for want of beds and facilities, and held that the failure of a government hospital to provide timely medical treatment to a person in need of it violates Article 21, awarded compensation, and directed the state to build a proper emergency treatment system, while acknowledging that financial resources condition what can be demanded. In Vincent Panikurlangara v. Union of India, decided in 1987 in the context of drug regulation, the Court had already described the maintenance and improvement of public health as among the primary duties of the state, since a healthy body is the foundation of all human activity.

The Court has also policed the boundary of its own construction. In State of Punjab v. Ram Lubhaya Bagga, decided in 1998, it upheld a state policy that limited the rates at which government servants could claim reimbursement for treatment in private hospitals, accepting that the right to health does not oblige the state to fund every treatment at any cost and that resource constraints are a legitimate part of policy. The resulting doctrine is a floor rather than an entitlement to everything: emergency treatment cannot be refused by the state's own hospitals, the state must maintain a functioning system of care and is liable when its failure to treat causes harm, occupational health is constitutionally protected, and beyond that floor the content of the right is filled in by policy, budgets and statutes rather than by adjudication. A second judicial layer reaches the private sector indirectly. In Indian Medical Association v. V. P. Shantha, decided in 1995, the Court held that the services of medical practitioners are services within the consumer protection statute, so that a patient who is a consumer may complain of deficiency in service before the consumer commissions, a route that made medical negligence claims far more accessible than a civil suit; and in Municipal Council, Ratlam v. Vardhichan, decided in 1980, the Court had enforced a municipality's statutory duty of sanitation against a plea of financial inability, treating public health obligations of local bodies as real duties rather than aspirations.

### What does Article 47 of the Constitution say about public health?

Article 47 places the improvement of public health among the primary duties of the State, alongside the raising of nutrition and living standards, and directs the State to endeavour to prohibit intoxicating drinks and drugs except for medicinal purposes. It sits in the Directive Principles: courts use it to interpret enforceable rights, but no court can enforce it directly.

The significance of the case law for the map is the inversion it performs. The one general statement of what the Indian state owes in health that a citizen can actually invoke was written by judges out of the right to life, not by Parliament out of a health statute, and its content reflects what litigation can see: emergencies, refusals, occupational injury, negligent treatment. What litigation cannot easily build is the prospective architecture of a health system, staffing norms, facility standards, referral chains, public health cadres, which is legislative work. That asymmetry is the practical core of the argument about the missing framework statute, taken up in the next part of this guide after recording one experiment that proves the legislative route is open. In 2023 Rajasthan became the first state to enact a right to health statute, the Rajasthan Right to Health Care Act, 2022, passed by its Legislative Assembly in March 2023 and brought into force that April, which entitles residents to free outpatient and inpatient care at public facilities and at designated private facilities, guarantees emergency treatment without prepayment, and creates grievance authorities, resting expressly on Articles 21 and 47. The Act was carried over the sustained protest of private practitioners in the state, and its working will be watched by every other state government, but its existence settles one question of competence: a legislature that wants to convert the judicial right into a statutory one can do so, because the subject was always within reach of the State List.

The judicial layer has two further features that complete its portrait. The first is its supervisory mode. In fields where the statutory machinery failed visibly, the Supreme Court has not merely declared rights but managed systems for years at a time: in the proceedings brought by Swasthya Adhikar Manch on clinical trials, the Court supervised the approval and compensation framework for trial participants after deaths and injuries in trials conducted on vulnerable subjects exposed the weakness of the drug regulator's oversight; in Devika Biswas v. Union of India, decided in 2016, the Court examined sterilisation camps conducted under the family planning programme, recorded the deaths and failures that assembly-line camps produced, and issued directions on consent, quality and accountability in sterilisation services. These cases belong on the map because they show the courts doing, case by case, the standard-setting and audit work that a health statute and a health inspectorate would do continuously, and doing it in the only currency litigation has, which is the reported scandal. The second feature is the limit the courts themselves announce. Judgment after judgment in the Article 21 line repeats that the right is conditioned by the state's resources and that courts will not fix budgetary priorities, which is why the right's floor, emergency care, non-refusal, occupational protection, enforceable municipal sanitation, has remained approximately where the 1990s cases set it. A legislature could raise the floor deliberately and fund the raising. That sentence is the whole of the framework argument in judicial dress.

The criminal law of medical error completes the judicial layer, and it is deliberately narrow. In Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab, decided in 2005, the Supreme Court held that a medical practitioner may be prosecuted for criminal negligence only where the negligence is gross and the act shows a reckless disregard for the patient's life and safety, that a private complaint must be supported by the credible opinion of another competent doctor, and that the arrest of a doctor should not be made in a routine manner, guidelines designed to keep the criminal process from becoming an instrument of pressure in what are usually civil disputes about outcomes. The same anxiety about the emergency door produced a statutory development outside the health statutes altogether: after the Supreme Court approved guidelines for the protection of good samaritans in the SaveLIFE Foundation proceedings in 2016, Parliament inserted a good samaritan protection into the Motor Vehicles Act by its amendment of 2019, shielding a person who renders emergency assistance to a road accident victim from civil and criminal liability and from procedural harassment. Emergency care in India is thus governed at the point of first contact by a transport statute, at the hospital door by the case law under Article 21 and the professional obligation recognised in Katara, and inside the hospital, in the states that have it, by the stabilisation duties of the clinical establishments legislation: three instruments from three different shelves for one continuous event, which a framework statute would have written as one chapter. Maternal care shows the courts working the same seam from the scheme side: in Laxmi Mandal, the Delhi High Court in 2010 confronted the death of a woman who had been denied the benefits of maternity schemes and care across facilities, and treated the failure as a violation of enforceable rights under Articles 21 and the Directive Principles, directing accountability within the very programmes whose guidelines were supposed to deliver the care. The pattern across all of this case law is consistent enough to state as a rule of the field: where the legislature has not written the entitlement, the courts will enforce the minimum that dignity requires, and where the executive has written the entitlement into a scheme, the courts will sometimes hold the executive to its own text, but neither operation produces the systematic law that only legislation can supply.

## The framework statute that was drafted twice and enacted never

The absence at the centre of the field is not an oversight. It has been noticed, drafted around and abandoned at least three times. The Union government circulated a model public health statute for state adoption as early as 1955 and revised the model in 1987, and the states largely declined to adopt it. In January 2009 the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare released a working draft of a National Health Bill, the most ambitious of the attempts, which proposed to provide for the protection and fulfilment of rights in relation to health and well-being, health equity and justice, and the underlying determinants of health as well as health care, and to place the existing accumulation of health enactments within one rights-based framework administered cooperatively by the Union and the states. The draft was never introduced in Parliament. In 2013 the government informed the Lok Sabha that it had no plans to introduce the National Health Bill, and ministers have repeated since, in answer to members asking for right to health legislation, that there is no proposal under consideration to bring such a law. In 2017 the ministry drafted a second, narrower instrument, the Public Health (Prevention, Control and Management of Epidemics, Bio-terrorism and Disasters) Bill, aimed at the outbreak end of the field: it defined public health emergencies to include epidemics, bioterrorism, natural disasters and chemical and nuclear incidents, proposed public health authorities at the national, state, district and block levels with defined powers, contemplated a public health cadre, and would have repealed and replaced the Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897. It too was never introduced. The framework statute has thus been written in a comprehensive version and in an emergency version, and the legislative record of both is identical: drafting, consultation, silence.

The case for enacting a framework, made by public health scholars and by the health movement for decades, is that the gaps documented in this guide are not accidents but consequences. The Jan Swasthya Abhiyan, the Indian network of the People's Health Movement, has campaigned for a statutory right to health on the ground that entitlements which rest on case law and scheme guidelines can be narrowed without any legislature voting on the change. The Public Health Foundation of India and the public health scholars associated with the movement for a dedicated public health cadre, among them K. Srinath Reddy, have argued that India administers health through generalist services because no statute creates the specialist public health workforce, surveillance duties and emergency gradations that a framework law would carry. On this view, a national statute would do four things the patchwork cannot: state the entitlement, organise the emergency powers with triggers and safeguards instead of the blank permission of 1897, standardise the informational duties of surveillance and notification, and discipline the states' facility standards through a floor below which care may not fall.

The federal objection, made by state governments in the consultations on the 2009 draft and repeated whenever the proposal resurfaces, deserves its strongest form, because it is the actual reason neither bill moved. Public health and hospitals are the states' constitutional province, and a Union framework statute faces a dilemma from which drafting cannot rescue it. If it respects the State List, its standards for state systems are advisory, and an advisory framework adds paper to a field that already has policies in abundance. If it binds the states, it legislates on Entry 6 in everything but name, and states with stronger systems, which have built them under different models and at their own fiscal cost, will resist a national standard written elsewhere as an encroachment and, in the harder version of the argument, as a device for shifting the Centre's preferences onto state budgets. The provider objection runs alongside: when Rajasthan legislated a right to health at the state level, where competence is not in doubt, private practitioners struck and protested against the emergency-treatment and reimbursement obligations the Act placed on them, which suggests that even a constitutionally clean framework will be fought on its substance by the interests it regulates.

The honest resolution of the argument begins by noticing that the Constitution has already answered the impossibility claim. The Article 252 route exists for exactly this configuration, a subject in the State List on which a group of states wants Parliament to legislate, and the field itself has used it twice, for transplantation in 1994 and for clinical establishments in 2010. A framework assembled by consent, applying where states invite it and open to adoption by others, would be constitutionally unimpeachable, and its partial map would be no worse than the partial maps the field already lives with. The objection that remains is therefore not constitutional but political: no group of states has yet wanted a binding statement of health entitlements and emergency powers enough to request one, and no Union government has wanted to spend its authority persuading them. The series' thesis thread runs through this series in several forms, statutes enacted and never notified, rules framed and never made. This is the further category the health field adds: the statute drafted and never introduced, where the failure occurs before Parliament is even asked, and where the responsibility belongs not to drafting or to administration but to the absence of a coalition that wants the law to exist.

The complication facing the framework case must be stated fairly, because it is the strongest version of the other side and it is empirical. The best-performing state health systems in India were not built by framework statutes. Tamil Nadu's public system, with its drug procurement corporation, its staffed primary health centres and its public health cadre descending from the Madras statute's administrative tradition, was built by executive organisation inside the State List, and Kerala's outcomes rest on a century of public provision and social development rather than on any rights Act. If framework legislation were the binding constraint on health performance, those systems could not exist. The federal objector can therefore say, with evidence, that the variable that matters is state administrative will and financing, and that a national framework Act would change the letterheads faster than the outcomes. The framework advocate's answer is narrower than the slogan and survives the point: a framework statute is not proposed as a substitute for state capacity but as the missing law for the subjects that are national by nature, cross-border disease, surveillance data, emergency gradation and the portability of entitlements, and as a floor for the states whose systems are failing their residents, for whom the absence of any statutory standard is not federalism working but federalism abandoned. Read with that narrowing, the two cases divide less than they appear to: both agree that services are built by states; they disagree about whether the citizen of a negligent state should hold a right against somebody. The drafted bills answered that the citizen should. The legislatures, by declining to introduce them, have so far answered that she should not, and the courts, with Article 21, have given her the smaller half of the claim in the meantime.

## Entitlements without statutes: missions, insurance and the scheme layer

A map of health law that stopped at statutes would misdescribe how much of Indian health care is actually governed, because a large share of it is governed by instruments that are not law at all. The National Health Mission, launched in 2005 as the National Rural Health Mission and later widened, is an executive programme: it moves central money into state health systems against approved plans, funds contractual staff, ambulances, and facility upgrades, and sets the operational norms of primary care for most of the country, all through guidelines, memoranda and funding conditions rather than through any Act. Ayushman Bharat, announced in 2018, added two further executive instruments: a network of health and wellness centres upgrading primary care, and the Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana, an assurance scheme that provides a cover of five lakh rupees per family per year for hospitalisation of entitled families, administered by the National Health Authority. Neither instrument creates a statutory entitlement. A family's eligibility, a hospital's empanelment and a package's rate are matters of scheme design that the executive can revise, and the beneficiary's remedies run through scheme grievance channels rather than through courts enforcing a right.

The distinction is not pedantry, and the data show why governments prefer the scheme form. The National Family Health Survey's fifth round, conducted from 2019 to 2021, found that households with any member covered by a health scheme or health insurance rose from 28.7 per cent in the fourth round to 41 per cent, and that institutional births rose from about 79 per cent to 88.6 per cent over the same interval, movements achieved almost entirely through programmes and incentives rather than through new legal rights. A scheme can be launched in a budget speech, targeted by administrative data, expanded or contracted with the fiscal year, and branded to its authors. A statute must survive Parliament, binds successors, creates enforceable duties and exposes the state to litigation when it fails. The preference for schemes is therefore rational for governments and consequential for citizens: health coverage in India has grown as a matter of policy, revocable and uneven, rather than as a matter of law, and the right to health recognised by the courts coexists with a delivery system whose operative rules sit in guidelines that no court will enforce line by line.

Insurance illustrates the same architecture from the private side. Health insurance as a business is regulated under the Insurance Act, 1938 and by the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India under its Act of 1999, as a branch of the insurance industry rather than of the health system, and the policyholder's protections, on cashless authorisation, claim repudiation and policy wording, come from insurance law and the Authority's regulations for health insurance and policyholder protection. The result is a citizen who meets three different legal orders in a single illness: the hospital answers to state facility law or to none, the insurer answers to insurance regulation, and the bill is contested, if at all, before insurance ombudsmen and consumer commissions rather than before any health authority. The fragmentation is not a conspiracy or an accident. It is the predictable output of a field that grew subject by subject, and it is the practical reason the map matters: a person who cannot identify which legal order governs his problem does not know where his remedy lives.

The scheme layer has a history that shows the preference hardening into a system. The Central Government Health Scheme has served central employees and pensioners since 1954 as an executive arrangement. The Employees' State Insurance Act, 1948 is the great exception, a genuine statute creating a corporation, a fund and enforceable entitlements for insured workers in covered employment, and it demonstrates that Parliament can legislate health entitlements when the subject reaches it through the labour and social insurance entries rather than through the health entry; the exception is old, large and instructive. The Rashtriya Swasthya Bima Yojana of 2008 carried publicly purchased hospitalisation insurance to families below the poverty line as a scheme, and the state schemes of the same period, of which Andhra Pradesh's Aarogyasri was the most influential, proved the electoral power of the assurance model that the Jan Arogya Yojana then nationalised in 2018. Policy documents crown the structure: national health policies were adopted in 1983, 2002 and 2017, each setting goals for expenditure, infrastructure and outcomes, and none of them is law; the 2017 policy's targets bind no one and are cited in argument exactly as aspirations are. The map should therefore mark a whole continent of Indian health governance as executive territory, in which the citizen is a beneficiary rather than a rights-holder, and remember that this is a chosen constitutional posture and not a drafting oversight: the entries would support statutes for much of this ground, as the employees' insurance precedent proves, and successive governments have preferred the flexibility of the scheme.

## Reading the field: how to find the statute and the regulator

The working rule of the field is that every health question in India must be asked twice, once for the subject and once for the state. A professional or administrator should begin from the activity and not from the institution: manufacturing or selling a medicine leads to the drugs statute and its central and state licensing authorities; opening a clinic leads first to the question whether the state has the central clinical establishments Act, its own Act, or neither; practising a system of medicine leads to the commission for that system and to the state council that keeps the register; running a programme leads to the scheme guidelines of the mission or the assurance scheme, which function as the operative law of the programme whatever their formal status. A citizen with a grievance faces the same map in reverse: professional misconduct goes to the state medical council and, on the ethics side, to the commission's registration board; a billing or service deficiency goes to the consumer commissions on the authority of the Shantha line of cases; a facility that should not be operating at all goes to the registering authority where a clinical establishments statute applies; and a refusal of emergency treatment by a government hospital states the classic Article 21 claim. Readers pursuing a specific grievance should treat this guide as legal information and a map of forums, not as advice on an individual case, because limitation periods and forum rules in each of these routes are unforgiving and turn on facts no general guide can see.

### Which Union ministry and regulators administer health law at the Centre?

The Ministry of Health and Family Welfare administers most central health statutes, through its health and health research departments and the offices housing the drug controller and the national programmes. The Ministry of AYUSH administers the commissions for the Indian systems and homoeopathy. The regulators are statutory bodies: the professional commissions, the food safety authority and the insurance regulator.

A note on sources belongs in any honest map, because health law is a field in which the secondary literature misleads with unusual confidence. The texts that govern are the Acts as published in the Gazette and consolidated on the India Code, the rules and notifications under them, and the Constitution itself; the errors that circulate in summaries and examination guides are systematic rather than random, and three of them have been corrected at the points where they arise in this guide: health is not a Concurrent List subject in general, the clinical establishments statute does not apply across India, and neither the National Health Bill of 2009 nor the public health emergencies Bill of 2017 was ever introduced, let alone enacted. A reader verifying any proposition here should begin from the bare text of the statute named, and should treat any source that gets one of those three points wrong as unreliable on the rest until it proves otherwise, because each of them is a load-bearing fact about the field's architecture.

For the researcher, the field's productive questions are the architectural ones this guide has pressed: which instruments rest on which constitutional entries, which statutes apply in which states, which entitlements are statutory and which are executive, and where the framework is missing altogether. For the aspirant, the examinable core is equally architectural, the Seventh Schedule entries with their numbers, the Article 252 device and its two health statutes, the Article 21 case line from Katara through Paschim Banga to Bagga, Article 47, and the sequence by which the council model of professional regulation gave way to the commission model between 2019 and 2023. Readers preparing teaching or examination material from this map will find it transfers well into slides and structured notes, and working through [the statute maps as a presentation deck in the ReportMedic PPT viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) is a practical way to revise the field's architecture entry by entry before testing it against the specialist articles.

Two modern developments will test any reader's command of the map, and both are best understood through it. The first is the common admission system in medical education. The single entrance examination for undergraduate and postgraduate medicine, administered nationally, was written into the medical council framework by amendment and carried into the National Medical Commission Act, 2019, which also contemplates a common final-year exit examination to serve both as a licentiate and as the gateway to postgraduate admission; the constitutional challenges to a uniform national test, brought on federalism and institutional-autonomy grounds, failed, the courts treating admission standards as part of the standards power rather than of state administration. The result is a field in which a student's entry into medicine is more thoroughly nationalised than a patient's entry into a hospital, an outcome no one designed and the constitutional entries fully explain. The second development is digital. Telemedicine was placed on a formal footing in 2020, when practice guidelines for registered practitioners were issued under the professional conduct framework of the medical council legislation, converting an ethically grey practice into a regulated one by subordinate instrument in a matter of weeks, a demonstration of how fast the field can move in its lower layers when it must. The Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission has since built health identifiers and registries for facilities and professionals as executive infrastructure. Neither development required a new Act, and both therefore confirm the field's deepest habit: where a statute is unavailable or inconvenient, Indian health governance legislates by guideline, rule and mission document, and the citizen's rights in the new territory are whatever those instruments say they are, enforceable mostly through the same councils, commissions and courts this guide has already mapped.

The plural character of Indian health care deserves a final statement, because the map's layers are not abstractions to the population that uses them. Survey evidence across rounds of the national surveys consistently shows that the larger part of outpatient care, and in most states a majority of hospital episodes by number, is obtained from private providers, from the single-doctor clinic and the small nursing home up to the corporate hospital chain, while the public system carries the preventive programmes, the medico-legal load, the teaching institutions and a disproportionate share of the poorest patients and the most serious emergencies in the districts where private capacity is thin. The legal significance of the pattern is that the statutes governing the private majority are precisely the field's thinnest layer: facility registration that depends on the state, professional discipline that reaches the doctor but not the enterprise, consumer liability that compensates after the event, and insurance regulation that governs the payer rather than the care. Parallel public systems complicate the picture further. The armed forces run their own medical services under military law; the railways maintain their own hospitals for their workforce; the employees' insurance system runs its own hospitals and dispensaries for insured workers; the Central Government Health Scheme purchases and provides care for central employees; and the Union territories without legislatures receive their services directly from the Centre. An Indian citizen's health law therefore depends not only on his state but on his employer and his uniform, and the universality that a framework statute would have supplied is replaced by a mosaic of memberships. None of this is irrational in origin: each parallel system was built for a defined population by the authority responsible for it. But it means that the question with which this guide opened, what does the Indian state owe a person who falls ill, has a different legal answer for the soldier, the railwayman, the insured factory worker, the central pensioner and the unorganised worker in the same town, and that difference is the field's most consequential feature in lived experience.

A single worked example will fix the whole map in memory. Consider the owner of a forty-bed nursing home in a mid-sized Indian town. To open lawfully, the enterprise needs the state's facility regime first: registration under the central clinical establishments Act if the state has adopted it, under the state's own Act if it has legislated, or under little more than local licensing if neither holds; building, fire and municipal permissions from the local tier; a licence for its pharmacy and for the storage of narcotic drugs under the drugs framework; authorisation for its biomedical waste under the environmental rules; registration of its ultrasound machine and compliance with the diagnostic techniques statute if it offers obstetric scanning; empanelment conditions and package rates if it seeks patients under the assurance scheme; and doctors and nurses whose registrations sit in the state councils under the national commissions' standards. If something goes wrong, the slices separate exactly as the professions section described: the council for the doctor, the consumer commission for the bill and the deficiency, the registering authority for the licence, the criminal court only for the grossest negligence. The owner who complains that health care is over-regulated and the patient who complains that it is unregulated are describing the same map from different ends: dense at the level of the product and the practitioner, thin at the level of the institution and the system, and different in every state.

A comparison with the systems India is usually measured against clarifies what is distinctive here, because the absence of a framework is not an Indian eccentricity but a choice with known alternatives. The United Kingdom built its post-war settlement on a single statute, the National Health Service Act, 1946, which imposed on the minister a duty to promote a comprehensive health service free of charge, and the whole administrative law of British health care hangs from that duty. Canada took the federal route India declined: health is provincial there too, and the national system rests on a federal statute, the Canada Health Act, 1984, which sets the conditions on which federal money flows to provincial insurance plans, achieving national principles through the spending power rather than through direct legislation. India has both instruments available in its own Constitution, the spending power under Article 282 and the consent device under Article 252, and it has used the first for missions and schemes while declining to attach statutory conditions of the Canadian kind, and the second for two narrow statutes while declining the framework. The comparison also disciplines the rhetoric of the debate: no serious system finances health without conditions, and no federal system imposes them without negotiation, so the Indian question is not whether the Centre may participate, which it plainly does, but whether its participation should crystallise into stated, justiciable standards of the kind the drafted bills proposed. That crystallisation is the step that remains untaken, and it is a political step, not a legal impossibility.

The workforce question, which every section of this guide has touched, is where the untaken step is felt most sharply at the ground level. Large parts of rural India receive their first and often only medical attention from practitioners without a recognised qualification in the system they practise, a reality the statutory registers do not describe and cannot wish away. The legislative attempts to meet it have been characteristically partial: the rural medical practitioner schemes some states created by statute or executive order, the contested proposal in the 2019 medical commission legislation for a limited licence for community health providers connected with modern medicine, resisted by the organised profession as a dilution of the standard and defended by its authors as an honest recognition of who actually treats rural India. The episode belongs on the map because it concentrates the field's tensions in one provision: the standards power says that only the fully qualified may practise; the state subject says the services must somehow be delivered; the courts say the unqualified practitioner who crosses systems is negligent by definition; and the population continues to consult whoever is there. A framework statute would have to answer this question in terms, as the drafted bills, tellingly, did not. Its absence from their texts is a reminder that even India's framework moments have preferred to legislate for the system that exists on paper over the one that exists in the district.

## The verdict on a field without a framework

The namable claim of this guide can be stated in its full form. India regulates drugs, doctors, colleges, clinics, transplants, terminations, mental health, surrogacy and epidemics through separate Acts written decades apart for separate problems; it has no general public health statute at the Union level; it has drafted that statute twice in this century without introducing it; two of its principal health statutes apply only in the states that consented to them; its largest entitlements are schemes that create no legal rights; and its one general statement of the right to health is a judicial construction resting on the right to life, bounded by the courts' own recognition of financial limits. None of this is the product of a design, and none of it is inexplicable: each feature follows from the constitutional decision to place health services with the states while letting the Union regulate the professions, the products and the borders between states, and from the political preference of every government for instruments it controls completely over statutes that would bind it.

The verdict this guide defends is that the field is more coherent than it looks and less defensible than it should be. It is coherent because the constitutional logic is consistent and the statutes mostly do the jobs their entries allow. It is less defensible than it should be because the two hardest tests of a health legal system, a fast-moving epidemic and a patient turned away, are met by the field's weakest instruments, a four-section statute from 1897 improvised upon by a disaster law, and a right that must be litigated after the refusal rather than claimed before it. The remedy most often proposed, moving health to the Concurrent List, is also the least available, since it requires a constitutional amendment that states have no incentive to gift to the Centre. The remedy the Constitution already supplies, consent legislation under Article 252 building a framework state by state, sits unused for the framework purpose while working quietly for narrower ones. Until a coalition of states and a Union government decide that a stated entitlement is worth its costs, Indian health law will remain what this guide has mapped: a strong law of products and professions wrapped around an absent law of public health, and a right to health that lives in the reports of the Supreme Court rather than in the statute book. The statutes that follow in this cluster take up the map's largest territories one at a time, beginning with the drugs statute, and each should be read against the constitutional ground plan set out here, because none of them is intelligible without it.

One further navigation skill matters in this field more than in any other in the series, and that is recognising when the operative text is not the Act at all. A great deal of Indian health law is made in the subordinate layer: the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules carry the schedules, licensing forms and conditions that practitioners actually obey; the clinical establishments standards live in notifications; the conditions of the assurance scheme live in guidelines and tender documents; the conduct of doctors is governed in detail by professional conduct regulations framed under the commission statutes. The reader who stops at the parent Act will know the architecture and miss the law in action, while the reader who reads only the rules will miss the constitutional questions that decide whether the rules may exist. The two must be read together, and changes in the subordinate layer, a rescheduled drug, a revised package rate, an amended conduct regulation, are frequently the events through which the field actually moves between one amendment and the next.

For the aspirant, one study discipline follows from everything above, and it is worth stating before the questions that close this guide. Health law in the examinations is not tested as a list of Acts but as a set of locations: the candidate who can place a subject in its list and entry, name the statute that occupies it, state whether that statute applies nationally or by consent, and identify the regulator it created, can answer the factual and the analytical questions alike, because the examiners' favourite traps in this field are precisely the map's fault lines, the clinical establishments Act described as national, health described as a concurrent subject without qualification, the National Health Bill described as enacted, the right to health described as a fundamental right in terms. Each of those errors is common in print, and each is an error about architecture rather than about detail, which is why this guide has spent its length on architecture and left the detail to the statute articles that own it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which laws govern health care in India?

Health care in India is governed by a group of subject-specific statutes rather than by one code. The Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 governs medicines and devices; the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 governs food; the National Medical Commission Act, 2019 and its sister commission statutes govern medical education and practice; the Clinical Establishments Act, 2010 and state facility Acts govern hospitals where they apply; the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971, the Transplantation of Human Organs and Tissues Act, 1994, the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 and the surrogacy and assisted reproduction Acts of 2021 govern particular practices; and the Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897 with the Disaster Management Act, 2005 governs outbreak response, all of it resting on the Seventh Schedule division between the states and the Union and on state public health Acts beneath.

### Q: Is health a state subject or a central subject?

Primarily a state subject, with large central enclaves. Entry 6 of the State List gives the states public health and sanitation, hospitals and dispensaries, so services and hospitals belong to the states. The Concurrent List gives both levels the medical and other professions, medical education, drugs and poisons, adulteration of foodstuffs, population control and family planning, and the prevention of infectious disease spreading between states, and the Union List adds port quarantine, inter-State quarantine and the determination of standards in higher education. Parliament has therefore legislated extensively on doctors, colleges, drugs and food, while the running of the hospital system remains state business. Any one-word answer, state or central, misdescribes the field, which is why questions in examinations and in practice are framed entry by entry.

### Q: Does India recognise a right to health?

Yes, but as a judicial construction rather than a statutory entitlement. The Constitution does not list health among the fundamental rights, and Article 47, which makes the improvement of public health a primary duty of the State, is a Directive Principle that no court can directly enforce. The Supreme Court has nevertheless read a right to health into the right to life under Article 21: in Parmanand Katara in 1989 for emergency treatment, in Consumer Education and Research Centre in 1995 for the health of workers, and in Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity in 1996, where the failure of government hospitals to treat a patient in time was held to violate Article 21. The right so recognised is a floor centred on emergency care and a functioning public system, bounded by the Court's acceptance in Ram Lubhaya Bagga that resources limit what the state must fund.

### Q: Why does India have no single public health statute?

Because the competence to enact one is divided and the politics have never aligned. Public health and hospitals sit in the State List, so a Union framework statute would have to rest on consent under Article 252, on the Concurrent List entries, or on a constitutional amendment, and each route needs state cooperation that has not been forthcoming. A model public health statute circulated to states in 1955 and again in revised form in 1987 was largely not adopted; the National Health Bill drafted in 2009 and the public health emergencies Bill drafted in 2017 were both shelved without introduction, with state governments objecting to central encroachment and governments at the Centre unwilling to press the point. The field also grew historically by accretion, one statute per problem, so no drafting tradition of a framework existed when the question arose.

### Q: Who regulates hospitals and clinics under health law?

It depends on the state, which is the honest answer the question usually does not expect. Where the Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act, 2010 applies, in the states that requested or adopted it and in the Union territories, hospitals and clinics register with the district registering authority and answer to the state council and the national council structure under that Act, and must meet prescribed minimum standards and display their rates. States that did not adopt it regulate through their own statutes, Maharashtra under its nursing homes registration Act of 1949, Tamil Nadu under its private clinical establishments Act of 1997, Karnataka under its Act of 2007, West Bengal under its Act of 2017 and Kerala under its Act of 2018, with different standards and machinery in each. Government hospitals are run by their owning governments and are largely outside the registration regimes aimed at private establishments.

### Q: Which health laws apply only in some states?

The statutes enacted under Article 252 of the Constitution, because consent legislation applies only where states have asked for it or adopted it. The Transplantation of Human Organs and Tissues Act, 1994 began in Goa, Himachal Pradesh and Maharashtra and the Union territories, on resolutions of those three legislatures, and spread as other states adopted it. The Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act, 2010 began in Arunachal Pradesh, Himachal Pradesh, Mizoram and Sikkim and the Union territories, and applies elsewhere only on adoption, which a substantial group of states has made and several large states have not. The rest of the field's principal statutes, on drugs, food, professions, termination of pregnancy, mental health and surrogacy, are ordinary central legislation and apply across India, though the state public health Acts that organise sanitation and disease notification also differ from state to state by their own terms.

### Q: What did the draft National Health Bill propose?

The working draft released by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare in January 2009 proposed a rights-based framework for the whole field. It would have provided for the protection and fulfilment of rights in relation to health and well-being, health equity and justice, extending to the underlying determinants of health, sanitation, nutrition, safe water and a healthy environment, as well as to health care itself, and for achieving the goal of health for all. It contemplated a collaborative federal structure of health authorities, obligations on governments to provide essential public health services, mechanisms for surveillance and emergency response, and a schedule reconciling the many existing health enactments with the new framework. It was a draft for consultation only. It was never introduced in Parliament, and in 2013 the government told the Lok Sabha that it had no plans to introduce it.

### Q: Who regulates medical practitioners?

Registration and discipline are shared between the national commission and the state councils, and education is national. Under the National Medical Commission Act, 2019, the Commission and its Ethics and Medical Registration Board maintain the national register and set the standards of professional conduct, while the state medical councils, constituted under state statutes, keep the state registers in which practitioners are actually enrolled and hear complaints of professional misconduct in the first instance. Practitioners of the Indian systems of medicine answer to the National Commission for Indian System of Medicine and its state boards and councils, homoeopaths to the National Commission for Homoeopathy, and nurses, pharmacists, dentists and the allied professions to their own statutory councils and commissions. A practitioner's licence to practise therefore comes from a state register, the standards behind it are set nationally, and negligent treatment can additionally be pursued before the consumer commissions as a deficiency in service.

### Q: What is the difference between a health statute and a health scheme like Ayushman Bharat?

A statute is enacted by a legislature, creates rights and duties that courts will enforce, binds successor governments, and can be changed only by amendment. A scheme is an executive programme created by government decision and budget allocation, which can be launched, redesigned, retargeted or wound up administratively. Ayushman Bharat and its insurance component, the Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana, which provides a cover of five lakh rupees per family per year for hospitalisation of entitled families, are schemes: eligibility lists, hospital empanelment, package rates and grievance handling are all set by scheme guidelines and the National Health Authority, not by an Act, and no beneficiary holds a statutory right that a court will enforce against a change in the guidelines. The National Health Mission is a scheme in the same sense. Much of India's health coverage has grown through schemes, which is why it can expand quickly and also why it remains policy rather than entitlement.

### Q: Can Parliament make a public health law for the whole of India?

Not directly on the core subject, because public health and hospitals are in the State List, but it has several partial routes, and together they reach most of the territory. Parliament may legislate on the Concurrent List entries, professions, drugs, medical education and the prevention of disease spreading between states, and on the Union List entries for port quarantine and inter-State quarantine. It may legislate on a state subject for consenting states under Article 252, which produced the transplantation and clinical establishments statutes; on the strength of a Rajya Sabha resolution in the national interest under Article 249; during an emergency under Article 250; and to implement international obligations under Article 253, the route used for treaty-based subjects. A single Act binding every state's hospital system, without consent or amendment of the lists, lies beyond these routes, which is why every framework proposal has been designed as cooperative, advisory or consent-based.

### Q: How does repugnancy work between a state public health Act and a central health statute?

On a Concurrent List subject, Article 254 of the Constitution gives the central law primacy: to the extent of the repugnancy, the state law is void, unless the state law was reserved for the President's consideration and received assent, in which case it prevails within that state, though Parliament may later legislate to override it. That is why state variations survive best where they occupy ground the central law leaves open, or carry presidential assent. Two special features matter in this field. A statute enacted under Article 252 cannot be amended or repealed in its application to a consenting state by that state's legislature at all; only Parliament may amend it, which is a deliberate surrender of state power built into the consent device. And on State List subjects such as hospitals, a central law has no business being there in the first place unless a consent or emergency route supports it, so most apparent conflicts are really questions about which entry authorised the central text.

### Q: Which states have their own public health Acts?

Several states work under public health Acts of their own, many of them descended from provincial legislation of the colonial period. Tamil Nadu's public health statute descends from the Madras Public Health Act, 1939, which organised sanitation, notification of infectious disease, food premises and local health officers for the old Madras Presidency and still anchors the state's public health administration. The territory of Kerala inherited the Travancore-Cochin Public Health Act, 1955 on the same pattern. Other states rely on the public health chapters of their municipal corporation and panchayat Acts together with the Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897, rather than on a single public health code, and some states, Karnataka among them, have prepared comprehensive public health Bills that have not been enacted. The pattern explains why the permanent machinery against disease, as distinct from the emergency powers, looks different in every state.

### Q: What do the Directive Principles of State Policy say about health?

They make health a stated duty of governance without making it a court-enforceable right. Article 47 obliges the State to regard the raising of the level of nutrition and of the standard of living and the improvement of public health as among its primary duties, and to endeavour to prohibit intoxicating drinks and drugs except for medicinal purposes. Article 39 directs policy toward securing the health and strength of workers and against the abuse of the health of children; Article 41 speaks of public assistance in sickness and disablement; Article 42 requires provision for just and humane conditions of work and for maternity relief; and Article 45 concerns early childhood care. The courts use these provisions interpretively: they supplied the bridge by which the Supreme Court read a right to health into Article 21, and Article 47 is quoted in the preamble of the clinical establishments statute as its constitutional mandate.

### Q: How is the cost of health care divided between the Centre and the states?

States carry the larger share, because they own and staff the hospital and primary care system, and the pattern reported in the National Health Accounts is that state governments account for roughly two-thirds of government health expenditure. The Centre's contribution is deliberately leveraged rather than comprehensive: it funds the national programmes and the National Health Mission through cost-sharing transfers, conventionally shared sixty to forty between Centre and state for most states and ninety to ten for the north-eastern and Himalayan states, it finances its own institutions such as the central institutes, and it funds the assurance scheme's premiums jointly with the states. The Fifteenth Finance Commission added earmarked grants for health routed substantially through local bodies. There is no constitutional formula for health spending; the division is the product of ownership of the system plus the Centre's use of its fiscal advantage to buy priorities inside a state subject.

### Q: What part do municipalities play in public health law?

A foundational one, because the everyday subjects of public health, sanitation, water, waste, burial grounds and the inspection of food premises, are municipal functions. Article 243W read with the Twelfth Schedule lists public health, sanitation conservancy and solid waste management among the functions that may be entrusted to municipalities, and state municipal Acts convert that into duties enforceable against the local body. In Municipal Council, Ratlam v. Vardhichan in 1980 the Supreme Court enforced a municipality's duty to abate a sanitation nuisance against its plea of financial inability, holding that a local body cannot plead empty coffers against a statutory public health duty. Municipal corporations in the largest cities also run major hospitals directly. The registration of births and deaths, resting on the central Act of 1969, is executed through local registrars. Epidemics are declared by states, but the conditions that breed them are governed, or not, at this level.

### Q: Which laws govern Ayurveda, homoeopathy and other AYUSH systems?

Each recognised system has its own statutory commission and register, parallel to the framework for modern medicine. The National Commission for Indian System of Medicine Act, 2020, Act 14 of 2020, replaced the Indian Medicine Central Council Act, 1970 and governs education and practice in Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha and Sowa-Rigpa. The National Commission for Homoeopathy Act, 2020, Act 15 of 2020, replaced the Homoeopathy Central Council Act, 1973. State councils and boards under these frameworks keep the registers of practitioners, the Ministry of AYUSH administers the sector at the Centre, and the commissions set educational standards through autonomous boards on the same template as the National Medical Commission. The borders between systems are policed by law: in Poonam Verma v. Ashwin Patel in 1996 the Supreme Court treated a homoeopath's administration of allopathic medicine as negligence in itself, and cross-system practice remains one of the field's most litigated boundaries.

### Q: Which law protects the health data and medical records of a patient?

No dedicated health data statute exists, so protection is assembled from general law. Health information in digital form is personal data under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, which regulates its processing by consent and purpose limitation but does not create a special sensitive category for health records of the kind the earlier regime under the Information Technology Act's rules had recognised. Professional confidentiality is separately imposed by the medical profession's conduct regulations, which oblige a practitioner not to disclose a patient's information except in defined circumstances, and the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 and the HIV and AIDS Act, 2017 add confidentiality protections of their own for the conditions they govern. The Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission works through a health data management policy, which is executive policy rather than law. The result is protection that is real but scattered, and it is one of the field's recognised unfinished areas.

### Q: Are medical services covered by consumer protection law in India?

Medical services were brought within consumer protection law by the Supreme Court in Indian Medical Association v. V. P. Shantha, decided in 1995, which held that the service of a medical practitioner is a service under the Consumer Protection Act, 1986, so that a patient may complain of deficiency in service before the consumer commissions, except where the service is rendered free of charge to every patient. That ruling made the consumer commissions a principal forum for medical negligence claims. It was delivered under the 1986 Act. The Consumer Protection Act, 2019, which replaced it, does not name health care in its definition of service, and the place of professional services under consumer law has since been debated, with the Supreme Court indicating in the context of advocates that the treatment of professionals under the statute merits reconsideration by a larger bench, so the question should be treated as live rather than closed.

### Q: Why is health insurance regulated separately from health care in India?

Because the Constitution assigns the two activities to different lists and different regulators. Insurance is Entry 47 of the Union List, a Union subject, and the business of health insurance is regulated under the Insurance Act, 1938 by the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India, created by its Act of 1999, which licenses insurers, approves products and polices claims conduct as financial regulation. Hospitals and medical services, by contrast, are state territory, regulated, where they are regulated at all, under state facility law and professional law. The separation follows the design of the lists, not the experience of the patient, who meets the split as a practical problem: the hospital answers for the treatment, the insurer answers for the bill, the ombudsman and consumer commissions hear the payment disputes, and no single authority supervises the whole encounter. Publicly financed insurance schemes such as the Jan Arogya Yojana sit outside the commercial framework altogether, operating as government assurance rather than regulated insurance.

### Q: Is vaccination compulsory under Indian law?

No central statute makes vaccination generally compulsory, and the large national programmes, beginning with the Universal Immunisation Programme, operate as public health programmes offering vaccination rather than as legal mandates. Coercive powers exist at the margins: the Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897 and state public health Acts allow governments to prescribe measures and regulations in an outbreak, and particular settings, schools, employments or travel, have at times attracted vaccine conditions by executive order. When such conditions were imposed during the coronavirus pandemic, the constitutional question reached the Supreme Court, which held in Jacob Puliyel v. Union of India in 2022 that bodily integrity protected by Article 21 means no individual can be forced to be vaccinated, and that restrictions imposed on unvaccinated persons must satisfy the test of proportionality. Compulsion in Indian vaccination law is therefore exceptional, local and reviewable, not a standing statutory duty.
