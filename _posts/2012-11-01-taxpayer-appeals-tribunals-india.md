A tax dispute in India begins in a deceptively quiet way. No summons arrives, no courtroom fills. A notice of demand is served, an intimation appears in an online account, or an order is uploaded to a portal, and from the date of that service a clock starts running. Miss the period the clock measures and the order hardens into a liability that recovery officers may enforce against bank accounts and refunds, whatever the merits of the underlying assessment. This is the moment for which taxpayer appeals and tribunals exist. The taxpayer holding that order usually has a good legal remedy. What they frequently lack is a map: which forum hears the first challenge, how many days remain, what must be paid before the door opens, and what the forum at each stage is actually empowered to decide. This article maps that machinery, across both the direct tax system governed by the Income-tax Act 1961 and the indirect tax system governed by the goods and services tax statutes, because the two ladders differ at almost every rung and advice that confuses them is worse than no advice at all.

The promise of the statutory scheme is generous on paper. An assessment can be appealed to an appellate authority inside the tax administration, then to an independent tribunal that is the final judge of fact, then to a High Court on questions of law, and finally to the Supreme Court. Alongside that ladder stand parallel routes that can shorten or bypass it: a dispute resolution panel for transfer pricing and foreign company cases, rectification for mistakes apparent from the record, revision by a Commissioner, advance rulings for transactions not yet taxed, a dispute resolution committee for small taxpayers, and periodic settlement schemes under which the state offers to close a dispute for a defined payment. The difficulty is not the absence of remedies. The difficulty is that each remedy has its own trigger, its own period, its own price of entry, and its own limits, and the forums themselves have repeatedly been delayed, redesigned, or struck down and rebuilt. The right of appeal exists in the statute. Whether a forum exists to hear it, staffed and constituted in time, is a separate question that the record answers less reassuringly.

## Taxpayer appeals and tribunals: two systems, two ladders

India taxes income and consumption through separate legal codes administered by separate boards, and the appellate architecture of each grew independently. Direct tax appeals run under Chapter XX of the Income-tax Act 1961 from the Assessing Officer to the Commissioner (Appeals) or the Joint Commissioner (Appeals), then to the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, then to the High Court and the Supreme Court. Goods and services tax appeals run under the Central Goods and Services Tax Act 2017 from the adjudicating officer to an Appellate Authority, then to the Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal, then to the High Court and the Supreme Court. The shapes rhyme. The periods, the fees, the deposits and the powers do not, and the single most common error in published guidance is to quote a period or a percentage from one system as if it governed the other.

Three distinctions organise everything that follows. The first is the price of entry. An income tax appeal is nearly free: the fee for the first appeal is measured in hundreds of rupees, and no deposit of the disputed tax is demanded as a condition of filing, although recovery of the demand is a separate battle that must be fought with a stay application. A goods and services tax appeal is conditional on payment: a prescribed proportion of the disputed tax must be deposited before the appeal is entertained at all, and a further proportion before the second appeal, so that a fifth of the disputed tax stands paid before the tribunal stage is reached. The second distinction is who decides first. The first income tax appeal is heard by a senior officer of the same department that made the assessment, operating under faceless and largely written procedures, while the first independent forum is the Tribunal. Under the goods and services tax the first appeal is likewise departmental, and independence likewise arrives only at the tribunal, which for the first eight years of the tax did not exist at all. The third distinction is what the higher forums may touch. In both systems the tribunal is the last forum that can find facts. Above it, the High Court and the Supreme Court are confined to law, which is why the factual record built at the first two stages decides most tax litigation long before a constitutional court hears of it.

The table below is the findable core of this article. It places the two ladders side by side, rung by rung, so that a reader holding any order can locate the forum, the period, the price of entry and the deciding power that apply to it.

A taxpayer who reads only one section of this article should read the two that follow the table, because they convert the table's skeleton into the working knowledge the One Test of this series demands: the exact period, computed from the correct starting date; the exact sum payable, and to whom; and the exact boundary of what each forum can decide.

## How the ladder was built: a short history of the tax appeal

The shape of the modern ladder is a sediment of decisions taken across a century, and three of them explain almost everything about its present form. Under the Indian Income-tax Act 1922, the assessee aggrieved by an assessment appealed to an Appellate Assistant Commissioner, an officer of the department, and beyond that officer there was no appeal on the merits at all. What stood above the departmental tier was the reference: on an application by the assessee or the Commissioner, the appellate authority could state a case and refer a question of law to the High Court, whose opinion decided the question and bound the disposal of the appeal. The reference procedure had two structural weaknesses that defined the era's discontent. It excluded facts entirely, so an assessment wrong on its evidence but not on any statable point of law stood; and it was slow, since the case had to be drawn, settled and carried to a constitutional court for what was in substance an appellate function. Civil courts were barred from entertaining tax suits, so the assessee's effective remedies were the department's own officer and an advisory opinion from a distant court.

The reform that broke the pattern was the creation, in January 1941, of the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, inserted into the 1922 Act as section 5A. The Tribunal introduced into Indian tax law the idea that defines the system to this day: an independent, expert, mixed bench of judicial and accountant members, outside the assessing hierarchy, empowered to decide both fact and law and to dispose of the appeal finally. Assessees and the Commissioner alike could carry the Appellate Assistant Commissioner's order to it, and the reference to the High Court survived only on questions arising from the Tribunal's orders. The Income-tax Act 1961 rebuilt the code around that settlement. The reference procedure was converted into a direct appeal on a substantial question of law, the ancestor of section 260A; the departmental first appeal was retained, its holder later restyled from Appellate Assistant Commissioner to Commissioner (Appeals); the Tribunal was reconstituted by section 252 with its procedure in section 255; and revision, rectification and penalty appeals were folded into a single appellate chapter. Chapter XX of the 1961 Act, the code within the code that this article maps, is in substance the 1941 settlement written large.

The decades since have added institutions at the ladder's edges rather than replacing its centre, and the additions record the recurring diagnosis that the ladder clogs. The Settlement Commission of 1976 answered the clog with negotiated closure. The Authority for Advance Rulings of 1993 answered it with prevention, ruling before assessment instead of after. The Dispute Resolution Panel of 2009 answered it for transfer pricing by interposing a collegiate scrutiny before the demand existed. Faceless administration after 2019 answered it by removing the interface between appellant and officer. The Dispute Resolution Committee of 2022 answered it at the smallest scale by offering compromise instead of adjudication. The goods and services tax, arriving in 2017, copied the silhouette wholesale, a departmental first appeal, a tribunal, law-only appeals above, which is itself evidence of the design's staying power; and its tribunal's eight-year absence is evidence of the design's single point of failure, the dependence of a statutory remedy on an administrative act of constitution. Each addition was legislated as a relief to the system, and the system's pendency, set out later in this article, survived them all, which is why the history matters to the taxpayer reading it: the ladder's architecture has been stable for eighty years, and its capacity has never once been.

## The first income tax appeal: the Commissioner (Appeals) and the Joint Commissioner (Appeals)

The first appeal is the workhorse of the direct tax system, and its design explains its caseload. Section 246A of the Income-tax Act lists the orders against which an appeal lies to the Commissioner (Appeals): assessment orders, orders imposing penalty, orders treating a person as an agent of a non-resident, orders on withholding defaults, and a long catalogue of other determinations. Since 1 April 2023 a second first appellate authority stands beside it. The Finance Act 2023 rewrote section 246 to create the Joint Commissioner (Appeals), a designated authority for specified classes of cases, principally appeals against orders passed by officers below the rank of Joint Commissioner and the smaller disputes that the administration wished to clear faster; the Central Board of Direct Taxes then notified the e-Appeals Scheme 2023, on 29 May 2023, under which such appeals are allocated, in part randomly, and disposed of electronically. The two authorities exercise the same appellate powers, and an order of either is appealable onward to the Tribunal in the same way. The detailed account of the parent statute, its structure and its history is the business of the [complete guide to the Income Tax Act 1961](/2012/07/01/income-tax-act-1961-complete-guide/), which owns that ground; what matters here is the appellate machinery and how a taxpayer uses it.

The mechanics are prescribed by section 249. The appeal is filed in Form 35, electronically, accompanied by a fee that has remained nominal for decades: Rs 250 where the assessed total income does not exceed one lakh rupees, Rs 500 where it exceeds one lakh but not two lakh, Rs 1,000 where it exceeds two lakh, and Rs 250 for appeals whose subject matter is not an assessed income at all, such as many penalty appeals. Two features of the first appeal deserve emphasis because each quietly defeats appellants who learn of it late. The first is the admission condition in section 249(4): where a return has been filed, the appeal is not admitted unless the tax due on the income returned has been paid, and where no return has been filed, unless an advance tax equal to what was payable has been deposited, subject to a power in the appellate authority to exempt for reasons recorded. An appeal against an assessment that ignores tax admittedly payable on the appellant's own return is therefore liable to fail at the door. The second feature is the power of enhancement. Under section 251 the appellate authority may confirm, reduce, enhance or annul the assessment, and in a penalty appeal may confirm or cancel the penalty or vary it so as to enhance or reduce it. The first appeal is thus not a riskless remedy: a taxpayer who appeals invites a fresh examination of the whole assessment by an authority empowered to raise the demand, although fairness requires that enhancement be preceded by a reasonable opportunity of showing cause against it.

### How many days do you have to file the first income tax appeal?

Thirty days. Section 249(2) requires the appeal to be presented within thirty days of service of the notice of demand for an assessment or penalty appeal, and within thirty days of service of the order in other cases. The appellate authority may admit a late appeal where sufficient cause is shown.

The starting point deserves a paragraph of its own, because more appeals are lost to arithmetic than to law. The Income-tax Act measures the period from service of the notice of demand in assessment and penalty matters, which in a faceless administration means the date the order and demand notice are communicated through the portal and associated channels. A taxpayer who counts thirty days from the date of the order rather than from communication may waste days that cannot be recovered except by persuading the appellate authority, under section 249(3), that sufficient cause prevented timely filing. That condonation power is real but discretionary, and the case law on it rewards documented causes, such as portal failure, illness, or advice received late from a professional, over unexplained inertia. The prudent course treats the thirty days as measured from the earliest plausible date of communication and files within it.

Procedure before the first appellate authority is governed by section 250, and its shape has been transformed without any change to the section's appellate substance. The authority fixes a hearing, the appellant may appear in person or through an authorised representative, additional evidence is restricted by the rules governing its admission, and the order must be in writing, stating the points for determination, the decision on each, and the reasons. The Finance Act 2020 inserted subsections (6B) and (6C) into section 250, empowering the Central Government to notify a scheme for the disposal of appeals with no personal interface; the Faceless Appeal Scheme 2020 followed by notification dated 25 September 2020, and a revised Faceless Appeal Scheme 2021 replaced it by notification dated 28 December 2021, routing appeals through the National Faceless Appeal Centre to appeal units that the appellant cannot identify and does not visit. The consequences of that redesign, including the contested question of the personal hearing, are taken up in a later section of this article. For the first appeal's place in the ladder, what matters is that the authority deciding it remains an officer of the income tax department. Its independence is functional rather than institutional, which is why so much turns on the next rung.

The Joint Commissioner (Appeals) tier, the ladder's newest rung, repays a closer look because it embodies the administration's theory of the pendency problem. The cases assigned to it are those in which the order under appeal was passed by an officer below the rank of Joint Commissioner, which in practice captures the great mass of standard assessments, and the assignment is made by scheme rather than by the taxpayer's choice: the appellant files in Form 35 as before, and the system routes the appeal to the Commissioner (Appeals) or to the Joint Commissioner (Appeals) according to the notified classes. The theory is division of labour, that a dedicated tier for the standard case disposes faster because its officers carry a narrower class of dispute, and its electronic, randomly allocated procedure borrows the faceless logic without surrendering the titled appellate authority that faceless units anonymised. The risk the design runs is the one every subdivision runs, that two first appellate authorities with identical powers produce divergent practice, and that the ladder acquires a lottery at its first rung. The Tribunal, hearing appeals from both authorities under one section, is the corrective, and the early years of the tier will show whether the pendency at the first rung falls or merely redistributes.

One discipline governs the whole of this article and should be stated where the first practical steps are described. The account given here is general information about how the appellate system is built and how it is used; it is not advice on any individual case, and it cannot substitute for advice on one, because the limitation periods and forum rules described here are unforgiving of a wrong date or a wrong door, and only the record of a particular matter can supply either.

## Filing the appeal: the procedure in working order

Template accounts of appeals tend to stop at the period and the forum, but appeals are lost in the filing as often as in the hearing, and the working sequence deserves statement in prose. An income tax appeal begins with the order and the notice of demand, because the appellant must identify, from the face of the assessment, exactly what is being challenged: the computation, the disallowances, the penalty, or all three, since an appeal that omits a component leaves that component unchallenged and enforceable. Form 35, the prescribed memorandum, requires the appellant's particulars and permanent account number, the order appealed against and its date, the date of service of the notice of demand, the address for communication, the assessed income and the tax determined, the amount in dispute divided between disputed additions and other matters, a statement of facts, and the grounds of appeal, which must be precise and not argumentative, because a ground not taken is ordinarily a ground lost, and a ground taken in sweeping form invites the objection that it was never argued. The form is verified by the appellant, filed electronically through the income tax portal, and accompanied by the fee; where the appeal is defective, a defect notice gives an opportunity to cure, and the date of a cured filing is protected where the defect is removed within the time allowed. Against a practical point that surprises many first-time appellants, the portal acknowledgement and not the internal drafting date is the evidence of filing, and a limitation dispute will be decided on it. The order, the demand notice and the computation are office documents and spreadsheets in the ordinary case, and a party working away from the portal that issued them will open and read them in a document viewer of the ReportMedic kind; the tool is a matter of convenience, but the preservation of the complete set is a matter of proof, and the distinction returns at the close of this article.

The goods and services tax appeal is filed in Form GST APL-01 on the common portal, and its checklist differs in the way this article has emphasised throughout: the provisional acknowledgement issues only after the pre-deposit is made and reflected, because without the deposit the appeal is not entertained, and a final acknowledgement follows scrutiny of the application. The appellant must also certify that the matter has not been filed before another appellate forum on the same issue, a certification the statute demands to prevent parallel proceedings on one order. At the tribunal stage the memorandum is filed in Form GST APL-05, with the same discipline of deposit, statement of facts and grounds. In both systems the respondent department files its response, the appellant may file a rejoinder and written submissions, evidence is confined by rules, and the hearing, where one is held, is directed to the grounds taken. An appellant who understands that the appeal is a document before it is an argument will ordinarily fare better than one who treats the filing as a formality preceding the real contest.

Two ancillary mechanisms run alongside the filing discipline and reward early attention. The first is the application for stay, which in income tax is a separate proceeding from the appeal itself, addressed first to the Assessing Officer under section 220(6) and then upward within the administration, and which must be pursued in parallel with the appeal rather than after recovery begins; the mechanics and the governing standard are set out in the section on recovery below. The second is the application for admission of additional evidence. Before the first appellate authority, Rule 46A of the Income-tax Rules confines additional evidence to defined situations, among them refusal by the Assessing Officer to admit evidence tendered, prevention by sufficient cause from producing evidence called for, and the making of an order without a sufficient opportunity to adduce evidence relevant to a ground; evidence outside those situations requires the appellate authority to record its reasons for admission and to confront the Assessing Officer with it by remand report. Before the Tribunal, the corresponding rule similarly discourages fresh evidence save where the record below was defective for reasons beyond the appellant's control. The architecture reflects a deliberate policy: the assessment is where evidence belongs, the appeals are where the assessment is judged, and each higher forum defers the evidentiary opening more grudgingly than the one below it.

## The second appeal: the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal

The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal is the institution against which every Indian tribunal is measured, and its longevity is the first fact about it. It was constituted on 25 January 1941 under section 5A of the Indian Income-tax Act 1922, on the recommendation of a select committee that reported in the late nineteen thirties, after decades in which civil courts were barred from tax matters and the only challenge to an assessment lay inside the revenue administration or by way of an advisory reference to a High Court. It is the oldest tribunal in the country, frequently described as the mother tribunal, and its success is the standing argument for tribunalisation itself. When the Income-tax Act 1961 replaced the 1922 Act, the Tribunal was carried into the new code by section 252, under which the Central Government constitutes the Appellate Tribunal with as many judicial and accountant members as it thinks fit. In a deliberate structural choice, the Tribunal sits within the Department of Legal Affairs of the Ministry of Law and Justice rather than the Ministry of Finance, so that the department whose assessments it reviews does not administer it. Its benches, sixty-three in number across the country, ordinarily consist of one judicial member and one accountant member; a single member may dispose of a case where the assessed income does not exceed fifty lakh rupees, and special benches of three or more members are constituted for questions of unusual difficulty or conflict.

An appeal to the Tribunal lies under section 253 against orders of the Commissioner (Appeals) and the Joint Commissioner (Appeals), against assessment orders passed in conformity with Dispute Resolution Panel directions, against certain orders of senior departmental authorities, and against a miscellany of other determinations the section lists. Both sides may appeal: the assessee against an adverse order, and the department, on the direction of the Principal Commissioner or Commissioner, against an appellate order it considers wrong. The limitation history of this rung carries a lesson in reading the statute as amended rather than as remembered. For most of the Act's life the appeal had to be filed within sixty days of the date on which the order was communicated. The Finance (No. 2) Act 2024 recast the period with effect from 1 October 2024, substituting two months computed from the end of the month in which the order is communicated, a change that lengthens some periods and shortens none by accident but which will trap any adviser who files by a calendar learned under the older text. The respondent who has not appealed may nonetheless file a memorandum of cross-objections within thirty days of receiving notice of the other side's appeal, and the Tribunal treats that memorandum as if it were an appeal of its own. The Tribunal may admit a late appeal or cross-objection where sufficient cause is shown.

### What fee do you pay to appeal to the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal?

The fee under section 253(6) is Rs 500 where the assessed total income does not exceed one lakh rupees, Rs 1,500 where it exceeds one lakh but not two lakh rupees, one per cent of assessed income capped at Rs 10,000 above two lakh rupees, and Rs 500 for other subject matter.

The powers of the Tribunal, stated in section 254, are the widest in the ladder below the constitutional courts, and one limit defines them. After giving both parties an opportunity of being heard, the Tribunal may pass such orders on the appeal as it thinks fit: it may confirm, vary or reverse the order below, restore a matter for fresh examination, admit additional evidence within the governing rules, and decide questions of fact finally. Its findings of fact bind the High Court above it, which will not reweigh evidence, disturbing a finding only where it is perverse, resting on no evidence, or reached by ignoring material evidence. That finality is the Tribunal's great contribution to the system: it is the point at which the factual record closes. A litigant who has not placed evidence before the Tribunal will ordinarily find no forum further up the ladder willing to receive it. The corresponding limit is that the Tribunal is a creature of the statute that created it. It cannot strike down the Income-tax Act or any provision of it, however unjust the provision may appear, because the power to annul legislation belongs to the constitutional courts alone. A challenge to the vires of the charging provision must therefore travel by writ to a High Court even while the appeal on the assessment travels to the Tribunal, and the two proceedings occasionally run in harness.

Two further features of the Tribunal's procedure matter in practice. The first is interim protection: under section 254(2A) the Tribunal may stay recovery proceedings on application, subject to conditions it may impose, and the statute caps the aggregate life of such a stay at three hundred and sixty-five days, a ceiling that connects the Tribunal's calendar to the taxpayer's cash position in a way the concluding sections of this article examine. The second is the statutory expectation of pace: the same provision directs the Tribunal, where it is possible so to do, to hear and decide an appeal within four years from the end of the financial year in which it was filed. Departmental discipline also shapes what reaches the Tribunal, because the Central Board of Direct Taxes instructs its officers not to carry appeals upward where the tax effect falls below monetary thresholds that the Board revises from time to time, a policy examined with the record below.

The hearing itself has an established craft. Appeals are listed before benches whose composition is published in advance; the appellant or an authorised representative argues from a paper book containing the assessment order, the appellate order, the evidence and the authorities relied on, filed in advance under the Tribunal's rules; the department is represented by departmental representatives drawn from the income tax service, whose standing instructions bind them to the record. Adjournments are within the bench's control and are not a right, and the statutory ceiling on stays, three hundred and sixty-five days in aggregate, disciplines the tactic of converting interim protection into indefinite deferral: a stay lapses unless the delay is shown not to be attributable to the assessee, and benches have become markedly less indulgent of proceedings in which the party enjoying the stay is the party prolonging the case. After the hearing the bench reserves or pronounces its order, which is communicated to both parties and to the Commissioner concerned. A mistake apparent from the Tribunal's own order may be rectified by the Tribunal under section 254(2) within four years from the end of the month in which the order was passed, a jurisdiction ancillary to the appeal that corrects arithmetic and transcription without reopening argument. The fee structure and the absence of a deposit requirement at this rung make the Tribunal the most accessible independent forum in Indian law relative to the sums it decides, which is a considerable part of why its benches carry the prestige they do.

The successor statute does not disturb this design. The Income-tax Act 2025, which operates from 1 April 2026, restates the appellate ladder in reorganised and renumbered form, with the same first appellate authorities, the same Tribunal, and the same law-only appeals above it, while the savings that accompany the repeal of the 1961 Act keep every appeal for an earlier period inside the older Act's provisions, as the pillar article on the 1961 Act records. A taxpayer's forum for the foreseeable future is therefore determined by the period under dispute rather than by the date of the appeal, and the ladder described in this article will decide the large stock of pending matters long after the new code begins taxing new years.

## The law-only rungs: the High Court and the Supreme Court

Above the Tribunal, the character of the dispute changes. Section 260A permits an appeal to the High Court from an order of the Tribunal only where the High Court is satisfied that the case involves a substantial question of law. The appeal must be filed within one hundred and twenty days of the date on which the Tribunal's order is received, is presented as a memorandum of appeal precisely stating the question relied on, and is heard by a bench of not less than two judges. The High Court formulates the substantial question, hears the parties on that question, and answers it; parties may argue that a further question arises, and the Court may formulate it where satisfied, but the appeal is not an invitation to reopen the assessment. A question of law is substantial where it is not settled by the Supreme Court or the jurisdictional High Court, where it admits of genuine argument, or where the answer materially affects the rights of the parties; a concurrent finding of fact, however debatable, is not converted into a question of law by dressing a factual grievance in legal vocabulary. The perversity exception is the narrow door through which facts re-enter: a finding that no reasonable person could reach on the evidence, or one built on irrelevant material while ignoring the relevant, raises a question of law because it is not, in the eye of the law, a finding of fact at all.

### Can a High Court re-examine the facts in a tax appeal?

No. An appeal under section 260A lies only on a substantial question of law, and the High Court answers the question formulated rather than retrying the assessment. Findings of fact recorded by the Tribunal are final, disturbed only where perverse, resting on no evidence, or reached by disregarding material evidence.

The questions that characteristically qualify for the law-only rungs are questions about the statute rather than about the assessee: whether a receipt falls within a charging provision at all, how a deduction section is to be construed where two readings compete, whether a treaty provision overrides the Act on a point of residence or permanent establishment, whether an amendment operates retrospectively, and whether a provision as applied exceeds the legislature's competence. Questions of that class matter beyond the parties, because a High Court's answer binds the department and the Tribunal within its jurisdiction while a contrary answer from another High Court binds within its own, and until the Supreme Court resolves the divergence the same transaction can bear different tax in different states of the Union, a federal untidiness that the certification and special leave routes exist to cure. The converse discipline is equally important to the taxpayer planning an appeal: a grievance that the Tribunal believed the wrong witness, ignored a ledger, or drew an inference the evidence barely supports is, without perversity of the strict kind, a grievance without a forum above the Tribunal, and an appeal framed as law but argued as fact will be dismissed at admission, often with the observation that the dress does not change the body beneath it.

From the High Court the route runs to the Supreme Court under section 261, in a case the High Court certifies to be fit for appeal, applying the pattern of civil appeals under the Constitution and the Code of Civil Procedure; where certification is refused or the route otherwise unavailable, the aggrieved party may still seek special leave to appeal under Article 136, which the Supreme Court grants sparingly in tax matters, ordinarily where a question of general importance transcends the individual assessment. The combined effect of the two law-only rungs is that the Supreme Court's tax docket shapes doctrine for the whole country while touching the facts of almost none of it, and the practical litigation of a tax demand is, for nearly every taxpayer, finished at the Tribunal. That concentration of consequence in the second appeal is the strongest reason for the care this article gives to the Tribunal's procedure, and for the recurring constitutional anxiety, examined below, about who staffs it and on what terms.

## Writs, and where the ladder does not reach

Alongside the statutory ladder stands the constitutional safety valve, and no map of tax remedies is complete without it. The High Courts' power under Article 226 of the Constitution, and the Supreme Court's under Article 32, are not displaced by the appellate code: L. Chandra Kumar settled that tribunals function subject to that review, and a taxpayer may invoke it directly. The courts, however, police the boundary through the doctrine of alternative remedy: where the statute provides an efficacious appeal, a writ petition that merely rehearses the merits of an assessment will ordinarily be returned to the ladder, and writs are entertained, in practice, where the ladder cannot answer the complaint. Four situations recur in the case law. The first is a challenge to the constitutional validity of the taxing provision itself, which no tribunal may decide and which only a constitutional court can entertain. The second is a patent absence of jurisdiction, where the authority lacked the power to act at all, as distinct from exercising a conceded power wrongly. The third is a violation of the principles of natural justice so complete that the appellate process cannot cure it, such as an order passed without any hearing where the statute required one. The fourth is the emergency use of Article 226 during the missing years of the goods and services tax tribunal, when the second appellate forum did not exist and the writ court became, by necessity, the only forum able to examine both fact and law in goods and services tax disputes.

The writ route has its own discipline, and it is not a shortcut around limitation or deposit. A writ filed in place of an appeal that was available and allowed to lapse will face the objection of delay and of the forgone statutory remedy; a writ that seeks to avoid a pre-deposit will be scrutinised for exactly that motive, although High Courts have on occasion entertained writs where the deposit itself was the hardship in issue, or where the demand rested on an order passed without jurisdiction, treating the deposit condition as incapable of barring the constitutional jurisdiction. The Supreme Court's special leave jurisdiction under Article 136 performs a parallel function at the apex, available not as a matter of right and granted in tax matters where the question is one of general importance, where High Courts conflict, or where the certification route under section 261 was refused. The practical place of the writ in the system is therefore precise: it corrects the ladder's structural failures, it does not duplicate the ladder, and a taxpayer who treats it as an alternative first appeal will usually be sent back to climb.

## The appeal does not stop the tax: stay of demand and recovery

Filing an appeal and stopping recovery are separate acts, and confusing them is expensive. Under section 220(6), where an assessee has presented an appeal to the first appellate authority, the Assessing Officer may treat the assessee as not being in default in respect of the disputed amount until the appeal is disposed of, on such terms as the officer thinks fit. The discretion was for decades exercised without a common standard, until the Central Board of Direct Taxes issued an office memorandum dated 29 February 2016 prescribing that stay would ordinarily follow on payment of fifteen per cent of the disputed demand, and a further office memorandum dated 31 July 2017 raised the standard proportion to twenty per cent, with room to go higher or lower, with the approval of the Principal Commissioner, where earlier appellate decisions or court rulings on the same issue justify it. The memoranda are administrative guidance, not statute, and the Supreme Court settled their status in Principal Commissioner of Income Tax v LG Electronics India in 2018: the standard proportion does not fetter the discretion of the Assessing Officer or the Commissioner to grant stay on payment of a smaller amount where the merits and the balance of convenience support one, and a stay application must be decided on the prima facie case, the financial hardship, and the likelihood of recovery, rather than by mechanical reference to a percentage.

### Does filing a tax appeal stop recovery of the demand?

No. An appeal does not operate as a stay. While the first appeal is pending, the assessee must apply under section 220(6) to be treated as not in default, and the governing administrative standard expects payment of twenty per cent of the disputed demand, although the authorities retain discretion to accept less.

The contrast with the goods and services tax, where the deposit is a statutory precondition collected at the door of each rung, is instructive and returns in the treatment of pre-deposits below. In income tax the deposit is the price of interim peace rather than of admission, which is gentler in design and harsher in uncertainty: the taxpayer who cannot persuade an officer to grant a stay may face attachment of bank accounts or adjustment of refunds against the disputed demand while the appeal waits its turn, and the remedies against overzealous recovery are a further application upward within the administration or a writ petition to the High Court, each consuming the time the appeal itself was meant to save. Interest on delayed payment under section 220(2) continues to run through the period of a stay, to be collected if the appeal fails, so the deposit decision is also a wager about interest, and no account of the ladder is honest that omits it. Recovery has a quieter instrument that taxpayers meet more often than attachment: the set-off of refunds. Under section 245, where a refund is due to a person, the tax authorities may, after giving written intimation of the action proposed, set the refund off against any sum remaining payable under the Act by that person, so that a refund earned in one year can be absorbed by a disputed demand of another year while the appeal against that demand awaits hearing. The intimation requirement is the taxpayer's procedural protection, and adjustments recorded without it have been corrected on challenge; the substantive grievance, that set-off operates as recovery by another name and defeats the stay analysis conducted for the same demand, is answered in the department's practice by treating adjustment and stay as connected questions, and a taxpayer with a refund in prospect and a demand under stay application should expect the two files to meet. On the goods and services tax side the corresponding mechanics are simpler and harsher, since the deposit that unlocks the appeal is collected in advance and the balance is stayed by the statute itself, which is precisely the trade the pre-deposit sections of this article examine.


## The cost of the contest: interest, refunds and prosecution

An appeal suspends neither the economics nor the criminal exposure of a tax dispute, and a taxpayer deciding whether to climb the ladder should price the whole contest. Interest is the largest component. Where an appeal succeeds in part or wholly, the demand falls and interest is recomputed on the reduced amount; where it fails, interest for delayed payment under section 220(2) is recoverable for the period of default, and a stay does not erase the interest that accrues while the dispute runs, so the twenty per cent paid under the administrative standard also functions as a partial hedge against that accrual. On the other side of the ledger, where a deposit or a payment made during the dispute becomes refundable on success, section 244A pays interest on refunds at six per cent per annum for the period prescribed, and the goods and services tax statute carries its own interest provisions for delayed refunds, including on pre-deposits, under section 115 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act. The asymmetry between the interest a taxpayer pays on a confirmed demand and the interest the state pays on a refund is a standing grievance in the literature of tax administration, and it prices delay differently for the two parties: for the department, a pending appeal costs little beyond the time value of the suspended demand; for the taxpayer, a pending appeal against an unpaid demand accumulates interest at rates that can exceed commercial borrowing, which is one reason the settlement schemes examined later in this article find takers among taxpayers whose cases are arguable rather than hopeless.

Prosecution runs on a separate track altogether. The institution of criminal proceedings for offences under Chapter XXII of the Income-tax Act, and the corresponding offence provisions of the goods and services tax statute, is not barred by the pendency of an appeal, and an appellate victory does not by itself extinguish a prosecution launched on the same facts, although the record of the appellate outcome is material the prosecution must reckon with. Compounding provisions exist under which offences may be settled on payment, and the Dispute Resolution Committee and the settlement institutions described in this article could grant immunity within their spheres, but the default position is that penalty, interest, prosecution and appeal are four parallel consequences of one assessment, and a remedy obtained in one does not travel automatically to the others. Costs in the mercantile sense complete the account: professional fees across a four-rung dispute, management time across the years a dispute consumes, and the deposit capital locked at the indirect tax rungs, all of which are real prices of the remedy and none of which are recoverable in any meaningful measure under Indian tax procedure, where costs awarded by forums are exceptional and nominal. The ladder is cheap at the door and expensive on the stairs, and an honest account of it says so.

## The goods and services tax ladder: the same shape at a different price

The goods and services tax ladder mirrors the income tax ladder rung for rung, and then charges for admission. The statutory frame is set out in the [explainer of the GST Acts](/2012/07/15/gst-acts-india-explained/), which owns the detail of the appellate story; this article states the ladder at the depth a taxpayer comparing the two systems needs, and dwells instead on the two features that distinguish it: the deposit as a condition of entry, and the years in which the second rung was absent.

The first appeal lies under section 107 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act 2017 to the Appellate Authority against any decision or order of an adjudicating authority, to be filed within three months of the date on which the order is communicated, with a power in the Appellate Authority to condone a further delay of up to one month where sufficient cause is shown. It is not, however, entertained unless the appellant has paid the whole of the tax, interest, fine, fee and penalty admitted to be due, and a sum equal to ten per cent of the remaining amount of tax in dispute, subject to a statutory ceiling on that deposit. On payment of that amount, recovery of the balance is deemed to be stayed, an automatic protection the income tax system achieves only by application and discretion. The Appellate Authority, after hearing, may confirm, modify or annul the order, may refer the case back to the adjudicating authority with directions, and may enhance a tax, fee or penalty or confiscate goods only after giving the appellant a reasonable opportunity of showing cause; it may not travel to grounds that were not the subject of the order under appeal without hearing, and the revisionary authority under section 108 retains a separate power to call for and revise orders prejudicial to revenue.

The second appeal lies under section 112 to the Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal, within three months of the date on which the order is communicated, or the date on which the President or the State President of the Tribunal enters upon office, whichever is later, a formulation whose second limb exists because for years there was no office to enter. Admission requires payment, in addition to the admitted dues, of a further sum equal to ten per cent of the remaining disputed tax, subject again to a ceiling, so that the appellant stands twenty per cent paid before the Tribunal hears the dispute. The Tribunal is, like its income tax counterpart, the final authority on facts; the next appeal lies under section 117 to the High Court within one hundred and eighty days and only on a substantial question of law, and section 118 carries the route onward to the Supreme Court.

The Tribunal itself, when it came, was built on the familiar template with one deliberate variation. Its benches combine judicial members, technical members drawn from the revenue services of the Centre and the states, and its structure pairs a Principal Bench at New Delhi with State Benches across the country, reflecting the dual administration of the tax by two levels of government; appeals lie to it both from the Appellate Authority under section 112 and, by the statute's design, against orders of the revisional authority in defined situations, and it may confirm, modify or annul the order appealed against or refer the matter back with directions. Cross-objections lie at this stage as they do before the income tax Tribunal, to be filed within forty-five days of receipt of notice of the appeal, and the Tribunal may condone delay in filing the appeal itself by up to three months. The Appellate Authority below it works under a discipline the income tax first appeal lacks: the statute directs that it shall, where it is possible so to do, hear and decide every appeal within one year of its filing, and it must record brief reasons and communicate its order to both parties and to the jurisdictional Commissioners of both administrations, since an order under a dual tax binds two governments at once. These procedural details, and the litigation over the Tribunal's composition that preceded its launch, belong to the goods and services tax articles of this series; what the taxpayer comparing systems should retain is that the goods and services tax copied the income tax ladder's silhouette and then altered every dimension that determines its use: longer first periods, shorter condonation, deposits at the door, and a tribunal whose arrival the statute had to wait eight years to schedule.

The missing years require their own paragraph, because they are the largest single exhibit in this article's larger argument. The tax was rolled out on 1 July 2017 with an appellate tribunal promised by the design and absent in fact; the composition provisions of the statute were litigated, and the Madras High Court in the Revenue Bar Association case of 2019 struck down provisions on the constitution of the Tribunal's benches, the detail of which the GST pillar article owns. Parliament rewrote the provisions by the Finance Act 2023, the Tribunal was constituted and benches were launched only in September 2025, and in the interval the second rung simply did not exist. The law answered the gap with administrative grace rather than legislative speed: a removal of difficulties order dated 3 December 2019, the ninth in the series, counted the appeal period from the later of the communication of the order and the constitution of the Tribunal, so that no appellant was time-barred by the state's own delay, and taxpayers who could not wait carried their disputes to the High Courts under Article 226 of the Constitution, converting courts of law into courts of first appellate resort for a consumption tax administered across the whole economy. A taxpayer comparing the two ladders should register the asymmetry plainly: the income tax ladder's forums have existed continuously since 1941; the goods and services tax ladder was operated for more than eight years with its central institution missing, and the remedy for that absence was litigation about the remedy.

## Advance rulings and revision on the goods and services tax side

The goods and services tax built its own parallel devices, and one of them supplies a further instance of the missing-forum pattern. Chapter XVII of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act provides an Authority for Advance Ruling in each state, to which an applicant may carry questions on classification, the applicability of notifications, the time and value of supply, the admissibility of input tax credit, the liability to pay tax and the requirement of registration, on payment of a fee of five thousand rupees under each of the Central and State Acts, with an appeal under section 101 to an Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling within thirty days of communication. Because the tax is dual, divergent rulings between state authorities on identical transactions were foreseeable, and a 2019 amendment provided for a National Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling to resolve them; that national authority was never constituted, and the reconciliation of conflicting advance rulings was left to litigation and to the Council's clarifications, so the device designed to prevent disputes generated a class of its own. Rectification on the goods and services tax side runs through section 161, under which an authority may rectify an error or omission apparent on the face of the record within three months of the order, with a longer window of six months for clerical and arithmetical errors brought to its notice; revision runs through section 108, under which the Revisional Authority may call for the record of an order passed by a subordinate officer and revise it where the order is erroneous and prejudicial to revenue, subject to exclusions for matters under appeal and to a hearing where the revision is adverse. The pattern will be familiar from the direct tax half of this article: the ladder for the aggrieved, a revisional key for the administration, and a rectification hatch for patent error, replicated across the fiscal constitution with the numbers changed.

## The older indirect ladder: customs, excise and service tax

The goods and services tax absorbed most indirect taxes, but customs duties remain outside it, and the legacy of central excise and service tax disputes continues to be heard under the statutes that imposed them, so a complete map records the older ladder as well. Under the Central Excise Act 1944 and the Customs Act 1962, the first appeal lay to the Commissioner (Appeals), and the second to the Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal, constituted in 1982 and among the oldest of the specialist tribunals after the income tax Tribunal. The Finance Act 2014 substituted a graded deposit regime in section 35F of the excise statute and section 129E of the customs statute: seven and a half per cent of the duty or penalty in dispute at the first appeal, ten per cent at the tribunal stage, capped at ten crore rupees, replacing an older dispensation under which the deposit could be the whole demand unless waived for undue hardship. The pattern is the one the goods and services tax later generalised: a deposit graded by tier, expressed as a proportion of the dispute, capped in absolute terms, and owed as the price of being heard. Appeals from the legacy tribunal follow the familiar pattern, to the High Court on a substantial question of law and onward to the Supreme Court, with classification and valuation disputes channelled directly to the Supreme Court by the older statutes, a routing peculiarity that has shaped a generation of indirect tax precedent. The legacy tribunal also illustrates, in a quieter register, the longevity Indian tribunals achieve once constituted: created in 1982, it has sat continuously since, under a President and with benches at the major ports and manufacturing centres, and its survival across the absorption of excise and service tax into the goods and services tax, carrying a legacy docket that diminishes only by disposal, is a reminder that a tribunal, unlike a tax, is not subsumed when its parent levy is. Its remaining work is a closing account of the pre-2017 indirect tax era, conducted under the deposit and limitation rules of statutes that no longer levy anything, which is why this article records the ladder rather than omitting it as obsolete.

## What a pre-deposit does: the honest two sides

Because the deposit is the single feature that most distinguishes the modern Indian appellate design from the income tax tradition, it deserves the two-sided treatment the record supports rather than the treatment either the revenue or the taxpayer lobby prefers. The case for the deposit is coherent. A right of appeal that costs nothing to exercise invites appeals filed to defer payment rather than to correct error, and every year of deferral is a year in which assessed revenue is locked out of the exchequer while interest at regulated rates may underprice the state's cost of waiting. A deposit graded by tier filters the speculative appeal, compensates the state in part for the deferral, and gives the appellant a financial commitment proportioned to the seriousness of the claim. Recovery of the balance is stayed by force of the statute once the deposit is paid, which the income tax system does not guarantee at all. On this account the deposit is not a barrier but a bargain: a fraction of the dispute, capped, in exchange for an automatic stay and a forum.

The case against it is equally coherent, and it is strongest exactly where the tax system most needs legitimacy, among small and mid-sized businesses. A disputed demand in a consumption tax frequently exceeds the working capital of the enterprise disputing it, because the demand is computed on turnover-influenced figures, may include interest and penalty computed with it, and arises in a credit chain where the taxpayer has already borne tax on inputs. Twenty per cent of such a demand, accumulated across the two rungs, can consume the cash with which wages and suppliers are paid; the appeal that cannot be funded is, in substance, a remedy denied, whatever the statute says. The rejoinder that deposits are refunded on success, with interest, answers the arithmetic but not the timing, since refund follows a successful appeal concluded years later. Both propositions are true, and the honest conclusion is a design question rather than a slogan: the level of the deposit, the point at which hardship discretion operates, and the speed of refund decide whether the mechanism functions as a filter or as a toll, and legislatures setting the percentage are in substance deciding how much of the right of appeal a taxpayer must be able to afford.

A detail of the goods and services tax design sharpens the argument, because it shows the deposit operating without the safety valve its predecessor carried. Under the older excise and customs dispensation that the Finance Act 2014 replaced, the deposit could be dispensed with or reduced where the appellate authority was satisfied that depositing the duty demanded would cause undue hardship, subject to conditions safeguarding revenue; the discretion was litigated constantly, but it existed, and its existence conceded that a uniform percentage cannot measure hardship. The goods and services tax statute contains no corresponding dispensing power at either rung: the ten per cent and the further ten per cent are conditions of entertainment, and the only forum that has relieved against them in an appropriate case is the High Court in writ jurisdiction, moved by the taxpayer for whom the condition itself was the injury. The income tax system's answer to the same problem is differently defective: no deposit for admission, but a stay that depends on administrative discretion disciplined by an office memorandum rather than by the statute, so that the taxpayer's interim position turns on the judgment of officers of the very department that raised the demand, correctable by the Tribunal only at the second rung. Neither design has solved the problem the deposit debate is about, the separation of the appellant who can pay from the appellant who is right; they have merely chosen opposite points at which the unsolved problem bites.

## Routes beside the ladder: the panel, rectification and revision

Not every grievance belongs on the ladder. Three parallel routes handle classes of case the appellate sequence serves badly, and a taxpayer who mistakes one for another loses time that limitation does not forgive.

The Dispute Resolution Panel under section 144C addresses the structure of international tax disputes. Where an Assessing Officer proposes to make a variation to the income or loss returned, prejudicial to the interest of an eligible assessee, in consequence of an order of the Transfer Pricing Officer under section 92CA, or where the assessee is a foreign company, the officer does not pass a final assessment at once. A draft order is forwarded to the assessee, who may accept it or file objections, in Form 35A, before the Panel within thirty days of receipt. The Panel is a collegium of three Commissioners of Income Tax; it considers the draft order, the objections, the evidence and its own enquiries, and issues directions within nine months from the end of the month in which the draft order was forwarded. The directions bind the Assessing Officer, who completes the assessment in conformity with them within one month, and the resulting order is then appealable not to the Commissioner (Appeals) but directly to the Tribunal, so the panel route exchanges one rung of the ladder for an earlier and more collegiate scrutiny. Its logic is that transfer pricing disputes turn on method and comparables rather than on credibility, and a panel of three senior officers may correct a pricing position before a demand crystallises and a deposit argument begins.

Two features of the panel deserve emphasis because they define its clientele. The first is the eligibility boundary: the route exists only where the proposed variation flows from a Transfer Pricing Officer's order or where the assessee is a foreign company, so the domestic taxpayer with an ordinary high-pitched assessment has no access to it, however senior the scrutiny his case would benefit from, a limitation that has drawn criticism precisely because the panel's collegiate design answers a defect, the single-officer assessment, that is not confined to international cases. The second is the discipline of the panel's directions: the panel may confirm, reduce or enhance the variation proposed in the draft order, may consider matters not raised in the objections where the record supports them, and its proceedings are insulated from appeal at the direction stage, the taxpayer's remedy arising only when the Assessing Officer's conforming order converts the directions into an enforceable demand appealable to the Tribunal. The panel route therefore front-loads the taxpayer's effort into thirty days of objection drafting and a hearing before three Commissioners, and in exchange removes the first appellate rung entirely, a trade that suits record-heavy pricing disputes and fits almost nothing else.

Rectification under section 154 answers a narrower problem: a mistake apparent from the record. Where an error is patent, requiring no long argument to establish, the authority that passed the order may amend it, on application or of its own motion, within four years from the end of the financial year in which the order was passed, and an order enhancing an assessment or reducing a refund requires notice and hearing. The route is cheap and quick, and its discipline is its limit: a debatable question of law or fact is not a mistake apparent, and applications that attempt to reargue the assessment under rectification's flag fail for that reason. Revision completes the triad. Under section 263 the Principal Commissioner or Commissioner may revise an order that is erroneous in so far as it is prejudicial to the interests of revenue, after hearing, within two years from the end of the financial year in which the order was passed. Under section 264 the same authority may, on the taxpayer's application within one year, revise an order not appealable or not appealed, in the taxpayer's favour, and may not revise to the taxpayer's prejudice. The pairing is characteristic of the Indian design: the department holds a revisional key to its own errors in both directions, and the taxpayer's version of the key opens only downward. Revision under section 264 carries disciplines of its own that practitioners respect: it is not available for an order against which an appeal lies and was filed, nor ordinarily where the appeal remedy was available and the period for it was allowed to lapse without cause, since revision is a grace jurisdiction and not a second limitation period for the negligent; the application must be accompanied by the prescribed fee of five hundred rupees; and the Commissioner who entertains it acts on the record as it stands, without the appellate power to admit a fresh evidentiary case in the manner of the first appeal. Its enduring utility is for the order that is wrong in a way the appeal structure reaches badly: the demand raised under a provision already struck down, the exemption denied contrary to a binding jurisdictional precedent the Assessing Officer overlooked, the case in which the appeal period was consumed by the department's own delay in supplying the order under challenge. A taxpayer who treats revision as a spare appeal misunderstands it; a taxpayer who ignores it abandons the one door the department is directed to open in his favour.

## Advance rulings and the small taxpayer: the Board and the Committee

Two institutions stand outside the appellate sequence entirely, one designed to prevent disputes and the other to divert them, and the history of the first repeats the pattern this article traces in tribunals generally. Chapters XIX-B of the Income-tax Act created the Authority for Advance Rulings in 1993, chaired by a retired judge of the Supreme Court, to rule in advance on the tax consequences of transactions, principally for non-residents and for residents in defined categories, with rulings binding on the applicant and the department. The Authority accumulated delay, vacancies and a contested record, and the Finance Act 2021 replaced it, with effect from 1 September 2021, by a Board for Advance Rulings under section 245-OB, each Board consisting of two members of at least Chief Commissioner rank, sitting at Delhi and Mumbai, with pending applications transferred to it and an e-Advance Rulings Scheme of 2022 providing for electronic procedure. The change was defended on the ground of speed, and the trade is visible in the design: a ruling of the Board is appealable to the High Court under section 245W, where the Authority's rulings were not, and the applicant body that appears before serving officers of the department has recorded its apprehension about independence, a concern reflected in the Finance (No. 2) Act 2024, which opened a window for applicants to withdraw applications transferred from the Authority. Whether the Board is an improvement is therefore a question the record has not finished answering; what is settled is the shape of the exchange, judicial leadership surrendered for administrative throughput, in advance rulings as in tribunal reform generally.

The Dispute Resolution Committee under section 245MA, introduced by the same Finance Act 2021, works at the opposite end of the scale. A small taxpayer may apply to the Committee, under the e-Dispute Resolution Scheme 2022 notified on 5 April 2022, where the total income returned does not exceed fifty lakh rupees and the aggregate amount of variations proposed or made does not exceed ten lakh rupees, and where the order in question does not rest on search, survey, or information received under a tax information exchange agreement. Committees have been constituted for all eighteen Principal Chief Commissioner regions. The Committee may modify the variations in the specified order, and may reduce or waive any penalty or grant immunity from prosecution under the Act, and is directed to decide within six months from the end of the month in which the application was admitted. The design premise is that for the smallest disputes the cost of the ladder exceeds the amount at stake, and that a committee empowered to compromise variations and relieve penalties serves both the taxpayer and the administration better than a four-stage appeal over ten lakh rupees.

## Settlement, abolished and revived: the Commission and the Vishwas schemes

For forty-five years Indian tax law contained a door that the ladder did not: the Income Tax Settlement Commission, established in 1976 on the recommendation of the Wanchoo Committee, under Chapter XIX-A of the Act. A taxpayer could approach it at any stage before abatement, disclose income not disclosed before the Assessing Officer, pay the tax and interest on the disclosure, and seek a final settlement that carried immunity from penalty and prosecution, in exchange for a full and true disclosure that the Commission had power to investigate. The institution divided opinion throughout its life, between those who saw a pragmatic clearing house for complex and stalemate disputes and those who saw a preferential exit for the largest defaulters. The Finance Act 2021 closed it to new business: sub-section (5) of section 245C barred the making of applications on or after 1 February 2021, and section 245AA created an Interim Board for Settlement, of three Chief Commissioner-rank members, to dispose of the applications pending when the door closed. Even the closing produced litigation of the kind this article has learned to expect: because the Finance Act 2021 itself commenced on 1 April 2021, the Madras High Court in Jain Metal Rolling Mills held that applications filed between 1 February and 31 March 2021 were validly pending, and the Supreme Court dismissed the revenue's further proceedings, so the Interim Board received the files of the interregnum.

The abolition of the Commission did not abolish the state's appetite for settlement; it changed the instrument from a standing institution to periodic schemes, and the choice between the two forms is a genuine policy question rather than a succession of accidents. Parliament enacted the Direct Tax Vivad se Vishwas Act 2020, a standalone statute under which a declarant with an appeal, writ petition or special leave petition pending on the specified date could settle by paying the disputed tax, with interest and penalty waived, the fraction and conditions varying with who had filed the pending proceeding and when. The Finance (No. 2) Act 2024 repeated the pattern with a second scheme, effective from 1 October 2024, addressed to proceedings pending on 22 July 2024, including objections pending before the Dispute Resolution Panel and revisions pending under section 264, under which a new appellant settles at the amount of the disputed tax while interest-only and penalty-only disputes settle at defined fractions of those components, with search cases, prosecutions and undisclosed foreign income excluded. The goods and services tax legislated a narrower cousin: section 128A, inserted by the same Finance (No. 2) Act 2024 with effect from 1 November 2024, waives interest and penalty for specified demands for the financial years 2017-18, 2018-19 and 2019-20 where the tax is paid in full.

The choice between a standing settlement institution and periodic schemes is worth stating in its own terms, since Parliament has held both positions within five years. A standing commission settles continuously, accumulates expertise in the pricing of closure, and offers the taxpayer a door that exists when the dispute arises rather than when the state decides to open one; its pathology was the mirror image, a permanent parallel track whose terms the largest and best-advised taxpayers could plan around, converting a concession into an entitlement and an assessment into an opening bid. A periodic scheme answers the planning objection by surprise and sunset, and it harvests the accumulated stock in a single administrative season; its pathology is the expectation it creates, that the taxpayer who declines one scheme and litigates honestly is a fool if a better scheme follows, so that each scheme legislates the demand for the next. The abolition of the Commission and the enactment of two Vishwas schemes in the same half-decade is therefore not a reversal but a relocation of the same unsolved problem, and the taxpayer pricing a settlement offer should understand that the offer's terms reflect the state's estimate of its own appellate backlog at least as much as the merits of any individual case.

A settlement scheme question is a decision, not a topic, and it should be framed as one wherever it is discussed. Settling buys certainty and ends interest exposure, but it ordinarily requires payment of the disputed tax in full, abandons a case that might have been won outright, and is irrevocable once concluded. Holding out preserves the chance of complete success and costs years, professional fees, interest risk, and the deposit or stay payments the ladder demands as it goes. Which course serves a taxpayer depends on the strength of the case on its facts, the age and forum of the pending proceeding, and the taxpayer's liquidity, three variables no general article can weigh for anyone. What a general article can say is structural: a system that must repeatedly offer settlement schemes on concessional terms is disclosing, through its own legislation, that its ordinary appellate throughput is not clearing the stock of disputes, and the frequency of the schemes is evidence about the ladder, not only about the taxpayers who use them.

## Faceless appeals and the Taxpayers' Charter: remedy without presence, promise without right

The two reforms that most altered the experience of the first appeal in recent legislative cycles are the faceless scheme and the Charter, and each has an honest limit that summaries frequently miss.

The faceless design moves the first appeal, described earlier in this article, out of the office and onto a platform. Under the scheme architecture inserted into section 250 in 2020 and notified that year and in revised form in December 2021, appeals are assigned through the National Faceless Appeal Centre to appeal units; the appellant files submissions electronically, receives notices electronically, and is ordinarily not told which officer decides. The stated objects were consistency, the elimination of interface-based malpractice, and the redistribution of work to where capacity exists. Practitioners have pressed a consistent critique, recorded across the professional literature of the scheme's operation: that the removal of the oral hearing removed with it the ability to explain a case in person, to read from a paper book to a decision-maker who follows the argument, and to correct a misunderstanding at the moment it forms, and that written-only procedure advantages the drafted submission over the taxpayer whose case turns on records, trade practice, or the explanation of an honest discrepancy. The revised scheme of 2021 met the critique partway, removing the discretion to refuse a requested personal hearing by video conference; the underlying tension remains, between a system optimised for throughput and anonymity and a litigant's claim that justice in a fact-heavy appeal includes being heard by the person who signs the order.

The scheme's working parts deserve a paragraph, because they determine what a taxpayer actually experiences. On filing, the National Faceless Appeal Centre, and not any territorial Commissioner (Appeals), becomes the addressee and the conduit of the appeal: it assigns the appeal to an appeal unit through an automated allocation system, receives the appellant's submissions, obtains the Assessing Officer's report and rejoinder through the same conduit, and communicates the final order, prepared by the appeal unit and reviewed within the faceless hierarchy before issue. The territorial connection between the taxpayer and the deciding officer, the defining feature of the first appeal for eighty years, is deliberately extinguished; the officer deciding an appeal from a Mumbai assessment may sit anywhere in the country and is unknown to both parties. Defenders of the design point to the elimination of local influence and to load balancing across a national pendency; critics answer that an appeal is not a file-processing exercise, that the review architecture interposes officers between the appellant's words and the signature on the order, and that remand and enhancement decisions taken without any hearing carry the faceless assessment controversy into the appellate tier. The faceless appeal must also be distinguished from the faceless assessment under section 144B, with which it is often confused in summary accounts: the two are separate schemes under separate statutory provisions, the assessment scheme governing the making of the order and the appeal scheme governing its first review, and the pendency figures given in this article count appeals under the second, not assessments under the first.

The Taxpayers' Charter stands at the opposite pole: presence without power. Section 119A, inserted by the Finance Act 2020, empowered the Central Board of Direct Taxes to adopt and declare a taxpayers' charter, and the Charter was released on 13 August 2020 at the launch of the platform built around faceless administration. It sets out fourteen commitments of the department, covering fair and courteous treatment, timely decisions, confidentiality, the presumption of honesty unless there is reason to believe otherwise, a right to representation, mechanisms for accountability and complaint, and a commitment that the department will not act beyond the law, matched against six expectations of taxpayers, including honesty, record keeping and responsiveness. The limit must be stated plainly, because other accounts misstate it: the Charter creates no enforceable right. A commitment breached gives the taxpayer a grievance, to be carried through the department's complaint channels, and not a cause of action; no court will quash an assessment because the Charter was dishonoured, and no compensation follows from its breach. Its function is constitutional in the small sense, a published standard against which administrative behaviour can be measured in public debate and in the department's own accountability reporting. A grievance channel is not a remedy, and the taxpayer who treats the Charter as one will discover the difference at the point of need.


## The tribunalisation tax: why the forums keep being rebuilt

Every Indian statute that creates a specialist tribunal pays a price that this article names the tribunalisation tax: a delay measured in years while the tribunal's appointment, composition and tenure provisions are litigated into conformity with a body of Supreme Court doctrine that has been stable, in its essentials, since 1997. Parliament has paid the price repeatedly, in company law, in the goods and services tax, in the general law of tribunals, and in the redesign of collateral bodies such as the advance rulings authority, without drafting to the known standard in advance. For the taxpayer the tax is not abstract. It is the eight missing years of the goods and services tax tribunal, the decade and a half between the enactment of the company law tribunal framework and its working constitution, and the repeated suspension of forums whose members await appointment while their governing rules are struck down and rewritten.

The doctrine is not obscure, which is what makes the recurring non-compliance a legislative choice rather than a legal accident. In L. Chandra Kumar v Union of India in 1997, a seven-judge bench held that tribunals created under Articles 323A and 323B remain subject to the judicial review of the High Courts under Articles 226 and 227, that judicial review is part of the basic structure of the Constitution, and that tribunals function as courts of first instance whose decisions are then scrutinised by a division bench of the High Court. The constitutional text under which most of these bodies stand was itself an Emergency-era insertion: Articles 323A and 323B, added by the Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act 1976, empowered Parliament and the state legislatures to create tribunals for taxation among other subjects, and to exclude the jurisdiction of courts except the Supreme Court's special leave. The exclusion was the provision the courts would not let stand in its full ambition, and the quarter-century of doctrine that followed is best read as the judiciary negotiating the terms on which tribunalisation would be permitted at all. The corollary, developed across the following decades, is that a tribunal which substitutes for a High Court must possess commensurate independence: security of tenure, qualifications matched to the work, selection in which the judiciary has a real voice, and administration free of the department whose decisions it reviews. In Union of India v R. Gandhi in 2010 the Court upheld the National Company Law Tribunal and its appellate tribunal in principle while prescribing conditions on composition and independence, a history the company law articles of this series carry in detail at the [guide to the NCLT and NCLAT](/2012/05/01/nclt-nclat-explained/). In Madras Bar Association v Union of India in 2014 the Court struck down the National Tax Tribunal Act, Parliament's attempt to transfer the High Courts' substantial-question jurisdiction in tax matters to a national tribunal, holding that the substitution would dilute the judicial function the Constitution reserves. In Rojer Mathew v South Indian Bank in 2019 the Court struck down the Tribunal Rules of 2017, by which the executive had rewritten tenure and service conditions across tribunals through a money bill route. In 2021 the Court, in a further Madras Bar Association judgment, struck down provisions of the Tribunals Reforms Ordinance that reduced tenures and raised minimum ages, and Parliament's response was to re-enact substantially the same provisions in the [Tribunals Reforms Act 2021](/2021/08/15/tribunals-reforms-act-2021-explained/). In November 2025 the Supreme Court struck down the core provisions of that Act, sections 3 to 7, as re-enactments of provisions already held unconstitutional, and directed the constitution of a National Tribunals Commission within four months.

Read as a sequence, the cases describe a legislature and an executive repeatedly testing how much control over tribunal staffing the courts will tolerate, and a judiciary repeatedly returning the same answer. The directed remedy of the November 2025 judgment, a National Tribunals Commission to take appointments, tenure and administration out of the sponsoring ministries' hands, is the logical terminus of the doctrine: if the defect in every generation of tribunal legislation has been the executive's retention of the levers that independence forbids, the cure is to remove the levers to a body designed for the purpose, on the analogy of the commissions that service the judiciary itself. Whether the Commission is constituted within the four months the Court allowed, and with what composition, will determine whether the tribunalisation tax is finally retired or merely invoiced to another decade; the record assembled in this article does not justify confidence either way, and the prudent reader will treat the direction as one more entry in the sequence rather than its conclusion. The costs fall where costs in tax administration always fall, on the parties waiting for a forum. Vacancies persist while appointment rules are contested; members serve shortened tenures under provisions liable to be annulled; benches are constituted late, reconstituted, or not constituted at all. The income tax Tribunal's relative calm is the exception that proves the analysis: constituted in 1941, placed under the Law Ministry, staffed by a judicial and accountant membership with a settled convention of leadership by judges, it predates the constitutional contest and was designed inside the expectations the courts later articulated. The tribunals designed after 1997 were designed against those expectations, and paid for it. The series thesis thread of this article can be stated as a general rule: in Indian tax law the existence of a remedy in the statute book and the existence of a forum able to deliver it are two different facts, connected by administrative diligence rather than by law, and the second fact is the one the record keeps interrupting.

## The record: what the pendency figures show

A remedy's value is a function of time, and the time dimension of Indian tax appeals is documented in the government's own answers. In the Rajya Sabha, Unstarred Question No. 1038, answered on 9 December 2025 by the Minister of State for Finance, Pankaj Chaudhary, the administration placed the pendency of income tax appeals at the end of the financial year 2024-25 on record. Appeals pending before the first appellate authorities, the Commissioners (Appeals) and Joint Commissioners (Appeals), numbered 5,39,863, involving disputed amounts of Rs 16.75 lakh crore. Appeals pending before the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal numbered 22,960, involving Rs 3.64 lakh crore. Appeals pending before the High Courts numbered 34,486, involving Rs 5.65 lakh crore, and appeals pending before the Supreme Court numbered 6,338, involving Rs 25,403 crore. Across the four levels, more than twenty-five lakh crore rupees of income tax demand stood locked in dispute at the close of that financial year, a sum of the same order as a significant fraction of the Union Budget, suspended between the department's assessments and the forums' calendars.

The distribution matters as much as the totals. The stock concentrates overwhelmingly at the first rung, which is the rung staffed by the department itself, conducted without personal interface, and reached within thirty days of an assessment. The Tribunal, by contrast, carries a pendency smaller by an order of magnitude, and the constitutional courts carry small dockets of high value. A system in which the first departmental appeal holds more than half a million cases has not failed at adjudication in the courts; it has failed at the point where its own officers review its own officers, and the repeated settlement schemes described earlier in this article are the administration's periodic acknowledgement of that arithmetic. Disposal figures at each level respond to the same pressure: the first appellate authorities dispose of appeals in the lakhs across a normal year, and the stock nonetheless persists, because the inflow of new assessments that generate appeals refills what disposal drains.

Time compounds the stock in a way the raw pendency understates. An appeal decided at the first rung after several years produces, where the taxpayer succeeds in part, a revised demand and frequently a fresh dispute about the giving of effect to the appellate order; an appeal decided at the Tribunal produces, where the department carries it upward, a High Court proceeding in which the question framed may take years to reach hearing, during which the demand's status is governed by stay orders of varying vintage; and the taxpayer who wins at every rung recovers the deposit or the tax paid with interest computed at the modest statutory rate, having borne the dispute's cost in the currency of a business's scarcest resource, which is neither money nor patience but the management attention that a live tax dispute taxes continuously. The figures also carry a selection effect that flatters no one: the appeals that remain pending longest are disproportionately those in which the amount is largest or the question hardest, so the average age of the stock rises even where disposals are brisk, and a pendency snapshot taken in any year will always look worse than the flow through the system in that year. None of this excuses the reservoir at the first rung; it explains why reforms addressed only to the flow, faster disposal targets, monetary thresholds, settlement seasons, leave the stock standing.

The quality of the first rung aggravates the quantity. A standing criticism in appellate commentary is that first appellate orders confirm assessments at rates that suggest review within a department behaves differently from review across a boundary, and that the Tribunal's docket is correspondingly swollen with cases that a genuinely distanced first appeal would have ended. The faceless reorganisation was presented in part as an answer, since allocation severs the territorial link between the assessing and appellate chains; whether anonymised departmental review differs in outcome from territorial departmental review is a question on which the published record offers pendency and disposal numbers more readily than outcome analysis, and this article does not pretend otherwise. What can be said on the figures alone is structural: a first rung carrying 5,39,863 pending appeals at the close of the financial year 2024-25, against a Tribunal carrying 22,960, is not a filter at the head of the ladder. It is a reservoir, and every remedy described in this article operates downstream of it.

The department polices the upper rungs of its own litigation through monetary discipline. By circular issued in September 2024, effective 17 September 2024, the Central Board of Direct Taxes raised the tax-effect thresholds below which its officers do not file or pursue appeals: Rs 60 lakh for appeals to the Tribunal, Rs 2 crore for appeals to the High Courts, and Rs 5 crore for appeals to the Supreme Court, subject to exceptions for cases involving organised tax evasion, information from enforcement agencies, and questions of law of recurring importance. The thresholds are an admission, in administrative form, that a large share of departmental appeals cost more to litigate than they can return, and they operate as the single most effective docket-control device in the system, more effective than any exhortation to speed. No equivalent discipline restrains the inflow at the first rung, where the decision to assess is the officer's own.

## After the order: giving effect, remand and the second round

An appellate order is not self-executing, and the interval between winning an appeal and receiving its benefit is a stage of the remedy that summary accounts omit. When the Commissioner (Appeals) or the Tribunal decides in the taxpayer's favour, the Assessing Officer must give effect to the order: recompute the income and the tax, cancel or reduce the demand notice, and release or refund sums that became refundable, with the interest the statute allows on refunds. Delay at this stage is a documented grievance of the system, and the taxpayer's instruments against it are administrative escalation and, in a clear case, the writ court, which has not hesitated to direct that appellate orders be given effect within a stated time. The department, for its part, is not bound to accept the order as the end: it may appeal upward within its own periods, and the giving of effect proceeds in the shadow of that possibility, with the taxpayer's refund liable to be withheld in defined circumstances where a further proceeding is pending and the officer records reasons and obtains the prescribed approval, a withholding power that converts some appellate victories into a further wait.

Remand is the other outcome that disappoints appellants who mistake it for victory. Both the first appellate authority and the Tribunal may set aside an assessment or a part of it and restore the matter to the Assessing Officer for a fresh decision on the point remanded, ordinarily where the original order was passed without the enquiry or the hearing the law required, or where the evidence admitted at the appellate stage needs testing by the officer who can test it. A remand restarts the assessment clock on the remanded point, subject to the limitation the statute sets for completing a set-aside assessment, and the second assessment that emerges is itself appealable on the full ladder, so that a dispute's biography can contain two complete climbs. The case law disciplines the power: a remand is not to be ordered for the department's convenience where the record was complete and the failure was of argument rather than of enquiry, and the Tribunal in particular is expected to decide what it can decide on the record before it. For the taxpayer, the practical lesson is that the appeal should be argued to win on the record, not to expose the order's defects so completely that a fresh enquiry is the natural cure; the distinction between an annulment that ends the matter and a set aside that renews it is the difference between the two most common favourable outcomes on the ladder, and it is decided by the framing of the grounds as much as by the merits.

## Common mistakes that lose winnable appeals

The mistakes that end tax appeals are rarely mistakes of law. They are mistakes of sequence, timing and forum, and they repeat across the record with a regularity that justifies collecting them.

The first is counting the period from the wrong event, from the date of the order rather than its communication, or from an intimation seen late on a portal; limitation runs from the statutory starting event, and a condonation application is a confession that the count failed. The second is filing in one system by the other system's rules: three months carried into an income tax appeal that allows thirty days, or a deposit assumed where none is required for admission while the stay application that actually protects the taxpayer goes unfiled. The third is neglecting the admission conditions, filing a first income tax appeal without paying the tax due on the returned income, or filing a goods and services tax appeal without arranging the deposit, so the appeal is defective before its merits are read. The fourth is treating the tribunal stage casually, on the theory that the High Court will correct it: the High Court corrects law, not facts, and evidence not placed before the Tribunal is ordinarily evidence lost. The fifth is assuming protection that was never granted, that an appeal suspends recovery, that the Charter suspends an assessment, that a settlement declaration filed is a settlement concluded. A further mistake belongs to the parallel routes: treating an advance ruling as insurance rather than as a binding answer, since a ruling binds the applicant who obtained it, and an unfavourable ruling forecloses the transaction structure it condemns; applying to the Dispute Resolution Committee outside its eligibility fence, where the income or variation ceilings are exceeded or the order rests on search or treaty information, so the application fails at admission after the appeal period has been spent elsewhere; and treating a settlement declaration as reversible, when the scheme's consequence, payment made and proceeding closed, is designed to be final against both sides. The sixth is ignoring the department's own doors: the memorandum of cross-objections unfiled within thirty days, the revision window under section 264 unconsidered until its year runs out, the rectification of a patent error pursued by a full appeal instead. A seventh, quieter mistake is the destruction of the record itself. The taxpayer's protection in a limitation dispute is documentary: the order, the proof and date of its service, the acknowledgement of filing, the challan evidencing any deposit paid, and the computation by which the period was counted. Keeping that set complete and retrievable, in an [encrypted records vault built for exactly this kind of documentary proof](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), is the unglamorous precaution on which the glamorous arguments later depend.

Where the taxpayer cannot fund representation at all, the legal services framework is the residual route, and this series treats it at the [guide to the Legal Services Authorities Act 1987](/2021/06/15/legal-services-authorities-act-1987/), under which legal services authorities provide free legal services to eligible persons and organise Lok Adalats; the practical caution is that tax forums run on limitation, and assistance sought after the period expires arrives after the door it was meant to open.

## Who may argue: representation before the forums

A word belongs to the human machinery of the ladder, because a remedy is only as usable as the representation it permits. Before the first appellate authority and the Tribunal, an appellant may appear in person, through an authorised representative who is a legal practitioner, a chartered accountant, or a person otherwise qualified and authorised under the rules governing representation, or through a relative or employee in the situations the statute permits. The department appears by its own officers: the Assessing Officer or a departmental representative before the Tribunal, and counsel instructed on the record before the constitutional courts. The goods and services tax statute mirrors the pattern, permitting appearance through a legal practitioner, a chartered or cost accountant, a company secretary, or a goods and services tax practitioner enrolled for the purpose. The design consequence is that tax appeals are, at the first two rungs, among the least lawyer-bound proceedings in Indian law, and a substantial share of tribunal advocacy has always been done by accountants arguing from the record rather than by counsel arguing from precedent. That accessibility is a genuine feature of the system and should be weighed in any account of its costs: the door fees are small, the representation rules are permissive, and the forums sit in the commercial centres where the taxpayers are. The expenses that deter appellants are not the price of admission but the price of duration, and they are paid to time rather than to the state.

## The transition ahead: appeals under the Income-tax Act 2025

One forward fact belongs in this article, stated at the depth the pillar article on the 1961 Act has already fixed. The Income-tax Act 2025 operates from 1 April 2026 and reorganises the statute under which every previous period was assessed. As the pillar article records, the appellate design survives the reorganisation: the successor code restates the ladder with the same first appellate authorities, the same Appellate Tribunal, and the same law-only appeals to the High Court and the Supreme Court, in renumbered form, while its savings keep charge, assessment, appeal, penalty and prosecution for every previous year ending on or before 31 March 2026 inside the provisions of the 1961 Act. The practical consequence for the reader of this article is that nothing in the ladder described here becomes obsolete on the successor Act's commencement: the appeals of the coming years will be, in overwhelming proportion, appeals under the old Act for old years, decided by the same forums under the same periods and fees, and the new code's corresponding routes will govern disputes that have not yet arisen. Whether Parliament used the reorganisation to draft the tribunal provisions to the constitutional standard the courts have articulated, rather than leaving them to the next round of litigation, is a question the successor Act's first years will answer, and this series will record the answer where it belongs, in the pillar and the tribunal articles that own it.

## The verdict: a strong ladder with unreliable ground beneath it

The Indian taxpayer's appellate rights, read as legislation, are among the more complete remedial codes a revenue statute anywhere contains: an internal appeal with power to annul, an independent tribunal deciding facts finally, law-only appeals to constitutional courts, parallel panels for pricing disputes, rectification for patent error, revision in both directions, advance rulings to prevent disputes, a committee to divert small ones, and a deposit bargain that trades a fraction of the demand for an automatic stay on the indirect side. The defects of the system are not in this code. They are in the ground beneath it: forums constituted years late, appointments litigated in cycles, a first rung holding over half a million appeals, and a state that must periodically discount its own demands through settlement schemes because the ladder cannot clear them. The tribunalisation tax, the years paid while composition provisions are fought into conformity with doctrine settled in 1997, is the recurring price of building the forum after the right instead of with it.

There is a final asymmetry the verdict should record, because it conditions every reform proposal in this field. The department litigates as a repeat player with salaried counsel, no deposit burden of its own, monetary thresholds it sets for itself, and the power to wait; the taxpayer litigates as a single-case player with borrowed capital, interest running, and a business to run in the shadow of the dispute. Procedural design choices that look neutral between the parties, the length of the Tribunal's queue, the availability of a hearing, the percentage of a deposit, are not neutral in effect under that asymmetry, and the strongest justification for the institutions this article has described at their best, a genuinely independent tribunal deciding facts quickly and finally, is that they are the one point in the system where the asymmetry narrows. The history of the last three decades is the history of that point being promised in statute and postponed in administration, which is why a series about legislation must end an article about remedies with an observation about the record rather than about the text.

For the taxpayer holding an adverse order, the operational conclusion of this article is the One Test restated. Identify the system, because the two ladders differ in period, deposit and forum. Count from communication, not from the date on the order. Pay what the rung demands, a fee in hundreds of rupees on the direct side, a deposit in tens of per cent on the indirect side, and treat the stay of the balance as a separate application wherever the statute does not grant it automatically. Build the factual record at the first two rungs, because no forum above the tribunal will build it later. And preserve the documents, since in a system measured in years the file is the one part of the remedy entirely within the taxpayer's control. The right of appeal exists in the statute; whether a forum exists to hear it is a separate question, answered only by the record, and the record of the last three decades instructs every taxpayer to file early, file in the right system, and keep the proof.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you appeal an income tax assessment order?

An appeal against an assessment order lies to the Commissioner (Appeals), or to the Joint Commissioner (Appeals) where the case falls in the classes assigned to that authority, under sections 246A and 246 of the Income-tax Act 1961. It is filed electronically in Form 35 within thirty days of service of the notice of demand, with a fee of Rs 250, Rs 500 or Rs 1,000 graded by the assessed income. The appeal is not admitted unless the tax due on the income returned has been paid, and a late appeal may be admitted where sufficient cause is shown.

### Q: What does the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal decide?

The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, constituted under section 252 of the Income-tax Act, hears appeals under section 253 against orders of the first appellate authorities and against assessments passed on Dispute Resolution Panel directions. It decides questions of fact and of law, and its findings of fact are final, binding the High Court above it unless a finding is perverse or rests on no evidence. Under section 254 it may confirm, vary or reverse the order appealed against, restore a matter for fresh examination, and stay recovery within a ceiling of three hundred and sixty-five days. It cannot strike down the Act that created it.

### Q: What can you appeal to a High Court in a tax matter?

An appeal lies to the High Court under section 260A of the Income-tax Act only where the case involves a substantial question of law, and it must be filed within one hundred and twenty days of receipt of the Tribunal's order. The taxpayer may appeal the Tribunal's construction of a charging or deduction provision, its treatment of a treaty, or a ruling infected by perversity, but may not ask the Court to reweigh evidence or disturb findings of fact. The Court formulates the question, hears the parties on it, and answers it; the corresponding goods and services tax route is section 117, with a period of one hundred and eighty days.

### Q: How do you appeal a GST order?

An appeal against an order of an adjudicating authority under the goods and services tax lies to the Appellate Authority under section 107 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act 2017, filed in Form GST APL-01 within three months of communication of the order, with up to one further month condonable for sufficient cause. The appeal is entertained only on payment of the admitted dues in full and a pre-deposit of ten per cent of the remaining disputed tax, on which payment recovery of the balance stands stayed. A second appeal lies to the Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal under section 112.

### Q: What pre-deposit must you pay before a tax appeal?

The deposit depends on the system and the rung, and any single figure quoted without both is wrong. Under the goods and services tax, ten per cent of the disputed tax, subject to a statutory ceiling, must be deposited before the first appeal, and a further ten per cent of the remaining disputed tax before the tribunal appeal, taking the cumulative deposit to twenty per cent. Under the legacy customs and excise statutes the graded deposits were seven and a half per cent at the first appeal and ten per cent at the tribunal, capped at ten crore rupees. Income tax appeals require no deposit for admission; the twenty per cent figure there is the administrative standard for a stay of recovery, not a condition of filing.

### Q: What does the Taxpayers' Charter promise?

The Taxpayers' Charter, adopted by the Central Board of Direct Taxes under section 119A of the Income-tax Act and released on 13 August 2020, records fourteen commitments of the department, including fair, courteous and reasonable treatment, timely decisions, confidentiality of information, the presumption of honesty unless there is reason to believe otherwise, the right to representation, and mechanisms of accountability and complaint, matched by six expectations of taxpayers, including honesty, record keeping and timely response. The Charter creates no enforceable legal right: a commitment breached gives rise to a grievance through the department's complaint channels, not to a remedy in court, and no assessment is quashed for breach of it.

### Q: What happened to the Income Tax Settlement Commission?

The Settlement Commission, created in 1976 under Chapter XIX-A of the Income-tax Act on the Wanchoo Committee's recommendation, allowed a taxpayer to settle a case on full disclosure, with possible immunity from penalty and prosecution. The Finance Act 2021 abolished the route prospectively: section 245C(5) barred new applications on or after 1 February 2021, and an Interim Board for Settlement of three Chief Commissioner-rank members was created under section 245AA to dispose of pending applications. The Madras High Court in Jain Metal Rolling Mills read the cut-off as 31 March 2021, because the Finance Act itself commenced on 1 April 2021, and the Supreme Court dismissed the revenue's further proceedings.

### Q: Why do tax tribunals take so long to be constituted?

Because their appointment, composition and tenure provisions have repeatedly been drafted in terms the Supreme Court then strikes down. The governing doctrine, settled in L. Chandra Kumar in 1997, requires a tribunal that substitutes for a High Court to match its independence, with judicial participation in selection and security of tenure. Parliament's successive attempts, from the National Tax Tribunal struck down in 2014 through the Tribunal Rules struck down in 2019 and the Tribunals Reforms Act 2021 whose core provisions were struck down in November 2025, each restarted the process, leaving vacancies unfilled and, in the goods and services tax, leaving the tribunal unconstituted for over eight years. A National Tribunals Commission was directed in 2025 to break the cycle.

### Q: What is the Dispute Resolution Panel and who can use it?

The Dispute Resolution Panel under section 144C of the Income-tax Act is a collegium of three Commissioners that reviews a draft assessment order before it becomes final. It is available only to an eligible assessee: a person whose proposed variation in income arises from a Transfer Pricing Officer's order under section 92CA, and any foreign company. Objections are filed in Form 35A within thirty days of receipt of the draft order. The Panel issues binding directions within nine months from the end of the month in which the draft order was forwarded, the Assessing Officer completes the assessment in conformity with them, and the resulting order is appealable directly to the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal.

### Q: What counts as a substantial question of law in a tax appeal?

A substantial question of law is a question about the meaning or reach of the law that is neither trivial nor already settled: the construction of a charging or exemption provision where two readings genuinely compete, the effect of a tax treaty against the Act, the retrospectivity of an amendment, or a challenge to a finding of fact so perverse that the law treats it as no finding at all. It is not a disagreement with the Tribunal's weighing of evidence, however strongly felt. The High Court must be satisfied the question arises and is substantial, formulates it at admission, and confines the hearing to the question formulated, although a party may argue that a further question arises.

### Q: Can the department recover a disputed tax demand while your appeal is pending?

Yes, unless a stay is obtained, because an appeal does not itself suspend recovery. While the first appeal is pending, the assessee may apply under section 220(6) of the Income-tax Act to be treated as not in default; the governing office memoranda standardised stay on payment of fifteen per cent of the disputed demand in 2016 and twenty per cent from 31 July 2017, and the Supreme Court held in Principal Commissioner of Income Tax v LG Electronics India in 2018 that the percentage does not fetter the authority's discretion to accept less. At the tribunal stage, section 254(2A) permits a stay capped in aggregate at three hundred and sixty-five days. Recovery otherwise proceeds, including by set-off of refunds under section 245 after written intimation.

### Q: What replaced the Authority for Advance Rulings?

The Finance Act 2021 replaced the Authority for Advance Rulings with a Board for Advance Rulings under section 245-OB of the Income-tax Act, with effect from 1 September 2021, and pending applications were transferred to it. Each Board consists of two members of at least Chief Commissioner rank, sitting at Delhi and Mumbai, and applications proceed under the e-Advance Rulings Scheme 2022. Two design changes matter: the judge-led Authority gave way to serving senior officers of the department, and a Board ruling, unlike an Authority ruling, is appealable to the High Court under section 245W. Applicants' concerns about independence were recorded in the Finance (No. 2) Act 2024, which opened a window to withdraw transferred applications.

### Q: What is the Dispute Resolution Committee for small taxpayers?

The Dispute Resolution Committee under section 245MA of the Income-tax Act, operating through the e-Dispute Resolution Scheme 2022 notified on 5 April 2022, lets a small taxpayer seek a negotiated disposal instead of an appeal. Eligibility requires returned income not exceeding fifty lakh rupees and aggregate variations not exceeding ten lakh rupees, and orders founded on search, survey or tax information exchange material are excluded. Committees sit for all eighteen Principal Chief Commissioner regions. The Committee may modify the variations in the specified order and may reduce or waive a penalty imposable or grant immunity from prosecution, and it is directed to decide within six months from the end of the month of admission.

### Q: Do you get a personal hearing in a faceless tax appeal?

Under the Faceless Appeal Scheme 2020, notified on 25 September 2020, the first appeal was conducted in writing through the National Faceless Appeal Centre, and a personal hearing by video conference depended on approval within the scheme's hierarchy. The Faceless Appeal Scheme 2021, notified on 28 December 2021, altered that position by removing the discretion to refuse a hearing requested by the appellant, so that a hearing by video conference follows on request. Practitioners have nonetheless maintained the critique that written-only procedure removes the ability to explain a case in person to the officer who decides it, and that the quality of a hearing held with an appeal unit, rather than with a named appellate authority, remains the scheme's unresolved question.

### Q: What does a Vivad se Vishwas settlement require you to pay?

Under the Direct Tax Vivad se Vishwas Act 2020, and again under the scheme enacted by the Finance (No. 2) Act 2024 for proceedings pending on 22 July 2024, a declarant settles by paying the disputed tax in full, on which interest and penalty are waived; disputes confined to interest, penalty or fee settle at defined fractions of those amounts, and disputes the department had lost below and carried upward settle at a reduced fraction of the tax. Search cases, prosecution cases and undisclosed foreign income are excluded. Payment concludes the proceeding and is not reopened. The scheme opened on 1 October 2024, and settling trades the chance of complete success on appeal for certainty and an end to interest exposure.

### Q: Can the Commissioner (Appeals) increase your tax demand?

Yes. Section 251 of the Income-tax Act empowers the Commissioner (Appeals), and the Joint Commissioner (Appeals) exercising the same jurisdiction, in disposing of an appeal against an assessment to confirm, reduce, enhance or annul the assessment, and in a penalty appeal to vary the penalty upward or downward as well. Enhancement is not a formality exercised without warning: the appellate authority must give the appellant a reasonable opportunity of showing cause against the enhancement, and an enhancement founded on a source of income or a ground outside the appeal's scope exceeds the power. The possibility of enhancement is a real, if infrequently exercised, cost of the first appeal, and it is one reason the grounds of appeal are framed with care.

### Q: What happens if you miss the deadline for filing a tax appeal?

The appeal is liable not to be admitted, but most rungs carry a condonation power. The first appellate authority may admit an income tax appeal after the thirty days where sufficient cause for the delay is shown under section 249(3), and the Tribunal holds a corresponding power for its sixty-day, or, since 1 October 2024, two-month period. Sufficient cause is judged on the explanation offered, with documented causes such as late service, portal failure or professional error treated more favourably than silence. The goods and services tax first appeal is stricter: the Appellate Authority may condone no more than one month beyond the three months allowed. A missed deadline should therefore be met with a prompt filing and a specific, evidenced explanation, not with delay compounded.

### Q: Can the Income Tax Department appeal against an order in your favour?

Yes. Under section 253(2) of the Income-tax Act the Principal Commissioner or Commissioner may direct the Assessing Officer to appeal to the Tribunal against an order of the first appellate authority, and the department may carry questions of law onward to the High Court and the Supreme Court as the taxpayer may. Two disciplines shape the practice: the taxpayer served with notice of the department's appeal may file a memorandum of cross-objections within thirty days, treated as an appeal of his own; and the Central Board of Direct Taxes bars departmental appeals below tax-effect thresholds fixed by circular, raised in September 2024 to Rs 60 lakh for the Tribunal, Rs 2 crore for the High Courts and Rs 5 crore for the Supreme Court, subject to exceptions.

### Q: How many income tax appeals are pending in India?

On the government's own figures, placed before the Rajya Sabha in Unstarred Question No. 1038 answered on 9 December 2025 by the Minister of State for Finance, Pankaj Chaudhary, the pendency at the end of the financial year 2024-25 stood at 5,39,863 appeals before the first appellate authorities, the Commissioners (Appeals) and Joint Commissioners (Appeals), involving Rs 16.75 lakh crore; 22,960 appeals before the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal involving Rs 3.64 lakh crore; 34,486 before the High Courts involving Rs 5.65 lakh crore; and 6,338 before the Supreme Court involving Rs 25,403 crore. In all, more than twenty-five lakh crore rupees of income tax demand stood locked in dispute across the four levels at that date.

### Q: What can the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal not decide?

Three limits define the Tribunal. It cannot question the constitutional validity of the Income-tax Act or of any provision in it, because a tribunal created by a statute cannot annul its creator; a vires challenge must go to a High Court by writ. It cannot travel beyond the subject matter of the appeal before it, deciding the case of a person who has not appealed or reopening years that are not in issue, save within the recognised scope of the appellant's grounds and the respondent's cross-objections. And it cannot enhance an assessment beyond confirming or varying the order under appeal in the appellant's own case in a manner the statute does not authorise, a boundary that distinguishes its wide appellate powers under section 254 from a fresh assessing jurisdiction.
