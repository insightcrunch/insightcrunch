Ask a room of educated readers what media theory in the Marxist tradition says about the media and a single answer usually comes back, delivered with the confidence of a settled fact. The media, on this account, are owned by the powerful, filled with messages that serve the powerful, and absorbed by audiences too passive to notice what is being done to them. The answer has the shape of a theory and the texture of a cartoon. It misdescribes the tradition it claims to summarise in almost every particular that matters, because the tradition it names is not one theory but a family of them, divided against itself on the very question the cartoon treats as closed: whether audiences are acted upon by media or act upon what media give them. Media theory in the Marxist line is better pictured as an argument that has been running for the better part of a century than as a doctrine that was settled at some founding moment and handed down intact.

That internal argument is the subject of this article, and it is also the article's organising device. The tradition split, over the second half of the twentieth century, into two wings that studied different objects with different methods and frequently talked past one another. One wing, the political economy of communication, studied production: who owns media institutions, how they are financed, which laws and markets constrain them, and what those arrangements do to the range of voices that reach a public. The other wing, British cultural studies, studied reception and meaning: how texts are made sense of, how audiences decode what they watch and read, and how interpretation varies with the social position of the interpreter. Between the wings stands an older and darker inheritance, the culture industry analysis of the Frankfurt School, and off to one side a short lived but consequential episode, the screen theory of the 1970s, which asked not what audiences do with films but what films do to the spectator's very sense of self. A reader who can place these strands, state the quarrel among them, and then place the rival traditions that the whole family has argued against, pluralism, uses and gratifications, cultivation research, agenda setting, medium theory, and the postmodern dissolution of representation, possesses a working map of media theory as a contested field rather than a list of names. Supplying that map is the work of the pages that follow.

## The two wings of Marxist media theory

The division into two wings is not a courtesy extended to a messy history. It is the single most explanatory fact about the field, because the famous weaknesses attributed to Marxist media theory attach almost always to one wing and not to the other, and defences mounted on behalf of one wing are routinely mistaken for defences of the whole. The structural wing asks a question about the conditions under which messages are produced. Its evidence is institutional: share registers, advertising ledgers, licensing regimes, the career paths of editors, the economics of distribution. The interpretive wing asks a question about what happens to messages once they circulate. Its evidence is textual and ethnographic: the internal organisation of a programme, the talk of viewers in their living rooms, the fan letter, the romance reader's account of her reading. Neither body of evidence can be converted into the other, and a claim established on one side of the divide cannot be refuted by evidence gathered on the other, a point so elementary that the field's long quarrel is largely the record of its being forgotten.

Name the division and a rule follows, the rule this article will use throughout and will ask the reader to carry away. Call it the two-wing rule. Marxist media theory contains a structural wing that studies production and ownership and an interpretive wing that studies meaning and reception; they answer different questions, and most criticisms of the tradition succeed only against whichever wing the critic has chosen to describe. The rule sounds like a defensive manoeuvre, and a reader is entitled to suspect it of being one, so its test should be stated plainly. If the rule is sound, then the standard objections should sort themselves: objections drawn from audience research should land on claims the interpretive wing itself rejected, and objections drawn from market diversity should land on crude versions of the structural wing rather than on its careful statements. The later sections of this article put exactly that sorting to work, objection by objection, and the reader can judge whether the rule earns its keep or merely redistributes embarrassment.

### What is Marxist media theory?

A family of approaches treating media as shaped by ownership, funding, and the reproduction of social relations rather than as a neutral marketplace of ideas. It divides into a political-economy wing studying structure and ownership, and a cultural-studies wing studying meaning, ideology, and audience reception.

The family resemblance among these approaches is real even where the quarrels are bitter. Every strand treats the media as part of the material organisation of a society rather than as a glass pane through which events are viewed, and every strand supposes that the organisation of communication is connected, in ways that need specifying rather than assuming, to the organisation of power. What the strands disagree about is where the connection does its work: in the boardroom and the balance sheet, in the structure of the text, in the act of interpretation, or in the formation of the viewing subject. That disagreement, conducted over decades in journals, conferences, and competing textbooks, is not a scandal to be apologised for. It is what a living research tradition looks like from inside, and the summaries that iron it out into a single position deprive the reader of the only version of the field worth having, the version in which its parts can be used.

The signposting matters for a second reason. This series treats the parent concept in its own place, and the reader who wants the general theory of ideology before its media applications should begin with the [marxist theory of ideology guide](/2012/02/01/marxist-theory-of-ideology-guide/), which owns that question. What follows assumes only the outline: that beliefs and representations are connected to social relations, that the connection is neither a simple reflection nor an arbitrary one, and that media institutions are among the places where the connection is manufactured. With that much in hand, the genealogy can start where the tradition itself starts, with a handful of remarks in Marx that later generations built into edifices Marx never drew.

### Where did Marxist media theory come from?

It grew from Marx's claim that the class controlling material production also controls the production and distribution of ideas, extended in the twentieth century by émigré critics of mass culture, by scholars of press ownership, and by a Birmingham research centre that turned toward audiences and everyday meaning making in Britain and beyond.

## The inheritance: Marx on the production of ideas

Marx wrote no theory of the media, and honesty about that absence is the right beginning. What he left was a set of remarks about ideas and their production, scattered through works written for other purposes, which his successors mined with varying degrees of fidelity. The most quoted passage stands in The German Ideology, composed in the middle 1840s and unpublished in his lifetime, where Marx and Engels argue that the class which controls the means of material production controls at the same time the means of mental production, so that the ideas of those who lack the means of mental production are on the whole subject to it. The famous compressed sentence, that the ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas, has been treated by admirers as a finished theory of ideology and by detractors as a finished confession of crude determinism. It is neither. It is a claim about the distribution of the capacity to produce and circulate ideas, closer in spirit to a remark about printing presses and their owners than to a psychology of belief, and the whole subsequent history of the tradition can be read as an argument about how much more than that the sentence can be made to bear.

A second inheritance came from the analysis of the commodity. When Marx described how products of labour, once they enter exchange, take on a life and a set of relations that conceal the human relations that produced them, he supplied a template that media theorists would later apply to cultural goods: the film, the programme, the newspaper appear as self sufficient objects, their conditions of manufacture invisible, their meanings seemingly intrinsic. A third inheritance was journalistic rather than theoretical. Marx spent years as a working journalist and editor, quarrelled with censors in the Rhineland, wrote voluminously for the New York Tribune, and knew the economics of the press from the inside, the dependence on proprietors, advertisers, and the price of paper. The tradition's suspicion that a newspaper is a business before it is a forum was not an inference from first principles. It descended from an editor's experience of watching a journal's line bend toward the interests that kept it alive.

Between Marx's death and the emergence of anything recognisable as media theory, the connecting figures are those who asked what mass culture was doing to the societies that produced it. Georg Lukács, whose account of reification described consciousness itself taking the shape of the commodity form, gave later critics a way to talk about culture as a field in which social relations appear as relations among things. Antonio Gramsci, writing in a fascist prison, shifted the question from domination to hegemony, from rule by force to leadership by consent organised through the institutions of civil society, among which newspapers, schools, and churches stood prominently. The applied consequences of that shift for news are treated in the companion piece on [ideology and news media](/2012/04/15/ideology-and-news-media/), and Gramsci's concept recurs throughout what follows, because the interpretive wing of media theory is unthinkable without it. What matters at this stage is the pattern the inheritances set: a claim about who controls the means of producing ideas, a suspicion that cultural objects conceal their conditions, and an insistence that consent is manufactured rather than spontaneous. Each wing of the later tradition radicalised one part of that inheritance and neglected the rest, which is one reason the wings came to look like strangers.

## The Frankfurt inheritance: the culture industry, signposted

The first fully formed Marxist account of mass media arrived from exile. Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer, directors of the Frankfurt Institute for Social Research who had fled Nazi Germany for the United States, composed Dialectic of Enlightenment during the war years and published it in 1947. Its central chapter argued that film, radio, magazines, and popular music had been absorbed into a single culture industry, in which cultural goods are manufactured according to the logic of the factory: standardised in structure, differentiated in surface detail, and designed to reconcile their consumers to the existing order by occupying the leisure hours that might otherwise be turned against it. The argument was not that individual works carry propaganda messages. It was subtler and bleaker. Standardisation trains perception itself, teaching audiences to recognise the formula and take pleasure in recognition, so that amusement becomes the prolongation of work by other means and genuine experience withers for want of exercise.

This article signposts the culture industry rather than developing it, because the Frankfurt School has its own full treatment in this series, the [frankfurt school complete guide](/2015/06/01/frankfurt-school-complete-guide/), which owns the school's history and internal disputes. Three things nevertheless need saying here, because the culture industry's position inside the later map is persistently misread. First, the analysis belongs to neither wing of the later split. It was written before the split existed, and it combines a political economy of cultural production with a theory of reception, indeed with a theory of damaged perception, in a single argument. Second, its account of audiences is the strongest version ever offered of the claim the cultural studies wing would form itself against: the culture industry's consumer is not merely passive but actively trained into passivity, a distinction that matters when the objection from audience research is weighed later. Third, the Frankfurt analysis was an émigré's diagnosis of American commercial culture and of the fascist culture it had escaped, formed at a moment when radio demagogues and studio systems made the picture of centralised cultural power unusually plausible. Detached from that moment and repeated as a timeless verdict on all popular culture, it hardens into the caricature its critics attack. Restored to its moment, it remains the tradition's most formidable statement of what commercial standardisation does to form.

One boundary case deserves a sentence of its own. Walter Benjamin, associated with the Institute though never a member of it, answered the culture industry thesis from inside the same milieu with his essay on art in the age of its technological reproducibility, arguing that mechanical reproduction could shatter the aura of the unique artwork and open cultural goods to new, potentially critical uses. Adorno received the essay coldly. The exchange matters for the map because it shows the tradition, at its very origin, already arguing with itself about whether mass reproduction degrades or liberates, a first rehearsal for the quarrel between the wings that the rest of this article describes.

## The structural wing: the political economy of communication

The political economy of communication emerged as a named field in the 1960s and 1970s, drawing on the Marxist analysis of ownership and on an older Canadian tradition of inquiry into staples, empires, and communication associated with Harold Innis. Its founding gesture was to refuse the question that dominated media research of its day, the question of effects, and to substitute a prior one. Before asking what media do to people, ask what kind of institutions media are: who owns them, what they sell, to whom, under what rules, and with what consequences for the range of what can be said. Herbert Schiller's studies of American communications empire, beginning with Mass Communications and American Empire in 1969, traced the entanglement of American media corporations with military and commercial expansion. Armand Mattelart, working between France and Chile, examined the international flow of media products and the cultural dimensions of dependency. In Britain, Graham Murdock and Peter Golding set out the programme in essays of the 1970s, arguing that the production of culture under capitalism is subject to the same imperatives of accumulation as any other industry, and that a sociology of media which ignored ownership had abandoned explanation at the point where it should begin.

### What does the political economy wing actually study?

It studies who owns media institutions, how they are funded, which regulations govern them, and how those arrangements constrain what can be produced and distributed. Its methods are institutional and historical rather than textual, and its characteristic findings concern asymmetry: some voices enter public circulation with resources that others simply cannot match.

The wing's method follows from its question. It reads balance sheets, charters, licence conditions, and the memoirs of proprietors. It studies the structure of advertising markets, since a medium funded by advertising sells something specific and shapes its content around the sale. It attends to regulation as a prize fought over by interested parties rather than as a neutral frame. And it treats the professional ideologies of journalism, the claim to independence, the ritual of the balanced report, as data to be explained rather than as descriptions to be believed. The Glasgow University Media Group's studies of industrial news in the 1970s, though closer in method to content analysis than to political economy proper, supplied the wing with a durable exhibit: coverage of strikes and industrial conflict that systematically framed disputes from the standpoint of management and of the travelling public, treating one side's inconvenience as the story and the other side's grievance as the problem. Whatever refinements later research added, the exhibit fixed the wing's characteristic claim in a form anyone could inspect: the standpoint of news is structured before any individual journalist makes a choice.

The characteristic findings of the structural wing are best stated in its own careful terms, because the crude version, that owners dictate content line by line, is a version its serious practitioners never held. The careful claims are these. Concentrated ownership narrows the range of editorial positions that receive institutional backing, not by instruction but by the selection of personnel, the allocation of resources, and the quiet anticipation of what will be acceptable, a mechanism Murdock and Golding described as operating through the interplay of ownership, market position, and professional practice rather than through conspiracy. Advertising funding biases media toward audiences advertisers wish to reach and toward content that keeps those audiences in a buying mood, which is a bias of selection rather than of censorship. Dependence on official sources, driven by the economics of newsgathering, gives state and corporate institutions a standing advantage in defining events. And the global trade in media products moves overwhelmingly in one direction, from a small number of producing centres outward, so that the images through which much of the world imagines modern life are manufactured in a few places by a few firms. Each claim is empirical, each can be and has been contested with evidence, and none requires the hypothesis of a committee deciding what the public shall think.

## The blindspot: Smythe and the audience commodity

Within the structural wing, one argument stands apart for its ingenuity and its afterlife, and it belongs to the Canadian economist Dallas Smythe. In an essay of 1977 whose title named communications the blindspot of Western Marxism, Smythe observed that Marxist critics of media had examined programmes, films, and newspapers endlessly while never asking the economist's question: what is the commodity that commercial media produce? His answer inverted the common sense of the industry. The programmes are not the product. They are the inducement, the free lunch that assembles the real commodity, which is the audience itself, its attention packaged by size and demographic profile and sold to advertisers. On this account the audience works. Watching and listening are labour, the labour of learning to want the goods whose makers have purchased the audience's time, and the media institution is best understood as a factory whose raw material is human attention and whose finished goods are delivered, not to the viewers, but to the advertisers who pay.

The argument's consequences run through the rest of this article, so its precision matters. If audiences are commodities, then the political economist's central question shifts from what media say to what media sell, and the analysis of content becomes secondary to the analysis of the transaction that content exists to enable. The argument also hands the interpretive wing an unexpected gift, since an audience that works is not an audience that sleeps, and Smythe's later readers would extend his insight in directions he only sketched: the audience commodity analysis has been applied, in the platform era, to users whose clicks, posts, and viewing histories are harvested as data and sold in markets the users never see, an extension that treats unpaid digital activity as continuous with the older labour of watching. Critics of Smythe reply that the analogy strains at the point where watching a programme one enjoys is called work, that audiences extract genuine pleasure and use from the transaction, and that a concept of labour stretched to cover all attentive life loses the precision that made it explanatory. The dispute is live inside the tradition and is one of the places where the two wings, unusually, have something to say to each other, since the question of what audiences get from media is the interpretive wing's home ground.

## Filters, not instructions: the propaganda model

The structural wing's most widely debated single construction arrived in 1988, when Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky published Manufacturing Consent and proposed that the news media of the United States could be analysed through a propaganda model. The model directs attention to five filters through which news must pass before it reaches print or broadcast: the ownership of media by large corporations and the profit orientation that follows; the dependence on advertising revenue; the reliance on information supplied by government, business, and establishment experts; the discipline of flak, the complaints and pressures visited on inconvenient reporting; and a governing ideology that marks out the boundaries of legitimate concern. The model's distinctive claim is that no orders are required. Journalists internalise the filters as professional common sense, stories that would embarrass the institutions supplying access and revenue die quietly at a dozen desks, and the resulting output serves elite interests with a reliability that a censor's office might envy, while remaining, at the level of the individual newsroom, the product of routine and sincere professional judgement.

Reception of the model inside the tradition was divided in ways that illuminate the map. Political economists broadly welcomed it as a rigorous statement of their programme, though some found its functionalism too smooth, its media too uniformly servile, and its treatment of journalistic resistance too thin. Scholars in the interpretive line objected that the model had almost nothing to say about how audiences receive the filtered product, as though the completion of the filter settled the meaning of the news. Critics outside the tradition pressed harder points: that the model predicts a uniformity its own evidence, drawn heavily from paired comparisons of worthy and unworthy victims, cannot by itself establish; that elite disagreement, which opens space for vigorous reporting, is treated as an exception rather than as a structural feature of plural power centres; and that the model risks unfalsifiability when every deviation is attributed to elite division and every conformity to the filters. The model's defenders answer that it is probabilistic rather than mechanical, that it has generated testable comparisons across decades of foreign coverage, and that its central insight, that structure filters before conscience speaks, survives the corrections. The dispute is a fair specimen of the structural wing at work: a bold institutional claim, an evidence base of content comparisons, and an argument about mechanism that continues because the mechanism is genuinely hard to observe directly.

## The interpretive wing: cultural studies and the turn to meaning

British cultural studies began in adult education and literary criticism, passed through the founding texts of Richard Hoggart, Raymond Williams, and Edward Thompson, and acquired an institutional home in 1964 when Hoggart established the Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies at the University of Birmingham. Under Stuart Hall, who directed the Centre through the 1970s, it became the interpretive wing of Marxist media theory and one of the most influential intellectual formations of the postwar decades. Its founding conviction was double. Culture, in Williams's phrase as in the Centre's practice, was ordinary: the meanings made in everyday life deserved the same serious attention as the certified works of high culture, and the soap opera, the popular song, and the youth subculture were evidence about a society as surely as its novels were. And meaning was contested: a text does not carry its significance sealed inside it, because significance is completed, altered, or refused at the point of reception, by readers and viewers whose social situations give them different codes for making sense.

The wing's formation explains its temper. Where the Frankfurt analysts had looked at mass culture and seen standardisation, the Birmingham scholars looked at the same materials and saw appropriation: working class audiences taking commercial products and making them mean things their makers never intended. The Centre's studies of youth subcultures, above all Dick Hebdige's Subculture: The Meaning of Style in 1979, read the dress and music of mods, punks, and rastas as symbolic work, bricolage performed on commodities, in which the materials of the market were rearranged into statements about class, race, and refusal. Critics, some of them sympathetic, would later ask whether the celebration of such resistance confused style with politics and consumption with power. The question belongs to the quarrel examined below. Here the point is directional: cultural studies moved the centre of gravity of Marxist media analysis from the institution to the encounter, from what is sent to what is made of what is sent, and in doing so it changed what counted as evidence. The interview, the viewing session, the fan's own account of her pleasures became data, and a tradition that had inferred audiences from structures acquired audiences it could actually listen to.

Hall gave the wing its theoretical charter in an essay on cultural studies and its two paradigms, published in 1980, where he distinguished culturalism, the line of Hoggart, Williams, and Thompson, centred on experience and the creative making of culture, from structuralism, the line descending through Lévi-Strauss and Althusser, centred on the categories and codes through which experience is made possible at all. Hall refused to choose between the paradigms and insisted that the field lived in the tension between them, experience against structure, agency against determination. The refusal was characteristic, and it marked the interpretive wing's deepest difference from its structural sibling: where political economy sought the hard floor of ownership beneath the play of meanings, cultural studies suspected every floor of being another text. The wider tradition from which that suspicion descends is mapped in the [marxist cultural theory guide](/2020/06/01/marxist-cultural-theory-guide/), to which the reader wanting Williams and the cultural materialist line in full should turn.

### How did cultural studies change the picture of the audience?

It replaced the image of a passive mass absorbing messages with an account of audiences as interpreters who decode texts within their own social situations. Readings could follow the preferred meaning encoded in a programme, negotiate it, or oppose it outright, and that variability became the wing's central evidence against simple effects claims.

## Encoding and decoding: the wing's most durable model

If the interpretive wing has a single canonical construction, it is Hall's model of encoding and decoding, first circulated as a paper in 1973 and republished, in the form in which it is usually taught, in the collection Culture, Media, Language in 1980. The model begins from a refusal of the linear picture of communication, in which a sender transmits a message to a receiver who either gets it or fails to. Hall proposed instead a circuit. Production encodes meaning into a text under specific conditions: the institutional structures of broadcasting, the professional codes of programme makers, their assumptions about the audience, the frameworks of knowledge within which an event becomes a story. The finished text is therefore not a transparent report but a meaningful discourse, structured by the codes that made it, and it proposes a preferred reading, the interpretation its construction favours. At the other end of the circuit, audiences decode. Drawing on a distinction adapted from Frank Parkin, Hall set out three positions from which a text may be decoded: the dominant or preferred position, in which the viewer takes the encoded meaning whole; the negotiated position, in which the viewer accepts the general frame while reserving judgement on its application to her own circumstances; and the oppositional position, in which the viewer understands the preferred reading perfectly and rejects it, recoding the event within an alternative framework.

Two separations inside the model repay the student's attention, because conflating them is the commonest error in examination answers and in published summaries alike. The first separation is between encoding and decoding as moments: the model does not say that audiences can make a text mean anything at all, and Hall was explicit that polysemy is not pluralism, that the text's construction sets limits and exerts pressure toward its preferred reading even when that reading is refused. The second separation is between the technical question of comprehension and the political question of agreement. A viewer may decode a news report exactly as encoded, understanding its framing of a strike as a disruption caused by militants, and still reject that framing root and branch; misunderstanding is not resistance, and agreement is not the only evidence of successful communication. Oppositional decoding, in Hall's construction, is a feat of comprehension followed by refusal, which is why the model could claim to explain something the effects tradition could not: how a message can be received perfectly and still fail to persuade.

The model's authority rests partly on the empirical programme it licensed. David Morley's study of the television magazine programme Nationwide, published in 1980, showed recorded episodes to groups of viewers differentiated by class, occupation, and race, and examined how their decodings patterned. The results were suggestive and unruly in equal measure. Some groups decoded as their class position predicted; others, famously a group of black students and a group of shop stewards, broke the expected pattern in opposite directions, and Morley concluded that decoding is governed not by class in the raw but by the discourses and competencies that class position makes available, mediated through cultural repertoires. Later audience ethnographies widened the canvas. Ien Ang studied letters from Dutch viewers of the American serial Dallas and found pleasure, irony, and emotional realism where a culture industry reading predicted only manipulation. Janice Radway interviewed American romance readers and found women using commercial fiction to carve out time, declare a small independence inside domestic routine, and rehearse feelings their lives rationed. John Fiske pressed the line furthest, theorising the audience as a semiotic democracy of producers of meaning, and drew the sharpest fire from the political economists for it. The wing's findings, taken together, did not show that media lack power. They showed that power exercised through meaning meets interpreters, and that any theory of media influence which cannot describe interpretation will mistake its object.

## Screen theory: the spectator as an effect

Between the two wings in time, and belonging fully to neither, stands the episode of screen theory, named for the journal Screen, in which British and French theorists of the 1970s fused Althusser's account of ideology with Lacanian psychoanalysis and the semiotics of film. The fusion's source texts matter. Althusser's essay on ideology and ideological state apparatuses, examined in its own right in the article on [althusser ideology state apparatuses](/2012/03/01/althusser-ideology-state-apparatuses/), had argued that ideology interpellates individuals as subjects, that people are made into the kind of beings who recognise themselves in the hail of authority and experience that subjection as freedom. Screen theory asked what the cinema does to the person in the seat. Drawing on Jean Louis Baudry's account of the apparatus and Christian Metz's analysis of the imaginary signifier, its writers argued that the cinema's machinery, the darkened room, the immobile viewer, the image larger than life and seemingly self generated, positions the spectator as a transcendental subject, the still centre for whom the visible world arranges itself. Ideology, on this account, does not arrive in the film's message. It is built into the viewing situation, which reproduces, at the level of perception itself, the bourgeois fiction of the autonomous self.

Laura Mulvey's essay Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema, published in Screen in 1975, gave the episode its most famous application. Mulvey argued that classical Hollywood cinema organises looking along lines of sexual difference, constructing woman as image and man as bearer of the look, so that the pleasures of the cinema, scopophilia and identification alike, are structured by a patriarchal unconscious the spectator does not choose. The essay's influence ran far beyond film studies, and its central device, the male gaze, entered general criticism. Yet screen theory as a whole had a short life as a research programme. Its textualism was extreme: the spectator it described was a position constructed by the apparatus, not a person who might fidget, ironise, leave early, or talk back, and the audience research of the following decade, much of it from the Birmingham direction, treated its claims as exactly the kind of inference from text to effect that the interpretive wing existed to correct. Its defenders reply that screen theory asked a question ethnography cannot reach, about the constitution of the subject rather than the opinions of the audience, and that its account of positioning survived the episode, absorbed into later theories of spectatorship and into feminist film criticism long after the programme's Althusserian scaffolding was dismantled. On the map, it occupies an instructive coordinate: a Marxist media theory with no political economy at all and no empirical audience either, proof that the tradition's internal variety extends to positions both wings would come to reject.

## The quarrel: when the wings stopped talking past each other

For two decades the wings mostly ignored one another, publishing in different journals and citing different ancestors. The disagreement became explicit, and therefore usable, in an exchange conducted in the journal Critical Studies in Mass Communication in the mid 1990s, when Nicholas Garnham, a leading figure of the political economy wing, published an essay asking whether political economy and cultural studies could be reconciled or were headed for divorce, and Lawrence Grossberg, a leading figure of the cultural studies wing in the United States, replied with an essay whose title asked whether anyone else was bored with this debate. The exchange is the single most efficient document of the field's structure, because each participant stated the other's position in a form sharp enough to argue with, and the argument has never been settled so much as inherited.

Garnham's charge had several counts. Cultural studies, he argued, had drifted from its founding engagement with class and material life into a celebration of consumption and audience activity, in which the discovery that audiences make meanings became an excuse to stop asking who owns the means of making them. The wing's characteristic claims were inflated: resistance discovered in every act of viewing, pleasure treated as though it were itself a political achievement, textual ingenuity substituting for the analysis of institutions. Behind the methodological complaint stood a political one. A field that began by asking how consent to an unequal order is secured had ended, in Garnham's telling, by supplying the market with a flattering mirror, since the active, choosing, meaning making consumer of cultural studies is precisely the figure that advertising celebrates. Reconciliation, on his terms, required cultural studies to return questions of production, distribution, and economic constraint to the centre of its inquiries and to accept that meanings are made under conditions nobody chooses.

Grossberg's reply refused the terms while conceding fragments of the description. The debate itself, he suggested, had become ritualised, with each side performing a caricature of the other for audiences already persuaded. Political economy, in his counter charge, possessed no theory of culture worth the name: it could explain why media institutions behave as they do and then simply assumed that their products worked upon audiences in the directions their structures intended, an assumption the audience research of two decades had made untenable. Its Marxism had thinned into a sociology of ownership that repeated, at the level of method, the base and superstructure picture the tradition claimed to have outgrown. And its politics were nostalgic, measuring every development against a lost moment of class clarity. Where Garnham saw drift, Grossberg saw a division of labour that political economy wanted to convert into a hierarchy, with production as the real and reception as the decorative.

Between the named antagonists stood the figures who had been making versions of each argument for years, and the exchange is best read as the surfacing of a submerged literature. Jim McGuigan's Cultural Populism, published in 1992, had already criticised from a position sympathetic to cultural studies the tendency to applaud every popular pleasure as resistance. Murdock had written of the need to reconnect the analysis of symbolic goods to the analysis of material inequality, and Golding with Murdock would continue to insist that the celebration of audience activity sat oddly beside rising concentrations of communicative power. On the other side, defenders of the interpretive line replied that political economy's own record contained a conspicuous absence: decades of ownership studies had produced no account of why particular programmes move, delight, or disturb the people who watch them, and a theory of media that cannot speak of meaning has surrendered half its object to the psychologists and the marketers. No synthesis emerged from the exchange, and the field organised itself instead into an armed truce, with a minority tradition of scholars attempting genuinely integrated work and the majority citing within their wing.

### What did the two wings accuse each other of?

The political economists accused cultural studies of celebrating audience activity while ignoring the ownership and funding that decide which texts reach audiences at all. The cultural studies scholars replied that political economy treated audiences as an afterthought, inferred effects from structures, and had no account of meaning, pleasure, or interpretation worth the name.

## What each wing cannot see

The exchange clarifies what each wing's method systematically excludes, and stating the exclusions symmetrically is more useful than awarding the argument to either side. Political economy cannot see the text from inside. Its instruments register ownership, revenue, and regulation, and from these it infers constraints on content, but the inference skips the level at which media actually engage people, the level of form, genre, narrative, and address, where two programmes produced under identical ownership can differ in every politically relevant respect. The wing knows this and periodically confesses it, and its best practitioners read texts with great subtlety; the confession does not alter the fact that its theory gives it no principled way to derive the reading from the structure. Cultural studies cannot see the conditions of its own encounters. Its instruments register interpretation in the living room, but the living room's contents, the set, the subscription, the schedule, the archive of available programmes, were settled elsewhere by decisions its method brackets, and its celebrations of audience creativity can read, against the background of those decisions, like praise for the ingenuity of prisoners in decorating cells. That image is Garnham's polemical point in compressed form, and it is unfair in the way of good polemic: interpretation is not decoration, and the meanings audiences make feed back into production through ratings, markets, and reputations. But the asymmetry the image points at is real. The wings study different moments of a single circuit, production and reception, and each is tempted to treat its moment as the whole. The two-wing rule exists to resist exactly that temptation in the reader, whichever wing the reader finds congenial.

There is a further asymmetry of temperament that the map should record. The structural wing is drawn to what is durable: ownership changes slowly, funding models persist, regulatory settlements last decades, and its findings age correspondingly well. The interpretive wing is drawn to what is alive: genres mutate, audiences reorganise, new pleasures and new refusals appear with each cohort, and its findings date quickly while its method stays fresh. A student who grasps that difference has a reliable instrument for reading the literature of both wings, since it predicts which parts of a text will still be standing in twenty years and which will read as dispatches from a vanished media world.

## The media theory map

The map promised at the outset can now be drawn. The media theory map sets the two wings and their Frankfurt inheritance beside the traditions they argue with, and states for each its characteristic question, its unit of analysis, its method, its strongest finding, and its principal weakness. Read across a row and the tradition's identity appears. Read down the weakness column and the field's conversation appears, since most weaknesses were named first by the occupants of other rows.

| Tradition | What it explains | Unit of analysis | Characteristic method | Strongest finding | Principal weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Political economy of communication | Why media institutions produce what they produce | The firm, the market, the regulatory settlement | Institutional and historical analysis of ownership and funding | Structural asymmetry in which voices are resourced and amplified | Infers effects on meaning from structures it cannot read from inside |
| Cultural studies | How meanings are made, contested, and lived | The text in its encounter with situated audiences | Textual analysis joined to audience ethnography | Decoding varies systematically with social position and repertoire | Brackets the production decisions that settle what audiences encounter |
| Culture industry analysis | How commercial standardisation shapes perception | The cultural commodity and the consciousness it trains | Philosophical critique of form | Standardisation with surface variety as a durable industrial logic | A picture of reception too damaged to register appropriation or refusal |
| Screen theory | How the viewing situation constitutes the subject | The apparatus and the spectator position it constructs | Psychoanalytic semiotics of film | Ideology operates in the organisation of looking, not only in messages | A spectator who is a theoretical position rather than an empirical person |
| Liberal pluralism | How competing outlets check power and serve choice | The outlet within a competitive market of ideas | Comparative description of media systems | Entry and competition do widen the range of available positions | Treats unequal resources as though they were equal voices |

The table's last column is not a set of verdicts delivered from outside. Each weakness named there was formulated, in the sharpest version available, by a rival inside the conversation, and several were conceded by the traditions themselves in their mature statements. Pluralists concede that resources are unequal and argue that competition disciplines the inequality. Audience researchers in the gratifications line concede that the menu is made elsewhere and reply that a theory of choosing need not be a theory of catering. The map's purpose is placement, not execution: a reader who can say which row an argument lives in, and which row its best answer comes from, has the equipment this article set out to supply.

## The first rival: liberal pluralism

Liberal pluralism deserves presentation in the terms its defenders use, because the tradition's polemics have made a straw figure of it and the straw figure convinces nobody who was not already persuaded. The pluralist holds that media in a competitive system perform the function democratic theory assigns them: they scrutinise power, they carry a diversity of positions into public view, and they answer, through the audience's freedom to choose, to the preferences of citizens rather than to the instructions of owners or states. The market of ideas is not, in the sophisticated version, a claim that all ideas compete on equal terms. It is a claim about tendencies and correctives. Entry by new outlets disciplines incumbents. Advertisers seek audiences and therefore punish outlets that bore or offend them. Professional norms of verification, however imperfectly honoured, are real social facts with real sanctions behind them. And the decisive evidence, for the pluralist, is the observable record of media systems in open societies: governments embarrassed and occasionally destroyed by reporting, corporate misconduct exposed, minority positions carried from the margin to the centre within a generation by outlets that began as insurgents. A theory that predicts servility, the pluralist concludes, must explain away a great deal of insubordination.

The Marxist reply begins by conceding the record and disputing its interpretation. Insubordination, on the structural account, is real but patterned: it flourishes where elite opinion is itself divided, where official sources will brief against one another, and where the commercial interests of the outlet align with exposure, and it thins dramatically where those conditions fail, on questions of property, of foreign policy consensus, of the legitimacy of the economic order itself. The pluralist's market, further, is a market in which the price of a voice is not uniform. The cost of reaching a mass public through the dominant channels has historically been set at levels that exclude all but wealthy proprietors, large corporations, and the state, so that the diversity on display is diversity within a pre selected company, a parliament in which every member passed the same property qualification. To this the pluralist has a rejoinder of genuine force, which the honest map must register and which the section on the strongest objection develops: the price of entry has collapsed in the digital environment, the insurgent outlet is no longer a fantasy, and any structural theory framed for the age of three television networks owes its readers an account of what survives when the scarcity it presupposed is gone. The tradition's answer to that challenge occupies the closing sections of this article.

## The second rival: uses and gratifications

The uses and gratifications tradition grew from the same disappointment that produced cultural studies, disappointment with the picture of the passive audience, but it grew in the opposite disciplinary direction, toward individual psychology and the survey. Its early landmark is Herta Herzog's work in the 1940s on listeners to radio soap operas, who reported, in terms no effects researcher had thought to ask about, the emotional and practical uses they made of serials they were presumed to absorb like medicine. The programme was stated formally by Elihu Katz, Jay Blumler, and Michael Gurevitch in the early 1970s: research should ask not what media do to people but what people do with media, treating the audience as active, goal directed, and capable of reporting the needs, whether for information, identity, companionship, or diversion, that its media choices gratify. The tradition's findings are unfashionable in critical circles and solid on their own ground. People select. They select with purposes they can articulate, they abandon sources that fail them, and their patterns of use track the circumstances of their lives, loneliness, routine, curiosity, sociability, with a fidelity that no theory of manipulation has ever explained.

What the gratifications line explains better than any Marxist account is the texture of ordinary media use, and the concession should be made without throat clearing. Political economy has nothing to say about why one viewer finds a serial indispensable and her neighbour finds it unwatchable, and cultural studies, for all its talk of pleasure, often treats pleasures as texts to be read rather than as experiences to be described. The gratifications researchers asked users and believed the answers more readily than either wing did. The Marxist objection, when it comes, is structural and narrow: the tradition takes the menu as given. It asks why a person chooses among available gratifications and never who composed the list of available things, under what commercial logic, with what ingredients excluded before choosing began. A diner may choose with perfect freedom and still eat only what the kitchen decided to cook. The gratifications reply, that a theory of choosing is not obliged to be a theory of kitchens, is fair as far as it goes, and the standoff is the cleanest example in this field of two theories answering different questions while believing themselves to be in competition, a condition the two-wing rule was designed to diagnose in the Marxist house and which turns out to afflict the whole neighbourhood.

## The empiricist cousins: cultivation and agenda setting

Two further research traditions are sometimes mistaken for allies of the Marxist line because their findings sound critical, and sometimes dismissed by it because their methods are those of the survey laboratory. Both judgements are too quick. George Gerbner's cultivation research, developed from the late 1960s within his Cultural Indicators project, compared heavy and light television viewers and reported that heavy viewers' estimates of the world, its dangers, its professions, its chances of violence, drift toward the world as television represents it. The mean world finding, that heavy viewers overestimate their risk of victimisation in proportion to a fictional diet of violence, remains the programme's signature result. Its Marxist resonance is obvious, a slow ideological effect of exposure, and so is its difference: cultivation posits no owner, no interest, no ideology in the strong sense, only the cumulative pressure of a relatively uniform symbolic environment, an environment whose uniformity Gerbner, to be fair, traced in part to the industrial organisation of storytelling and called, pointedly, a new religion of the industrial age.

Agenda setting, founded on the Chapel Hill study conducted by Maxwell McCombs and Donald Shaw during the American presidential campaign of 1968, established that the issues the press emphasises and the issues voters judge important correspond closely, and summarised the finding in a borrowed aphorism: the press may not tell people what to think, but it is strikingly successful in telling them what to think about. The tradition that followed measured salience transfer with great technical refinement and later extended the claim to the attributes of objects, the framing of how issues are presented. For the Marxist map, agenda setting occupies an instructive middle ground. Its finding is entirely compatible with the structural wing, since who sets the agenda is a question about sources and institutions, and Herman and Chomsky's filters can be read as an account of why the agenda takes the shape it does. But the tradition itself is deliberately agnostic about interests, and its effects are the modest, measurable kind that the interpretive wing's ethnographers find too thin to capture what media do in a life. Both cousins therefore serve the map as controls: they show what a media effect looks like when it is specified tightly enough to be measured, and they set the standard of modesty against which the grander claims of every tradition, Marxist claims included, have to be judged.

## The third rival: the medium as the message

Marshall McLuhan's Understanding Media, published in 1964, advanced the claim from which an entire rival tradition descends: the medium is the message, meaning that the personal and social consequences of any medium result from the new scale it introduces into human affairs, not from the content it carries. Print reorganised consciousness toward the linear and the private; electronic media retribalise, restoring simultaneity and involvement; content is the juicy piece of meat the burglar carries to distract the watchdog of the mind. McLuhan's ancestry complicates the map usefully, because he drew on Innis, the same Innis whose political economy of communication fed the structural wing, and the two traditions are thus cousins who read their grandfather in opposite directions: the political economists took Innis's monopolies of knowledge as an account of power, while McLuhan took his media biases as an account of perception, and dropped the power.

Presented at full strength, medium theory is the rival that embarrasses everyone else on the map, because it points at a level of analysis the others systematically miss. Two societies receiving opposite messages through the same medium resemble each other in ways their ideologies cannot explain, and the transformations wrought by print, by broadcasting, and by the networked screen proceeded with an indifference to content that any theory centred on messages must struggle to accommodate. The Marxist objection is equally clear. A theory in which media operate as autonomous environments has no place for owners, for advertisers, for the state, or for audiences who argue back; technology develops according to its own logic and human beings adapt, which is determinism wearing the dress of prophecy. Raymond Williams, who engaged McLuhan more seriously than any other Marxist of his generation, granted the perception that form matters and rejected the evacuation of intention: technologies, Williams argued, are looked for and developed with purposes in mind, are selected by institutions, and are shaped in use, so that the medium's form is itself a social product rather than a destiny. The verdict the honest map records is that medium theory identified a real stratum of effects and then explained it with a theory too empty to hold the stratum, leaving the stratum itself as an open problem each tradition since has had to raid or ignore.

## The fourth rival: the dissolution of the real

Jean Baudrillard radicalised McLuhan's line until it broke with Marxism altogether, and his position is the fourth rival the brief for this article requires at full strength. In works culminating in Simulacra and Simulation in 1981, Baudrillard argued that the signs of media culture have passed through successive orders of simulation to reach a stage at which they no longer represent or distort an underlying reality but precede and generate it: models come first, territories follow, and the result is the hyperreal, a condition in which the distinction between representation and reality, on which ideology critique depends, has imploded. His famous provocation, that a later twentieth century war existed as a media event whose simulations did the work of the event itself, was designed to scandalise a tradition that believed it could measure coverage against fact. If Baudrillard is right, then the Marxist analysis of media is not wrong in its details but obsolete in its founding gesture, since there is no longer an undistorted real for distorted representations to be measured against, and the political economist's owners are merely rearranging the furniture of a simulation that owns them all.

The tradition's replies divide by wing. The structural wing answers empirically that the distinction between representation and reality continues to do work wherever it is tested: casualties, prices, verdicts, and weather refuse to dissolve into their coverage, and a theory that cannot distinguish a famine from its broadcast has not transcended ideology critique but abandoned description. The interpretive wing, or much of it, absorbed the provocation differently, treating simulation as an exaggerated name for genuine phenomena, the detachability of images from their origins, the circulation of signs through an economy of attention, which cultural analysis could use without buying the metaphysics. Baudrillard's own trajectory aids the map's judgement: having begun inside a Marxist critique of consumption, he concluded that production itself had ended as the organising fact of society, a conclusion his former allies read as the reductio of his method, in which a brilliant diagnosis of image culture inflates into a total ontology that no evidence could disturb. What the rivals explain here, to borrow the question the FAQ section puts directly, is the phenomenology of a media saturated environment, the felt unreality of public events, better than any tradition on the map that insists on sober distinctions. What they cannot supply is a reason to prefer one arrangement of the saturation to another, and the Marxist traditions continue to find that silence disabling.

## Advertising: the commercial hinge between the wings

No part of the media system joins the two wings as concretely as advertising, and the companion article on [advertising consumption ideology](/2012/06/01/advertising-consumption-ideology/) owns the subject in full; a compressed statement belongs here because advertising is where the structural and the interpretive analyses most plainly require each other. The structural fact is Smythe's: advertising is not an adjunct to commercial media but their economic engine, the transaction for which audiences are assembled, and it follows that the system's centre of gravity sits not in the newsroom or the studio but in the market for attention. The interpretive fact is that advertisements are texts of extraordinary compression and craft, which work, when they work, by attaching goods to meanings, identities, and longings that audiences actively negotiate, parody, resist, and occasionally believe. A political economy that stops at the transaction cannot explain why one campaign moves a market and another becomes a joke; a cultural analysis that stops at the text cannot explain why the jokes all arrive inside a system that sells the audience's attention regardless of who wins the argument about taste. The hinge case also disciplines both wings' rhetoric. Against the structural wing's bleaker portraits, advertising's failures are legion and public, campaigns collapsing under audience ridicule at a cost no theory of manipulation budgeted for. Against the interpretive wing's sunnier portraits, the ridicule is itself harvested as engagement, and the system's indifference to which meaning prevails, so long as attention is delivered, is the deepest fact Smythe taught the field to see.

## The strongest objection, stated in full

The objection this article is required to answer is not a debating point but an accumulation of evidence, and it should be stated as its holders state it. Begin with reception. Decades of audience research, some of it from inside the tradition and much of it from outside, have shown audiences interpreting messages against the grain, ignoring campaigns wholesale, mocking propaganda, and extracting from commercial culture materials for purposes its producers would deplore. The hypodermic picture of effects collapsed in the middle of the twentieth century under the weight of studies showing selective exposure, interpersonal mediation, and the two step flow of influence through opinion leaders, and nothing discovered since has restored it. Move to supply. The tradition's structural wing predicted, across the decades of broadcasting, a uniformity of content ground out by concentrated ownership, and the record shows instead a churn of formats, the collapse of gatekeepers, the multiplication of outlets beyond any earlier imagining, and, in the networked environment, entry costs so low that the scarcity premise on which the ownership argument rested appears to have been repealed by engineering. Conclude with method. The tradition, say its critics, protects itself: conformity of output is credited to the filters, deviation is credited to elite division or to the resourcefulness of audiences, and a theory that can explain every outcome after the fact predicts none of them before it. On this showing, Marxist media theory survives as a vocabulary of suspicion in search of a mechanism, kept alive by the pleasures of unmasking rather than by victories in open empirical competition.

The pluralist and the gratifications researcher, who make much of this objection, add a political count that should not be softened. A theory that treats the beliefs of ordinary audiences as manufactured and their pleasures as symptoms claims, implicitly, a standpoint outside the manufacture from which the theorist surveys the deception, and the claim is never earned. The audiences whose testimony contradicts the theory are discounted as the deepest victims of the process the theory describes, a move available to any doctrine that wishes to be immune to the evidence of the people it describes. Cultural studies, note, was born partly from revulsion at exactly this move inside the Marxist house, which is why the objection, pressed from outside, converges with the interpretive wing's internal critique and draws strength from the convergence.

## The reply, and the concession it must carry

The reply that the evidence supports requires the objection to be disassembled, because it bundles claims that fare very differently. The first claim, that audiences are passive recipients upon whom effects are worked, has not held, and conceding this costs the mature tradition almost nothing, since its interpretive wing was founded on the refutation and its structural wing's careful statements never required it. Content uniformity, the second claim, has also failed as a prediction in its simple form. Commercial media systems generate novelty compulsively, owners tolerate and even finance dissent that sells, and any account implying a monotone output is refuted by a week of channel surfing or its networked equivalent. The tradition overreached on both points for decades, in its textbooks and its polemics if not always in its best research, and the concession should be recorded as plainly as the objection was: those were the claims its critics quoted, they were the claims its publics remembered, and they did not survive contact with reception research and with the diversification of supply.

The third claim is the one the reply contests, and it is the structural core the two-wing rule exists to protect. Stripped of the failed predictions, the structural wing asserts an asymmetry: that the capacity to place messages before mass publics, to sustain them, to repeat them, to give them the production values and the distribution that make them unavoidable, is distributed as unequally as other forms of wealth, and that this asymmetry shapes the environment within which all the active, resistant, pleasure taking interpretation documented by audience research takes place. That claim has held better. It survives the collapse of entry costs in a modified form, because the abolition of the printing press barrier was followed by the erection of new bottlenecks, the platforms that own the channels of discovery, the algorithms that rank attention, the advertising markets that decide which voices are paid to persist, so that the political economy of the networked environment studies a smaller number of vastly larger gatekeepers than the broadcasting age supplied. It survives, too, the testimony of audiences, because the claim was never that interpretation is unfree; it was that freedom of interpretation operates on materials whose selection the interpreter does not control, and the ethnographic record, which shows viewers negotiating brilliantly among the options offered them, is silent about the options never offered. Whether this restated core still deserves the name of a theory of ideology, or has become a sober sociology of communicative inequality that several traditions could sign, is a genuine question the closing assessment takes up.

### Which questions do the rivals answer better?

Pluralism answers questions about competition and entry among outlets. Uses and gratifications answers questions about why individuals choose the media they choose. Cultivation and agenda setting measure long term shifts in perception and salience. Each rival is strongest exactly where its unit of analysis matches the question being asked of it.

## Ownership and content: determination as a question of degrees

The question whether ownership determines content is the one on which public argument about this tradition most often founders, because the word determine smuggles in a mechanical picture that the serious literature abandoned early. The structural wing's mature position is probabilistic and mediated. Ownership sets the boundaries of the possible: it selects the senior personnel, fixes the commercial strategy, decides which ventures are capitalised and which are starved, and establishes the climate in which editorial judgement operates, so that the content which emerges is constrained without being dictated, and surprising departures are possible precisely because the constraint works through anticipation rather than instruction. The historical record supplies cases at both poles. Proprietors have intervened nakedly, killing stories, dismissing editors, and bending titles to campaigns, and the record of such interventions is part of the wing's evidence. It also supplies long periods in which valuable reporting proceeded under owners indifferent or hostile to it, protected by professional norms, by the commercial value of credibility, and by competition. A theory that cannot accommodate both poles is not being tested against the record; it is being recited over it.

The interpretive wing adds a second mediation that public argument usually omits. Even where production is constrained, the realised meaning of content is settled at reception, so that the chain from ownership to effect passes through two links, text and decoding, at each of which the determination weakens and other causes enter. This is why the strongest form of the structural claim is about agendas and absences rather than about beliefs: about which subjects are covered lavishly and which are covered thinly or not at all, which standpoints are treated as the natural centre of a story and which appear as interested pleading, which experts are installed as the voices of record. Absence is the structural wing's best evidence precisely because it cannot be negotiated by an active audience. A viewer can decode a report oppositionally; she cannot decode the report that was never made, on the industry never investigated, from the standpoint never commissioned. Put the two wings' claims together and a combined position emerges that neither wing owns outright: ownership strongly influences what is available to be interpreted, weakly and unpredictably influences what is made of it, and the tradition's recurring error, on both sides, has been to argue as though strength at one link implied strength at the other.

## Form, genre, and the ideology of the text itself

Between structure and reception stands the text, and the tradition's most distinctive methods were built to read it. The interpretive wing inherited from literary studies and from semiotics the premise that a text's politics are carried not only in its statements but in its organisation: in what its narratives treat as a problem and what as a solution, in which characters are granted interiority, in how its genres divide the world into the normal and the deviant. News, on this reading, is not a window but a form, with conventions, the inverted pyramid, the reliance on accredited sources, the dramatisation of events over processes, that systematically favour some accounts of the world. Hall and his collaborators gave the argument its classic application in Policing the Crisis, published in 1978, a study of the moral panic over mugging in Britain which traced how police, courts, and press, the primary definers of the event, supplied the terms in which a secondary definition by journalists then circulated, so that a rise in street robbery, itself entangled with the statistics that announced it, became a parable of national decline requiring authoritarian remedy. The study remains the interpretive wing's most complete performance: textual analysis, institutional history, and an account of hegemony as the organisation of consent around a crisis, executed in a single work.

Genre analysis extended the same premise into entertainment. The soap opera, the crime series, the western, the reality format each encode a repertoire of solutions to the contradictions of the societies that consume them, and the wing's scholars learned to read those repertoires neither as propaganda nor as innocent diversion but as the imaginative processing of real tensions, work, family, money, respectability, into forms that can be inhabited. Feminist scholarship inside the tradition transformed this analysis from within. Scholars such as Angela McRobbie examined the magazines addressed to girls and asked what femininities they rehearsed; others took the romance, the soap, and the melodrama seriously as women's genres at a time when the male dominated wing was still treating them as evidence of false consciousness, and the correction permanently changed what cultural studies counted as a political text. The point for the map is that the textual level is not a compromise between the wings but a territory with its own methods and its own findings, which political economy cannot derive from ownership and audience research cannot reach by interview, and which any complete account of media power has to pass through.

## The state, regulation, and the public service test

A map drawn only from commercial media would misrepresent the structural wing, whose analyses of the state and of public service broadcasting supply some of its most instructive cases. Public broadcasting was the broadcasting settlement of much of Europe and the standing counterexample to the claim that media under capitalism are simply businesses: an institution funded by a licence fee or by taxation, chartered to inform, educate, and entertain, and governed at arm's length from both market and ministry. The political economists treated the arrangement without sentimentality. In studies of the British settlement, Garnham and others argued that public service broadcasting represented a genuine, historically won decommodification of a cultural sphere, a space in which programmes could be made for citizens rather than for audiences sold to advertisers, and that its defence was therefore a material interest of everyone the commercial system underserves. They also documented its limits: appointments and funding settlements gave governments leverage that was periodically used, charter renewals disciplined managers more reliably than any censor, and the institution's own class composition inflected its sense of the national audience it served.

The case matters beyond its setting because it tests the tradition's categories in both directions. Against crude political economy, the public broadcaster shows that ownership form alters output in ways audiences can detect, that a non commercial institution inside a capitalist society is possible, durable, and different, which means the structural variables are real variables rather than a single fate. Against celebratory pluralism, the same case shows how thoroughly a formally independent institution can be disciplined by funding and appointment without a single instruction being issued, which is the structural wing's mechanism in its purest observable form. Regulation more generally is treated by the wing as a terrain of struggle rather than a rulebook: spectrum allocation, licensing, merger control, and content quotas are prizes over which corporations, states, professions, and publics contend, and the history of any media system is in large part the history of those settlements and their erosion. The interpretive wing, characteristically, studied the same institutions from the other end, asking how public service ideals were encoded in programme forms and how audiences used a broadcasting commons in their domestic lives, and the two accounts of the public broadcaster, as settlement and as text, illustrate the circuit logic better than any abstract statement of it.

## Global flows: the media imperialism thesis and its critics

The structural wing's most ambitious extension carried the analysis across borders. If communication within a society is structured by ownership and funding, then the communication between societies should show the same pattern at a larger scale, and from the late 1960s Schiller, Mattelart, and others argued that it did: a one way flow of programmes, formats, news, and advertising models from the United States and a handful of metropolitan centres to the rest of the world, carried on infrastructures owned in the centre, financed by the centre's advertisers, and saturated with the centre's assumptions about consumption and modernity. The thesis, debated under the name of media or cultural imperialism, acquired institutional expression in the demands for a New World Information and Communication Order that convulsed UNESCO in the late 1970s and early 1980s, when governments of the developing world sought international rules for a flow they experienced as domination. The episode is a reminder that media theory has occasionally had ministries for its audience.

The critics of the thesis came from every direction, and the map must give them their due. Empirical researchers found the flows less one way than advertised: regional producers, in Latin America, in India, in the Arab world, built industries that dominated their home markets and exported within their regions; audiences preferred local and regional content when it was available at comparable quality, a pattern summed up in the principle of cultural proximity; and imported programmes were decoded through local frameworks in ways the thesis's strong versions never modelled, so that the same serial could anchor quite different conversations in Amsterdam and in Caracas. The interpretive wing supplied much of this criticism from inside the Marxist house, which is one reason the imperialism debate reads, in retrospect, like the two-wing quarrel conducted on a world stage: a structural claim of great sweep, an ethnographic correction of great specificity. The thesis in its strongest form did not survive the correction. What survived is its asymmetry core, restated for an era of platforms: the infrastructures of global circulation, the cables, the satellites, the app stores, the recommendation systems, the advertising exchanges, are owned in very few places, and the terms on which the world's cultures meet are set by the owners of the meeting places, whoever wins the argument about the content that flows through them.

## Feminism inside the tradition: the correction that stayed

The wings described so far were both, in their founding generations, largely male in personnel and in preoccupation, and the most consequential internal development of the interpretive wing was the feminist intervention that began in the 1970s and never entirely ended. Its opening complaint was empirical: the Birmingham Centre's celebrated studies of youth subcultures were studies of boys, their public styles and public spaces, while the culture of girls, conducted in bedrooms and around magazines, music, and romance, was invisible to the field's methods and beneath its notice. Its theoretical complaint cut deeper. A tradition organised around class had no adequate account of the media's construction of femininity and masculinity, of the sexual division of domestic labour that structured viewing itself, of who controlled the set, whose programmes counted as serious, whose pleasures were pathologised. Feminist scholars supplied the missing accounts in work that crossed the wings: analyses of women's magazines and of the industries behind them, ethnographies of women viewers that found in despised genres resources for negotiating domestic power, and critiques of screen theory's gaze that rebuilt spectatorship theory around the possibility of female and queer looking.

The intervention matters to this article's map for two reasons. It demonstrates that the tradition, at its best, could be corrected from inside by scholars who shared its premises and indicted its practice, a capacity the objection section weighs when it asks whether the field can learn. And it produced the clearest case of the two-wing rule operating in reverse: feminist work showed that a criticism framed against one part of the tradition, the class reductionism of the subcultural studies, left other parts standing and indeed armed them, since the ethnographic methods the critics used were cultural studies' own. The sexual division of attention, who watches what, where, and with whose permission, became a permanent category of the field, and no subsequent account of audiences, Marxist or otherwise, has been able to proceed as though the viewer were an ungendered abstraction.

## After Birmingham: the interpretive wing moves on

The Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies closed as an institution long after its founding generation dispersed, and the wing it named underwent a transformation that Garnham's side of the quarrel cites as vindication and Grossberg's side cites as maturation. Through the 1980s and 1990s cultural studies expanded enormously, above all in the United States and Australia, and as it expanded its centre of gravity moved: from class to identity, from production to consumption, from the analysis of ideology to the celebration of difference and hybridity. Race, gender, sexuality, diaspora, and nation displaced the factory and the union as the field's organising topics, and the word Marxist appeared less and less often in the field's self descriptions. Hall himself, whose own work on race and on Thatcherism never abandoned the analysis of power, watched the transformation with a mixture of pride and alarm, remarking in his later reflections on the field's institutional success that its theoretical fluency had been purchased partly at the price of its political edge, and that the encounter with the material world of institutions, the world political economy never left, remained the discipline's unfinished business.

A fair map records both readings of this migration. On the critical reading, the interpretive wing confirmed the structural wing's prophecy: a method that brackets production will drift toward the celebration of consumption, because consumption is where its evidence lives, and the drift ends with a field supplying sophisticated rationalisations for the market it set out to criticise. On the sympathetic reading, the migration followed the object. Class in the old industrial form genuinely receded as the organising experience of the societies the wing studied; identities that the classical tradition had treated as secondary turned out to be primary in people's dealings with media; and the wing's tools, encoding and decoding, the analysis of representation, the ethnography of use, proved portable to the questions that replaced the old ones. The two readings are not equally easy to hold at once, but the field's own history holds them in tension, and the student who meets cultural studies in its dispersed form needs the Birmingham original to understand what the diaspora carried away and what it left behind. For the map, the salient fact is asymmetry between the wings' fates: the interpretive wing conquered the curriculum and lost its name, while the structural wing kept its name and, for a generation, lost the curriculum. The platform era reversed the second of those fates, which is the subject of the next section.

## The platform era: the structural wing's second life

The arrival of the great platforms, the search engines, social networks, video services, and app marketplaces through which a growing share of the world's communication passes, returned the structural wing's questions to the centre of public argument with a force nobody in the field had enjoyed for decades. The questions are the wing's founding ones, translated without strain. Who owns the infrastructures of circulation, and what follows from the ownership? What is sold, and to whom: in the platform economy the audience commodity analysis requires almost no adaptation, since the user's attention and behavioural data are the products, the advertisers and data buyers the customers, and the content, supplied in large part by the users themselves, the inducement, a configuration Smythe would have recognised as his argument with the labour made literal, since the audience now produces the free lunch as well as consuming it. Scholars such as Christian Fuchs rebuilt a Marxist political economy explicitly on this ground, treating digital labour as a category of the same analysis that once covered the factory and the broadcasting audience. Regulators and legislators, pursuing their own quarrels with the platform firms, found themselves commissioning ownership studies, market analyses, and funding inquiries that reproduce the wing's method under other names.

The interpretive wing's instruments, meanwhile, found their own platform applications, which keeps the map honest: encoding and decoding reappear wherever a meme, a clip, or a campaign is remade by its audiences at industrial scale, and the ethnography of use thrives in the study of fandoms, gaming communities, and diasporic media. What has not revived is the expectation that either wing will absorb the other. Platform research programmes sort themselves, with a fidelity that would have depressed both parties to the 1990s exchange, into studies of the firms and studies of the users, published in different journals and citing different ancestors. The two-wing rule therefore closes this survey where it began, as a description of the field's permanent geometry. The tradition's questions have proved more durable than any of its answers, and the questions come in pairs: who owns the means of communication, and what do people make of what reaches them. A media theory that asks only the first becomes an account of machinery without experience. A media theory that asks only the second becomes an account of experience without machinery. The Marxist tradition's claim on the reader's attention is that it named both questions early, quarrelled over their relation in public, and left a map on which the quarrel can still be followed.

## How the wings work: a note on methods and evidence

Because the map is meant to be used, a word on how each tradition generates its claims will help the reader weigh them. The structural wing works from documents and aggregates: ownership records, accounts, policy archives, content counted across samples large enough to support claims about pattern. Its characteristic vice is the inferential leap from pattern to intention, checked by its characteristic virtue, which is that its claims can be falsified by recounting. The interpretive wing works from texts and encounters: close readings whose warrant is the reading's explanatory power, and interviews or observations whose warrant is fidelity to the informants' own sense making. Its characteristic vice is generalisation from the vivid case, checked by its characteristic virtue, which is that it can discover phenomena, irony, refusal, pleasure, that no counting instrument was built to detect. The empiricist cousins work from measurement under controlled comparison, and their vice and virtue are the familiar pair of the laboratory: precision about small questions, silence about large ones. Medium theory and its postmodern descendant work from the diagnostic essay, a form whose warrant is illumination rather than verification, and the reader should price their claims accordingly, neither dismissing insight because it arrives as aphorism nor mistaking aphorism for evidence. The working rule the persona sections below will repeat is that a claim should be believed in proportion to the fit between its scope and its method, and that most scandals in this field are scope violations: ethnographies asked to settle questions of structure, ownership studies asked to settle questions of meaning, aphorisms asked to settle anything.

Production studies deserve a sentence as the nearest thing the field has produced to a bridge. Ethnographies of newsrooms and studios, in a line running from the news manufacture studies of the 1970s to later work inside television and publishing, observe the moment where structure becomes text: the deadline that selects the story, the format that selects the angle, the professional instinct that selects the source. Both wings cite the genre and neither owns it, and students looking for a dissertation topic that the two-wing rule would approve could do worse than start where the circuit's links are forged in observable practice.

## Misreadings this article exists to correct

The summaries against which this article is written commit a small set of recurrent errors, and collecting them is a service to the reader who will meet them elsewhere within the week. It is an error to treat Marxist media theory as a single position, when its history is a quarrel between wings that disagree about the field's central question. It is an error to attribute to the tradition the claim that audiences are passive, when the claim belongs to a Frankfurt inheritance that the interpretive wing was constituted to refute and when the structural wing's careful work requires only that audiences be unequally addressed, not that they be asleep. It is an error to merge the culture industry analysis with British cultural studies as variants of one view, when the second defined itself against the first's account of reception and the two disagree about the most basic fact in the field, what an audience is. It is an error to dismiss pluralism as naive, when its empirical claims about entry, competition, and the disciplining power of audience choice are serious claims that the collapse of entry costs has made more serious. And it is an error to pronounce the field dead at the moment when finance ministries and competition authorities are conducting its characteristic inquiries into the largest communication firms in history, under the pressure of the questions the structural wing has asked for fifty years.

## A short exercise in placement

The map earns its keep only in use, so consider three arguments a reader might meet in a single morning of commentary and place each before judging it. A columnist argues that a handful of firms control what the world watches and that democracy is therefore a façade. The argument lives in the structural wing's row, in its crude rather than its careful form, and its best answer comes from two directions at once: from the pluralist row, with the evidence of insubordination and entry, and from the tradition's own careful statements, which claim asymmetry rather than control and would require the columnist to specify the mechanism. A media scholar argues that fans of a maligned serial are engaged in creative resistance to the values the serial sells. This lives in the interpretive wing's row, at its Fiskean edge, and its best answer is Garnham's: to ask what the resistance costs the owners, what it changes in the distribution of communicative resources, and whether the word resistance survives the answers. A regulator argues that the ranking systems through which citizens find news are the new commanding heights of public communication and should be governed as such. This argument lives in no single row, which is why it is interesting: it is the structural wing's question asked about the medium theorists' object, the infrastructure of attention itself, and it illustrates where the field's live frontier runs. Placement does not settle arguments. It tells the reader which evidence is relevant, which concession is owed, and which row's weakness the argument is about to exhibit.

## The quantified audience: ratings, metrics, and Smythe's revenge

The audience commodity argument lay semi dormant for a generation, respected as an ingenuity and rarely used as a tool, until the measurement of audiences became the media system's central industry and gave the argument a second life. Ratings had always been the practical form of the commodity Smythe described: the audience that advertisers buy is not the crowd of actual viewers but the measured audience, the panel, the sample, the demographic cell, and scholars of the ratings system showed how the measurement itself shapes production, since programmes are made for the audience the instruments can see. A programme beloved by viewers the panel undercounts is, commercially, a programme nobody watches. The digital environment generalised the condition. Every act of attention on a platform is recorded, aggregated, and traded, and the audience commodity is no longer assembled nightly by statisticians but continuously, by the infrastructure itself. The interpretive wing's contribution to this development is the study of how users understand and game their own measurement, curating feeds, performing for algorithms, splitting identities across accounts, activities that confirm Smythe's premise in the act of resisting it: the audience works, knows that it works, and negotiates the terms of its work with the only tools it has, which are the tools the platform supplies. A theory of the audience commodity that began as a correction to an inattentive Marxism now sits at the junction of the wings, one of the few constructions both sides cite without embarrassment.

## Objectivity as a ritual: the tradition studies journalism

The tradition's account of journalism deserves separate statement because it is the part of the theory that practitioners most often mishear as an accusation of personal dishonesty. Its classic formulations, several of them from scholars adjacent to the structural wing, describe objectivity not as a property of reports but as a strategic ritual of the profession: the practices, attribution to sources, the separation of fact and comment, the presentation of both sides, by which journalists defend their work against criticism and organise the gathering of news under deadline. The ritual's structural consequences are what interest the theory. Attribution outsources judgement to sources, and the sources best placed to be quoted, organised, available, and accredited, are institutions; the both sides convention grades standpoints by their resemblance to the institutional centre; the deadline rewards the event that can be narrated by tonight over the process that explains the decade. None of this requires a journalist to be anything other than conscientious, which is precisely the point the theory exists to make: the bias it alleges is a property of the organisation of newswork, not of the politics of news workers, and the evidence for it is to be sought in the routines, the beats, the source lists, the news values, that any newsroom ethnography can observe. Journalists who have read the argument in this form have often recognised their own working lives in it, and the ethnographic literature the argument generated, studies of news manufacture from inside the newsroom, remains among the most durable empirical work in the field's Marxist neighbourhood.

## The tabloid question: a test case for both wings

No single object has served as a testing ground for the wings' disagreement as reliably as the popular tabloid press. To the culture industry inheritance, and to a long line of commentary in its shadow, the tabloid is the commodity form at its purest: sensation standardised, politics dissolved into personality and scandal, the working class reader addressed as a bundle of appetites and resentments and confirmed in both. To the interpretive wing, that portrait commits every error in its catalogue. It infers the reader from the text; it treats pleasures it does not share as symptoms; and it ignores what ethnographic and historical work on popular papers actually found, readers using the tabloid's irreverence as a resource, enjoying its disrespect for the respectable, reading its politics with a scepticism its critics reserve for themselves, and sustaining, through the popular press, a plebeian public sphere with its own idioms of judgement. The structural wing's rejoinder refocuses the question: whatever readers make of the tabloid, the tabloid's owners have historically made of it a political instrument of unusual directness, and the paper's campaigns, its enmities, its disciplined enthusiasms, are matters of record that no amount of inventive reading dissolves. The tabloid thus exhibits the circuit whole. Production really is concentrated and instrumental; the texts really are formulaic in ways the Frankfurt analysis named; reception really is more knowing and more various than the formula implies; and the political effect of the whole arrangement is not deducible from any one of those facts. A student who can hold all four clauses in one paragraph understands the field better than a partisan of either wing who can hold only two.

## What counts as an effect: the long argument with psychology

Running beneath the quarrel of the wings is an older argument between the whole Marxist family and the psychological tradition of effects research, and the map is incomplete without it, because the rivals of the earlier sections inherited their terms from it. The effects tradition began, in the propaganda anxieties of the interwar years, with a model of direct and powerful influence that its own research promptly dismantled: the studies associated with Paul Lazarsfeld and his school found selective exposure, selective retention, and influence flowing through opinion leaders, and installed a paradigm of limited effects that ruled American communication research for a generation. The Marxist traditions objected to the paradigm from both wings at once, a rare unanimity. The objection, stated most sharply by critics such as Todd Gitlin in an influential dissection of the dominant paradigm, was that limited effects research had defined effect so narrowly, as short term change in stated opinion or vote, that it guaranteed its findings in advance: an experiment or a panel study cannot detect the slow formation of the categories within which opinions are stated, the agenda within which choices appear, or the hegemony that decides what counts as a realistic position before any campaign begins. The cousins of the earlier section, cultivation and agenda setting, can be read as the effects tradition's own internal answer to that objection, attempts to recover long term and cognitive effects within a measurable frame. The field's methodological peace, insofar as one exists, rests on the recognition that effect is a family of phenomena occurring at different timescales, and that the Marxist traditions' distinctive claims concern the slowest and deepest of them, the formation of common sense, which is also the level at which evidence is hardest to gather and rhetoric easiest to substitute for it. That is a reason for humility in the tradition's practitioners, not for the dismissal its critics sometimes attempt.

A practical consequence follows for anyone designing research in this area, and it generalises the peace into a rule of method. The timescale of the claimed effect should dictate the instrument: surveys and experiments for the season, content analysis across years for the agenda, institutional history across decades for the structure of supply, and ethnography across a viewing life for the formation of taste and expectation. Studies that borrow the instrument of a shorter timescale to test a claim pitched at a longer one will reliably report that nothing happened, and studies that do the reverse will reliably report portents. Much of the published combat between the traditions reduces, on inspection, to that single mismatch, repeated with different vocabularies by scholars who would have agreed with one another's findings had the findings been stated at their proper scale.

## Three texts that carry the tradition

A reader who wants the tradition at first hand, rather than in summary, faces a shelf that the map can usefully organise into a route. The route begins with Hall's encoding and decoding essay, short enough to read in an hour and dense enough to reread for a term, because it contains the interpretive wing's charter and its qualifications in a single text, and because its three decoding positions give the beginner a tool that works on the next programme she watches. It continues with Smythe's blindspot essay, the structural wing in its most inventive form, which teaches the reader to ask of any medium the economist's question, what is sold and to whom, a question that reorganises everything seen afterward, from the scheduling of commercial television to the design of a social feed. The third station is the Garnham and Grossberg exchange itself, read as a pair, because it performs the field's structure instead of merely describing it, and because each text, in attacking the other wing, states that wing's case with a clarity its own members rarely match. Around these three, the reader can arrange the larger monuments as appetite dictates: Policing the Crisis for the interpretive wing at full stretch, Manufacturing Consent for the structural wing's boldest construction, Dialectic of Enlightenment's culture industry chapter for the inheritance both wings claim and criticise. The route is offered as the article's practical residue, since a map that does not end in reading has failed at the purpose maps serve.

## The quarrel's second act: attempts at reconciliation

The exchange of the mid 1990s did not end the quarrel, but it changed its etiquette, and the decades since have produced a literature of attempted reconciliation that the map should register before closing. The attempts share a strategy: they rebuild the analysis as a circuit and assign each wing its moment, production, text, reception, consumption, with the moments linked by the passage of the commodity and the meaning through them, so that no moment is foundational and each constrains the others. David Morley's later work explicitly reconnected reception to the material geographies of media distribution. Scholars of media industries built production studies into a subfield with its own journals. Political economists such as Mosco proposed a mature statement of their wing in which commodification is only one of three entry points, alongside the structuring of social relations and the spatial reach of communication, a broadening designed to answer the charge of economism from inside the wing itself. Cultural studies, in its more reflective statements, conceded the bracketing of production as a genuine loss and began citing ownership research it would once have disdained. Whether these courtesies amount to a synthesis or to a polite partition is a judgement the reader is now equipped to make. The criterion this article proposes is the one the two-wing rule supplies: a reconciliation is real where a single study's findings in one moment of the circuit change what can be claimed in another, and ceremonial where the moments are merely juxtaposed between the same covers. By that criterion the reconciliation is real in the best work and ceremonial in the average textbook, which is perhaps the most that can be said for any peace treaty in an academic field.

## Why Britain: the social formation that produced cultural studies

The interpretive wing's Britishness is not an accident of personnel, and explaining it displays the tradition's own method turned on itself. Postwar Britain combined a powerful public broadcaster, a surviving industrial working class with strong institutions of its own, an adult education movement that carried university teaching into union halls and mining towns, and a New Left formed by the twin shocks of 1956, the Soviet invasion of Hungary and the Suez adventure, which sent a generation of socialists looking for a politics that began from culture and everyday life rather than from the party and the plan. Hoggart's The Uses of Literacy, published in 1957, studied the working class culture of his own Leeds childhood and asked what the new commercial entertainments, the milk bars, the magazines, the paperback thrillers, were doing to an older, denser fabric of life; his answer was neither celebration nor lament but close description, and the method, autobiography disciplined by scholarship, became the wing's signature. Williams supplied the theory of the whole formation in the idea of a long revolution, the slow democratisation of learning and communication as the real content of social change. Thompson supplied the historical depth and the polemic against any Marxism that treated ordinary people as the bearers of structures rather than the makers of their history. When Hall, a Jamaican Rhodes scholar who had edited the New Left Review and taught in adult and further education before joining Hoggart's Centre, drew these lines together with the structuralism arriving from France, the result was a Marxism whose central evidence was experience and whose central suspicion was of any theory that could not make room for it. The wing's later quarrel with political economy was therefore also a quarrel between national formations: the political economists, several of them formed by the same New Left, insisted that the British settlement itself, its broadcaster, its press, its advertising market, be made an object of analysis, and that a cultural studies which celebrated the audience's corner of that settlement while ignoring its architecture had mistaken the part it could see for the whole it could not.

The formation also explains the wing's characteristic blind spot at its origin, which its feminist critics exposed. The everyday life the founding texts described was the everyday life of men and boys, its public houses, workplaces, and street corners, while the media that filled the home, the serial, the magazine, the afternoon film, were gendered feminine and correspondingly undervalued in a hierarchy of seriousness the wing had inherited from the literary culture it opposed. The correction, when it came from scholars such as McRobbie, Dorothy Hobson, and Christine Geraghty, did not merely add women to the sample. It changed the theory's picture of where ideology works, relocating much of it from the public spectacle to the domestic routine, from the message beamed at a mass to the programme woven through housework, childcare, and the management of feeling, and it modelled the way the tradition at its best revises itself: by showing that its own categories, experience, the ordinary, the audience, had been applied with a partiality their logic forbade.

## The American encounter: Adorno, Lazarsfeld, and the two kinds of research

The tradition's methodological self understanding was sharpened by an encounter in American exile that reads like a parable of the wings before the wings existed. In the late 1930s the Princeton Radio Research Project, directed by the Austrian émigré sociologist Paul Lazarsfeld, employed Adorno to direct its music study, and the collaboration between the apostle of empirical administrative research and the philosopher of the culture industry failed instructively. Lazarsfeld wanted to know what listeners liked, in categories that could be counted, and how programmes might be improved for the broadcasters who funded the inquiry. Adorno wanted to know what the structure of broadcast music was doing to listening itself, and produced memoranda on the atomisation of musical experience and the fetish character of hit songs that his employers found unusable. Lazarsfeld, to his credit, wrote the distinction the episode revealed into an essay of 1941, contrasting administrative research, conducted in the service of media institutions and their practical problems, with critical research, which asks whether the institutions and their problems are themselves the problem. The essay remains the cleanest one page map of the methodological divide that runs through the whole field, because it shows that the quarrel is not between rigour and speculation but between two decisions about whom research serves, a decision that precedes method and quietly governs it. The effects tradition that Lazarsfeld's school founded inherited the administrative decision and its blindnesses, which the Marxist critics of the dominant paradigm would catalogue a generation later; the culture industry analysis inherited the critical decision and its corresponding vice, an immunity to the audiences it theorised, whose actual testimony administrative research had at least been obliged to collect. The two wings of the later tradition can be read as attempts, from opposite sides, to escape the inheritance of that failed collaboration, political economy by giving critical research an empirical object in ownership and cultural studies by giving it empirical ears in the audience.

## Determination rethought: limits, pressures, and the long leash

The word that decides whether the tradition's claims are sensible is determination, and the tradition's own best theorist of the word was Williams, whose account of it was devised precisely for cultural questions. Against the mechanical picture, in which a base fixes a superstructure as a foundation fixes a building, Williams proposed that determination in social life means the setting of limits and the exertion of pressures: real forces that make some outcomes impossible, others costly, others easy and rewarded, within which human practice is genuinely creative and genuinely answerable for what it makes. Transferred to media, the formula dissolves the crudest dispute in this article. Ownership determines in Williams's sense. It sets limits, since a commercial broadcaster will not, except by accident or subsidy, produce what destroys its revenue model; it exerts pressures, since careers, budgets, and formats reward some kinds of content and starve others; and within those limits and pressures, journalists, producers, and audiences make things that cannot be read off from the ownership structure, including things the structure's owners deplore. Hall's work runs on the same fuel: his insistence that class relations are lived through race, gender, and nation, and that no correspondence between economic position and cultural meaning is guaranteed, is Williams's determination applied to the politics of a whole society, and his concept of articulation, the joining of elements that have no necessary belonging, is the positive machinery that replaces the old reflection model. A student who masters this single distinction has the key to the tradition's mature form, because nearly every position on the map can be restated as a claim about how tight the determination runs at a particular link: the culture industry says very tight at the link of form, political economy says tight at the link of supply and loose at the link of meaning, cultural studies says loose at the link of meaning and refuses to be hurried about the rest, and the rivals say, in their different idioms, that at the links they study the determination is either absent or belongs to some other cause entirely.

## Donald Duck in Chile: the imperialism thesis at street level

The media imperialism argument acquired its most famous popular text in Chile, where the Belgian born scholar Armand Mattelart, having settled in Santiago, joined with Ariel Dorfman to publish in 1971 a close reading of Walt Disney comics under the title How to Read Donald Duck. The book took the comics sold to children across Latin America and decoded them as ideology in narrative form: a world of treasure hunts in colonised landscapes whose natives gratefully assist the ducks, of wealth that appears by luck and inheritance rather than by labour, of societies without production, without parents, and without history, in which the relations of the metropolitan centre to the periphery are naturalised as adventure. The book became a bestseller in Latin America, was banned and burned after the Chilean coup of 1973, and survives as the emblematic performance of ideology critique applied to entertainment: no messages inserted by conspirators, only the quiet pedagogy of a form. Its critics, including later scholars sympathetic to its aims, raised the two objections this article has taught the reader to expect. From the reception side, no Chilean child was consulted in its pages, and subsequent audience research in Latin America showed readers making local and sometimes oppositional sense of imported material in defiance of the text's preferred pedagogy. From the production side, Latin America's own media industries were already building the regional empires, the telenovela exporters of Mexico and Brazil, that would complicate any simple geography of centre and periphery. The episode is preserved here because it contains the whole argument of this article in miniature: a brilliant textual reading, an absent audience, an asymmetric structure, and a political fate, suppression by a dictatorship, that testified to the stakes all parties believed were in play when children read comics.

## Resistance and its discontents: how much can a reading do?

The interpretive wing's most contested word is resistance, and the internal argument over it is the sharpest test of the wing's intellectual honesty. In the strong version associated with Fiske's work of the late 1980s, popular culture is a battlefield on which subordinate audiences raid the products of the culture industries and remake them into pleasures and meanings that evade, mock, or invert the dominant order: the torn jeans, the fan's rewritten ending, the shopper's tactical use of the mall are semiotic guerrilla warfare, and their accumulation constitutes a progressive politics of the everyday. The criticism, mounted from inside the house by McGuigan and from the political economy side by Garnham, is that this version empties resistance of content. An act that costs the powerful nothing, changes no distribution of resources, and leaves every structure intact has been promoted by a metaphor, and the promotion serves identifiable interests: it flatters the scholar as the discoverer of heroism in the ordinary, it flatters the audience as already emancipated, and it flatters the industries, whose ideal consumer is precisely the active, creative, meaning making chooser of the semiotic democracy. The defenders' counter argument deserves its weight. Political change has preconditions in consciousness; the refusal of deference, practised in style and taste long before it reaches a ballot or a picket line, is a historical force the tradition's own Gramscian inheritance should recognise, since hegemony is lost in the culture before it is lost in the state; and a standard of resistance that counts only what moves resources immediately would have dismissed, in their day, most of the cultural materials out of which actual movements were built. The mature position the field settled toward treats resistance as a scalar and situated judgement rather than a compliment: a reading is resistant relative to a specified power, at a specified cost, with specified consequences, and the scholar's task is to fill in the three specifications rather than to distribute the word as praise. In that chastened form the concept survives, and the distance between it and the Fiskean celebration measures the distance the wing travelled under fire from its sibling.

## Reading a text against the grain: the interpretive method in practice

Because summaries of cultural studies often leave its method as a slogan, an account of the method at work belongs in this article. The analyst begins where the structuralist inheritance directs, inside the text, asking how it is organised: what oppositions structure it, whose point of view the camera, the narrative, or the headline adopts, what is present as unargued background and what must be argued for, how its closure resolves the tensions it raises and what the resolution costs. She then asks the encoding question in its concrete form: under what institutional and generic conditions was this made, by whom, for which imagined audience, with what taken for granted about that audience's world. Only then does she turn to decodings, her own included, treating them as data about the repertoires available in a culture rather than as verdicts, and comparing the readings that different social positions make likely, the professional critic's, the fan community's, the casual viewer's half attention, the hostile reading of the group the text represents. The point of the sequence is discipline. Each stage checks the one before: the text's organisation limits the readings that can honestly be attributed to it, the conditions of encoding limit the intentions that can plausibly be ascribed, and the observed decodings limit both, since a reading no actual audience produces may be a possibility of the text but is not a fact of the culture. Practised this way, the method is neither the free association its detractors describe nor the decoding of a hidden message its novices expect. It is a way of holding the circuit's middle links up to the light, and its results, the preferred reading specified, the negotiated and oppositional variants documented, are the findings the structural wing must somehow accommodate and the effects tradition must somehow measure, which is why this method, for all the quarrels around it, became the field's common property.

## Consent in the entertainment state: the Gramscian thread

Gramsci's concept of hegemony is the thread that runs under both wings and surfaces wherever the tradition explains the normal, uncoerced operation of media power, and its media application deserves a fuller statement than the signposts so far. Hegemony names the condition in which a social order secures the active consent of those it subordinates, not by deceiving them about facts but by organising the common sense within which facts are interpreted, so that the order's arrangements appear as the natural furniture of the world and alternatives appear, when they appear at all, as fantasy or threat. Applied to media, the concept redirects attention from the exceptional case, the censored story and the proprietor's instruction, to the ordinary case: the range of opinions treated as the spectrum of reasonable debate, the questions that structure an interview, the casting of roles in the national story, the slow education of feeling by which some lives are presented as exemplary and others as cautionary. Hall's work on Thatcherism gave the concept its most influential political application, analysing how a governing project could construct a popular authoritarianism out of genuine grievances, law and order, resentment of bureaucracy, the defence of the family, and win consent for an economic transformation that injured many of those who consented. Media, in that analysis, were neither mirrors nor masters but the terrain on which the construction was assembled, amplifying some articulations of grievance and starving others. The concept's discipline is worth stating, since it is the one most often abused in casual use. Hegemony is not a synonym for dominance and still less for brainwashing: it implies concessions, negotiation, and the real incorporation of subordinate interests in partial forms, and it is always incomplete and contestable, which is why the tradition that uses it carefully pairs every analysis of consent with a search for the counter hegemonic work that media also, undeniably, carry.

## Propaganda and the state: the tradition's dark school

The tradition learned some of its sharpest lessons from the state's use of media in the age of total war, and the episode belongs on the map because it anchors the structural wing's claims about sourcing and agenda in events nobody disputes. The First World War demonstrated that modern states could organise persuasion as an industry, with ministries, budgets, and professional staffs, and the interwar exposure of wartime fabrication left a generation of scholars, Harold Lasswell among them, treating propaganda as a technique to be analysed scientifically. Marxist analysts drew a different moral from the same record than the effects researchers did. Where administrative research asked how persuasion works and how its targets might be defended or reached, the Marxist line asked whose persuasion apparatus a peacetime society maintains without naming it as such, and found its answer in the ordinary structures the structural wing studies: the news agency dependent on official access, the broadcaster chartered by the state, the press baron whose patriotism and whose property point the same way. The Second World War and the Cold War supplied the tradition with its standing archive of cases, from the management of war reporting to the cultural programmes by which the superpowers subsidised magazines, exhibitions, and broadcasting across the contested world, programmes whose exposure periodically embarrassed the pluralist picture of an independent cultural sphere. The lesson the tradition drew is not that all media are propaganda, a conclusion its careful writers avoid, but that the boundary between information and state persuasion is an institutional achievement rather than a natural fact, maintained where structures maintain it and collapsing where they do not, and that a media theory without a theory of the state will mistake the achievement for the nature of things.

## The map in the curriculum: why this field is taught as a quarrel

There is a final, reflexive reason the two-wing structure matters, which is that it has become the form in which the subject is transmitted. Textbooks and syllabi across the English speaking world organise media theory as a sequence of positions in contention, Marxist approaches against pluralist, effects against uses, text against audience, and students meet the tradition first as one contestant in a tournament of theories. The arrangement has costs this article has resisted where it could. It freezes living research programmes into doctrines with dates of death, encourages the examination technique of the balanced survey in which every theory receives a paragraph and none receives a hearing, and conceals the extent to which the positions on the map grew out of one another's failures, pluralism out of the ruins of the hypodermic model, cultural studies out of the ruins of the culture industry's audience, platform political economy out of Smythe's blindspot. It also has one great virtue, which the tournament's critics concede: it teaches placement before judgement, and placement, the ability to say which question a theory answers and which it cannot hear, is the beginning of theoretical literacy in any field. The reader who has followed this article's map holds the tournament's bracket in a stronger form than the textbooks supply, because the bracket here comes with the quarrel's history, its evidence, and its unfinished business attached.

## Free labour and participatory culture: the argument renewed online

The first decades of the networked media environment staged the wings' quarrel again in new costumes, and the episode is worth recording because both sides can plausibly claim it. On one side stood an optimistic literature of participation, associated above all with Henry Jenkins and his account of convergence culture, in which audiences armed with cheap tools of production and distribution cease to be audiences at all and become participants: fans building vast collective intelligences around the narratives they love, citizens routing around gatekeepers, grassroots media challenging the monopoly of the professionals. The picture descends, knowingly or not, from the interpretive wing's discoveries, the active audience finally equipped with the means of production, and it arrived with the gratifications tradition's weakness built in, a tendency to take the menu of platforms as a fact of nature and to celebrate the choices made within it. On the other side stood a Marxist literature that began, remarkably early, with Tiziana Terranova's account of free labour at the turn of the century: the volunteering, building, moderating, and content producing activity on which the digital economy floats, simultaneously voluntary and exploited, given freely and captured as value. Terranova's essay is the closest the field has come to a genuine synthesis in a single text, since it takes the interpretive wing's finding, that audiences are productive, and subjects it to the structural wing's question, who owns the product of that productivity, arriving at the audience commodity's platform form by logic rather than by analogy. The subsequent literature on digital labour, on playbour, on the data relations of the platform economy, descends from that junction. Jenkins's participants and Terranova's free labourers are the same people described from the two ends of the circuit, and a media theory adequate to its century is the one that can keep both descriptions in focus without letting either dissolve the other.

## A single broadcast, four analyses

The map's use can be fixed by running one object through it. Take a flagship television news bulletin of the commercial era at the height of its powers. The structural wing analyses the bulletin as an industrial product: the network's ownership and its regulatory bargains, the advertisers whose purchases schedule its breaks and select its audience, the economics that staff its foreign coverage thinly and its weather handsomely, the source dependencies that fill its running order with the day's institutional agenda. The analysis explains why the bulletin exists in this form and why its silences fall where they do, and it says nothing reliable about what any viewer made of last night's edition. The textual analyst, the interpretive wing's first shift, reads the bulletin as a form: the anchor's performed neutrality, the grammar of the package with its correspondent's voice laid over pictures cut to confirm it, the hierarchy of the running order teaching importance by position, the closing human interest item discharging the tensions the hour accumulated. She explains how the bulletin proposes a world and a preferred position from which to view it. The ethnographer completes the circuit in the living room, where the bulletin competes with conversation, homework, and the telephone, where its authority is borrowed for arguments it never envisaged and its solemnities are mocked by viewers who nevertheless plan around it, and where its real effects, on what a household takes to be discussable and urgent, are assembled from fragments no content analysis counted. The rival traditions then take their slices: the gratifications researcher explains why this household chooses this bulletin, loyalty, habit, the trusted face; the agenda setting researcher measures the correspondence between the bulletin's emphases and the public's ranking of issues across the season; the medium theorist remarks that all four analyses are about content while the bulletin, as television, has already done its deepest work by making the world's events into a nightly domestic ceremony regardless of which events they are. No slice refutes another. Each is blind where another sees, and the bulletin, whole, is visible only to a reader who can move the analyses past one another without confusing their currencies.

## The tradition's institutions: journals, departments, and a long march

A school and tradition article owes its reader one further piece of the map, the institutional carriers without which ideas do not survive their authors. The structural wing was carried by journals such as Media, Culture and Society, founded in 1979 as a home for the sociological study of media that the wing represented, and by university departments of media and communication, several of them in the newer British universities, where political economy became a teachable curriculum with textbooks to match, Vincent Mosco's synthesis of the field foremost among them. The interpretive wing was carried by the Birmingham Centre's stencilled papers, which circulated as samizdat through the 1970s and were collected at the decade's end, by the journals Screen and Cultural Studies on either side of the Atlantic, and in time by entire associations and conference circuits in which the wing's vocabulary became the profession's common tongue. Screen theory, by contrast, was carried almost entirely by a single journal and a single generation of film departments, which explains the completeness of its collapse when the journal changed direction and the generation dispersed: constructions that live in one institution die with it, while constructions that live in curricula, textbooks, and associations survive the refutation of their details. The platform era's revival of political economy is being carried, characteristically, by a mixture of academic programmes and regulatory demand, research centres funded to study data and platforms whose reports ministries actually read, a carrier the broadcasting age's structural wing, dependent on sympathetic departments, never enjoyed. The reader mapping the field's future could do worse than watch the carriers: the next wing war, if there is one, will be announced in the founding of journals before it is declared in any manifesto.

## Questions to carry away: the toolkit in prose

For the reader who finishes this article in a library rather than in an argument, its apparatus compresses into a sequence of questions that can be put to any medium, any outlet, any text, in order. Ask first who owns the means by which this reaches you, and what that owner sells, and to whom, since the answer locates the object in the structural wing's territory and predicts whole classes of fact about it. Ask what the object is as a text, how it is organised, what reading it prefers, what it treats as background and what as scandal, since the answer locates it for the interpretive wing's first method. Ask then who actually receives it, in what situations, with what repertoires, and what they are observed to make of it, and refuse to let the first two answers substitute for this one. Ask what the rivals would notice that you have not: the pluralist's question about entry and insubordination, the gratifications question about the user's own purposes, the cultivation question about the slow drift of a heavy diet, the medium question about the form beneath the content. And ask, finally, the two-wing question of the whole inquiry: which claims in it rest on production, which on reception, and whether any conclusion has been smuggled across the border between them. A reader who runs that sequence habitually has acquired the tradition's usable core, whether or not she ever calls herself by its name.

## Williams on television: flow against the medium theorists

One construction from inside the tradition deserves rescue from the survey summaries, because it answers medium theory on its own ground and anticipates the platform environment with unsettling accuracy. In Television: Technology and Cultural Form, published in 1974, Raymond Williams argued against both the technological determinists, for whom television's effects flowed from the device, and the content analysts, for whom television was a neutral pipe for programmes. His counter concept was flow: the real object of broadcast television, he proposed, is not the discrete programme but the planned sequence, the evening's braided stream of programmes, trailers, advertisements, and announcements, designed as a whole to hold the viewer through the night. The implications run in several directions at once. For the analysis of content, flow dissolves the unit the critics thought they were judging, since a programme's meaning is inflected by its neighbours, by the advertisements that interrupt and frame it, and by the channel's promise of what comes next. For political economy, flow is the commodity form of broadcasting made visible as form: the stream exists to deliver the audience across the evening to the advertisers who bought segments of it, so that Smythe's transaction can be watched happening in the organisation of the text itself. For medium theory, flow is the demonstration Williams promised: the apparently technical character of the medium, its continuous availability and domestic flow, is not a property of the apparatus but a social form, selected and developed, in his phrase, with purposes in mind, since other televisions were technically possible and were, in public service systems, partially built. The concept's afterlife confirms its reach. The scheduled stream gave way, in the platform environment, to the algorithmic feed, an unending, personalised flow assembled for each user from an inventory no editor sequences, and analysts of that environment reach for Williams's word because no newer one does the work: the feed is flow perfected, the planned sequence automated, the audience delivered not across an evening but across a life, one refresh at a time. A concept coined for three channel broadcasting that still organises the analysis of the algorithmic feed is the strongest evidence this article can offer for its recurring claim, that the tradition's best constructions were better than the polemics conducted in their names, and that the map's rows are best judged by the tools they leave in the reader's hands.

There is a methodological moral in Williams's example that the closing assessment will generalise. Flow was produced by a scholar who watched the object closely, in his own living room and in hotel rooms abroad where he compared broadcasting systems channel by channel, and who refused the two shortcuts his field offered, the deduction of the object from its ownership and the deduction of its effects from its intentions. The tradition's classics share that refusal. Hall's model came from a study group arguing over news photographs; Smythe's commodity from an economist's irritation at a literature that never asked what was sold; Morley's decodings from a room full of viewers who declined to behave as theory predicted. Where the tradition has been sterile, it has usually been sterile at a distance from its objects, in the commentary on commentary that every successful field accumulates. That is a failing it shares with its rivals, and naming it here is part of the neutrality this article owes: the map is not a league table of virtue but a guide to where the evidence for each kind of claim is made.

## Closing assessment

What, then, does the tradition amount to, weighed whole? Its permanent contributions are three. It established that media are institutions before they are messages, and that the analysis of ownership, funding, and regulation is not an optional preface to media studies but part of its core. It established, through the interpretive wing, that meaning is made at reception under unequal conditions, and that no account of media power which omits the audience's work can describe its object. And it left, in the quarrel between those establishments, a working demonstration that a research tradition can contain its own best critics, since the most damaging objections to each wing were formulated by the other and the field is more usable for the damage. Its permanent liabilities are also three. A tendency, strongest in its polemical rather than its research literature, to let suspicion outrun evidence and to treat the unmasking of an interest as the refutation of a claim. A chronic difficulty in specifying the mechanisms that connect structure to text to effect with the precision its empiricist cousins achieve on smaller questions. And a recurring temptation, visible in both wings at their worst, to know the conclusion of an inquiry before the inquiry begins. Weighed against the rivals, the tradition remains the only one on the map that insists on asking both of the field's founding questions at once, about ownership and about meaning, and the insistence, more than any single finding, is what a reader should carry away: the two-wing rule as a habit of mind, applied to every media argument, including the ones made in this article's own tradition and including this article.

## For students and exam candidates

Media theory is examined heavily at school and undergraduate level, and the answers that score are the ones that treat it as a map rather than a list. Learn the two wings as positions with questions attached: political economy asks who owns and funds media and what that does to what circulates; cultural studies asks how meanings are made and remade at reception. An answer that can state the two-wing rule, illustrate each wing with one well understood construction, Smythe's audience commodity on one side and Hall's encoding and decoding on the other, and then deploy the quarrel between the wings as evaluation has the architecture markers reward. Keep the separations clean. In Hall's model, separate comprehension from agreement and polysemy from pluralism; in Smythe, separate the commodity, the audience, from the inducement, the programme. Place the rivals accurately and credit them before criticising them: pluralism with its market of ideas and its evidence of insubordination, uses and gratifications with its active and purposive user, cultivation and agenda setting with their measured and modest effects. The commonest failure is the theorist list, names in a row with a doctrine apiece and no relations among them; the second commonest is attributing to the whole tradition a claim, passive audiences, manufactured consent by instruction, that one wing or one text is made to carry for all. Save your notes and build a citation linked reading list for the theorists as you meet them; keeping each thinker attached to a wing, a text, and an opponent is the revision habit that turns this topic from a memory test into an argument you can run, and a tool such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) is built for exactly that kind of organised study.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The productive way to teach this material is to stage the quarrel rather than to survey the positions. Set Garnham against Grossberg early, in extract, and let the class discover that each man's description of the other is the clearest short statement of both wings available; the exchange teaches the field's structure faster than any lecture on it. Follow with a placement exercise on the model of the one in this article: distribute unattributed arguments from journalism and from scholarship and require the room to assign each to a row of the media theory map and to name the row from which its strongest answer would come. Two misconceptions need preempting. Students arrive believing that the tradition claims audiences are passive, and the quickest corrective is Morley's Nationwide study, whose unruly decodings make the interpretive wing's point in an afternoon. They also arrive believing that ownership settles content, and the corrective there is the paired case method, two outlets of similar ownership and divergent output, two of divergent ownership and similar output, from which the class can build the mediated account itself. Keep the treatment balanced by requiring every criticism of a wing to be answered from within the wing's own best texts before rival traditions are allowed their turn.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For researchers, the live literature divides as this article's map predicts. The structural wing's programme is being pursued, under the banner of platform studies and the political economy of digital media, in work on data extraction, infrastructural ownership, and the governance of ranking systems, and its open problems are problems of mechanism: how ownership and funding translate into the behaviour of algorithmic systems whose outputs no proprietor scripts. The interpretive programme persists in audience and fan studies and in the ethnography of platform use, with its open problem the reverse one: how local acts of interpretation aggregate into the cultural facts that move markets and elections. Integrated work remains rare enough to be a career opportunity, and production studies, the ethnography of the institutions where structure becomes content, are its most promising site. Historians should note that the field's archive is unusually self documenting, since the quarrel between the wings was conducted in print and the Birmingham Centre's papers and the journals of both camps repay archival work. Journalists, finally, are this tradition's object as well as its readers, and its claim about their profession deserves a precise statement: it is not that journalists serve owners, but that the economics and sourcing routines of news production systematically advantage some definitions of events, a claim testable against any week's coverage and worth testing rather than resenting.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Marxist media theory?

Marxist media theory is a family of approaches that treat media as institutions embedded in the ownership, funding, and power relations of a capitalist society, rather than as neutral channels for messages. It divides into a political economy wing, which studies who owns media, how they are financed, and how those arrangements constrain what circulates, and a cultural studies wing, which studies how meanings are encoded in texts and decoded by audiences in different social positions. The two wings share the premise that communication is connected to the reproduction of social relations, and they disagree, productively and in public, about where that connection does its work.

### Q: Who are the main Marxist media theorists?

The names organise themselves by wing. The Frankfurt inheritance contributes Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer, authors of the culture industry analysis, with Walter Benjamin as its great internal dissenter. The political economy wing is represented by Herbert Schiller, Armand Mattelart, Dallas Smythe, Graham Murdock, Peter Golding, and Nicholas Garnham, with Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky as the authors of the propaganda model. The interpretive wing centres on Stuart Hall and the Birmingham Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies, with Richard Hoggart and Raymond Williams as founders, and David Morley, Ien Ang, and Dick Hebdige among its researchers. Laura Mulvey stands for screen theory, the tradition's psychoanalytic episode.

### Q: What is the political economy of communication?

The political economy of communication is the structural wing of Marxist media theory: the study of media as industries and institutions, concerned with who owns them, how they are funded, how advertising and markets shape their output, and how states regulate them. Its method is historical and institutional rather than textual, working from ownership records, accounts, and policy settlements. Its characteristic claim is not that owners dictate content but that the arrangements of production create systematic asymmetries, deciding which voices are resourced and amplified and which never reach a public, and setting the limits within which journalists, producers, and audiences then act with whatever freedom those limits leave.

### Q: How does cultural studies differ from political economy?

The two differ in object, method, and evidence. Political economy studies production: ownership, funding, and regulation, using institutional and historical methods, and it explains the conditions under which media content is made. Cultural studies studies reception and meaning: the organisation of texts and the interpretations audiences make of them, using textual analysis and ethnography, and it explains what media do in people's lives once content circulates. Each regards the other's object as secondary, which is the substance of their famous quarrel. They are best understood as analyses of different moments of one circuit, the moment of making and the moment of meaning, neither of which can be reduced to the other.

### Q: What are the main alternatives to Marxist media theory?

The principal rivals are liberal pluralism, which treats media as a competitive marketplace of ideas in which entry and audience choice discipline power; uses and gratifications, which studies audiences as active users selecting media to satisfy their own needs; the measured effects traditions of cultivation and agenda setting, which quantify how exposure shapes perceptions of risk and judgements of importance; medium theory in McLuhan's line, which locates media power in the form of the medium itself; and the postmodern position associated with Baudrillard, which holds that representation has detached from reality altogether. Each rival answers some questions better than the Marxist traditions do, and the honest comparison is by question, not by slogan.

### Q: What is encoding and decoding?

Encoding and decoding is Stuart Hall's model of how media meaning travels. Producers encode meaning into a text under institutional conditions and professional codes, constructing a preferred reading, the interpretation the text is built to favour. Audiences then decode from one of three broad positions: the dominant position, accepting the preferred reading; the negotiated position, accepting the frame while adapting it to their own circumstances; or the oppositional position, understanding the preferred reading and rejecting it in favour of an alternative framework. The model's key discipline is the separation of comprehension from agreement: a viewer can decode exactly as intended and still refuse the meaning entirely.

### Q: Is Marxist media theory still used?

Yes, in both its wings, though often under changed names. The political economy programme thrives in research on platform ownership, data extraction, and the governance of the infrastructures through which communication passes, and Smythe's audience commodity argument has become a standard tool for analysing services funded by advertising and attention. The cultural studies programme persists in audience, fan, and reception studies and in the analysis of representation, even where its practitioners no longer use the Marxist label. The tradition's questions, who owns the means of communication and what audiences make of what reaches them, structure far more research than the tradition's name is credited with.

### Q: What did Screen theory argue?

Screen theory, named for the journal Screen and flourishing in the 1970s, fused Althusser's theory of ideology with Lacanian psychoanalysis and film semiotics. It argued that the cinema apparatus itself, the darkened auditorium, the immobilised spectator, the larger than life image, positions the viewer as a self sufficient subject, so that ideology operates not through a film's message but through the organisation of looking the viewing situation installs. Laura Mulvey's account of the male gaze is its most famous product. The programme declined as audience research demonstrated the variety of actual spectators, but its central thought, that the subject who watches is partly made by the watching, survived in later spectatorship theory.

### Q: Does media ownership determine content?

Not in the mechanical sense of owners dictating output, which the serious literature does not claim. Ownership determines in the weaker and more defensible sense of setting limits and exerting pressures: it selects senior personnel, fixes commercial strategy, rewards some kinds of content and starves others, and creates a climate in which editorial judgement anticipates what will be acceptable. Within those pressures, professional norms, competition, and the commercial value of credibility leave real room for reporting that embarrasses owners and governments. The defensible claim is about agendas and absences, about what is covered lavishly and what is never commissioned, rather than about journalists taking instructions.

### Q: What is the strongest criticism of Marxist media theory?

The strongest criticism combines an empirical charge with a methodological one. Empirically, decades of audience research show interpretation, resistance, and indifference where the tradition's cruder statements predicted absorption, and the diversification of media supply has falsified predictions of uniform content ground out by ownership. Methodologically, the tradition is accused of protecting itself, crediting conformity to its filters and deviation to special circumstances, so that no evidence could count against it. The criticism lands hardest on the polemical versions of the theory. Its careful research programmes answer by narrowing their claims: structural asymmetry in communicative resources rather than control of content, and meanings contested at reception rather than imposed by texts.

### Q: How does the culture industry thesis differ from a simple effects model?

A simple effects model imagines messages injected into audiences and looks for resulting changes in opinion or behaviour. The culture industry thesis of Adorno and Horkheimer is not about messages at all. It argues that commercial culture is standardised in its very form, that its products train perception toward recognition and away from sustained attention, and that amusement under these conditions reconciles people to the existing order by occupying and shaping their leisure. The damage it alleges is therefore formal and dispositional rather than attitudinal: not that a film persuades you of a proposition, but that an entire diet of formulaic pleasure alters what kinds of experience you remain capable of having.

### Q: What should a student separate in Stuart Hall's encoding and decoding model?

Three separations earn marks. First, separate the moments: encoding by producers under institutional conditions, and decoding by audiences, are distinct processes, and success at the first does not guarantee the second. Second, separate comprehension from agreement: an oppositional decoder understands the preferred reading perfectly and rejects it, whereas a confused viewer has not decoded oppositionally at all. Third, separate polysemy from pluralism: Hall insisted that texts are open to several readings but not to any reading, since their construction exerts pressure toward the preferred meaning. A student who keeps those separations clean can use the model analytically instead of reciting it as a slogan about active audiences.

### Q: Why do political economy and cultural studies accounts of media clash?

They clash because each treats the other's central object as an afterthought, and each has a politics invested in the slight. Political economy holds that cultural studies, by bracketing ownership and funding, celebrates the audience's activity inside an enclosure it never examines, and drifts toward applauding the consumer choices the market also applauds. Cultural studies replies that political economy infers effects from structures, has no working account of meaning, pleasure, or interpretation, and reproduces the base and superstructure picture the tradition claims to have outgrown. The quarrel became explicit in the journal exchange between Nicholas Garnham and Lawrence Grossberg in the mid 1990s and remains unsettled because both charges are partly true.

### Q: How does Dallas Smythe's audience commodity argument change media analysis?

Smythe asked what commercial media actually sell, and answered that it is not programmes but audiences: the attention of viewers and listeners, packaged by size and demographic profile and sold to advertisers, with programmes serving as the inducement that assembles the commodity. The argument relocates the analysis of media from content to transaction. It explains why content takes the forms it does, why some audiences are courted and others ignored, and why audience measurement is the industry's central science. Extended to advertising funded platforms, where users supply both the content and the behavioural data that are sold, the argument has become one of the tradition's most used tools.

### Q: What do rival theories explain that Marxist media theory handles badly?

Rival theories are strongest at the levels the Marxist traditions neglect. Uses and gratifications research explains the texture of individual choice, why particular people seek particular media for articulable purposes, better than any account that begins from structure. Pluralism explains the observable insubordination of media in open societies, the exposures and embarrassments of the powerful, which structural accounts handle awkwardly. Cultivation and agenda setting supply measured, quantified effects of a kind the tradition rarely attempts. Medium theory identifies consequences of a medium's form that content analysis of any school misses. The Marxist traditions answer at the level of institutions and interpretation, and the price of that altitude is weakness at these lower levels.
