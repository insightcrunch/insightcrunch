Set The Great Gatsby beside Absalom, Absalom! and the American Dream stops being one idea. It becomes two incompatible machines for grinding people down. That is the payoff of reading Fitzgerald vs Faulkner on the American Dream: not a ranking, not a regional curiosity, but a diagnosis. Both writers watched a man build himself from nothing, and both watched the building collapse. What separates them is what they think the wreckage is made of. For Fitzgerald it is a personal history a man tried to rewind. For Faulkner it is a collective history a whole region cannot put down.

![Fitzgerald vs Faulkner on the American Dream](/assets/images/blog/blog-05.webp)

The comparison is usually skipped because the two writers look nothing alike on the page. Fitzgerald wrote a novel you can finish on a train ride. Faulkner wrote sentences you can lose an afternoon in. Fitzgerald's America runs from West Egg to the Plaza Hotel. Faulkner's runs through a single invented Mississippi county he mapped and populated across a dozen books. The surface difference is so loud that the shared subject goes unheard. And the shared subject is enormous: an American who believes the future can be built by will, and an author who knows the ground under that man is already occupied by something he did not put there.

## What Is Being Compared in Fitzgerald vs Faulkner on the American Dream

This article compares two authors, not two novels, though two novels do most of the work. On the Fitzgerald side, The Great Gatsby, published in 1925, with its self-invented protagonist and its narrator who arrives late and understands slowly. On the Faulkner side, principally Absalom, Absalom!, published in 1936, with Thomas Sutpen, a poor boy from the Virginia mountains who is turned away from a plantation door and spends the rest of his life constructing a dynasty to answer that insult. Around these sit the rest of each writer's output: Fitzgerald's stories of the moneyed young, Faulkner's Yoknapatawpha novels including The Sound and the Fury and Light in August and Go Down, Moses.

The comparison is not arbitrary. Sutpen is the nearest thing American literature has to a second Gatsby: a man with a name he made, a house he built to be seen, a woman at the center of a plan, and an ending that dismantles every piece of it. The similarity is close enough that the differences become legible. When two writers build the same machine and it fails in two different ways, the failure modes tell you what each writer believes about the country.

Fitzgerald and Faulkner were near contemporaries. Fitzgerald was born in 1896 in Saint Paul, Minnesota. Faulkner was born in 1897 in New Albany, Mississippi. Both spent time writing for Hollywood studios in the 1930s and 1940s, work neither found congenial. Faulkner outlived Fitzgerald by more than two decades and received the Nobel Prize in Literature for 1949. They were not friends and did not form a school. What they share is a subject and a period, and the subject is the weight of what has already happened.

### What is the core difference between Fitzgerald and Faulkner on the American Dream?

Fitzgerald's dreamer fails because he tries to repeat a personal past that was his own for one summer. Faulkner's fails because he inherits a collective past, the South's history of slavery and defeat, that was never his to choose. One man is defeated by memory. The other is defeated by history.

## The Genuine Points of Contact

Comparative essays fall apart when the points of contact are decorative. These are structural.

Both writers center a self-made man with a manufactured name. James Gatz becomes Jay Gatsby at seventeen, at the moment he rows out to Dan Cody's yacht. Fitzgerald states the operation plainly: "The truth was that Jay Gatsby of West Egg, Long Island, sprang from his Platonic conception of himself." The name is not a disguise laid over a person. It is the person. Sutpen arrives in Jefferson, Mississippi, with a name nobody in the county has heard, a band of enslaved workers, and a French architect, and he builds a plantation out of swamp. Neither man inherits a position. Each manufactures one and then defends it as though it were inherited.

Both stage the dream as an architectural project. Gatsby's mansion exists to be visible across the bay. Sutpen's Hundred exists to be visible from the road. Neither house is a home in any workable sense. Gatsby does not use most of his rooms and does not drink at his own parties. Sutpen builds his house before he has furniture, a wife, or a plan for children. The house is the argument, and the argument is addressed to somebody who humiliated the builder years earlier.

Both make a woman the terminal of the design. Daisy is not a person Gatsby wants so much as the proof that the five years since 1917 can be canceled. Sutpen's marriages are steps in a design; the women in them are instruments of legitimacy and heirs. Fitzgerald and Faulkner both understand that this instrumentalizing is itself the corruption, and both make the reader feel it as cruelty rather than merely observing it.

Both refuse the consoling ending. Neither writer lets the design fail through bad luck alone. Gatsby dies in his pool because Tom Buchanan pointed George Wilson at the wrong house, but the deeper cause is that Gatsby was already dead as a project the afternoon Daisy could not say she had never loved her husband. Sutpen's line ends in fire, madness, and an idiot heir. In both books the collapse is internal to the design, not imposed on it from outside.

Both use a narrator who is implicated. Nick Carraway is Daisy's cousin, Tom's classmate, Gatsby's neighbor, and Jordan's lover. He is not a camera. Faulkner goes further and hands the Sutpen story to multiple narrators, Rosa Coldfield and Mr. Compson and Quentin Compson and Shreve, none of whom has the whole of it and all of whom bend it. Both writers make the act of telling part of the subject.

### Do both writers treat what came before as inescapable?

Yes, and this is the strongest ground for the comparison. Gatsby's famous claim that the past can be repeated is refuted by the novel's own final sentence about boats borne backward. Faulkner's Southerners cannot get free of a regional history they did not commit. Neither book allows a character to start clean.

## The Decisive Differences

The points of contact set up the comparison. These differences are what it delivers.

### Personal memory against inherited history

Gatsby's obstacle is a specific five-year interval. He met Daisy in Louisville in 1917, lost her while he was overseas, and organizes an entire adult life around canceling that loss. When Nick tells him you cannot repeat what has gone, Gatsby's answer is one of the most quoted refusals in American fiction: "Can't repeat the past?" he cried incredulously. "Why of course you can!" The scale of what he wants to undo is human. It fits inside one biography. He is asking for a rewind, and Fitzgerald's cruelty is to show that a rewind of that scale is exactly what modern money almost buys.

Sutpen's obstacle is not five years. It is the entire structure of the antebellum South, its economy, its racial law, and its defeat in war. Sutpen's design fails at the point where his own racial classification system, the one his society taught him, turns on his family and forbids the marriage that would complete the plan. He is destroyed not by a memory he cannot recover but by an inheritance he cannot refuse and did not author. Fitzgerald's man wants a do-over. Faulkner's man wants an exemption, and the South does not issue those.

This is the difference worth building an essay on. Both books say the dream fails. Fitzgerald locates the failure in time. Faulkner locates it in history. Time is a personal medium; history is a social one. That single relocation changes everything downstream: the prose, the structure, the length, the number of narrators, and the kind of hope each book withholds.

### Region as argument, not as setting

The Eastern setting of Gatsby is not neutral scenery. Nick's closing pages reframe the whole novel as a Western story about the East, four people from the Midwest who could not adapt to a coast that ran on inherited money and social defense. The East in Fitzgerald is where money arrives already made and where the new arrival is measured and found provisional. It is a place with a short memory and a long fence.

The South in Faulkner is the opposite kind of place: a region with a memory so long it functions as a nervous system. Nobody in Yoknapatawpha is unaware of what happened in the 1860s. Characters are defined by what their grandparents did, by land their families held or lost, by racial descent tracked across generations. Where Fitzgerald's East forgets fast and judges by present appearance, Faulkner's South forgets nothing and judges by descent. A self-made man is a problem in both places, but he is a different problem. In the East he lacks credentials. In the South he lacks lineage, which is a thing no money buys and no schedule earns.

The reading that treats these as unrelated because the regions differ has it backwards. The regional difference is the comparison's content. Two men attempt the same act of self-creation in two places, and each place refuses him for a reason that reveals what the place is.

### Two ways of building a sentence

Fitzgerald's sentence is a controlled rise and fall, built for a final cadence that lands. He writes toward a closing clause you can feel arriving. "So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past." The rhythm is iambic under the prose, the metaphor is compact, and the sentence closes. It is a poet's sentence in the sense that it wants to be finished and remembered.

Faulkner's sentence is a controlled refusal to close. It accumulates subordinate clauses, doubles back, revises its own nouns, and holds the reader inside an unresolved period for a page at a time. This is not decoration. A sentence that will not end is the formal equivalent of a history that will not end. Faulkner built a syntax that behaves the way his subject behaves. Fitzgerald built a syntax that behaves the way loss behaves, a rise and a fall and a silence after.

Read this way, the prose difference stops being a matter of taste and becomes evidence. If you want to argue that Fitzgerald treats the backward pull as elegy and Faulkner treats it as ongoing condition, the syntax proves it before the plot does.

### How does Faulkner's prose differ from Fitzgerald's?

Fitzgerald writes toward cadence and closure, with compact metaphor and sentences that resolve. Faulkner writes long accumulating periods that defer resolution, doubling back and qualifying. The difference is formal argument: Fitzgerald renders loss as something completed, Faulkner renders history as something still in progress.

### Structure: the compressed frame against the recursive excavation

Gatsby runs about fifty thousand words across nine chapters and a single summer, with one narrator recalling events from a fixed later vantage. The design is a frame: Nick tells you at the start that he has already made his judgment, then walks you to it. Information arrives roughly in order, with two deliberate delays, the truth of Gatsby's origins in Chapter Six and the truth of the accident in Chapter Eight.

Absalom, Absalom! runs several times that length and refuses order entirely. The story is assembled from partial tellings, decades apart, by narrators who did not witness most of it and who supplement with conjecture. Faulkner's structure enacts historiography: this is what it feels like to reconstruct a past from fragments, rumor, and inherited grievance. Fitzgerald's structure enacts memory: this is what it feels like to have been there and to be sorting it afterward.

Both are modernist solutions to the same problem, how to narrate a story whose meaning is retrospective, and both belong to the movement examined in the series overview of [Gatsby's place among modernist American fiction](/2028/08/21/gatsby-modernist-fiction/). They diverge on whether retrospect belongs to a person or to a community.
## Gatsby and Sutpen: The Closest Pairing in American Fiction

Put the two protagonists side by side and the argument sharpens.

Both begin with a wound delivered by class. Gatsby's is diffuse: a boy in North Dakota with parents his imagination would not accept, a beach, a torn green jersey, a yacht anchoring offshore. Sutpen's is precise: a boy sent to a plantation door with a message and told by a servant to use the back entrance. Fitzgerald gives us a boy who wants upward; Faulkner gives us a boy who wants revenge on a specific system. The wound determines the shape of the ambition. Gatsby's has no defined enemy, which is why it can be redirected onto a woman. Sutpen's has an enemy from the first page of his life, which is why it hardens into what Faulkner calls a design.

Both convert the ambition into a schedule of self-improvement. Gatsby's boyhood timetable, produced by his father at the funeral from a copy of Hopalong Cassidy, is dated September 12, 1906, and its entries are a boy's version of a program: "Rise from bed 6:00 A.M.," "Study electricity, etc.," "Practise elocution, poise and how to attain it," "Read one improving book or magazine per week," "Be better to parents." Sutpen's version is not written down, but it is stricter, and it runs decades. Both men treat a life as an engineering problem with a stated deliverable.

Both are undone at the point where the design meets a social fact it cannot renegotiate. For Gatsby, that fact is that Daisy has a history with Tom, five years of it, including a daughter, and that she will not perjure herself about it in a hotel room. The design required her to say the years did not happen. She will not, and the design collapses in the length of one afternoon. For Sutpen, the fact is racial descent as his society defines it, and the collapse takes a generation. In both cases the fact was always in the design's blind spot because the design was built by a man who believed sufficient will could get around it.

Both are seen through a narrator who is not sure whether to admire them. Nick's ambivalence is on the record in the first pages: Gatsby represented everything for which he had an unaffected scorn, and Gatsby turned out all right at the end. Quentin's ambivalence about Sutpen is more agonized because Quentin is a Southerner narrating his own inheritance. But the structural move is identical. Neither writer will hand you a verdict on the dreamer. Each makes you assemble it.

The difference in the endings is the difference in the books. Gatsby dies alone in a pool and almost nobody attends the funeral; the estate disperses, the house goes quiet, and Nick goes home to the Midwest. It is an ending of erasure. Sutpen's ending propagates: the house burns, but the consequences continue into other lives and other books. Fitzgerald ends a story. Faulkner ends a chapter of something still running.

## What Each Writer Illuminates in the Other

A comparison earns its place when each side changes how you read the other. This one does, in both directions.

### What Faulkner shows you about Gatsby

Reading Faulkner first makes Gatsby's silences audible. The novel is remarkably incurious about where American money came from before the 1920s. Tom's fortune is described as enormous and inherited, and the novel does not ask what built it. Faulkner asks that question constantly and answers it with slavery, land seizure, and violence. Once you have read Absalom, Absalom!, Tom Buchanan's inheritance stops being a given and starts being a question the novel declines to open.

Faulkner also exposes how compressed Gatsby's time horizon is. Gatsby's tragedy occupies one biography. Nobody in the novel is destroyed by something their grandparents did. Nick's family story about a wholesale hardware business and an ancestor who sent a substitute to the Civil War is offered lightly, almost as a joke about self-mythologizing. In Faulkner that same material would be the engine of the book. Set beside Yoknapatawpha, Gatsby looks like a novel about a country that has agreed to forget, and Fitzgerald's final pages, with their vision of Dutch sailors' eyes and a continent that once made a man hold his breath, read as a sudden and deliberate widening of a frame that had been kept narrow on purpose.

### What Fitzgerald shows you about Faulkner

Running the comparison the other way is less common and more useful. Fitzgerald's compression exposes how much of Faulkner's difficulty is a choice rather than a necessity. Gatsby proves that a modernist treatment of retrospection, unreliable telling, and the corrosion of an ideal can be done in nine chapters and fifty thousand words with total clarity of surface. Faulkner's density is doing specific work, but it is not the only way to do that work, and readers who assume difficulty equals depth should be made to account for Gatsby.

Fitzgerald also isolates the erotic and social specificity Faulkner often abstracts. Daisy's voice, Jordan's chin, the shirts, the hotel room in August with the mint julep going warm: Fitzgerald renders desire at the level of the object. Faulkner's designs are often more idea than sensation. Reading Gatsby beside Absalom shows how much of Sutpen's story is argument rather than scene, which is not a flaw but is a fact worth naming when a student claims Faulkner is simply richer.

### The Darker Account of America

The instinct is to say Faulkner, because his subject is slavery and his consequences propagate across generations. The instinct is defensible and probably right on scope. But Fitzgerald's account has a specific bleakness Faulkner does not reach for. Faulkner's characters are crushed by something real: an actual history, actual crimes, actual land. There is a there there. Fitzgerald's Gatsby is crushed by something that was never real at any point. Daisy in 1917 was already a projection. The green light was a light on a dock. Nick's final image is not of a man destroyed by history but of a man destroyed by a promise the continent made and never meant, "a fresh, green breast of the new world," seen for one "transitory enchanted moment" and never available afterward to anyone.

Faulkner's is the darker history. Fitzgerald's is the darker metaphysics. A strong essay names the distinction rather than picking a winner.

## The Two Clocks of the American Past

The comparison yields a reusable framework, and naming it makes it portable to other pairings. Call it the two clocks of the American past.

A personal clock runs the length of one life. It starts when a character forms the desire and it can, in principle, be reset, which is why characters governed by it believe in second chances, reinvention, and the do-over. Gatsby runs on a personal clock. So do most American success narratives, and so does the optimism the country exports.

A collective clock runs the length of a society's history. It started before the character was born and it cannot be reset by anything the character does. Sutpen runs on a collective clock, though he behaves as if he were on a personal one, and that mismatch is his destruction. Faulkner's Southerners are people who know what clock they are on and cannot get off it.

The framework predicts things. It tells you why Fitzgerald's novel is short and Faulkner's is long: a personal clock has a start and a stop, and a collective clock does not, so one story can be closed and the other can only be interrupted. It tells you why Fitzgerald needs one narrator and Faulkner needs four: personal memory has an owner, collective history does not. It tells you why Gatsby's ending is elegiac and Faulkner's is unresolved. And it gives an essay a thesis with an engine in it rather than a list of similarities.

The framework also generates the counter-reading you should pre-empt. A reader can object that Gatsby's final page abandons the personal clock entirely and switches to a collective one, ending on continents and sailors and a future that recedes from all of us. That objection is correct, and it is the most interesting thing about the novel's ending. Fitzgerald spends nine chapters on a personal clock and then, in a page and a half, tells you the personal clock was always a small dial on a much larger instrument. Faulkner starts where Fitzgerald ends. That is the whole comparison in one sentence, and an essay that says it has said something worth reading.
## Where the Two Writers Actually Overlapped

The comparison is sometimes dismissed on the grounds that Fitzgerald and Faulkner belonged to different literary worlds. The record is more entangled than that.

They were born a year apart, Fitzgerald in 1896 and Faulkner in 1897, which places both squarely in the generation that came of age around the First World War and published its major work between the mid-1920s and the late 1930s. Fitzgerald served in the army and never reached France, a biographical fact he transferred to Gatsby with the terms reversed, giving his protagonist the overseas service he had missed. Faulkner enlisted with the Royal Air Force in Canada and did not see combat either, then spent years letting people believe otherwise. Both men, in other words, began their careers with a fiction about themselves, which is a strange and instructive thing to notice in two writers whose greatest characters are men who fabricate a self.

Both wrote for Hollywood studios during a period of financial pressure. Fitzgerald went to Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer in 1937 and worked on studio assignments until his death in 1940. Faulkner wrote screen work across the 1930s and 1940s, including at Warner Bros., and his studio credits are more numerous than Fitzgerald's. Neither regarded the work as central. What matters for the comparison is that both men experienced, at close range, an industry that manufactures glamour on schedule, which is the industrial version of what Gatsby and Sutpen attempt privately.

Their reputations moved in opposite directions at the same moment. Fitzgerald died in 1940 with his books effectively out of circulation and his reputation at its lowest. Faulkner spent the late 1930s and early 1940s with most of his novels out of print and was rescued critically by the Viking Portable Faulkner in 1946, then by the Nobel Prize for 1949. The Gatsby revival arrived in the same postwar decade. Two writers whose work had nearly vanished were both reinstated by a generation of critics reading American fiction through modernist standards, and the standards that rescued one rescued the other.

### Did Fitzgerald and Faulkner know each other's work?

They were contemporaries in a small literary field and each was aware of the other, though they were not friends, did not correspond substantially, and shared no publisher or circle. Faulkner's public rankings of his contemporaries in later interviews are well known and did not center Fitzgerald. Treat the connection as parallel development, not influence.

## The Modernist Inheritance They Shared

Both writers inherited the same set of technical problems from the generation just ahead of them, and the shared inheritance explains the overlap better than any personal connection would.

The first inherited problem is the unreliable or limited narrator. Henry James and Joseph Conrad had already established that a story told by a partial witness produces meaning the witness does not control. Fitzgerald adopts this in a single narrator whose judgments the reader is invited to test. Faulkner takes the same premise and multiplies it, giving one story to several tellers so that reliability becomes a question about the community rather than about a person. Same inheritance, different amplification.

The second is the collapse of chronological order. Both writers refuse to tell events in the order they happened. Fitzgerald withholds Gatsby's origin until Chapter Six and the truth of the accident until Chapter Eight, so that the reader's understanding of every earlier scene is revised retroactively. Faulkner dissolves order almost entirely. In both cases the technique carries an argument: if the meaning of an event depends on what you learn later, then the past is not fixed, it is under continuous revision by the present. This is a modernist proposition and both novels are built to demonstrate it.

The third is the symbolic object doing the work of statement. Fitzgerald has the green light, the eyes on the billboard, the ash heaps. Faulkner has the house, the ledger, the land. Neither writer states his theme; both attach it to a thing and let the thing recur. The difference is that Fitzgerald's objects are luminous and few, while Faulkner's are documentary and many, which follows again from the personal clock and the collective clock.

### How does each author handle time as a narrative device?

Fitzgerald treats time as a distance to be measured: five years since Louisville, one summer in the present, a retrospective narrator sorting it afterward. Faulkner treats time as a substance characters are suspended in, with no reliable before and after, because a collective history is always simultaneously present.

## Time, Witnesses, and the Problem of Telling

The narrators are where the two projects diverge most visibly, and where a comparative essay finds its most concrete evidence.

Nick Carraway is a single consciousness with a stated bias, a class position, and a personal stake. He announces in the first paragraphs that he is inclined to reserve judgment, then spends the book judging. He is Daisy's second cousin once removed, he knew Tom at New Haven, he rents next to Gatsby, and he sleeps with Jordan. Every claim he makes about the summer is filtered through those relations. But he is one filter, and the reader can model him. A careful reader of Gatsby can say with some confidence where Nick is shading the account.

Faulkner denies that comfort. When four people narrate the same events across seventy years, the reader cannot model a single filter, because there is no single filter. Rosa Coldfield's version is fueled by injury. Mr. Compson's is fueled by fatalism. Quentin's is fueled by his own unresolved relationship to the South. Shreve, a Canadian, supplies invention from outside. The story that emerges is a composite nobody authored, which is a precise formal image of what a regional history actually is.

The consequence for the American Dream is direct. In Fitzgerald, one man can be assessed, and Nick's final assessment carries weight because it is a person's judgment about another person. In Faulkner, assessment is impossible in that form, because the object of judgment is not one man's ambition but a whole society's arrangement, and no single witness stands outside it. Fitzgerald can end with a verdict. Faulkner can only end with a chorus.

### How do the narrators of the two writers differ as witnesses?

Nick is a single implicated narrator whose bias can be traced and corrected for, which lets Fitzgerald deliver a verdict. Faulkner distributes the telling among several partial narrators across generations, so the account becomes a composite nobody owns, and judgment becomes structurally unavailable.

## What Faulkner Adds That Fitzgerald Leaves Out

Three things, and naming them precisely is more useful than saying Faulkner is deeper.

Race as structure rather than as incident. Gatsby contains race, and its most explicit appearance is Tom Buchanan's anxious recitation of a pseudo-scientific book about the fall of civilization, an outburst the novel presents as evidence of Tom's stupidity and menace. Race in Gatsby is a symptom of a character. In Faulkner, race is the load-bearing wall of the society and the mechanism by which designs fail. That is not a difference of degree.

Labor and land. Gatsby is a novel of consumption. Money appears already made and is spent on shirts, cars, parties, and hydroplanes. Where the money came from is either criminal and vague, in Gatsby's case, or inherited and unexamined, in Tom's. Faulkner writes about production: who cleared the land, who worked it, who holds the deed, what the ledger records. The valley of ashes is Fitzgerald's one sustained image of labor, and it is a landscape rather than a workforce, which is itself a comment on how the East sees the people who service it.

Duration. Fitzgerald's consequences stop at the property line and the funeral. Faulkner's consequences propagate into other novels, other families, other decades. A reader can finish Gatsby. Nobody finishes Yoknapatawpha, because the design is that consequences do not conclude.

None of this makes Fitzgerald the lesser writer. It makes him a writer with a different aperture. Gatsby is a novel about the moment of desire, rendered with a precision Faulkner never attempted and probably could not have produced. Absalom, Absalom! is a novel about the machinery underneath desire, rendered at a scale Fitzgerald never attempted. Each is complete on its own terms, and a student who declares one superior has usually just declared a preference for one aperture.
## Three Misreadings to Retire

The Fitzgerald and Faulkner pairing attracts three errors so consistently that pre-empting them is half the work of a good essay.

The first is treating the two as unrelated because their regions differ. This is the error the brief for this comparison exists to correct. Region is not a barrier to comparison here; region is the variable being tested. Both writers place a self-invented man inside a social order and watch the order reject him. Holding the man constant and changing the order is how you learn what the order is made of. A reader who says the books cannot be compared because one is set on Long Island and the other in Mississippi has confused the setting with the experiment.

The second is ranking. Essays that decide Faulkner is the greater writer because he is harder, or that Fitzgerald is the greater writer because he is more readable, have substituted a preference for an argument. Difficulty is a technique with costs and returns, not a measure of value. Fitzgerald's clarity buys him a novel that a seventeen-year-old can finish and an adult can reread for thirty years finding new things. Faulkner's density buys him a formal enactment of historical entanglement that clarity could not produce. Both purchases are real.

The third is assuming that because both books critique the American Dream, they critique the same thing. They do not. Fitzgerald critiques the belief that a personal past can be recovered by money and will. Faulkner critiques the belief that a person can found a house exempt from the crimes that built every other house around it. Collapsing these into one complaint about materialism loses the entire content of the comparison. The series discussion of [the corruption of the American Dream](/2024/04/29/gatsby-american-dream-corruption/) traces how many distinct arguments hide inside that single phrase.

### Do the two writers agree about what defeats their characters?

No. Fitzgerald's man is defeated by the impossibility of recovering a lost personal moment. Faulkner's is defeated by a social order that will not grant the exemption his plan requires. One defeat is temporal and private. The other is structural and public. Both are final, but they are not the same failure.

## Reading Order, Difficulty, and Where a Student Should Start

Practical advice, since most readers meet this comparison as an assignment.

Read Gatsby first. It is shorter, its surface is transparent, and its structure is legible on a first pass. Reading it first also gives you a working model of modernist retrospection that makes Faulkner's much larger version comprehensible instead of merely confusing. A reader who starts with Absalom, Absalom! without any prior experience of nonlinear narration usually mistakes difficulty for incoherence and gives up.

Expect the difficulty to be located differently in each. Gatsby's difficulty is interpretive, not textual: the sentences are clear and the meanings are contested, which is why the novel supports decades of disagreement about whether Nick is reliable and whether Gatsby is admirable. The series guide to whether [The Great Gatsby is hard to read](/2022/04/18/is-great-gatsby-hard-to-read/) works through this distinction. Faulkner's difficulty is textual first: the sentences themselves must be parsed before interpretation begins. A page of Faulkner may require rereading to establish who is speaking and when.

For Faulkner, do not begin with Absalom, Absalom! either. The Sound and the Fury is more famous and harder. As I Lay Dying, at about the length of Gatsby, is the sane entry point: multiple narrators, a compressed action, and a manageable page count. Read it, then go to Absalom for the Sutpen material that makes the Gatsby comparison work.

Do not attempt to quote Faulkner extensively in a comparative essay. His sentences do not excerpt cleanly, and an accurate paraphrase you have built yourself demonstrates more control than a long block quotation that swamps your paragraph. Quote Fitzgerald, whose sentences are designed to be liftable, and describe Faulkner.

## Influence Downstream

The two clocks did not stay in the 1920s and 1930s. American fiction after 1945 divides, roughly, along the same line, and the division is useful for placing later books.

Writers working the personal clock produce novels about self-invention, aspiration, and the near miss: the ambitious arriviste, the suburban striver, the reinvented immigrant. The narrative promise is that the character had a chance. Fitzgerald is the ancestor, and the lineage runs through a great deal of postwar American realism and into contemporary novels about wealth and class where the question is whether a particular person can cross a particular line.

Writers working the collective clock produce novels about inheritance, complicity, and the crime that keeps paying out. Toni Morrison's work is the most consequential development of this line, taking Faulkner's premise that the past is an active agent and relocating the point of view from those who benefited to those who were owed. Faulkner is the ancestor, and his influence on Latin American fiction, particularly on writers who inherited a similar problem of a violent regional history narrated by its descendants, is documented and substantial.

Which writer has had the greater influence is a question with two honest answers. Faulkner's technical influence on other novelists is larger, and the Nobel citation and the international reception reflect it. Fitzgerald's cultural influence is larger, because Gatsby became the book through which a whole country teaches itself to name its own myth, a curricular position examined in the series article on [Gatsby in the canon of high school reading](/2028/11/20/gatsby-canon-high-school/). Novelists learned more from Faulkner. Readers learned more from Fitzgerald.
## Four Passages That Carry the Argument

A comparative claim is only as strong as the sentences under it. Four Fitzgerald passages do most of the work in this pairing, and each has a Faulkner counterpart that can be described rather than quoted.

### The Platonic conception, Chapter Six

Nick's account of Gatsby's origin is the novel's most theoretical paragraph and its most important for this comparison. "The truth was that Jay Gatsby of West Egg, Long Island, sprang from his Platonic conception of himself." Two claims are packed into that sentence. The first is that the self here is not developed but conceived, produced whole from an idea rather than grown from circumstance. The second is that the conception belongs to the boy himself, which makes Gatsby simultaneously the sculptor and the statue. Nick continues that he was "a son of God" who "must be about his Father's Business," which places the self-invention inside a religious vocabulary and then names the business as "the service of a vast, vulgar, and meretricious beauty." The theology is real and the object is cheap, and Fitzgerald means the collision.

Faulkner's counterpart is Sutpen's account of his boyhood at the plantation door. The structure is identical in one respect and opposite in another. Identical: a moment of humiliation produces a designed self. Opposite: Sutpen's design is aimed at a specific social order with a specific mechanism, while Gatsby's is aimed at nothing more definite than upward. That is why Gatsby's ambition can be captured entirely by one woman and Sutpen's cannot be captured by anything less than a dynasty. A vague wound produces a romantic project. A precise wound produces an institutional one.

### The hotel room, Chapter Seven

The scene in the Plaza suite is where the personal clock stops. Gatsby's demand is not that Daisy leave Tom, which she has effectively agreed to, but that she declare she never loved him. He needs the five years annulled, not merely superseded. Daisy's refusal is small and devastating: she loved Tom too, once, and saying otherwise would not be true. The design required a rewrite of the record and she will not supply it. Fitzgerald puts the collapse in a hotel suite in August with the heat unbearable and a bottle of whiskey on the table, which is to say he puts a metaphysical failure inside a domestic argument, and the ordinariness of the setting is doing deliberate work.

Faulkner has no equivalent scene because he cannot. There is no room a Southern history can be argued in and no afternoon it can lose. His collapses happen through arrival, revelation, and descent, over years. The absence of a Faulknerian hotel room is itself evidence for the two-clock model: a personal clock can be stopped in one conversation, and a collective one cannot be stopped at all.

### The father and the schedule, Chapter Nine

Henry Gatz produces a ragged copy of a book called Hopalong Cassidy with a schedule printed on the last flyleaf, dated September 12, 1906. The entries begin "Rise from bed 6:00 A.M." and proceed through "Study electricity, etc.," "Practise elocution, poise and how to attain it," and a set of general resolves that includes "Read one improving book or magazine per week," "Save $5.00," and "Be better to parents." The father's comment is that it just shows you, and then that Jimmy "was bound to get ahead. He always had some resolves like this or something."

What the schedule reveals is that the self-invention was underway years before Dan Cody's yacht, and that it was borrowed. This is a boy copying a national script about industry and self-improvement, a script with a long American pedigree examined in the series article on [the self-made man myth in The Great Gatsby](/2025/01/13/self-made-man-myth-gatsby/). The pathos is that the script worked, in the sense that he did get ahead, and that getting ahead did not produce what the script promised.

Faulkner's counterpart is the ledger, the deed, and the family record. Where Fitzgerald gives one boy's timetable, Faulkner gives documents that outlive the people who wrote them and that continue to determine outcomes for people who never read them. A schedule is a plan for a life. A ledger is a claim on other lives. The two documents are the two clocks in physical form.

### The last page, Chapter Nine

Nick, on the beach, imagines the island as Dutch sailors first saw it, "a fresh, green breast of the new world," a sight that "compelled into an aesthetic contemplation he neither understood nor desired," and names the experience "a transitory enchanted moment." Then the pivot: Gatsby's dream was already behind him, and the future we chase is "the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us." Then the sentence everyone knows, "So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past."

That final page abandons the personal clock. For nine chapters the book has been about one man's five years. In a page and a half it becomes about a continent, a colonial arrival, and a permanent backward current affecting everyone. Fitzgerald steps, briefly, onto Faulkner's ground. He does not stay. Faulkner never leaves it.

An essay that uses these four passages in order has an argument with a shape: origin, collapse, evidence, and concession. That is a stronger spine than a list of similarities, and it is built entirely from Fitzgerald's sentences with Faulkner supplied as the contrast that makes them legible.
## Objects, Houses, and the Argument They Carry

Both writers refuse to state a theme and both attach it to a thing instead. Comparing the things is one of the fastest routes into the difference between the two projects.

Fitzgerald's central object is a light at the end of a dock: small, distant, artificial, and belonging to somebody else. Its power comes from being unreachable and from meaning something only to the man looking at it. When Daisy is finally standing next to him, Nick observes that Gatsby's "count of enchanted objects had diminished by one," which is a devastating piece of accounting. The object was never the thing. The distance was the thing. That is a personal-clock object: it exists inside one man's psychology and evaporates when the psychology is satisfied.

Faulkner's central object is a house on land, built by workers whose names the county knows, standing on a deed that predates the builder's arrival and outlasts his death. Nobody's psychology makes it appear or disappear. It can burn, and its consequences continue after the fire. That is a collective-clock object: it exists in the record, it is transferable, and it binds people who never chose it.

Fitzgerald has a collective-clock object too, and it is the strongest evidence that he understood exactly what Faulkner would spend a career on. The valley of ashes is not psychological. It is a landscape produced by industry, sitting between West Egg and Manhattan, and everyone must pass through it to get from the money to the city. Fitzgerald describes it as a place "where ashes grow like wheat into ridges and hills and grotesque gardens," populated by "ash-grey men" who "swarm up with leaden spades" and "stir up an impenetrable cloud." Above it hangs a billboard whose enormous eyes "look out of no face" and, as the paint fails, continue to "brood on over the solemn dumping ground."

That passage is Faulknerian in everything but syntax. It says the wealth on both eggs is produced somewhere, that the somewhere is a ruin, that the ruin has people in it, and that the only thing watching is an abandoned advertisement. Fitzgerald put it in Chapter Two and returned to it three more times, and then he let it go. A Faulkner novel would have made it the subject. The choice to keep it as a corridor rather than a country is precisely the aperture difference, and a comparative essay that notices Fitzgerald building a collective-clock object and then declining to develop it has found something genuinely arguable.

The counter-reading here deserves a hearing. A reader can say that the valley is fully developed, that it produces George and Myrtle Wilson, that Myrtle's death occurs at its edge and George's revenge comes out of it, and that the novel's plot is therefore driven by the ash heaps rather than by the mansions. That reading is strong. What it cannot claim is that the valley's history is examined. The novel never asks who owns the dumping ground, what the ash is from, or how long it has been accumulating. Faulkner would have asked all three in the first chapter. Fitzgerald asks none, and the silence is the finding.

## The Verdict and the Deciding Factor

The verdict this article defends: Fitzgerald and Faulkner are not opposed writers who happen to share a country. They are two settings of the same instrument, and the setting is the scope of the past.

Fitzgerald's version is the one American culture repeats, because a personal clock leaves room for the belief that a different person, better timed or better funded, might have made it. Gatsby fails, but the failure is legible as a near miss. His dream "must have seemed so close that he could hardly fail to grasp it," and that phrasing keeps the possibility technically alive even while the sentence buries it. This is why the novel functions as both a critique of the American Dream and, perversely, as an advertisement for it, a doubleness examined at length in the series treatment of [the American Dream in The Great Gatsby](/2024/04/22/american-dream-great-gatsby/).

Faulkner's version leaves no such room. Sutpen's design was not a near miss. It was structurally void from its first premise, because the premise required a society to make an exception it does not make. No better timing helps. No amount of money helps. Faulkner closes the door Fitzgerald leaves ajar.

The deciding factor, then, is not which writer is greater, a question that produces bad essays. The deciding factor is which model of the past a reader thinks the country actually runs on. If American failure is a matter of individuals missing their moment, Fitzgerald has the sharper account. If it is a matter of a society reproducing its founding arrangements, Faulkner does. The strongest position, and the one this article takes, is that Fitzgerald's last page concedes Faulkner's point. The novel that spent nine chapters on one man's five years ends by admitting that all of us are borne backward by something much older than any of us, and the sentence that says so, "So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past," is Fitzgerald handing the collective clock the final word.

That concession is what makes the comparison worth writing about. Fitzgerald did not write a Southern novel. He wrote an Eastern one that ends by looking over its shoulder at a continent, and Faulkner spent thirty years writing what is over that shoulder.

## How to Write a Comparative Essay on These Two Writers

Comparative essays fail in predictable ways. They alternate paragraphs of unconnected description, or they announce similarity without saying what the similarity buys. Here is a method that avoids both.

Start with a thesis that names a difference inside a shared commitment. Not "both writers explore the American Dream," which is a topic, not a claim. Something closer to: "Both novels destroy a self-made man, but Fitzgerald locates the destroying force in personal memory and Faulkner locates it in inherited history, and Gatsby's final page quietly concedes that Faulkner is right." That thesis has a shared commitment, a named difference, and a defensible surprise.

Organize by axis, never by author. A paragraph on the nature of the past that handles both writers is worth three paragraphs that handle one writer each. The table above gives six ready-made axes; a five-paragraph essay uses three of them, and a longer paper uses all six with the last as the conclusion.

Anchor every axis in a specific passage. On the Fitzgerald side, the schedule in Chapter Nine, the hotel scene in Chapter Seven, Nick's account of the Platonic conception in Chapter Six, and the closing page are the four passages that do the most work. On the Faulkner side, use Sutpen's boyhood at the plantation door and the collapse of the design; describe them accurately rather than quoting at length, since Faulkner's prose resists excerpting and a paraphrase you control beats a quotation you do not.

Handle the counter-reading in the body, not in a defensive final line. The obvious objection is that the writers are too dissimilar to compare, being separated by region, style, length, and temperament. Answer it early by conceding the surface difference and showing that the difference is the finding. Two writers who share a subject and diverge in method give you a controlled experiment; two writers who resemble each other give you a coincidence.

Close on the concession rather than on a summary. An essay that ends by restating its three axes has ended weakly. An essay that ends by showing Fitzgerald's last page adopting Faulkner's model of history has ended on an argument, and graders reward that. For a fuller treatment of the passage that makes this move, see the series analysis of [Gatsby's claim that the past can be repeated](/2024/06/24/gatsby-repeat-the-past/), and for the theme in its own right, [the past and the repetition of time](/2024/06/17/gatsby-past-and-time/). Readers who want to check the closing passage line by line can [read and annotate The Great Gatsby free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/great-gatsby-annotated-text.html), which keeps the full text beside the notes so a comparative argument can be built directly against Fitzgerald's sentences.


A longer paper can add a fourth move that shorter essays skip: test the framework against a third text. If the two-clock model is real, it should sort books you were not given. Try it on a novel of immigrant arrival, where the personal clock usually dominates and the collective one enters only through the country left behind. Try it on a novel of inherited wealth, where the collective clock is present but suppressed, the way Tom Buchanan's fortune is present and unexamined. A framework that sorts unfamiliar cases is a contribution; a framework that only describes the two books you were assigned is a summary with a name attached. Graders and readers can tell the difference immediately, and the ability to extend a model is the single clearest signal that a student understood the comparison rather than memorizing it.

One practical warning about evidence. Faulkner's reputation for difficulty tempts students into vagueness, and vagueness about Faulkner is the fastest way to lose an otherwise good comparative essay. Do not write that Faulkner shows the burden of history. Write that Sutpen, turned away from a plantation door as a boy, spends his life constructing a dynasty designed to make that refusal impossible, and that the design fails at the point where the racial law he accepted disqualifies his own son. Specificity about the Faulkner side is what proves you did the reading, and it is what makes the Fitzgerald side, which everyone has read, look freshly examined rather than recycled.

## Closing Verdict

The pairing of Fitzgerald with Faulkner is not a stunt and not a regional footnote. It is the clearest available demonstration that the American Dream, in serious American fiction, is not one idea being agreed about but one question being answered two ways. Fitzgerald asked whether a man can retrieve a moment. Faulkner asked whether a society can put down a crime. Both answered no, and the no is differently shaped.

Read them in the order Fitzgerald wrote and Faulkner extended: Gatsby first, for the personal clock and the beautiful terminal cadence, then Absalom, Absalom!, for the collective clock and the refusal to end. Then go back to Gatsby's last page and notice that Fitzgerald got there first, in a page and a half, and then stopped. That stop is the difference between a novel about a man and a body of work about a country, and recognizing it turns a comparison into an argument.
## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do Fitzgerald and Faulkner compare on the American Dream?

Both build a protagonist who invents himself from nothing and both destroy him, but they disagree about what does the destroying. Fitzgerald's Gatsby is undone by a personal past: a five-year gap since Louisville that he expects money and will to close. When Daisy refuses to say those years did not happen, the project ends inside an afternoon. Faulkner's Sutpen is undone by a collective past: the racial and economic order of the antebellum South, which he accepted as his own framework and which then disqualifies his family. Fitzgerald's dreamer wants a rewind. Faulkner's wants an exemption. The country grants neither, but the refusals come from different places, one from time and one from history, and that difference organizes every other contrast between the two writers.

### Q: How do the personal and collective pasts differ?

A personal past belongs to one biography and has a start date. Gatsby's begins in Louisville in 1917 and could in principle be edited by a sufficiently powerful person, which is exactly what he attempts. It generates hope, because it looks like a scheduling problem. A collective past began before any living character and belongs to no one, so it cannot be edited by anyone. Faulkner's Southerners are inside a history of slavery, war, and defeat that predates their consent. The practical difference shows in the endings. Gatsby's story closes: he dies, the house empties, Nick goes home. Faulkner's does not close, because the consequences move into other families and other books. One is an elegy for a man. The other is a report on a condition still running.

### Q: How do the Eastern and Southern visions of America contrast?

Fitzgerald's East is a place with a short memory and a hard perimeter. Money there is either inherited and unquestioned, as Tom's is, or new and provisional, as Gatsby's is. Nobody asks what built the old fortunes. Status is assessed on present appearance, which is why Gatsby's performance almost works and why its failure is a matter of manners rather than lineage. Faulkner's South has the opposite architecture: a memory so long it functions as law, where a family's standing is tracked by descent across generations and no amount of money purchases what was not inherited. The self-made man is a problem in both regions, but the East refuses him for lacking credentials and the South refuses him for lacking ancestry. The contrast is the comparison's payload.

### Q: How do both writers treat the past as inescapable?

Fitzgerald states it and Faulkner enacts it. Fitzgerald gives Gatsby the loudest refusal in American fiction, the insistence that of course you can repeat what has gone, and then spends the remaining chapters disproving it, ending on the image of boats borne back ceaselessly. The novel's own last sentence overrules its protagonist. Faulkner does not stage a debate about it, because in Yoknapatawpha nobody imagines escape is available. The inescapability is built into the syntax, the chronology, and the multiplication of narrators. Fitzgerald argues the point; Faulkner assumes it and shows what living under the assumption looks like. Both arrive at the same finding by opposite routes, which is why the comparison holds even though the books look nothing alike.

### Q: Are the two writers unrelated?

The dismissal usually rests on surface evidence: different regions, different sentence lengths, different temperaments, no friendship, no shared publisher, no correspondence of consequence. All of that is accurate and none of it settles anything. They were born a year apart, published their major work in the same twelve-year window, inherited the same modernist problems from James and Conrad, both wrote for Hollywood under financial pressure, and both had their reputations restored by the same postwar generation of critics. More importantly, they wrote the same character twice: a man who builds a name and a house to answer a class wound and is destroyed by a social fact his design ignored. Two writers who independently produce that figure are not unrelated. They are responding to the same country.

### Q: How do their prose and structure contrast?

Fitzgerald writes sentences that resolve. The rhythm rises and falls, the metaphor is compact, and the period lands where you can feel it coming. The novel matches this at scale: nine chapters, one summer, one narrator, roughly fifty thousand words, information released in a controlled order with two deliberate delays. Faulkner writes periods that refuse to resolve, accumulating clauses and revising their own terms, and his structure matches: recursive chronology, several narrators separated by decades, a length several times Fitzgerald's. Neither is decoration. A sentence that closes is the formal image of a loss that has finished happening. A sentence that will not close is the formal image of a history still in progress. The syntax proves the thesis before the plot does.

### Q: Did Fitzgerald and Faulkner know each other's work?

They were aware of each other in a small literary field, but the relationship was not personal or professional in any substantive sense. They shared no publisher, no editor, and no circle, and there is no significant correspondence between them. Faulkner's later public assessments of his contemporaries, delivered in interviews and classroom sessions, are well documented and did not place Fitzgerald at the center. The honest description is parallel development rather than influence: two writers reading the same predecessors and answering the same national question independently. For comparative essays this is an advantage, since it removes the temptation to argue that one borrowed from the other and forces attention onto what each did with a shared problem.

### Q: Which writer offers the darker verdict on America?

Faulkner offers the darker history and Fitzgerald offers the darker metaphysics. Faulkner's subject is a real crime with real beneficiaries and consequences that propagate across generations, which is as dark as a national subject gets. But his characters are at least destroyed by something that exists. Fitzgerald's Gatsby is destroyed by something that was never there. Daisy in 1917 was already a projection, the light on the dock was a light on a dock, and Nick's closing image describes a continent that made a promise for one transitory moment and never meant it. Faulkner says the country did something terrible. Fitzgerald says the country promised something it never had. A strong essay names both rather than choosing.

### Q: What does Faulkner add that Fitzgerald leaves out?

Three things, stated precisely. Race as structure rather than incident: in Gatsby race appears mainly as a symptom of Tom Buchanan's character, while in Faulkner it is the load-bearing wall of the society and the mechanism by which designs collapse. Labor and land: Fitzgerald writes consumption, with money already made and spent on shirts and cars, while Faulkner writes production, tracking who cleared the ground, who worked it, and what the ledgers record. Duration: Fitzgerald's consequences stop at the funeral, while Faulkner's move into other families and other novels. None of this makes Fitzgerald smaller. It makes his aperture narrower on purpose.

### Q: Should a student pair these two authors in an essay?

Yes, and it is one of the strongest available pairings, provided the thesis names a difference inside a shared commitment rather than announcing that both writers explore the American Dream. Organize by axis and never by author: a paragraph handling the nature of the past in both writers beats three paragraphs describing each separately. Anchor each axis in a passage. Quote Fitzgerald, whose sentences excerpt cleanly, and paraphrase Faulkner, whose do not. Handle the objection that the two are too dissimilar early, by conceding the surface difference and showing that the difference is the finding. Close on Fitzgerald's last page adopting Faulkner's model of history rather than on a summary of your own points.

### Q: Is the American Dream even present in Faulkner?

It is present and it is savaged. Thomas Sutpen's design in Absalom, Absalom! is a rags-to-dynasty story: a poor boy from the mountains, humiliated at a plantation door, who acquires land, builds a great house, and sets out to found a line. Every element of the standard American success narrative is there, including the self-naming and the architecture of display. What Faulkner adds is the source of the capital and the terms of the social contract, which the optimistic version of the story omits. The dream in Faulkner is not absent; it is shown with its financing attached. That is why the pairing with Gatsby works, since Fitzgerald shows the same dream with the financing kept deliberately vague.

### Q: How does each author handle time as a narrative device?

Fitzgerald treats time as a measurable distance. There are five years since Louisville, one summer in the present, and a narrator writing from a fixed later vantage, and the novel's tension comes from the gap between those coordinates. Information is withheld and then released, so earlier scenes are revised retroactively, but the timeline itself remains reconstructible. Faulkner treats time as a medium his characters are suspended in. Events from the 1830s, the 1860s, and 1910 occupy the same narrative present because a collective history does not recede. The practical test is whether a reader can draw a timeline. For Gatsby, yes, and doing so is a standard exercise. For Absalom, the attempt is itself the point.

### Q: Does the Southern setting change the meaning of failure?

Substantially. In Fitzgerald's East, failure means being found out: exposed as new money, as a bootlegger, as a man performing a background he does not have. It is a failure of credential, and it happens in public, in a hotel room, in front of the people whose opinion constitutes the verdict. In Faulkner's South, failure means the design was void from the beginning because the society will not issue the exemption it required. It is a failure of premise, not of performance, and no better execution avoids it. The consequence is that Fitzgerald's failure feels like a near miss while Faulkner's feels like an arithmetic result, and that difference in feel is what makes Gatsby quotable and Absalom implacable.

### Q: How do the narrators of the two writers differ as witnesses?

Nick Carraway is one implicated consciousness whose bias can be traced. He is Daisy's cousin, Tom's classmate, Gatsby's neighbor, and Jordan's lover, and he announces a disposition to reserve judgment before spending the book judging. A careful reader can model him and correct for him, which is why Fitzgerald can end on a verdict that carries weight. Faulkner distributes the same function across several narrators separated by decades, none of whom witnessed most of what they describe and all of whom supplement with conjecture. The result is a composite account nobody authored, which is a formal image of what a regional history actually is. Fitzgerald ends with a judgment. Faulkner ends with a chorus.

### Q: What common modernist inheritance do the two share?

Three technical problems handed down from Henry James and Joseph Conrad. First, the limited or unreliable witness, which Fitzgerald adopts in one narrator and Faulkner multiplies across several. Second, the collapse of chronological order, so that meaning is assigned retroactively and the past becomes something the present keeps revising. Fitzgerald does this with two controlled delays; Faulkner dissolves sequence almost entirely. Third, the symbolic object standing in for statement, so that a green light, a billboard, a house, or a ledger carries the argument the prose declines to make outright. Both writers accepted all three and differed only in amplitude, which is why the comparison is a controlled experiment rather than a coincidence.

### Q: Which writer had greater influence on later American fiction?

Two honest answers, depending on the measure. Faulkner's technical influence on other novelists is larger. His methods for narrating inherited guilt through multiple partial voices were developed by Toni Morrison into something new and were absorbed by a generation of Latin American writers facing a similar problem of violent regional history told by its descendants. Novelists learned craft from Faulkner. Fitzgerald's cultural influence is larger. Gatsby became the text through which an entire country teaches itself the name of its own myth, which no Faulkner novel has done. Readers learned a vocabulary from Fitzgerald. If the question is who changed how novels are built, Faulkner. If it is who changed how a nation talks about itself, Fitzgerald.

### Q: Which author is harder to read for a first-time student?

Faulkner, and the difficulty is located differently in each writer. Faulkner's difficulty is textual: the sentences must be parsed before interpretation can begin, and a page may need rereading simply to establish who is speaking and when. Fitzgerald's difficulty is interpretive: the sentences are transparent and the meanings are contested, which is why the novel supports decades of disagreement about Nick's reliability. A student who mistakes textual ease for shallowness will underread Gatsby, and a student who mistakes textual difficulty for depth will overread Faulkner. Read Gatsby first for a working model of modernist retrospection, then start Faulkner with As I Lay Dying rather than with the two famous hard ones.

### Q: Do the two writers agree on what defeats their characters?

They do not, and the disagreement is the whole value of the pairing. Fitzgerald's Gatsby is defeated by the impossibility of recovering a personal moment, a defeat that is temporal, private, and legible as a near miss. Faulkner's Sutpen is defeated by a social order that will not grant the exemption his design required, a defeat that is structural, public, and void from its first premise. Better timing might plausibly have saved Gatsby; nothing saves Sutpen. Both writers refuse the consoling ending, but Fitzgerald leaves the door of possibility technically ajar while Faulkner closes it. The most interesting move in either book is that Fitzgerald's final page walks over and closes it too.
