The Companies Act 1956 vs 2013 comparison is the most repeated exercise in Indian company law, and it is usually performed badly. The standard version counts sections, announces that 658 became roughly 470, and concludes that India obtained a shorter and simpler statute. That conclusion survives only because the counting stops at the boundary of the Act itself. The reader who has to decide something real, whether the reform delivered what it promised, whether an old precedent still governs a new petition, whether a private company gained or lost room to operate, needs a different comparison: one that follows the detail into the rules, tests each subject against the benchmark the reformers set for themselves, and ends with a verdict that names its deciding factor.

That is the comparison this article performs. The benchmark is not imported from commentary. It is the stated objective of the Expert Committee on Company Law chaired by Dr J. J. Irani, whose report of 31 May 2005 supplied the architecture of the 2013 Act: a compact statute, stripped of redundant provisions, with the operative detail moved into delegated legislation so that the law could adapt without returning to Parliament for every adjustment. Measured against that objective, the verdict of this article can be stated at the outset and then earned in the sections that follow. The reform delivered a genuinely new governance architecture and failed its own test of brevity and simplicity, because the detail it removed from the statute did not disappear. It moved, into rules, forms and notifications that are longer in aggregate than the sections they replaced and that change far more often. Shorter statute, longer law. The deciding factor in every dimension examined below is where the detail went.

Two cautions govern everything that follows. The first is about the older Act's reputation. The Companies Act 1956 is routinely described as a colonial survival, which it was not: it was enacted by the Parliament of independent India as Act 1 of 1956, on the design of the Bhabha Committee, and it was amended so often across fifty-seven years that the statute in force in 2012 was in substance a different instrument from the one that commenced on 1 April 1956. The second caution is the mirror image. The Companies Act 2013 is routinely described as a clean break, which it also was not: very large parts of it re-enact the 1956 text in reorganised form, and for several years after its enactment the two statutes operated side by side, each governing a different slice of corporate life. A comparison that begins from either slogan will mislead. The full account of how the older statute declined and why Parliament chose replacement over further amendment belongs to [the article on the replacement of the Companies Act 1956](/2012/02/15/companies-act-1956-replacement/), and the full account of what the 2013 Act requires belongs to [the complete guide to the Companies Act 2013](/2012/02/01/companies-act-2013-complete-guide/). This article owns only the comparison between them, and the decision it supports.

## The question the comparison has to answer

A comparison earns its length only if somebody is stuck on a decision. Four readers are stuck on this one. The practitioner is stuck on authority: a dispute drafted under the new Act turns on a concept, oppression, charges, scheme sanction, that the courts spent five decades elaborating under the old one, and the brief practically writes itself in 1956 citations if those citations still hold. The company secretary is stuck on burden: the board wants to know whether the new regime is genuinely lighter than the old, and the honest answer determines how compliance is staffed and budgeted. The aspirant is stuck on method: professional papers ask the comparison directly, and the answer that scores is the one that classifies changes correctly rather than reciting a feature list. The legislator is stuck on design: whether the principles-based drafting instruction, issued once in India at full scale, produced what it was supposed to produce is the best available evidence for whether to issue it again.

Each of those decisions turns on a different axis, so the comparison has to run on several axes at once. Length and structure decide the legislator's question. Corporate forms decide what a promoter can build. Board composition and directors' duties decide how power inside the company is disciplined. Audit decides whether the numbers can be trusted and who answers when they cannot. Shareholder remedies decide what a minority can actually do. Related party transactions decide how the oldest abuse in company law, the diversion of value to the promoter's other interests, is policed. Winding up decides how failure is processed. Enforcement decides what the state does to those who default. No single axis carries the verdict, but one factor recurs on every axis, and it is the factor the Irani design itself put in issue: the location of the operative detail, in the statute or beneath it.

The sections that follow take the benchmark first, because a reform should be judged against its own stated purpose before it is judged against anyone else's. They then do the arithmetic honestly, set out a change classification covering the significant subjects of company law, and work through the dimensions in turn. The verdict, its deciding factor, the adjustments different readers should make to it, and the cases where it does not hold, close the argument.

## The benchmark: what the Irani Committee asked for

The Expert Committee on Company Law was constituted on 2 December 2004 under the chairmanship of Dr J. J. Irani, and its terms of reference are the cleanest statement of the reform's purpose that exists. The committee was asked to advise on bringing about compactness by reducing the size of the Act and removing redundant provisions, on recasting the law so that it could be interpreted easily and without ambiguity, on providing greater flexibility in rule making so that the framework could respond to evolving business models, and on protecting the interests of stakeholders including small investors. Its report, submitted on 31 May 2005, answered with a design: a smaller, principles-based statute that would state the essential obligations and governance structure, delegate the procedural and threshold detail to rules that the executive could update, shift decisions that had required government approval to shareholders, recognise new forms such as the One Person Company, and differentiate the compliance load so that small and private companies carried a lighter regime than companies with significant public interest.

Three features of that design matter for the verdict. First, compactness was not an aesthetic preference. It was the mechanism by which the statute was supposed to stop accumulating the accretions that had made the 1956 Act, at 658 sections and fifteen schedules, one of the longest statutes on the Indian book. Second, delegation was the price of compactness, accepted openly: the detail had to live somewhere, and the committee preferred rules that could be amended quickly to sections that could be amended only by Parliament. Third, the committee's test was therefore never the section count alone. A statute of 470 sections surrounded by a small, stable body of rules would have met the design. A statute of 470 sections surrounded by a large, unstable body of rules meets the letter of the count and defeats the purpose of the design, because the regulated community does not experience the Act and the rules as two things. It experiences one body of law, and it measures that body's length, complexity and rate of change as a whole.

The reform's supporters and its critics both tend to skip this benchmark, because it is unforgiving to each side's slogan. The supporter who celebrates 470 sections must explain the rules. The critic who says nothing changed must explain the class action, the audit regulator and the codified duties, none of which has any 1956 counterpart. The comparison below holds both to the benchmark.

## The Companies Act 1956 vs 2013 arithmetic: fewer sections, more law

The arithmetic itself is not in dispute. The Companies Act 1956 contained 658 sections arranged in thirteen Parts, with fifteen schedules. The Companies Act 2013, Act 18 of 2013, assented to on 29 August 2013, contains 470 sections arranged in twenty-nine chapters, with seven schedules. The fall is 188 sections, a reduction of a little over a quarter of the old Act's numbered length. The new Act was also brought into force in stages, with a first tranche of 98 sections notified in September 2013 and the main body of the statute operative from 1 April 2014, a commencement pattern that itself anticipated the years of dual operation examined later in this article.

What the arithmetic conceals is the redistribution that produced it. Three distinct operations were performed on the 1956 text, and only one of them is a reduction. The first operation was genuine deletion: machinery that had outlived its purpose, such as the statutory meeting, share warrants to bearer, and the provisions on sole selling agents, was dropped and not replaced. The second operation was relocation within the statute book: the winding up and revival machinery was marked for exit from company law altogether, a movement completed by the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code of 2016. The third operation, and by volume the largest, was transfer downward: obligations whose operative content had sat in sections of the 1956 Act were restated in the 2013 Act as framework provisions whose thresholds, forms, timelines and procedures were left to be prescribed by rules. The section count fell because the counting unit changed. A section of the 2013 Act is, on average, a frame around delegated content in a way that a section of the 1956 Act typically was not.

The evidence for that reading is inside the 2013 Act's own operation. The class action illustrates it precisely. Section 245 created the remedy in 2013 and was brought into force on 1 June 2016, but the numerical thresholds that determine who may actually file, expressed in the section as a hundred members or such percentage as may be prescribed, were prescribed only in 2019, when the National Company Law Tribunal Rules were amended to fix the percentage at five per cent of members or of issued share capital for an unlisted company and two per cent for a listed one. For three years the remedy existed as a frame awaiting its operative numbers. The same pattern recurs across the statute: the classes of company bound by the woman director requirement, by independent director requirements beyond listed companies, by internal audit and by secretarial audit are all identified through rules, so that the practical reach of the Act cannot be read off the Act. A reader who wants to know whether a given company must rotate its auditors, appoint a woman director or conduct a secretarial audit reads a section for the obligation and a rule, often amended, for its application.

None of this makes the delegation a mistake. The Irani Committee recommended it deliberately, and the alternative, a Parliament that must amend the statute to change a filing threshold, had demonstrably failed under the 1956 regime, which accumulated amendment after amendment without ever becoming coherent. The point is narrower and decisive for the comparison: the reform's success on its own terms cannot be assessed by the section count, because the section count was the one number the design guaranteed would fall regardless of what happened to the total law. Assessed by total law, by the volume of binding text a company must actually comply with and by the frequency with which that text changes, the 2013 settlement is larger and less stable than the one it replaced. That is the honest answer to the section-count argument, and it is the foundation of the verdict.

## The change classification: what kind of change each subject underwent

Aggregate counts cannot say which parts of company law actually changed. For that, each significant subject has to be classified by the kind of treatment it received. The classification used here has five categories, applied consistently. A subject is classified as renumbered only where the 2013 provision carries forward the operative rule of the 1956 provision in substantially the same terms under a new number. It is redrafted without substantive change where the text was reorganised, modernised or consolidated but the governing rule a practitioner applies is the same rule. It is substantively changed where the 2013 Act altered the rule itself: a different approver, a different forum, a different threshold logic, a new prohibition or a new defence. It is newly created where the 2013 Act introduced a subject the 1956 Act did not contain at all. And it is relocated to another statute where the subject was removed from company law and re-housed elsewhere, a category with no counterpart in most statutory comparisons and the one that matters most here, because the largest subject in the 1956 Act, exit, left the building.

The table below applies that classification to the significant subjects of Indian company law. Every row has been checked against the two texts, because a mapping table with wrong correspondences is worse than no table, and because the credibility of the verdict depends on the classification being defensible row by row. The subjects that were simply dropped, with no successor and no relocation, cannot appear in a table of correspondences and are treated in the prose that follows it.

Read as a whole, the table disposes of both slogans at once. Against the claim that nothing changed, the newly created rows are structural additions with no 1956 equivalent: a codified law of directors' duties, a statutory board-composition regime, a class action, an audit regulator, a corporate social responsibility obligation and a defined fraud offence. Against the claim that everything changed, the renumbered and redrafted rows show the working core of the old statute, meetings, minutes, accounts, dividends, buy-backs, annual returns, carried across with its rules intact, which is why practitioners experienced the transition as reorganisation more than rupture in the routine subjects. The centre of gravity sits in the substantively changed rows, and they share a signature: in almost every one, what changed is not the existence of the control but its location and its operator. Approval moved from the government to shareholders and boards. Adjudication moved from the High Courts and the Company Law Board to a tribunal. Detail moved from the section to the rule. The 2013 Act is best understood not as a new law of companies but as a new distribution of authority over the same law, with a layer of genuinely new governance machinery added on top.

The count is worth stating exactly, because tallies in this territory are usually quoted from press material rather than compiled. Across the forty-four significant subjects classified in the table, five were renumbered only and ten redrafted without substantive change, so fifteen subjects, just over a third, crossed with their governing rules intact. Fifteen were substantively changed. Twelve were newly created. Two were relocated out of company law altogether. The proportions are the argument in miniature: continuity is the largest single block only when the carried categories are combined, the changed and created categories together account for twenty-seven of the forty-four subjects, and the centre of the distribution sits exactly where the prose analysis puts it, in subjects whose rules were rewritten rather than repealed or preserved.

The dropped subjects complete the picture and are easily missed because they leave no row. The statutory meeting, which Section 165 of the 1956 Act required a public company to hold early in its life together with a statutory report, has no successor; the information function it served is performed, if at all, by the continuous disclosure regime. Share warrants to bearer, issued under Sections 114 and 115 of the 1956 Act, disappeared, a deletion consistent with the new Act's insistence on identified holders. The sole selling agency provisions were dropped. The managing agency system had been abolished decades earlier and is a reminder that the 1956 Act was itself a palimpsest of reforms, not a single coherent design that 2013 swept away.

## Incorporation and corporate forms: the widest door, the same doorway

On formation, the two Acts differ less than the reform's publicity suggested, and the difference they do make lies at the margins of who may incorporate and in what shape. The basic act of incorporation, association of persons subscribing to a memorandum and obtaining a certificate from the Registrar, passed across from Section 12 of the 1956 Act to Section 3 of the 2013 Act with its logic intact. What changed is the population of available forms. The 1956 Act offered the public company, the private company, the guarantee company, the unlimited company and the licensed Section 25 company for charitable and similar objects, the last of which survives as the Section 8 company under a new number and a new name. The 2013 Act added three forms that answer real gaps. The One Person Company, defined in Section 2(62) and permitted by Section 3(1)(c), gave a single promoter limited liability and separate legal personality without the fiction of a second shareholder holding a token share. The small company, defined in Section 2(85), created a statutory category whose compliance load could be lightened without dismantling the framework. The dormant company, under Section 455, gave companies formed for a future project, and companies that had gone inactive, a recognised status with minimal obligations instead of a choice between full compliance and striking off.

The private company, which is where most Indian incorporation actually happens, illustrates the comparison in miniature. The 1956 Act capped a private company's membership at fifty; Section 2(68) of the 2013 Act raised the ceiling to two hundred, a change that mattered to closely held companies with employee shareholders and layered family holdings. The core restrictions that define privateness, restricted transfer, no invitation to the public to subscribe, no public deposits, carried across. The burdens that practitioners complained of in the early operation of the 2013 Act, the application of provisions drafted with listed companies in mind to family businesses, were then eased not by amending the Act but by exemption notifications issued for private companies under Section 462, beginning in 2015. A comparison confined to the two bare Acts therefore misstates the private company position twice over: the 2013 Act as enacted was harder on private companies than its reputation, and the 2013 Act as administered was softened by delegated instruments that a section-level comparison never sees. The pattern recurs so often in this comparison that it deserves to be named here, because it will recur in the verdict: under the 2013 settlement, the lived law of a subject is frequently found one level below the statute.

### Does the Companies Act 2013 govern a company incorporated under the 1956 Act?

Yes. A company incorporated under the 1956 Act, or under any earlier company law, is a company for the purposes of the 2013 Act, whose definition in Section 2(20) expressly includes companies incorporated under previous law. Incorporation under the old statute is not a grandfathered status, and no company had to reincorporate.

The 2013 Act governs such a company's meetings, accounts, directors and filings from its commencement, subject to the savings in Section 465 for acts already done and rights already accrued under the repealed statute. That answer matters in practice because incorporation dates in India reach back, in some cases, more than a century, and a surprising amount of professional anxiety in the transition years was spent on a non-question. No company's constitution had to be re-executed; what changed was the statute under which its continuing life is regulated. The memorandum and articles framed under the 1956 Act remained effective, read subject to the 2013 Act, which by Section 6 overrides the memorandum and articles where they conflict with it. The genuinely difficult transition questions were never about existence. They were about proceedings on foot, approvals pending and defaults already committed, and they are taken up in the discussion of the transitional decade below.

## The board: composition legislated, duties codified

The board is where the 2013 Act departed most deliberately from its predecessor's philosophy, and the departure can be stated in one sentence. The 1956 Act regulated what directors did, transaction by transaction, largely through approvals and disclosures; the 2013 Act regulates who directors are, how the board is composed, and what duties they owe, and trusts composition to discipline conduct. The earlier statute's characteristic instrument was the permission: loans to directors required the approval of the Central Government under Section 295, contracts in which directors were interested required board approval and, above prescribed capital, government approval under Section 297, and an increase in the number of directors beyond twelve required government sanction under Section 259. The later statute's characteristic instruments are structural: a statutorily defined class of independent directors, board committees with mandated composition, a code of conduct in a schedule, and a codified statement of duties.

Section 166 is the centrepiece. Under the 1956 Act, a director's duties were fiduciary duties known to the common law, developed by the courts out of the director's position as an agent and trustee of the company's assets, and nowhere stated in the statute. Section 166 states them: to act in accordance with the articles, to act in good faith to promote the objects of the company for the benefit of its members as a whole and in the best interests of the company, its employees, the shareholders, the community and the environment, to exercise duties with due and reasonable care, skill and diligence and independent judgment, to avoid conflicts of interest, not to achieve undue gain, and not to assign the office. Codification changed the litigation posture of the duty. A claimant under the 1956 Act argued from principle and precedent; a claimant under the 2013 Act starts from a statutory list and argues about its reach, including the live question of how far the codified duties displace or merely restate the common law they summarise. Either way, the subject moved from the law reports into the statute book, which is why the classification table records it as created rather than changed: the duties existed, but a statutory law of directors' duties did not.

Composition followed the same logic. Listed public companies must have at least one-third of their board as independent directors under Section 149(4), with the statutory definition of independence in Section 149(6), a code for independent directors in Schedule IV, a separate meeting of independent directors, and performance evaluation built into the board's routine. Prescribed classes of unlisted public companies were brought within the independent director requirement by rules. At least one woman director is required for listed companies and for prescribed classes of public companies, under the proviso to Section 149(1) read with the rules. The audit committee in Section 177 and the nomination and remuneration committee in Section 178 became statutory organs with mandated composition and, in the audit committee's case, oversight of related party transactions and a vigil mechanism for directors and employees to report concerns. Key managerial personnel, a defined category in Section 2(51) spanning the chief executive or managing director, the company secretary, the whole-time director, the chief financial officer and prescribed officers, were made a statutory appointment for listed and prescribed companies under Section 203, replacing the 1956 Act's narrower and lonelier recognition of the company secretary in Section 383A.

### Did independent directors exist before the Companies Act 2013?

Yes, but as a listing condition rather than as company law. Independent directors entered Indian corporate governance through Clause 49 of the listing agreement, introduced by the Securities and Exchange Board of India in 2000 following the Kumar Mangalam Birla Committee, binding only on listed companies as a matter of their contract with the stock exchange.

The Companies Act 1956 contained no definition of an independent director and no statutory code for one. The 2013 Act converted a market-access condition into a statutory institution, with a definition, tenure rules and a schedule of conduct. The conversion is easy to underrate because the population of independent directors barely changed overnight; the same individuals sat on the same boards. What changed was the source and the sanction. A Clause 49 independent director answered to a listing regime enforced by the exchange and the securities regulator; a Section 149 independent director holds a statutory office, with a fixed tenure of up to five consecutive years, a second term possible only by special resolution, a cooling-off period, liability confined by Section 149(12) to acts occurring with the director's knowledge, consent or connivance or where the director lacked diligence, and a code in Schedule IV that speaks of upholding ethical standards and safeguarding the interests of all stakeholders, particularly minority shareholders. The 2013 Act also capped the board itself differently: the default maximum moved from twelve directors, expandable with government approval, to fifteen, expandable by special resolution of the shareholders, which is the Irani philosophy in a single detail, the shareholder meeting replacing the ministry as the approving authority.

The counter-reading deserves its full strength here, because it is the deepest objection to the whole governance design. Independent directors are, in the typical Indian listed company, nominated through a process the promoter dominates, remunerated modestly relative to the liability the office carries, and dependent for information on the management they are meant to question. Legislating composition legislates the appearance of a check more certainly than the reality of one, and the record of corporate failures in which boards full of formally independent directors presided over collapse is the standing evidence. The 2013 Act's defenders answer, correctly, that the comparison is not with an ideal board but with the 1956 position, in which the statute imposed almost no composition discipline at all and the promoter's board was lawful by default. On that comparison the change is real even if its behavioural yield remains unproven, and the classification stands: created, not merely changed.

## Audit: from an appointment to an accountability chain

Under the 1956 Act, audit was a bilateral arrangement between the company and its auditor, supervised at a distance by the profession's own institute. The auditor was appointed at each annual general meeting under Section 224, held office until the next, was qualified under Section 226, and reported to the members. The statute contained no rotation, no regulator of audit quality outside the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India, and no statutory list of services an auditor was forbidden to sell to the audit client. The Satyam disclosure of January 2009, in which the accounts of a major listed company were confessed to have been falsified over years while audits were signed, exposed what that architecture could not do, and the audit chapters of the 2013 Act are unintelligible except as a response to it.

The 2013 Act replaced the bilateral arrangement with a chain. Appointment under Section 139 is for a term of five years, and for listed companies and prescribed classes, Section 139(2) imposes rotation: an individual auditor may serve one term of five consecutive years and an audit firm two terms of five consecutive years, followed by a cooling-off period of five years. Section 141 recasts qualifications and disqualifications, Section 143 strengthens the auditor's powers and duties including a direct obligation to report suspected fraud to the Central Government, and Section 144 prohibits the auditor from rendering specified non-audit services, among them bookkeeping, internal audit, design of financial information systems and investment advisory, to the audit client and its group. Two audits that the 1956 Act did not know at all were added: internal audit under Section 138 for prescribed classes, and secretarial audit under Section 204 for listed and prescribed companies, extending the audit idea from the accounts to the compliance system itself.

Above the chain sits the institution with no 1956 counterpart at all. The National Financial Reporting Authority, created by Section 132 and constituted in 2018, is an independent regulator for audit: it recommends accounting and auditing standards, monitors and enforces compliance with them, oversees the quality of service of the professions, and can investigate professional misconduct by auditors of listed and prescribed companies, with power to impose penalties and to debar. The Serious Fraud Investigation Office likewise received a statutory footing in Sections 211 and 212, having existed since 2003 as a body created by executive resolution, and Section 212 arms it with investigation powers and treats its report as a police report for prosecution purposes. The comparison on audit is therefore not between two versions of one arrangement. It is between a self-regulated profession operating inside a disclosure statute and a supervised profession operating under a regulator, a rotation clock and a fraud-reporting duty. Of all the dimensions in this comparison, audit is the one where the claim that the 2013 Act changed the substance most completely is hardest to resist, and the classification table reflects that: appointment substantively changed, independence substantively changed, and the regulator newly created.

## Accounts and financial statements: the reporting spine rebuilt

Financial reporting is the dimension on which the two Acts look most alike at a distance and differ most in operation. Both statutes require books of account, an annual balance sheet and profit and loss account laid before the annual general meeting, an audit, and filing with the Registrar. The 1956 provisions, Sections 209 to 217 in the main, passed into Sections 128 to 134 of the 2013 Act in recognisable form, and the classification table accordingly records books of account as redrafted and the dividend and remuneration neighbours as carried. Beneath that continuity, four changes rebuilt the reporting spine.

The first is consolidation. Under the 1956 Act, a holding company was required by Section 212 to attach the accounts of its subsidiaries to its own; consolidated accounts as the primary group statement were demanded of listed companies through the listing agreement and accounting standards, not by the Companies Act itself. Section 129(3) of the 2013 Act made consolidated financial statements a statutory obligation for every company with subsidiaries, associates or joint ventures, prepared in Schedule III, which replaced Schedule VI of the old Act as the format of the financial statements. The second is the financial year. The 1956 Act permitted a company to choose its financial year, and Indian corporate reporting ran on a calendar of scattered year-ends. Section 2(41) of the 2013 Act imposed a uniform financial year ending on 31 March, with a tribunal-approved exception chiefly for companies consolidating with foreign parents, converting a matter of housekeeping into a tool of comparability. The third is the content of the financial statements themselves: the definition in Section 2(40) includes the cash flow statement as a constituent document, where under the old regime it had again been a listing requirement rather than a statutory one for companies generally. The fourth is responsibility. The Board's report under Section 134 carries a directors' responsibility statement wider than its Section 217 ancestor, the financial statements are signed on behalf of the Board by officers including the chief financial officer where one is appointed, and Section 131 introduced a mechanism, unknown to the 1956 Act, by which a company may revise its financial statements or the Board's report for earlier years with the tribunal's approval where they do not comply with the Act.

Each change follows the same comparative logic as the audit reforms, with which it interlocks. The 1956 Act's reporting law was a floor of minimum documents, above which the real disciplines for significant companies were supplied by the listing agreement and the accounting standards of the profession. The 2013 Act absorbed those disciplines into the statute, made them general rather than listing-dependent, and attached them to the new audit regulator's standard-setting power under Section 132. Whether that absorption counts as the statute growing or as the statute finally describing the law that already governed listed companies depends on the reader's company: for a listed group, 2013 codified its existing world; for a large unlisted company, it imposed a reporting regime of a weight the 1956 Act had never placed on it. That divergence, between the comparison as experienced by the listed sector and the comparison as experienced by everyone else, is one of the recurring findings of this article, and it qualifies every aggregate statement about burden that follows.

## Meetings and the machinery of shareholder decision

The general meeting is where the two Acts' philosophies meet in procedure, and the comparison here is of machinery rather than of power. The skeleton carried across entire: the annual general meeting of Section 166 became Section 96, the requisitioned extraordinary general meeting of Section 169 became Section 100, the twenty-one days' notice of Section 171 became Section 101, and the minutes of Section 193 became Section 118, each with its rule substantially intact. Even the quorum provision shows the drafting temper of the two statutes in contrast. Section 174 of the 1956 Act fixed the quorum for a public company at five members personally present unless the articles required more, one number for every company however vast. Section 103 of the 2013 Act scales the quorum to membership: five members for a company with up to a thousand members, fifteen for up to five thousand, and thirty beyond that, a small provision that captures the new Act's instinct for graduated, size-sensitive regulation where the old Act used a single figure.

Around the inherited skeleton, the 2013 Act built the machinery of participation at a distance. Postal ballot, which had entered the 1956 Act as Section 192A in 2000, was retained and expanded in Section 110. Electronic voting, which the 1956 Act never contemplated, was created by Section 108 and made mandatory for listed companies and prescribed classes by rules, so that a shareholder's vote no longer requires presence or a proxy. Directors were expressly permitted to participate in Board meetings through video conferencing under Section 173(2), a recognition the old Act's meeting provisions, built around physical presence, had been unable to give cleanly. The proxy institution itself was tightened: Section 105 confines a proxy's role, denies the proxy a vote on a show of hands and the right to speak, and the rules cap the number of members for whom one person may act as proxy. Shorter notice for a general meeting, possible under both Acts with the consent of an overwhelming majority, was recast in Section 101 with the consent threshold stated at ninety-five per cent.

The comparative significance of this machinery is easy to miss because none of it redistributes power in the dramatic manner of a class action. It redistributes attendance. The 1956 meeting was a physical event in a registered office city, at which the dispersed shareholder was represented, if at all, by a proxy form returned to management; the low turnouts and routine approvals of that era were structural, not accidental. The 2013 meeting, with electronic voting, postal ballots and video participation, made the vote of the absent shareholder a practical possibility, and in doing so made shareholder approval, the very mechanism to which the new Act transferred so many decisions from the government, a more credible instrument than it had been when the Irani Committee recommended the transfer. A comparison that asks only who approves, and not how the approvers can actually act, understates what changed here.

## Shareholder remedies: the minority acquires a second weapon

The oppression and mismanagement remedy is the oldest shareholder remedy in Indian company law, and its continuity across the two Acts is the single most instructive example of how much of the 2013 Act is the 1956 Act wearing a new number. Sections 397 and 398 of the 1956 Act allowed members meeting the thresholds of Section 399 to apply for relief where the affairs of the company were being conducted in a manner oppressive to members or prejudicial to public interest, or where mismanagement threatened the company's interests. Sections 241 and 242 of the 2013 Act re-enact that remedy before the National Company Law Tribunal, with the eligibility thresholds in Section 244. The governing ideas carried across entire: the Supreme Court's reading of oppression in Shanti Prasad Jain v. Kalinga Tubes Ltd as conduct involving a visible departure from fair dealing and a lack of probity, and its elaboration in Needle Industries Ltd v. Needle Industries Newey (India) Holding Ltd, where the Court held that the conduct complained of must be burdensome, harsh and wrongful, and that illegal conduct alone, or a mere loss of confidence between factions, is not enough, remain the framework within which Section 241 petitions are argued and decided.

The changes are nonetheless real, and they are of three kinds. The forum changed, from the Company Law Board and, for winding up in the alternative, the High Court, to a single tribunal constituted on 1 June 2016. The text widened: Section 241 speaks of conduct prejudicial to public interest, prejudicial or oppressive to members, or prejudicial to the interests of the company itself, and it adds a forward-looking limb for material changes in management or control likely to produce such conduct. And the tribunal's remedial powers under Section 242 were expressed more expansively, including the power to regulate the conduct of the company's affairs in future, to direct purchase of shares, to restrict transfer or allotment, and to terminate or modify agreements. The Supreme Court's decision in Tata Consultancy Services Ltd v. Cyrus Investments Pvt. Ltd. in 2021, which set aside the appellate tribunal's order reinstating a removed executive chairman and held that removal from a position, without more, does not establish oppression, was decided under the new sections while reasoning continuously with the older jurisprudence, which is the continuity thesis demonstrated in a single leading case.

Alongside the inherited remedy, the 2013 Act placed an entirely new one. Section 245 created the class action, under which the prescribed number of members or depositors may apply to the tribunal on behalf of the class for orders restraining ultra vires and unlawful acts, voiding resolutions procured by suppression of facts, and claiming damages or compensation from the company, its directors, its auditors and its advisers. Nothing in the 1956 Act resembled it; the Indian shareholder's representative route had been confined to the representative suit under the Code of Civil Procedure, which company practice rarely used. The Irani Committee had recommended class actions in 2005, and the Satyam episode gave the recommendation its moral force, since Satyam's shareholders pursued their remedy in the United States while no equivalent vehicle existed at home. The honest assessment must add that the class action's practical record has been thin: brought into force only in June 2016, with its filing thresholds prescribed only in 2019, it has been more significant as a statement of direction than as a working docket. The minority shareholder of the 2013 settlement is better armed on paper, armed chiefly with an old weapon in a new forum, and possessed of a new weapon whose battlefield use remains largely untested.

## Related party transactions: permission gives way to approval

No comparison between the two Acts shows the change of governing philosophy more cleanly than the treatment of transactions between a company and its own insiders. The 1956 model was a permission model administered by the executive. Section 297 required the prior approval of the Board for contracts with firms, companies and persons in which directors were interested, and where the company's paid-up share capital was not less than one crore rupees, the prior approval of the Central Government as well. The theory was that a ministry could screen self-dealing before it happened. The practice was delay, formulaic applications, and a screen that caught the honest paperwork while determined diversion travelled by routes the section did not reach.

### Who approved a related party contract under the Companies Act 1956?

The Board of Directors approved it first, and for larger companies the Central Government approved it as well. Section 297 of the 1956 Act required prior Board approval for contracts for the sale, purchase or supply of goods, materials or services, and for underwriting contracts, where a director was interested directly or indirectly.

Where the company's paid-up capital was one crore rupees or more, the Central Government's prior approval was also mandatory, subject to exceptions that included contracts between two public companies. The ministry, not the shareholders, was the second gatekeeper. The 2013 Act abolished the ministry's gate and built two others in its place. Section 188 requires the consent of the Board by resolution at a meeting for specified categories of related party transaction, among them sale, purchase or supply of goods or materials, selling, buying or leasing property, appointment of agents, appointment of a related party to an office or place of profit, and underwriting. For transactions crossing prescribed thresholds, prior approval of the shareholders is required, and related parties may not vote on the resolution. The audit committee, under Section 177, must approve related party transactions in the companies required to have one, and the disclosure obligations run through the Board's report and, for listed companies, through the securities regulator's listing regulations, which impose their own materiality thresholds and shareholder approval requirements. The interested director's disclosure duty, which the 1956 Act carried in Section 299, appears in Section 184, and the bar on an interested director participating or voting is built into the new scheme.

The classification is substantively changed, and the direction of the change is the Irani direction exactly: the state withdrew from ex ante permission and substituted informed approval by the organ with the money at stake, supported by disclosure. Whether shareholders in promoter-dominated companies can operate that gate independently is the standing question, and it is the same question that hangs over independent directors, because the two reforms share one assumption. What cannot be said is that the change is cosmetic. A practitioner advising on a material related party transaction under the 2013 Act runs a different process, through different organs, on a different documentary record, from anything Section 297 ever required, and cases under the old section about the validity of government approval have almost nothing to say to the new process. On this dimension, unusually, the old case law is precisely what does not carry.

## Capital raising and deposits: the money rules tightened and codified

The provisions by which a company raises money from the public show the 2013 Act at its most ambivalent: simultaneously liberalising the forms of capital and tightening the conditions on which the public's money may be taken. The prospectus chapters illustrate the mixture. The 1956 Act's offer provisions, Sections 55 to 66, passed into Chapter III of the 2013 Act, Sections 23 to 41, carrying with them the shelf prospectus of old Section 60A, which became Section 31, and the red herring prospectus of Section 60B, which became Section 32, while the civil and criminal liability provisions for misstatements in a prospectus were restated and the penalty exposure increased. A genuinely new instrument appeared in Section 41, which gave statutory recognition to global depository receipts as a route for Indian companies to raise capital abroad.

Private placement is the sharper contrast, because it shows codification itself as a form of tightening. Under the 1956 Act there was no private placement code at all: the boundary between a public offer and a private arrangement was inferred from Section 67, which defined when an offer was made to the public and spared offers that were not calculated to result in the securities becoming available to others. Practice built a large edifice of preferential allotments and private placements on that inference, regulated for listed companies by the securities regulator's guidelines. Section 42 of the 2013 Act replaced the inference with a regime: an offer by private placement may be made only to identified persons, capped by rules at two hundred in a financial year for each kind of security, with no public advertising, no marketing, monies received only through banking channels into a separate account, allotment within sixty days, and a return of allotment filed with the Registrar, with penal consequences for breach that treat the offer as a public offer made in contravention of the Act. Preferential allotment by existing shareholders' special resolution, which the 1956 Act carried in Section 81(1A), was recast within Section 62. The classification of the whole subject as substantively changed records a real migration: from a boundary drawn by interpretation to a boundary drawn by a compliance code.

Deposits travelled in the opposite direction along the same road, from permission to restriction. Section 58A, inserted into the 1956 Act in 1974 after a generation of deposit defaults by companies outside the banking system, empowered the Central Government to regulate the acceptance of deposits, and the regime lived in rules made under it. Chapter V of the 2013 Act, Sections 73 to 76, brought the regime into the statute and hardened its centre: a company may accept deposits from its members under Section 73 only on conditions that include shareholders' approval, a deposit repayment reserve and compliance with prescribed terms, while deposits from the public at large may be accepted only by an eligible public company, one meeting prescribed thresholds of net worth or turnover, under Section 76, with credit rating and other safeguards. The company that funds itself from the savings of strangers was a familiar figure of the 1956 era and a recurring source of loss; the 2013 settlement treats that figure as an exception to be licensed by size and rating rather than as an ordinary incident of incorporation. On deposits, as on related party transactions, the comparison shows the same exchange in opposite directions: the executive's discretionary rulebook was replaced by statutory gates, and the gates were set higher.

## Corporate social responsibility: the obligation with no ancestor

Section 135 is the provision most often singled out as the emblem of the 2013 Act, and for the comparative question its significance is classificatory. Every other major subject in this article has a 1956 ancestor whose treatment can be compared. Corporate social responsibility has none. The 1956 Act left corporate giving to the articles, the Board's general powers and the tax code; a company that wished to spend on social purposes did so as a matter of discretion, constrained by the doctrine that expenditure must benefit the company. Section 135 made the subject statutory for companies crossing thresholds of net worth, turnover or net profit fixed in the Act itself: such a company must constitute a corporate social responsibility committee of the Board, formulate a policy, and ensure that it spends at least two per cent of the average net profits of the three preceding financial years on the activities listed in Schedule VII, or explain the shortfall in the Board's report, a comply-or-explain design that later amendments hardened by requiring unspent amounts to be transferred to specified funds or special accounts.

For the comparison, the point is not whether mandatory corporate social responsibility is wise, a debate this article records without needing to settle. The point is what its presence proves about the character of the 2013 Act. A legislature that believed its task was to shorten and simplify the statute book does not usually invent an entirely new field of corporate obligation with its own committee, its own schedule of permitted activities, its own reporting line and, in time, its own penalty provisions and registration regime for implementing agencies. Section 135 is the clearest single evidence that the 2013 Act pursued two projects at once that pulled against each other: the Irani project of a compact, principles-based framework, and a governance-and-stakeholder project that kept adding substantive programmes the framework then had to carry. The tension between those projects, visible here in one section, is the tension the verdict has to resolve.

## The subjects that left: winding up, sickness and the separate code

The largest single block of the 1956 Act was its ending machinery. Part VII, running from Section 425 to Section 560, governed winding up by the court, members' and creditors' voluntary winding up, and winding up subject to the court's supervision, administered through the High Courts and the official liquidators. Chapter VI-A, inserted in 2002, would have created a regime for the revival of sick industrial companies inside the Act, replacing the external apparatus of the Sick Industrial Companies (Special Provisions) Act; it was never brought into force, and the sickness jurisdiction remained with the Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction while the chapter slept in the statute book. A comparison of bulk alone would say that the 2013 Act, with its Chapter XX on winding up and Chapter XIX on revival and rehabilitation of sick companies, reproduced this architecture in modern dress.

The operative history is different, and it is the strongest exhibit in the entire comparison. The 2013 Act's revival chapter was omitted by the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code of 2016 before it had ever been brought into force, the second Indian attempt to house corporate rescue inside the Companies Act to die uncommenced. The ground of inability to pay debts was removed from the tribunal winding up jurisdiction and re-founded as the insolvency resolution process of the Code, voluntary winding up followed it into the Code in 2017, and the Sick Industrial Companies Act was repealed with the Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction dissolved. What remains of winding up in the Companies Act is the residual jurisdiction, principally for winding up on grounds other than insolvency, administered by the tribunal. In under three years from the main commencement of the 2013 Act, the subject that had occupied more than a fifth of the 1956 Act's sections had been relocated almost entirely to a separate code with its own institutions, its own professionals and its own adjudicatory track.

### Why did winding up leave the Companies Act?

Because the rescue and insolvency project outgrew the statute that hosted it. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code of 2016 was designed as a single code for corporate insolvency, individual insolvency and the regulation of insolvency professionals, with time-bound resolution as its organising promise.

Keeping corporate winding up inside the Companies Act would have split one subject between two statutes and two philosophies, an exit statute administered as liquidation and a resolution statute aimed at revival. Parliament chose consolidation in the Code, and the Companies Act's winding up and revival chapters were omitted or cut back accordingly.

The classification of these rows as relocated, rather than changed, is what distinguishes this comparison from a routine old-Act-new-Act exercise, and it carries a lesson about statutory reform generally. The 1956 Act's length was partly the length of a code that tried to contain a company's whole life from incorporation to dissolution. The 2013 settlement broke that ambition deliberately: incorporation and governance in the Companies Act, exit and rescue in the insolvency code, securities markets with the securities regulator, competition with the competition statute. Judged as company law alone, the 2013 Act is a smaller book partly because it governs less of the company's life. Judged as the law a company must actually obey, the relocation changed the address of the obligations, not their existence, which is the arithmetic point of this article recurring at the level of the whole statute book.

## Enforcement: the criminal statute of 2013 and the retreat that followed

The enforcement comparison has a shape unlike any other dimension, because it is a comparison among three positions rather than two. The 1956 Act enforced itself through a dense undergrowth of fines and a smaller number of imprisonable offences, accumulated over decades, in which procedural defaults and substantive wrongdoing were punished in the same register and compounding under Section 621A did much of the practical work of sorting them. The 2013 Act as enacted sharpened the register dramatically. It multiplied monetary penalties, attached imprisonment to a wide range of defaults, and at its apex created, in Section 447, a defined offence of fraud carrying imprisonment of six months to ten years and a fine extending to three times the amount involved, with a higher minimum where public interest was involved. Fraud had no statutory definition under the 1956 Act; the 2013 Act defined it expansively, to include acts, omissions, concealment of facts and abuse of position committed with intent to deceive or to injure the interests of the company, its shareholders or its creditors, whether or not there was wrongful gain or loss.

The third position is the 2013 Act as amended. The amendment Acts of 2015 and 2017 adjusted individual provisions, and then the direction of travel reversed. The Companies (Amendment) Act of 2019 recategorised sixteen compoundable offences as civil defaults to be dealt with by penalty under an in-house adjudication mechanism centred on Section 454, in which an adjudicating officer imposes a monetary penalty without a criminal prosecution. A Company Law Committee review of the remaining compoundable offences followed, and the Companies (Amendment) Act of 2020 carried the programme much further: more defaults were moved into in-house adjudication, penalties for a set of offences were omitted altogether, imprisonment was removed from provisions where it had attached to technical defaults, including the default provisions around buy-back, and rationalised penalty amounts replaced several open-ended ones. Criminal liability was deliberately re-concentrated on fraud and on conduct involving deception or harm to public interest. Even the corporate social responsibility default travelled the full arc in miniature: made punishable with imprisonment by the 2019 amendments, it was converted back into a monetary penalty in 2020.

The manner of the reversal is itself evidence. The decriminalisation programme began not with a considered amendment bill but with the Companies (Amendment) Ordinance of November 2018, promulgated while Parliament was not in session and re-promulgated before it was enacted as the amendment Act of 2019, a sequence that indicates a government persuaded that the criminal exposure of directors and officers for technical defaults had become urgent enough to legislate by ordinance. The 2019 Act also carried the first tightening of the corporate social responsibility regime, and the Company Law Committee's wider review then supplied the programme for 2020. A reform whose penalty chapter required emergency correction within five years of commencement, and systematic reconstruction within seven, had not calibrated its enforcement design to the conduct it governed; that much both sides of the decriminalisation debate accept, differing only on whether the correction restored proportion or surrendered deterrence.

The seven-year arc from enactment to decriminalisation is unusually fast for a foundational statute, and the brief for this comparison is right to identify it as the strongest single evidence that the original enforcement design was misjudged. A legislature does not dismantle the penalty architecture of a seven-year-old statute, on the advice of its own committee, unless the architecture has been found in operation to punish the wrong conduct: to expose directors and officers to prosecution for filing delays and procedural lapses, to deter competent persons from accepting board office, and to consume enforcement capacity on defaults that injured no one, while prosecution for genuine fraud remained as difficult as it had always been. The defenders of the original design reply that the retreat overshot, that monetary penalties on companies are paid out of shareholders' funds and deter promoters hardly at all, and that the fear of Section 447 was the one sanction that concentrated minds in boardrooms. Both positions are live, and both are really arguments about a question the 1956 Act never had to face squarely, because its penalties had grown by accretion rather than by design: what criminal law is for inside a regulatory statute. The comparative verdict on enforcement is therefore double. Against the 1956 Act, the amended 2013 settlement is more rationally ordered, reserving prosecution for fraud and processing defaults administratively. Against its own enacted text, the 2013 Act stands corrected by its authors within seven years, and any account of the reform that quotes the 2013 penalty regime without the 2019 and 2020 amendments is describing a statute that operated, in that form, only briefly.

## The schedules: where both Acts always kept their detail

The argument that the 2013 Act invented delegation does not survive a reading of its predecessor's schedules, and the schedules deserve a section of their own because they are the honest middle ground of this comparison. The 1956 Act's fifteen schedules carried much of its operative weight: Table A in Schedule I supplied the default articles of association that most companies adopted in whole or part, Schedule VI prescribed the form and content of the balance sheet and profit and loss account, Schedule XIII governed the appointment and remuneration of managerial personnel where profits were inadequate, and Schedule XIV prescribed rates of depreciation. A company lawyer of the old regime lived in the schedules as much as in the sections, and the schedules could be amended by notification, which made them the 1956 Act's own flexible layer.

The 2013 Act's seven schedules continue the device with changed content. Schedule I supplies the model memoranda and articles in its Tables, Schedule III replaced Schedule VI as the financial statement format, Schedule V took over Schedule XIII's territory on managerial remuneration, and Schedule IV, the code for independent directors, is a schedule doing a job no 1956 schedule ever attempted, stating norms of conduct for a class of office-holder. One change within the schedules captures the philosophical shift in miniature. Depreciation under the 1956 Act was governed by Schedule XIV's prescribed rates: the statute told every company the percentage at which a plant or a ship or a building was to be written down. The 2013 Act's Schedule II prescribes useful lives for asset classes and leaves the company to compute depreciation on that basis, with disclosure and justification where its estimate differs. Rates commanded from the centre became lives estimated by the enterprise under a stated framework, the permission-to-judgment migration in its purest form.

The schedules therefore qualify the article's deciding factor without overturning it. Delegation was not new in 2013; what was new was its scale and its descent from schedules, which sit inside the Act and are read with it, to a free-standing universe of rules, forms and class-specific exemption notifications that must be consulted separately and that change without any single document recording the whole law. A reader of the 1956 Act who had the Act had, schedules included, substantially the whole of company law in one volume. A reader of the 2013 Act who has the Act has the frame of company law and an index to the rest. That difference in the experience of holding the law is the deciding factor stated in the language of the bookshelf, and it is why the section count, the one number that improved, is the least informative number in the entire comparison.

## Does the old case law still decide new cases?

For the practitioner, this is the comparison's most valuable question, and it has a structured answer rather than a slogan. The 2013 Act repealed the 1956 Act by Section 465, with savings for actions taken, rights accrued and proceedings pending under the repealed statute, and the general principles of the General Clauses Act 1897 protect continuity where an enactment is repealed and re-enacted. On top of that statutory continuity sits a doctrine of interpretation: where Parliament re-enacts words that have received an authoritative judicial construction, the re-enactment is presumed to adopt that construction, while deliberate change in the words is read as a deliberate change in the law. The practical rule that emerges, and that the courts have in fact followed, is that precedent under a 1956 provision remains authoritative for a 2013 provision that carries the same operative rule, persuasive where the rule is similar, and of little use where the 2013 Act changed the substance. This comparison is general legal information about how the two statutes relate; which Act and which precedent govern a live dispute turns on its own dates and facts, and on a matter where forum and limitation turn on the answer, advice on the specific case is indispensable.

Applied across the classification table, the rule sorts itself. In the renumbered and redrafted rows, the old jurisprudence governs almost without friction: the learning on meetings, minutes, charges and dividends accumulated under the 1956 Act continues to be cited because the rules being interpreted are the same rules. The oppression remedy is the showcase, where Shanti Prasad Jain and Needle Industries, decided under Sections 397 and 398, supply the operative tests under Sections 241 and 242, and where the Supreme Court in the Tata and Cyrus Investments litigation reasoned within that inherited framework while applying the new sections. The principles for sanctioning a scheme of arrangement laid down under Section 391, including those in Miheer H. Mafatlal v. Mafatlal Industries Ltd on the court's supervisory rather than appellate role and its insistence on disclosure, informed approval and fairness, structure applications under Section 230 for the same reason: the test was carried across with the provision.

In the substantively changed rows, the carry-over fails in proportion to the change. Decisions under Section 297 on the necessity and effect of Central Government approval cannot construe Section 188, which abolished that approval and substituted shareholder consent. Decisions on the tenure of auditors appointed annually under Section 224 say nothing about rotation under Section 139, which has no past. In the newly created rows there is nothing to carry: Section 166's codified duties are argued from the statutory text, with the common law as background rather than as the operative source; the class action, the audit regulator and corporate social responsibility generate their own jurisprudence from a standing start. And in the relocated rows the old company law learning on winding up survives mainly as background to a code whose philosophy, resolution within strict timelines rather than liquidation administered at leisure, is pointed in the opposite direction from the 1956 winding up cases. The answer to whether judgments under the 1956 Act still apply is therefore not yes or no but a map, and the map is the classification table itself.

## The decade of two statutes: the transition cost no text comparison captures

Every comparison of the two Acts that proceeds section by section misses the cost that practitioners remember most clearly, because it appears in neither text. The 2013 Act was not commenced at once. A first tranche of 98 sections was notified in September 2013, the main body followed from 1 April 2014, the tribunal provisions waited until the National Company Law Tribunal was constituted on 1 June 2016, and the striking off chapter arrived only in December 2016. Because the repeal of the 1956 Act was tied to the commencement of the corresponding new provisions, subjects whose 2013 provisions had not been notified continued to be governed by the 1956 Act, and for years the correct answer to a company law question depended on identifying which statute governed that subject on that date. Proceedings before the Company Law Board and the High Courts were transferred to the tribunal in stages under Section 434, and the transfer itself generated litigation about which stage a pending matter had reached.

### Why did both Acts apply at the same time for several years?

Because commencement was staggered and repeal followed commencement. The Companies Act 2013 was brought into force in tranches, beginning with 98 sections in September 2013 and the main body from 1 April 2014, while some chapters waited years for the institutions they depended on.

Section 465 repealed the 1956 Act subject to savings, and provisions of the old Act corresponding to uncommenced new provisions continued to operate. The result was a dual regime in which the governing statute varied by subject and by date, the defining practical experience of the transition.

The emblem of the dual regime is the producer company. Part IXA of the 1956 Act, inserted in 2002, created the producer company, and the 2013 Act as enacted contained no corresponding chapter, so producer companies simply continued to be governed by the repealed statute's surviving part for years after the repeal, until the Companies (Amendment) Act of 2020 inserted a producer companies chapter into the 2013 Act. For the better part of a decade, an entire class of Indian companies lived under a part of an Act that had otherwise ceased to exist. The episode is not a footnote. It demonstrates that replacement, for a statute of this size, is not an event but a process measured in years, and that the process has real costs: dual compliance calendars, advice hedged by commencement notifications, and a generation of examination answers and practice notes that must specify a date before they can state the law. The reform intended to reduce burden was accompanied by a decade of transitional uncertainty in which both statutes partially applied, and any honest verdict on the reform must charge that decade against it, because it was a foreseeable consequence of choosing full replacement over amendment for a statute of 658 sections.

## The forum: from High Courts and a Board to a tribunal system

Company disputes under the 1956 Act were distributed across a patchwork that every practitioner had to memorise. Winding up, reduction of capital and the sanction of schemes belonged to the High Courts, exercising a specialised company jurisdiction. Oppression and mismanagement petitions, in the Act's later decades, lay before the Company Law Board, a quasi-judicial body of the executive that also handled a miscellany of approval and rectification matters. Prosecutions went to the ordinary criminal courts, and appeals and writs returned everything, sooner or later, to the High Courts. The system's virtues were judicial seniority and a deep body of High Court learning; its vices were delay, the competition of company work with the general docket, and a split of related questions between a court and a board that could not bind each other.

The 2013 Act consolidated the civil side of that patchwork into a tribunal system: the National Company Law Tribunal as the forum of first instance for oppression, class actions, schemes, revival questions, rectification and most applications under the Act, and the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal above it, with a further appeal to the Supreme Court on questions of law. The Company Law Board was dissolved and its pending matters transferred. For offences, the Act created special courts in Section 435, designated courts intended to try company offences expeditiously, and for the resolution of disputes without adjudication it created a mediation and conciliation panel in Section 442, neither of which had any 1956 ancestor. The design intention was the standard tribunal argument: specialisation, speed, and a single door for company disputes.

The comparative assessment must be honest in both directions. Consolidation is real, and a generation of practitioners litigates company law in one forum family instead of three. But the tribunal system inherited the weakness that had haunted its 1956-era predecessor design, for the 2002 amendment to the old Act had attempted the same consolidation and then failed for fourteen years to constitute the tribunal it created, a failure owned and examined elsewhere in this series. The 2013 tribunal was itself constituted only on 1 June 2016, nearly three years after the Act, and its benches have carried the transferred arrears of the Board and the High Courts alongside the new filings under a statute that made the tribunal the answer to almost every question. A forum reform should be judged on disposal and quality over time, and on both measures the evidence remains incomplete; what the comparison can say with confidence is structural. The 1956 Act distributed company justice among institutions that already existed and were already busy. The 2013 Act bet the administration of company law on institutions that had to be built, and for its first years the bet left parts of the statute without a functioning forum, which is the institutional face of the transitional cost assessed below.

## The Registrar and the administrative state: enforcement by office rather than by court

One dimension of the comparison belongs to neither the boardroom nor the courtroom, and it is the dimension most companies experience directly: the Registrar of Companies and the administrative machinery of the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. The 1956 Act was administered through paper filings, physical inspections of registers, and prosecutions launched in magistrates' courts for defaults as minor as a late annual return, a system whose practical sanction was less the penalty than the summons. Two changes, one begun under the old Act and one completed under the new, transformed that experience. Electronic filing arrived first: the MCA21 e-governance programme moved the registry online in 2006, while the 1956 Act still governed, a reminder that administrative modernisation and statutory replacement were parallel projects rather than one causing the other. The 2013 Act then rebuilt the sanctioning machinery around the registry itself.

Compounding, the mechanism by which an offence is settled on payment without a full prosecution, existed under Section 621A of the 1956 Act and was retained in Section 441 of the 2013 Act with its forums recalibrated between the tribunal, the Regional Director and the authorised officer according to the fine involved; it is one of the quiet continuities of the administrative law of companies. What was new was adjudication. Section 454, present in the 2013 Act from the outset for specified defaults, made the Registrar and authorised officers adjudicating officers who could impose penalties after a hearing within the department, and the decriminalisation amendments of 2019 and 2020 converted that provision from a side channel into the main road of enforcement, as offence after offence was reclassified from a crime triable by a magistrate into a default priced by an officer. The striking off power tells the same story in the Registrar's hands: Section 560 of the 1956 Act was used sparingly across six decades, while its successor provisions, Sections 248 to 252, notified in December 2016, became the instrument of mass deregistration drives in which, the Minister of State for Corporate Affairs informed the Rajya Sabha, more than 3,82,000 companies were struck off over one three-year period. The administrative state of the 2013 settlement is simply more capable than its predecessor: it files electronically, adjudicates in-house, and removes the dead from the register at industrial scale. Whether that capability is a compliance dividend or a new source of arbitrary power depends, as with the governance machinery, on the discipline of its exercise; but as a matter of comparison there is no serious doubt that the state's administrative grip on the register strengthened even as its role in approving transactions was withdrawn. The 2013 Act took the government out of the boardroom and put it, better armed, in the registry.

## Two theories of the company: the permissioned association and the governed market actor

Beneath the section-level comparison sits a difference in what each statute imagines a company to be, and naming it explains why the changes cluster as they do. The 1956 Act was drafted in the decade of the licence and the permit, and its company is an association that exists by registration but acts by permission. The permissions are everywhere once they are looked for. A public company could not commence business or exercise borrowing powers on incorporation alone: it needed a certificate of commencement of business under Section 149, granted when the Registrar was satisfied that the capital and prospectus conditions had been met. The company could not lend to its own directors without the Central Government's approval under Section 295, could not contract with interested directors above a modest size without approval under Section 297, could not exceed twelve directors without sanction under Section 259, could not pay its managers beyond the ceilings and approvals of the remuneration sections, and held its inter-corporate loans and investments within the statutory limits and approvals of Sections 370 and 372A. The theory was not foolish. In an economy of scarce capital, concentrated family ownership and thin markets, the ministry's file was meant to be the minority shareholder's and the public's first line of defence, because no other institution, no active shareholder base, no financial press of consequence, no tribunal, existed to perform the task.

The 2013 Act's company is a different creature: a market actor whose internal governance is specified by law and whose decisions are taken by its own organs under disclosure, with the state retreating to registration, standard-setting and punishment after the fact. The certificate of commencement disappeared as a concept; the 2013 Act substituted a simple declaration under Section 11 that the subscribers had paid for their shares, and then omitted even that requirement in 2015. Government approval for directors' loans became a near-prohibition in Section 185, policed by the Board and the shareholders rather than licensed by the ministry. Inter-corporate loans and investments were gathered into Section 186, governed by Board approval, special resolution beyond stated limits expressed as proportions of capital and reserves, and disclosure, without a ministry file. Remuneration remained capped by Section 197, but approvals within the cap moved decisively to shareholders. At every point the pattern is identical: the prior question the state asked, may this company do this act, was replaced by the posterior questions, was the act approved by the right organ, was it disclosed, and who answers for it.

The comparison of theories also explains the 2013 Act's characteristic anxieties. A permission regime fails by capture and delay, and the 1956 Act's critics could point to both. A governance regime fails when its organs are not independent of the persons they govern, and the 2013 Act's critics point to promoter-dominated boards and passive shareholder meetings with equal justice. Neither statute escaped the central difficulty of Indian corporate life, which is that the typical company has a controlling shareholder and the law is written for the protection of everyone else. The 1956 Act addressed that difficulty with a policeman at the gate, who waved most traffic through and could be waited out. The 2013 Act addressed it with architecture inside the building: independent directors, committees, auditors on a rotation clock, a regulator above the auditors, and remedies, old and new, before a tribunal. Architecture is the more ambitious answer and, where its occupants behave as designed, the more effective one. The verdict of this article credits it accordingly, while recording that architecture, unlike a policeman, cannot be photographed doing its work, and that the evidence of its working remains the thinnest part of the reform's record.

## The case for the road not taken: amending the 1956 Act instead

A defended verdict must survive the strongest alternative, and the strongest alternative to the 2013 settlement was never the status quo. It was the road India had been travelling for decades: continued amendment of the 1956 Act. That road deserves its case stated properly, because the evidence for its viability is written into the old Act's own history. The 1956 Act absorbed, by amendment, much of what is credited to its successor. The Sachar Committee's reforms came through the amendment of 1988. Deposit regulation had arrived by amendment in 1974. Buy-back of securities, unknown to the original Act, was inserted as Section 77A in 1999. The governance wave of the amendment of 2000 brought postal ballots and the first statutory governance requirements, and electronic filing followed. Producer companies were created by amendment in 2002, and director identification numbers by amendment in 2006. A statute that could acquire a deposit code, a buy-back code, a producer company code and a director identity system by amendment was not incapable of acquiring independent directors, codified duties or a class action by the same route, and several of those reforms had been recommended by committees whose work predated the replacement decision by years.

The amendment road also held two advantages that replacement surrendered. The first was continuity of interpretation: every amendment to the 1956 Act entered a statute whose case law was settled, and the cost of the 2013 transition, the dual-regime decade examined in this article, would have been avoided entirely. The second was honesty about burden: an amending legislature must justify each addition on its own merits, while a replacing legislature can present a total that looks smaller than the sum of its parts by changing the counting unit, which is, on the argument of this article, close to what occurred.

The case fails, in the end, on the evidence of the old Act's physical condition. Amendment had been practised so continuously that the statute's architecture had ceased to describe its contents: insertions crowded the numbering with lettered sections, 58A for deposits, 77A for buy-back, 192A for postal ballots, 266A onwards for director identification, 383A for company secretaries, 424A onwards for a sickness chapter that was never commenced, so that the section number of a provision ceased to tell a reader where the subject lived. Institutions created by amendment, above all the tribunal inserted in 2002, could be left unbuilt for fourteen years because an amendment creates no political moment at which the whole settlement is judged. And the failures that mattered most by 2009, the absence of an audit regulator, of a class action, of any statutory law of directors' duties, were failures of the statute's conceptual frame, which treated governance as a matter of permissions; amendment had had fifty years to correct that frame and had instead decorated it. The classification table is the final answer to the counterfactual: a reform in which twenty-seven of forty-four significant subjects are changed in substance or created outright is not a repair job. It is a replacement that the subject matter had already performed in fact, and the 2013 Act's real offence against good legislative practice was not that it replaced the old statute, but that it replaced it while claiming the virtues of lightness that its own design had traded away.

## The verdict: a governance reform that failed its own brevity test

The comparison can be decided, and the decision should be given in the terms the benchmark supplies. The Irani Committee asked for a compact, principles-based statute that would be shorter in substance and not merely in numbering, with detail delegated so the law could adapt, decisions moved from government to shareholders, and small companies relieved of burdens designed for large ones. Against that commission, the reform's record divides cleanly.

On governance architecture, the reform delivered beyond its brief. The codified duties of Section 166, the statutory independent director and the board committees, the audit chain capped by an independent regulator, the class action, the fraud offence, the shareholder approval model for related party transactions: these are structural additions that changed who holds power inside an Indian company and who answers for its exercise, and none of them existed in the 1956 settlement. A critic who calls the 2013 Act the 1956 Act renumbered has not read the classification table, where the created and substantively changed rows outnumber the carried rows across every subject that involves the exercise of power.

On its brevity and simplicity commission, the reform failed, and the deciding factor is the one named at the outset: the location of the detail. The detail did not leave the law. It moved from sections to rules, from a text amended occasionally by Parliament to a body of delegated legislation amended frequently by notification, supplemented by exemption notifications that vary the Act's application by class of company, by circulars that interpret it, and by a commencement history that staggered its own arrival over years. The total body of binding company law grew, its rate of change increased, and its accessibility fell, because a rule is harder for a small company to track than a section and because the operative answer to a compliance question routinely requires the Act, a rule, a form and the amendments made to each. Add the enforcement misjudgment of 2013, corrected by the legislature itself in 2019 and 2020, and the transitional decade in which two statutes ran in parallel, and the pattern is consistent: the reform was at its best when it redesigned institutions and at its weakest when it promised lightness.

The verdict follows. India's company law reform delivered a better governance statute and a heavier legal regime, simultaneously and by the same design choices. The claim that Indian company law was simplified in 2013 is an artefact of counting sections rather than counting duties, and it should be retired. The claim that nothing changed is an artefact of reading the carried rows and skipping the created ones, and it should be retired with it. What the reform actually proves, for any legislature contemplating the same instruction again, is that a principles-based drafting mandate redistributes complexity rather than abolishing it, and that the honest place to audit such a reform is the rule book, not the statute book.

## The verdict adjusted by reader

The general verdict holds for the system, but the four readers who opened this article stand in different places inside it, and each deserves the adjusted answer. The practitioner should treat the classification table as the working tool: cite 1956 authority freely in the carried rows, with the re-enactment presumption behind the citation; argue from the statutory text in the changed rows, where the old cases are most dangerous precisely because they feel familiar; and expect the new jurisprudence of the created rows to be built for years to come, which is where advocacy can still shape the law. The company secretary should budget for the 2013 settlement as the heavier regime, whatever the section count says, with the qualification that the burden is uneven: after the exemption notifications and the decriminalisation amendments, a small private company that keeps its filings clean carries less criminal risk than it did under the enacted 2013 text, while a listed company carries a governance apparatus the 1956 Act never imposed on anyone.

The aspirant should answer the examination comparison the way this article is built: benchmark first, classification second, verdict third, with the deciding factor named, because that structure is what separates a distinction answer from a feature list. The comparison is a staple of the professional papers, and candidates preparing for them will find the structured treatment in [the corporate law exam study guide](/2012/06/15/corporate-law-exam-study-guide/), while those who revise from slides can [work through the Companies Act comparison as a presentation in the ReportMedic viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) before attempting a timed answer. The legislator or staffer should draw the design lesson in its full form: delegation bought adaptability and spent legibility, criminalisation bought a brief appearance of seriousness and was repurchased at the price of two amendment Acts, and a replacement statute of this size should be commenced with its institutions ready, because the tribunal's three-year wait and the producer companies' longer one were the costs least necessary and most corrosive of confidence in the reform itself.

## Where the verdict does not hold

Three qualifications bound the verdict, and stating them is part of defending it. First, the brevity failure is a failure against the Irani benchmark, not against every benchmark. A reader who values adaptability over legibility, who would rather have a threshold changed by notification in a month than by amendment in a session, can consistently judge the delegation a success; the verdict here treats the committee's own compactness objective as the governing test, and a reader who rejects that test will reach a different conclusion on the same facts. Second, the governance success is a success of architecture, not yet of outcomes. Whether independent directors check promoters, whether rotation improves audits, whether the class action will ever be used at scale, are empirical questions on which the evidence remains mixed and incomplete, and the 2013 Act cannot answer them by its own text. The verdict credits the design for existing and for changing the distribution of formal power; it does not certify the results. Third, the comparison is between the 1956 Act as it stood at repeal and the 2013 Act as amended to its decriminalised settlement. A comparison run at 2015 would have scored enforcement very differently, and a comparison run a decade later may score the governance machinery differently again, as the tribunal's jurisprudence matures. A statute that is still being amended into its final shape is entitled to a verdict that knows its own date, and this one is a verdict on the reform as it settled, not as it was announced.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What are the main differences between the Companies Act 1956 and 2013?

The differences fall into four groups. Some subjects were carried across with their rules intact under new numbers, including meetings, minutes, annual returns, dividends and buy-backs. A second group was substantively changed: related party transactions moved from government approval to board and shareholder approval, auditors acquired rotation and independence restrictions, and oppression petitions moved to a tribunal. A third group was created outright, including codified directors' duties in Section 166, independent directors, the class action in Section 245, the National Financial Reporting Authority and corporate social responsibility in Section 135. A fourth group left company law altogether, as winding up and corporate rescue moved to the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code of 2016. Above all, the 2013 Act moved operative detail from sections into rules, so the shorter statute carries a larger body of total law.

### Q: Did the Companies Act 2013 make compliance harder?

For most companies, yes in volume and no in kind, and the balance depends on the company's size. The 2013 Act added obligations the 1956 Act never imposed: board composition requirements, committee structures, new audits, expanded Board report disclosures, and a fraud-reporting duty, all of it specified through rules that change frequently and must be tracked continuously. Against that, it removed the old regime of government approvals for routine transactions, exempted private and small companies from a series of provisions by notification from 2015 onwards, and, through the amendments of 2019 and 2020, converted most procedural defaults from crimes into monetary penalties decided in-house. A listed company unquestionably carries a heavier governance load than it did under the 1956 Act. A small private company that files on time carries more paperwork than before but considerably less criminal risk than the 2013 Act as originally enacted would have imposed.

### Q: Which director duties were new in the Companies Act 2013?

The duties themselves were mostly not new; their statutory form was. Under the Companies Act 1956 a director's duties existed only at common law, as fiduciary obligations developed by the courts: to act in good faith, for a proper purpose, and with reasonable care. Section 166 of the Companies Act 2013 codified them for the first time in Indian company legislation. The codified list requires a director to act in accordance with the articles, to act in good faith to promote the company's objects for the benefit of its members as a whole and in the best interests of the company, its employees, the shareholders, the community and the protection of the environment, to exercise due and reasonable care, skill, diligence and independent judgment, to avoid situations of direct or indirect conflict of interest, not to achieve undue gain or advantage, and not to assign the office. What was genuinely new was the breadth of the beneficiary class written into the section, which names employees, the community and the environment alongside shareholders, and the fact that breach of a duty became breach of a statute.

### Q: Did the Companies Act 2013 really have fewer sections than the 1956 Act?

Yes, as a count of numbered sections in the Act itself. The Companies Act 1956 contained 658 sections and fifteen schedules. The Companies Act 2013 as enacted contained 470 sections in twenty-nine chapters with seven schedules, a reduction of 188 sections, a little over a quarter. What the count does not show is where the removed content went. Part was deleted, part was relocated, notably winding up to the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, and a large part was transferred into rules, forms and notifications made under the Act, which carry the operative thresholds and procedures. The Irani Committee had recommended exactly that transfer in the name of compactness and flexibility. So the statement that the 2013 Act has fewer sections is accurate and also incomplete: the total body of binding company law grew, because the rules beneath the shorter statute are extensive and are amended far more often than sections ever were.

### Q: What changed for private companies under the Companies Act 2013?

Three things changed at once, in different directions. The definition widened: the maximum number of members for a private company rose from fifty under the 1956 Act to two hundred under Section 2(68), which relieved closely held companies with many employee or family shareholders. The obligations initially tightened: as enacted, the 2013 Act applied several provisions designed with public companies in mind to private companies, including restrictions on loans to directors, deposit conditions, and filing and approval requirements that family businesses experienced as a sharp increase in burden. The administration then softened the position by delegated legislation: from June 2015 onwards, exemption notifications under Section 462 disapplied or modified a series of provisions for private companies, and later amendments continued the relief. The practical result is that a private company's position cannot be stated from the Act alone. It is the Act as varied by the exemption notifications, which is the clearest small-scale demonstration of this article's central point about where the operative law lives.

### Q: Which Companies Act 1956 concepts were dropped?

Several familiar institutions of the 1956 Act have no successor in the 2013 Act. The statutory meeting, required by Section 165 early in a public company's life together with a statutory report, was abolished; continuous disclosure replaced the single early report. Share warrants to bearer, issued under Sections 114 and 115, disappeared, consistently with the new Act's insistence on identified holders of securities. The provisions on sole selling agents were dropped. The certificate of commencement of business for public companies under Section 149 was replaced briefly by a declaration requirement in Section 11 of the 2013 Act, which was itself omitted in 2015, so the gate through which a public company once passed before trading simply ceased to exist. The managing agency system is sometimes listed among the dropped concepts, but it had been abolished in 1970, decades before the replacement, and belongs to the 1956 Act's own internal history rather than to the comparison between the two statutes.

### Q: Was the Companies Act 2013 later softened by amendments?

Yes, substantially, and in two distinct waves. The amendment Acts of 2015 and 2017 adjusted individual provisions, removing the minimum paid-up capital requirement, making the common seal optional, and refining disclosure and related party provisions. The structural softening came in 2019 and 2020 and concerned enforcement. The Companies (Amendment) Act of 2019 recategorised sixteen compoundable offences as civil defaults punishable by penalty under an in-house adjudication mechanism, a programme begun by ordinance in November 2018. The Companies (Amendment) Act of 2020 extended it after a Company Law Committee review: more defaults moved to in-house adjudication, penalties for some offences were omitted, and imprisonment was removed from provisions dealing with technical defaults. The corporate social responsibility default travelled the same arc, made punishable with imprisonment in 2019 and converted back to a monetary penalty in 2020. Criminal liability was deliberately re-concentrated on fraud and serious wrongdoing, which is a significant qualification to any description of the 2013 Act based on its original text.

### Q: Do judgments under the Companies Act 1956 still apply?

Very often, yes, and the answer follows the structure of the change. The 2013 Act repealed the 1956 Act with savings in Section 465, and where a 2013 provision re-enacts a 1956 provision in substantially the same terms, courts treat the earlier interpretation as carried with the words, in line with the settled presumption that Parliament legislating in the same language adopts its received construction. The oppression jurisprudence is the leading example: Shanti Prasad Jain v. Kalinga Tubes Ltd and Needle Industries, decided under Sections 397 and 398, supply the operative tests in petitions under Sections 241 and 242, and the Supreme Court reasoned within that framework in the Tata and Cyrus Investments case in 2021. The carry-over fails where the new Act changed the substance. Judgments about Central Government approval under Section 297 cannot construe Section 188, which abolished that approval, and the created subjects, codified duties, class action, corporate social responsibility, have no old case law to inherit.

### Q: Which Act is better for minority shareholders, the Companies Act 1956 or 2013?

The Companies Act 2013, on paper decisively and in practice by a smaller margin. The minority's core remedy, the petition against oppression and mismanagement, exists in both statutes, as Sections 397 and 398 of the 1956 Act and Sections 241 and 242 of the 2013 Act, with the governing tests carried across from the older case law. Everything around that core improved in 2013. The forum became a specialist tribunal rather than a government-constituted board. Section 241 widened the text to conduct prejudicial to the company itself and to anticipated prejudice from a change of control. Section 245 added the class action, which had no 1956 equivalent at all, allowing members and depositors to claim damages from the company, its directors and its auditors collectively. Disclosure, the raw material of every minority action, deepened through expanded financial reporting and Board report obligations. The qualification is enforcement reality: thresholds, cost and the tribunal's arrears still decide whether the better text produces a better outcome for a small shareholder in an unlisted company.

### Q: How did the two Acts differ on private placement of securities?

The 1956 Act had no private placement regime as such. It defined, in Section 67, when an offer of securities counted as an offer to the public, and spared arrangements that were not calculated to make the securities available to others; private placements and preferential allotments were built on that inference, supplemented for listed companies by the securities regulator's guidelines and by Section 81(1A) for preferential issues. The Companies Act 2013 replaced the inference with a code. Section 42 permits an offer by private placement only to identified persons selected by the Board, caps the number of offerees at two hundred in a financial year for each kind of security as prescribed, forbids public advertising and marketing, requires the money to come through banking channels into a separate bank account, requires allotment within sixty days, and treats a breach as a public offer made without a prospectus, with corresponding penalties. The contrast is between a boundary policed by interpretation and a boundary policed by a compliance procedure.

### Q: Which Act gave auditors more independence, the Companies Act 1956 or 2013?

The Companies Act 2013, by a wide margin, because the 1956 Act barely addressed independence as a legal problem at all. Under Section 224 of the 1956 Act the auditor was appointed at each annual general meeting and could continue indefinitely; Section 226 set qualifications and a short list of disqualifications; there was no rotation, no statutory bar on selling other services to the audit client, and no regulator of audit quality outside the profession's own institute. The 2013 Act attacked dependence from four directions. Section 139(2) imposed rotation for listed and prescribed companies, one five-year term for an individual auditor and two for a firm, with a cooling-off period. Section 141 expanded disqualifications, Section 144 prohibited specified non-audit services including bookkeeping and internal audit, and Section 143 added a duty to report suspected fraud to the Central Government. Above the arrangement sits the National Financial Reporting Authority under Section 132, an independent regulator that can investigate auditors and debar them, an institution the 1956 settlement never created.

### Q: Did the Companies Act 2013 change the maximum number of directors a company can have?

Yes, in both the number and the approving authority. Section 255 and the surrounding provisions of the 1956 Act proceeded on a board of the size the articles fixed, but Section 259 required the approval of the Central Government for an increase in the number of directors beyond twelve, for companies to which the section applied. The Companies Act 2013 set the default ceiling higher and changed the gatekeeper: Section 149(1) permits a company up to fifteen directors, and a company may appoint more by passing a special resolution of its shareholders, with no role for the government at all. The change is small in itself and characteristic in what it shows. The 1956 Act treated the size of a company's board as a matter on which a ministry should pronounce. The 2013 Act treats it as a matter for the shareholders who bear the consequences, which is the Irani Committee's philosophy of moving decisions from government approval to shareholder approval expressed in a single threshold.

### Q: Why does the Companies Act 2013 rely on rules for so much of its content?

Because it was designed to. The Expert Committee on Company Law chaired by Dr J. J. Irani, reporting in 2005, recommended a compact, principles-based statute whose essential obligations would sit in the Act while thresholds, forms and procedures sat in rules that the executive could amend quickly as business practice evolved. The 2013 Act followed that design closely, which is why so many of its provisions operate through phrases such as may be prescribed, and why the classes of company bound by obligations like auditor rotation, internal audit and secretarial audit are identified in rules rather than in the sections. The design bought adaptability: filing forms, thresholds and procedures can be updated by notification without a parliamentary session. It cost legibility and stability, because the operative law on a subject is the Act plus the relevant rules plus their amendments, and a reader of the bare Act cannot tell whether a given obligation binds a given company. Both consequences were foreseeable, and the committee accepted the trade deliberately.

### Q: Which corporate forms exist under the Companies Act 2013 that had no 1956 equivalent?

Three forms are genuinely new, alongside renamings that are sometimes mistaken for new forms. The One Person Company, recognised by Sections 2(62) and 3(1)(c), allows a single individual to incorporate with limited liability and a nominee to succeed to membership, where the 1956 Act required at least two members for a private company and seven for a public one, forcing sole promoters into nominal second shareholdings. The small company, defined in Section 2(85) by prescribed ceilings on paid-up capital and turnover, created a statutory class entitled to a lighter compliance regime, a category the 1956 Act did not use. The dormant company, under Section 455, gave a company formed for a future project, or one that has become inactive, a recognised status with minimal obligations and a route back to active status. By contrast, the Section 8 company is not new: it is the 1956 Act's Section 25 licensed company for charitable and similar objects, renumbered and renamed rather than created.

### Q: How did the two Acts differ on shareholder meetings and voting?

The skeleton of the meeting carried across: the annual general meeting, the requisitioned extraordinary general meeting, twenty-one days' notice and minutes all survive in recognisable form in Sections 96 to 118 of the 2013 Act. The machinery of participation is what changed. The 1956 Act assumed a physical meeting: voting was by show of hands or poll among those present, proxies carried the absent shareholder's vote within limits, and postal ballot, added as Section 192A only in 2000, was the single exception. The 2013 Act built participation at a distance into the statute. Section 108 created electronic voting and the rules made it mandatory for listed and prescribed companies, Section 110 retained and expanded postal ballot, and Section 173(2) recognised directors' participation in Board meetings by video conferencing. The quorum rule also changed character, from a flat five members for a public company under Section 174 to a scale in Section 103 that rises with the size of the membership. The direction is consistent: the meeting stopped being a place and became a process.

### Q: How did the two Acts differ on fraud?

The 1956 Act had no definition of fraud and no single fraud offence. Fraudulent conduct by officers was punished through scattered provisions, several of them concentrated in the winding up part of the Act, including offences relating to falsification of books, false representations and fraudulent trading, each with its own ingredients. The Companies Act 2013 made fraud the apex offence of the statute. Section 447 defines it expansively, covering any act, omission, concealment of any fact or abuse of position committed by any person, with intent to deceive, to gain undue advantage, or to injure the interests of the company, its shareholders, its creditors or any other person, whether or not there is wrongful gain or wrongful loss. The punishment is imprisonment of not less than six months extending to ten years and a fine up to three times the amount involved, with a minimum of three years' imprisonment where the fraud involves public interest. The 2013 Act also gave the Serious Fraud Investigation Office a statutory basis in Sections 211 and 212. When the decriminalisation amendments of 2019 and 2020 withdrew criminal liability from procedural defaults, fraud was deliberately left untouched as the core case for prosecution.

### Q: What is the difference between a Section 25 company and a Section 8 company?

There is no difference of substance, because they are the same institution under two statutes. Section 25 of the Companies Act 1956 empowered the Central Government to license an association formed for promoting commerce, art, science, religion, charity or any other useful object, which intended to apply its profits to its objects and prohibit dividends, to register as a company with limited liability without using the word Limited in its name. Section 8 of the Companies Act 2013 re-enacts that licensing regime for companies formed to promote commerce, art, science, sports, education, research, social welfare, religion, charity, protection of the environment or similar objects, on the same conditions of applying income to the objects and paying no dividend. The change of label is why practitioners speak of Section 8 companies where their predecessors spoke of Section 25 companies. The classification used in this article records the subject as renumbered only: the licensing authority, the conditions and the consequence, incorporation without the Limited suffix, all carried across, with procedural detail restated in the incorporation rules.

### Q: How did the role of the Registrar of Companies change under the Companies Act 2013?

The Registrar's office changed from a registry that received paper and initiated prosecutions into an adjudicating authority in its own right. Under the 1956 Act, the Registrar maintained the register, inspected documents, and enforced the Act largely by launching prosecutions in magistrates' courts or reporting defaults for prosecution, a slow instrument that made the summons a greater burden than the fine. Electronic filing through the MCA21 system began in 2006, in the old Act's last decade. The 2013 Act then armed the office directly. Section 454 made Registrars and authorised officers adjudicating officers who could impose penalties for specified defaults after a hearing, and the amendments of 2019 and 2020 moved default after default into that in-house mechanism. The striking off jurisdiction shows the change of scale: Section 560 of the 1956 Act was used sparingly, while Sections 248 to 252 became the instrument of mass deregistration, with the government informing the Rajya Sabha that more than 3,82,000 companies were struck off over one three-year period. The Registrar of the 2013 settlement does not wait for a court to punish a default.

### Q: Can a company still be governed by the Companies Act 1956 for any purpose?

For most purposes, no, but the pockets in which the old Act survived are instructive. Section 465 of the Companies Act 2013 repealed the 1956 Act with savings for rights accrued, actions taken and proceedings pending, so a dispute about a pre-repeal transaction may still be decided by 1956 law as the law then in force, and proceedings on foot at the transfer were continued under the arrangements in Section 434. Beyond savings, whole subjects lived on under the repealed statute because the new Act had not replaced them. Producer companies are the standing example: created by Part IXA of the 1956 Act in 2002, they had no chapter in the 2013 Act as enacted and continued to be governed by the old Part for years, until the Companies (Amendment) Act of 2020 inserted a producer companies chapter into the 2013 Act. A newly incorporated company is governed wholly by the 2013 Act and the codes around it, but a lawyer who cannot read the 1956 Act cannot advise on the legacy questions that still arrive with old companies, old charges and old proceedings.

### Q: How did the treatment of company deposits change from the Companies Act 1956 to 2013?

The 1956 Act came to deposits late and by delegation. Section 58A was inserted in 1974, after repeated instances of companies collecting money from the public and failing, and it empowered the Central Government to prescribe the limits and conditions on which companies could invite deposits; the substance of the regime lived in the acceptance of deposits rules made under that section. The Companies Act 2013 brought the subject into the statute as Chapter V, Sections 73 to 76, and narrowed the gate. A company may accept deposits from its own members under Section 73 only with shareholders' approval and on conditions that include a deposit repayment reserve and prescribed safeguards. Deposits from the public may be accepted only by an eligible company under Section 76, a public company meeting prescribed thresholds of net worth or turnover, subject to credit rating and other conditions. The direction of travel matches the rest of the comparison: a regime that lived in executive rules under a permissive section became a statutory regime with a restricted class of takers, on the reasoning that the savings of strangers require a gate in the Act itself rather than in a rulebook.
