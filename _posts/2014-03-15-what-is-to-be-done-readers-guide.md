What Is to Be Done? is probably the most consequential pamphlet in the Marxist tradition and almost certainly the most carelessly described. Supporters cite it as the founding charter of a kind of party. Opponents cite it as the founding confession of an authoritarian temperament. Both citations usually rest on a single paraphrase, repeated from textbook to textbook until it hardened into common knowledge: that Lenin taught that workers, left to themselves, can achieve only a limited trade union outlook, and that socialist consciousness must therefore be delivered to them by intellectuals organised in a disciplined vanguard. That paraphrase is not false in every particular, but it conceals the two facts that change the text's meaning. The argument about consciousness arriving from outside the economic struggle is a passage Lenin quotes, at length and with explicit approval, from Karl Kautsky, the leading theorist of orthodox Marxism. And Lenin himself, five years after publication, warned readers against treating the pamphlet as a programme, describing it instead as a polemical correction that had deliberately bent the stick against one deviation and should be straightened before use.

This guide reads the pamphlet the way its author eventually asked for it to be read: as a document of a specific fight, in a specific movement, under specific conditions of illegality, whose claims can be separated from the uses later made of them. The separation is this article's governing discipline, and it has a name drawn from the author's own vocabulary. The bent stick is the claim that organises everything that follows: Lenin described this text as a deliberate overcorrection against a named opponent, and a century of argument, friendly and hostile, has been conducted over a pamphlet its author asked his readers not to take straight. Restoring that warning to the text does not settle the political argument about the organisational model the pamphlet advocates. It settles the prior question of what the text says, who actually said its most famous sentence, and under what conditions its prescriptions were justified by their author. A companion treatment of the author himself is available in the [lenin intellectual biography](/2014/03/01/lenin-intellectual-biography/), and readers who want the vanguard concept detached from this text should go there second; this guide stays with the pamphlet.

One boundary should be drawn at the outset, because the pamphlet's fame invites trespass. This article does not assess the record of the state that later claimed descent from the pamphlet's party, and it delivers no verdict, favourable or hostile, on that record. Historians dispute that record among themselves, and the dispute belongs to their literature. What can be established with documents is narrower and, for a reader of the text, more useful: what the pamphlet argues chapter by chapter, which of its famous formulations are quotations, what its author said about it once the fight had moved on, and how the standard reading of it was assembled by later hands. Where the political criticism of the organisational model is stated, it is stated at full strength and attributed to the critics who made it, including critics writing from inside the same movement in the pamphlet's own decade. Where the defence is stated, it is likewise given its strongest form. The reader is trusted with both.

## The occasion: a newspaper, an exile circle and a movement in pieces

The pamphlet was written across the autumn and winter of 1901 and 1902 and published in Stuttgart in 1902. Its author, Vladimir Ilyich Ulyanov, was in his early thirties, living in exile, and signing himself with the pen name N. Lenin, which he had taken up only months earlier. He was not a famous man. He was one editor among six on the board of a small newspaper, Iskra, The Spark, printed abroad and smuggled into Russia in false-bottomed suitcases and the bindings of books. The party on whose behalf he wrote, the Russian Social-Democratic Labour Party, had been founded at a congress in Minsk in 1898 whose central committee was arrested almost immediately afterwards, and it existed less as an organisation than as a scatter of local circles, study groups and factory committees, each with its own habits, its own literature and its own quarrels. The pamphlet cannot be understood apart from that scatter, because its central proposal is a machine for ending it.

The movement's raw material was impressive and its organisation was not. The eighteen nineties had seen a wave of strikes across the empire's industrial centres, weavers and metalworkers stopping factories in St Petersburg and beyond, and a generation of young intellectuals had gone to prison or exile for the crime of carrying socialist literature into those strikes' vicinity. What the strikers had not produced, in the eyes of the Iskra editors, was a politics. The strikes were economic in demand and local in scope, and they subsided when their demands were met or their leaders arrested. Around that gap between energy and organisation a whole tendency had formed, and it is the pamphlet's named enemy. The tendency's adherents worked in papers such as Rabochaya Mysl, Workers' Thought, and Rabocheye Dyelo, Workers' Cause, and they held, in various registers, that the workers' own economic struggle was the school in which the class would educate itself, that political demands should grow out of that struggle rather than be imported into it, and that the intellectuals' task was to serve the movement the workers were already making rather than to lecture it. Their most quoted document was a short statement of 1899 known as the Credo, drafted by Yekaterina Kuskova, which argued that the Russian movement should concentrate on the economic struggle and leave political liberalism to the liberals.

Lenin's board had a strategy for the scatter, and the pamphlet is that strategy's manifesto. Iskra was to be more than a newspaper. A single paper, produced abroad beyond the censor's reach, distributed by a network of agents inside the empire, arguing one line in one voice, could do what no congress of arrested delegates could: give the scattered circles a common text, a common vocabulary and a common discipline, and so assemble a party around a printing press. Lenin had sketched the scheme in an Iskra article of May 1901, Where to Begin?, and had drafted the pamphlet's argument in correspondence through that year. The pamphlet's full title in its Russian form carries a subtitle about the burning questions of the movement, and burning was the register throughout. It is a polemic, written to wound named opponents, several of whom were former collaborators, and its exaggerations belong to that genre. Recognising the genre is not an excuse offered on the author's behalf. It is a fact about the document that its author later insisted on, and any reading that ignores it will mistake the temperature of the argument for its content.

Three circumstances of production deserve emphasis because later readers habitually forget all three. The first is illegality. Every organisational prescription in the pamphlet is written for a movement whose meetings were raided, whose presses were seized, whose members operated under police surveillance and faced Siberian exile as a routine professional hazard. Arguments that sound, to a reader in a constitutional state, like a taste for conspiracy are presented in the text as reluctant adaptations to that environment, and the text says so explicitly and repeatedly. The second circumstance is dependence on the German example. The Russian movement measured itself against the German Social Democratic Party, the largest and most successful Marxist party in the world, whose Erfurt Programme of 1891 and whose theoretical journal, Die Neue Zeit, edited by Kautsky, constituted the orthodoxy of the age. To be a Marxist in 1901 was, in large part, to be a pupil of that party's science, and the pamphlet's author understood himself as its pupil rather than as a founder of anything new. The third circumstance is youth, the movement's rather than the author's. Russian social democracy was perhaps a decade old as a practical force, its cadres thin, its theory largely translated, and the pamphlet's impatience is the impatience of a man who believes the material for a party exists and only the assembling is lacking. Whether that belief was justified is one of the questions the pamphlet's readers have argued about ever since, and the argument is taken up in its proper place below. The text comes first.

## What Is to Be Done? chapter by chapter

The pamphlet has five chapters and a short preface, and its architecture is worth seeing whole before its parts are quarrelled over. The first chapter attacks a slogan. The second builds the theory of consciousness on which the whole argument rests. The third contrasts two kinds of politics. The fourth turns from ideas to organisation, and the fifth proposes the instrument, a common newspaper, by which the organisation is to be assembled. The movement of the book is therefore from doctrine to machinery: what workers can and cannot generate unaided, what politics follows from that limit, what sort of people must carry the wider politics, and what physical apparatus can hold those people together across an empire. Readers who meet the pamphlet in extracts usually meet the second chapter alone, and the extraction distorts the whole, because the second chapter's strong claims about consciousness are the premises of an argument whose conclusions are organisational and whose conditions are stated in the fourth and fifth chapters. The guide follows the book's own order.

### Why does the pamphlet open with an attack on freedom of criticism?

Because the slogan was, for Lenin, the entry point through which the German revisionist controversy had reached the Russian movement. Freedom of criticism sounded like tolerance; he argued it functioned as permission to replace Marxist theory with fashionable bourgeois ideas while keeping the party label, and that a movement without a settled theory could not settle anything else.

The chapter's target is Eduard Bernstein's revision of Marxism in Germany and its Russian echoes. Bernstein had argued that the movement was everything and the final goal nothing, and had presented that revision as the fruit of free critical inquiry. Lenin's counter is not a defence of dogma for its own sake, though the chapter's title, which pairs dogmatism with freedom of criticism, concedes the pairing his opponents alleged. His counter is that criticism is never socially weightless: in a society divided into classes, the ideas available for import are produced by institutions, universities, newspapers and salons, that belong overwhelmingly to the possessing classes, so that a party which relaxes its theoretical guard does not become open minded but becomes a conduit. Whether the reader accepts that argument or not, its structure should be noticed, because the same structure recurs in the second chapter's treatment of spontaneity. In both places Lenin's claim is that there is no neutral ground: a movement either elaborates its own theory or absorbs somebody else's, and the somebody else is determined by who owns the means of intellectual production. The argument is the pamphlet in miniature, and it explains why a book ostensibly about organisation spends its first pages on a question of literary politics.

### Did Lenin say workers cannot develop socialist consciousness on their own?

He endorsed that argument, but quoted it from Kautsky, whose 1901 article he cited as orthodox authority. Lenin later stated the pamphlet was a polemical corrective which bent the stick against economism and should not be read as a general programme.

The second chapter, on the spontaneity of the masses and the consciousness of the Social Democrats, is the pamphlet's core and its most contested ground. Lenin begins from the strike wave of the eighteen nineties and grants it a generous reading: the strikes were, in his phrase as usually translated, an embryonic form of consciousness, the moment at which the class began to sense itself as a class. But embryonic is the operative word. What the strikes generated, he argues, was trade union consciousness, the conviction that workers must combine, fight employers and press for labour legislation, which is a politics that accepts the framework it bargains inside. Socialist consciousness, a grasp of the relations of all classes to one another and to the state, and of the workers' place in the whole, is a different order of knowledge, and it does not arise from the economic struggle alone because that struggle acquaints the worker with one relation only, the relation to an employer. The celebrated formulation follows, and its provenance is the subject of a later section: class political consciousness, Lenin writes, can be brought to the workers only from without, from outside the sphere of relations between workers and employers, from the sphere of the relations of all classes and strata to the state and the government.

The chapter then states the hard edge of the doctrine. The spontaneous development of the working class movement, he writes, leads to its subordination to bourgeois ideology, because spontaneity is the movement's infancy and bourgeois ideology is older, more elaborated and equipped with incomparably greater means of dissemination. Between the two ideologies he allows no third: since humanity has produced no third ideology, and since a society torn by class antagonisms cannot have a non-class or above-class ideology, any belittling of socialist ideology and any turning away from it strengthens the rival. Readers have found that passage chilling and have found it bracing, and both reactions are responses to a real feature of the text rather than to a mistranslation. What the passage is doing should nonetheless be marked precisely. It is an argument about the sociology of ideas under capitalism, about which class owns the presses and the universities, and its conclusion is a conclusion about propaganda work: that socialists who neglect theory and agitation abandon the field to a better equipped opponent. The leap from that sociology to a general theory of the worker's mind is the leap the standard reading makes and the text, read with its author's later gloss, does not quite make. The distinction matters and is developed in the section on the Kautsky borrowing.

### What did Lenin mean by trade union consciousness?

He meant the outlook produced by the economic struggle on its own ground: the conviction that workers must organise, strike for better terms and win protective laws, without yet connecting those fights to the structure of the state or to the relations among all classes. It is real consciousness, in his account, but bounded by the bargaining table.

The third chapter converts the second's distinction into a contrast of political styles. Against him stood a formula of his opponent A. Martynov, of Rabocheye Dyelo, proposing to give the economic struggle itself a political character. Lenin's scorn for that phrase is the chapter's engine. To give the economic struggle a political character, he argues, means in practice to fight for reforms the existing order can grant, labour laws and factory inspection, and to call that politics is to shrink the word. Social Democratic politics, as the chapter defines it, is the organisation of political exposures: the party takes up every manifestation of tyranny and oppression, in whatever class it occurs, the flogging of peasants, the persecution of religious sectaries, the censorship of students, the corruption of officials, and teaches the workers to read each as evidence about the political system as a whole. The Social Democrat's ideal figure is not the secretary of a trade union but what the pamphlet calls the tribune of the people, a public accuser of the regime on every front at once. The passage is the pamphlet's most expansive democratic strain, and it sits awkwardly beside the standard reading's portrait of a narrow conspirator, which is one reason the full text repays reading against the paraphrase. The party Lenin wants in this chapter is omnivorous in its concerns and pedagogical in its method, training its audience to connect a factory fine in one province with a ministerial order in the capital. Whatever else the later tradition did with the pamphlet, this chapter's ideal of the tribune is in the text, in the author's own hand, and no honest summary may omit it.

The fourth chapter changes key. Its subject is described by its title as the primitiveness of the economists and the organisation of revolutionaries, and its tone is that of a craftsman disgusted by amateur work. The local circles, Lenin argues, are narrow, unstable and perpetually raided because they are built on handicraft methods: every member does everything, records are kept carelessly, arrests decapitate organisations that have no trained continuity. Against this he sets his most famous organisational demand, for professional revolutionaries, people trained in the craft of conspiracy and agitation, maintained by the party, stable in leadership, few enough to be trained and secret enough to survive. The chapter closes with the pamphlet's rhetorical summit, an appeal that has been quoted at eleven words ever since: "Give us an organisation of revolutionaries, and we shall overturn Russia!" The fifth chapter defends the plan for an all Russian political newspaper against critics who called it a desk bound scheme, and argues that the paper's agent network, its regular rhythm of production and distribution, and its common line would supply the scaffolding around which a real party could be built, a metaphor the guide returns to in its own section. The book ends not with a vision of the future society, of which it contains almost nothing, but with a printing schedule. That ending is characteristic, and a reader who grasps it has grasped the pamphlet's genre: it is a builder's manual written in the language of a theoretical treatise.

## Economism was a position, not an insult

The word economism does so much work in the pamphlet, and in a century of later polemic, that a reader can easily take it for a term of abuse meaning something like vulgar concern with wages. It was not. It named a real tendency with real proponents, real publications and a real argument, and the pamphlet's own polemic is unintelligible unless that argument is given in its strongest form first. The economists observed, correctly, that the Russian workers' movement was young, that its actual struggles were economic in content, and that the strike wave had arisen without waiting for intellectuals to authorise it. They inferred, plausibly, that politics should be built outward from the struggles workers were actually conducting, since a politics imported from exile newspapers risked floating above the class it claimed to lead. They suspected, not unreasonably, that intellectuals who proposed to bring the workers a finished doctrine from outside had appointed themselves the class's tutors, and that tutelage was a relation with its own dangers. And they could point to the condition of the Iskra organisation itself, a handful of exiles arguing in print, as evidence that the proposed alternative to spontaneity was, at that moment, mostly paper.

Several of those points have never been answered by being refuted; they have been answered by events, which is a different thing, and the events are contested. What the pamphlet establishes against economism is narrower than its tone suggests. It establishes that the economists' own documents, above all the Credo, proposed to surrender independent political action to the liberals, and that a movement which renounces politics in the name of realism does not thereby escape politics but receives the politics of others. It establishes that the economic struggle, however heroic, produces demands the existing order can in principle satisfy, and that a party content with those demands has accepted the order's premises. It does not establish, and in its careful moments does not claim, that workers are incapable of political thought, or that the economic struggle is contemptible; Lenin calls the strikes heroic and their consciousness embryonic, which is the language of immaturity, not of incapacity. The distinction between a stage and a ceiling is the distinction on which the whole later argument about the pamphlet turns, and readers should hold it firmly, because the economists' defenders, then and since, have argued that Lenin's stage hardened into a ceiling in the hands of his successors. That charge belongs to the section on the strongest objection. Here the point is simpler: economism was the name of an interlocutor, and the pamphlet is a dialogue with it, not a monologue. A reading that treats the economists as cardboard has already stopped reading.

The tendency also had a sociology that the pamphlet's author understood well. Economism flourished in the period of the movement's practical infancy, when the work available was local, the literature scarce and the police efficient, and when the theoretical inheritance arrived in fragments through translation. Its adherents included experienced worker activists as well as intellectuals, and its papers were not foolish. To say this is not to reverse the pamphlet's verdict but to restore its proportions: Lenin thought he was arguing against the theoretical capitulation of a young movement, and he thought the capitulation dangerous precisely because its carriers were sincere and its appeal obvious. Polemic against a worthless opponent does not require a book. The book exists because the opponent was serious.

## The Kautsky borrowing: whose sentence is it anyway?

The second chapter's decisive passage arrives with a footnote's worth of framing that most summaries delete. Lenin introduces the question of how socialist consciousness arises and then, instead of answering in his own voice, quotes at length from an article Karl Kautsky had published in Die Neue Zeit in 1901, commenting on a draft programme of the Austrian Social Democratic Party. The quoted passage argues that modern socialist consciousness can arise only on the basis of deep scientific knowledge; that the carrier of science is not the proletariat but the bourgeois intelligentsia, in whose individual members' minds the doctrine was elaborated before being introduced into the class struggle; and it ends with the sentence the tradition would spend a century attributing to the man who quoted it: "the vehicle of science is not the proletariat, but the bourgeois intelligentsia". Lenin's comment on the passage is that Kautsky's words are profoundly true and important. The endorsement is total and explicit. The authorship is Kautsky's, and the text says so on its face.

### Where did the formula of consciousness from outside come from?

It came from the German party's theoretical journal, where Kautsky, discussing the Austrian draft programme in 1901, argued that socialist consciousness is introduced into the class struggle from outside the economic fight. Lenin quoted the passage as settled orthodoxy and built his second chapter around it.

Three consequences follow from the attribution, and the guide takes them in ascending order of importance. The first is bibliographical honesty: any summary that presents the from without doctrine as Lenin's invention misattributes a quotation, and the misattribution is not a nuance but the central fact about the passage. The second is interpretive. Kautsky was not an marginal figure but the pope of Marxist orthodoxy, the co-author of the Erfurt Programme and the teacher of a generation, and Lenin quotes him precisely because the citation settles the matter within the movement's shared science. The pamphlet's posture throughout is that of an orthodox pupil disciplining local deviation by appeal to the common doctrine, not that of a heresiarch founding a new one. A reader who knows the passage is Kautsky's reads the whole book differently: its claims to novelty shrink, and its claims to fidelity grow, which is the opposite of the impression left by the standard paraphrase. The comparison between the two men's later positions, when the pupil and the teacher became the bitterest of enemies, is pursued in the companion treatment of the [lenin vs kautsky debate](/2014/07/01/lenin-vs-kautsky-debate/), and the general question of how consciousness is conceived across the tradition is treated in the guide to [class consciousness explained](/2011/09/15/class-consciousness-explained/).

The third consequence concerns the gloss Lenin himself supplies, which the paraphrase also deletes. Having quoted Kautsky, Lenin explains what from without means in his own usage: consciousness is brought from outside the economic struggle, from outside the sphere of relations between workers and employers. It is not brought from outside the working class as a social body, delivered by aliens to passive material. Political knowledge concerns the relations of all classes to the state, and nobody, worker or intellectual, acquires it from the bargaining table alone; it must be constructed from material gathered across the whole of society, which is why the pamphlet's third chapter sends its tribunes to study every class and every grievance. On this reading the doctrine is about the division of intellectual labour and the geography of knowledge, not about the mental capacity of workers, and the strongest version of the standard reading, in which the passage expresses contempt for the class, loses its textual basis. Critics of the reassessment reply that a gloss cannot fully domesticate a formulation, that Lenin chose the strong words, repeated them without the gloss in other writings of the period, and built an organisation consonant with them. That reply is real, and it is answered in the reassessment section rather than here. What the borrowing establishes beyond dispute is the lineage: the sentence came from the headquarters of orthodoxy, and the pamphlet's author said so at the time, in print, in the passage itself.

## Spontaneity and consciousness: the argument's machinery

Beneath the famous passage sits a pair of concepts that do quieter and better work than the quotation they support, and a reader's guide should give them their due. Spontaneity, in the pamphlet's Russian term stikhinost, names what arises elementally, without deliberate theoretical direction: the strike that erupts over a foreman's abuse, the riot that answers a price rise, the union formed because combination is the only available reply to the wage bargain. Lenin's treatment of spontaneity is double, and the doubleness is often flattened. On one side, spontaneity is the movement's life and proof: the awakening of the masses from their torpor, the embryonic consciousness he honours. On the other, spontaneity worshipped as a principle becomes what he calls tailism, the policy of trailing behind the movement's existing level and consecrating it, which leaves the movement's ideas to be supplied by the surrounding society. The enemy in the pamphlet's vocabulary is never the spontaneous movement; it is the bowing to spontaneity by those whose task is to raise it. That is a distinction between a force and an attitude toward the force, and it survives even a hostile reading of the text.

Consciousness, the paired term, is equally specific. It does not mean awareness in general, or literacy, or intelligence. It means the theoretical grasp of the whole: of how the classes stand to one another, of what the state is and does, of where the workers' struggle sits in the total movement of the society. Marx's own treatment of consciousness, against which the extra questions in this guide's apparatus invite comparison, tied its development closely to the class's own practical activity and to the communists as the section of the class that pushes forward the interests of the movement as a whole; the pamphlet inherits that framework and adds its stress on theory's institutional carriers, the party press and the trained agitator. The tension between the inherited framework and the added stress is the genuine intellectual problem the pamphlet poses, and it is a better problem than the cartoon quarrel between a Lenin who trusted workers and a Lenin who despised them. Both cartoons fail the text. The text's author believed the class would make the revolution and believed it would not make the theory unaided, and he held both beliefs in the same chapter without sensing a contradiction, because for him theory and class were joined in the party rather than opposed. Whether that join is stable, whether an institution that monopolises the joining can keep faith with both terms, is the political question the pamphlet bequeaths. It is not answered by quotation, and the guide does not pretend otherwise.

The machinery had a test within four years of publication, and the author's own reaction to the test belongs in any honest exposition. When revolution actually arrived in 1905, with soviets invented by workers without any party's instruction and a general strike organised from below, Lenin's language changed measurably. He wrote that autumn that the working class is instinctively and spontaneously drawn toward socialism, urged that the party's gates be thrown open to workers in mass, and treated the soviet not as an embarrassment to his theory but as a discovery. Defenders of the pamphlet read the shift as the theory working: the corrective had done its work, the stick could rest nearer the vertical. Critics read it as the theory refuted by its author's own experience and quietly shelved. Both readings agree on the fact, which matters more than either: the strong formulations of 1902 were instruments for a phase, and their author revised them when the phase changed. An author who revises his instruments in public is poorly served by disciples and prosecutors alike freezing him at his most quotable.

## The organisation argument: professionals under a police state

The fourth chapter's demand for professional revolutionaries is the pamphlet's most durable legacy and its most argued-over page. The proposal, stripped of its polemical heat, is this. A revolutionary organisation under the conditions of Russian autocracy should rest on a stable core of people who make the work their profession: trained in agitation, in print production, in the conspiratorial techniques of evading surveillance, maintained where necessary by the organisation, and continuous in membership so that arrests remove individuals without dissolving the whole. Around that core the widest possible periphery of sympathising workers, local groups and support networks should be drawn in, but the core's standards, secrecy, training, continuity, are what convert a scatter of circles into a party. The justification offered in the text is relentlessly circumstantial. Broad internal democracy, elections to every post, open membership and published proceedings, are goods the author explicitly says are impossible under a gendarme regime, where they would amount to handing the police a directory. The German party can afford its openness because Germany has political legality; Russia has the knout and the Okhrana, and forms must fit the ground they stand on.

### How does the pamphlet describe the professional revolutionary?

As a trained full time worker for the movement: schooled in theory and in the techniques of illegal work, supported by the party where needed, stable in post so that arrests cannot decapitate the organisation, and valued for continuity and craft rather than for social origin or enthusiasm.

Two features of the argument deserve extraction from the polemic because later debate habitually loses both. The first is that the professional is defined by function and training, not by class origin or by intellectual status. The pamphlet's complaint against the existing circles is that they are staffed by students and amateurs who rotate, improvise and vanish; its remedy is a cadre in which workers themselves are to be trained up to the professional standard, and it says with emphasis that the organisation must include workers promoted to the front rank of the work. The professional revolutionary is a job description before it is a sociology. The second feature is the argument from stability. An organisation of professionals is proposed as the means by which the movement's memory survives its repression: experience, contacts, printing skills and funds persist in a trained core when a wave of arrests would otherwise return every locality to the beginning. Readers who judge the proposal should judge it against the environment the text specifies, an empire without legal parties, without a free press, without rights of assembly, where the alternative organisational forms on offer were the study circle and the terrorist group. Judged in a vacuum, secrecy looks like a preference; judged against the Okhrana's files, it looks like a condition of existence. That contextual judgement does not settle whether the forms outlived their conditions or deformed what they built, and the guide takes up that question where it belongs, in the objection section. It settles only that the text justifies its machinery by conditions it names, which is the fourth column of the claims table and the column the standard reading most often omits.

The chapter also contains the pamphlet's most human passage, and its presence complicates the portrait of the author as a cold engineer of obedience. Defending the charge that his organisational plan is a dreamer's scheme, Lenin quotes the novelist Pisarev on the virtue of dreaming: a dream that runs ahead of events, he argues, is the imagination's working instrument, and the fault lies not in dreaming but in dreaming without the serious labour that connects the dream to the real movement. The passage matters for the close reader because it shows the pamphlet's author thinking about the relation between imagination and organisation, and because it is the point at which the builder's manual admits its own utopian charge. The men and women who built underground organisations did so on a wager that a different Russia was possible, and the text, at its most characteristic, holds the wager and the filing system in a single hand. Critics who find the combination sinister and admirers who find it heroic are responding to the same page. The guide's task is only to ensure the page is read.

## The newspaper as scaffolding

The fifth chapter's proposal looks, at first glance, like the least theoretical part of the book and the easiest to skip. A common newspaper, produced abroad at regular intervals, distributed by a maintained network of agents, is offered as the party's organising instrument. Lenin's metaphor for it has become the chapter's shorthand: the newspaper is the scaffolding erected around a building under construction. It marks the structure's outline, shows each builder where the others are working, and allows the work of separate hands to be checked against a common plan; when the building stands, the scaffolding can come down. Applied to the movement, the paper's production and distribution oblige the scattered circles to perform common tasks on a common schedule, to maintain couriers, funds, safe addresses and codes, and to argue about a single line in a single forum. The organisation is to be built by doing the work of the paper, so that the party exists as a practice before it exists as a congress.

### What work was the newspaper meant to do in the plan?

It was to be collective propagandist, collective agitator and collective organiser at once: one text carrying one line to every circle, its agent network training the scattered groups in common tasks, common discipline and common timing, so that a party would exist in practice before any congress could declare it.

The proposal had a polemical target as specific as every other part of the book. An objection had been raised, by the writer signing himself L. Nadezhdin among others, that a network of local organisations mattered more than a newspaper and that the Iskra plan subordinated living work to a sheet of print. Lenin's reply inverts the objection: under Russian conditions the local organisations could not be federated by resolutions, since their delegates would be arrested, nor by correspondence alone, since correspondence does not train; they could be federated by a shared enterprise with deadlines. History delivered a partial verdict quickly. The Iskra network did become the skeleton around which the party's 1903 congress was convened, and the agents of the paper were recognisably the party's first professional cadre, which is as close as organisational theories usually come to experimental confirmation. The same history also showed the scaffolding's danger, visible in the same years: a machine built to hold a movement together is also a machine that can be captured, split and turned, and the congress the newspaper convened dissolved into the Bolshevik and Menshevik factions within days of meeting. The instrument worked, and its working was the beginning of the quarrel. Both halves of that sentence belong in the record, and the discipline of this guide, which separates the text's claims from the tradition's uses, is designed for exactly such double entries.

For the concept the pamphlet does not itself theorise, the party form as a general idea rather than as this book's proposal, the companion treatment of the [vanguard party explained](/2014/04/01/vanguard-party-explained/) takes the question up directly, and readers should follow the link rather than ask this text to bear weight its author laid elsewhere.

## The claims and attributions table

Everything argued so far can be compressed into the artefact this guide exists to deliver. The claims and attributions table sets the pamphlet's major claims side by side with the four questions the paraphrase never asks: what exactly does the text say, whose words are they, by what conditions does the text justify the claim, and did the author maintain it afterwards. Read down the second column and the pamphlet looks familiar. Read across the rows and it changes character: its most notorious sentence turns out to be a quotation defended as orthodoxy, its most rigid organisational demands turn out to carry their conditions in the same breath, and its author's later voice keeps interrupting the doctrine attributed to him. The table is the article's discipline made visible, and it is offered as the reader's permanent equipment for any summary of the pamphlet encountered elsewhere: run the summary's claims through the four questions and watch which columns it cannot fill.

The fifth row is the one the standard reading gets half right: the demand for professionals is indeed the pamphlet's own and is indeed central, but its justification in the text is a named set of conditions, autocracy, illegality, the police, and a claim whose justification is conditional cannot be quoted honestly without its conditions. Between the quotation that was reported as invention and the condition that was reported as essence, the received pamphlet was substantially rebuilt by its transmitters. ## The title and the novel behind it

The pamphlet's title is a quotation too, and recognising it was the first readers' initiation test. What Is to Be Done? was the title of Nikolai Chernyshevsky's novel of 1863, written in the Peter and Paul Fortress where its author was imprisoned, and for two generations of Russian radicals it was the scripture of the new people: men and women who lived rationally, worked cooperatively and prepared, in their own conduct, the transformed world. Its hero Rakhmetov, the rigorist who sleeps on nails to school himself for the cause, became the template of the professional revolutionary long before the pamphlet gave that figure a job description, and Lenin, who reread the novel repeatedly and said it had ploughed him over, took the title as a banner. The borrowing announces the pamphlet's lineage in the Russian revolutionary tradition, a lineage older than Russian Marxism and steeped in the populism Marxism defined itself against, and it announces an intention: to answer the question the novel posed, what is to be done, with an organisation instead of an example.

### Why did Lenin take his title from a novel?

Because Chernyshevsky's novel was the sacred text of the Russian revolutionary generations before Marxism, and its question was the question every radical tradition had to answer. Borrowing the title claimed that inheritance for social democracy and promised an answer in organisation rather than in exemplary lives.

The title's afterlife confirms the guide's larger theme. In the century since, the question form has been lifted by writers of every tendency, usually without awareness of the novel, so that the pamphlet's most visible feature is itself an unattributed quotation. A text famous for supposedly teaching that ideas must be imported turns out to have imported its own title, its own central formula and its own hero's silhouette from the culture around it. None of that diminishes the pamphlet. It situates it, as its author situated himself, inside a tradition he was working with inherited tools. The myth of the solitary innovator, whether brandished by worshippers or prosecutors, requires a text without sources, and this text's sources are on its title page.

## What happened next: congress, split and a revolution's corrections

The pamphlet appeared in the spring of 1902, and the party it proposed to assemble met in the summer of 1903, in Brussels and then London, at the Second Congress of the Russian Social-Democratic Labour Party. The congress adopted a programme, the Erfurt style document whose minimum and maximum demands framed Russian orthodoxy, and then split. Because the split followed the pamphlet by a year and consumed the movement for a generation, the two events are fused in the standard memory, and one of the guide's misreadings must be corrected here in plain terms: the pamphlet did not cause the split, and the split was not about the pamphlet's doctrines. The fracture came over the first clause of the party rules, the definition of a member. Lenin's draft required personal participation in one of the party's organisations; Martov's rival formula admitted those who cooperated under an organisation's guidance without joining it. The difference looks scholastic and was, in one sense, a dispute about the party's boundary in a police state; but it was a dispute about a rule, conducted between men who had co-edited the same newspaper and co-written the congress's draft rules, and the labels Bolshevik and Menshevik derive from majority and minority in a later vote on the editorial board, after the congress's arithmetic had been changed by walkouts. Readers who load the pamphlet with responsibility for the schism are reading history backwards from its consequences.

The organisational argument continued in print. Lenin's One Step Forward, Two Steps Back of 1904 defended congress discipline against the minority's boycott of the central organs, and drew the two critiques that have shadowed the organisational model ever since. Rosa Luxemburg, writing in 1904 on the organisational questions of Russian social democracy, warned that an ultra centralism which transferred initiative from the class to a committee would sterilise the movement it meant to protect, substituting the central committee's errors for the class's own instructive ones. Leon Trotsky, the same year, in Our Political Tasks, a pamphlet dedicated to the veteran Pavel Axelrod and aimed at the majority's internal regime, pushed the logic to its famous image of substitutionism: "the party organisation at first substitutes itself for the party as a whole", then the committee for the organisation, and finally, in his warning, a single dictator for the committee. Both critiques were written by revolutionaries of the same broad movement, not by its enemies, and both were aimed as much at the post congress practice as at the pamphlet of 1902, a targeting distinction the standard reading again tends to blur. The full case against the tradition that descends from these disputes is gathered in the companion treatment of the [case against leninism](/2014/06/15/case-against-leninism/), which readers should consult for the prosecution's complete file; this guide records only that the earliest and sharpest counts were filed by insiders, within two years, against named practices.

### How did a real revolution alter Lenin's organisational language?

Within three years the party's gates were being thrown open on his urging. The revolution that began in 1905 produced soviets and mass strikes the pamphlet had not foreseen, and Lenin responded by praising the class's instinctive movement toward socialism and demanding mass recruitment, language his earlier polemic would have flagged as spontaneity worship.

The correction came from experience rather than from debate, and it is the more telling for that. The soviets of 1905 were organs of workers' self organisation invented in struggle, owing nothing to any party's plan, and the author of the pamphlet recognised them at once as a discovery of the class itself. His writings of that autumn read, against his writings of 1902, like a second author: the working class is instinctively and spontaneously Social Democratic, the party must recruit workers by the thousand and trust them in its committees, the youth must be given bold tasks. Nothing in the later writings formally retracts the pamphlet, and the guide does not claim a retraction where none exists. What exists is a shift of stress so large that citing the two periods interchangeably, as the standard reading does when it builds a timeless system out of 1902, misrepresents both. A thinker whose central category was the concrete condition could hardly have done otherwise: the conditions had changed from conspiracy under a stable autocracy to open mass struggle under a cracking one, and the organisational language changed with them. The pamphlet's defenders call this consistency of method beneath changing conclusions. Its critics call it an admission. The textual fact, that the conclusions changed, is the part the guide insists on, because it is the part a reader can verify.

## Bending the stick: the author's own correction

In 1907 Lenin gathered his journalism of the Iskra period into a collection titled Twelve Years and wrote a preface that has become the second most important document in the pamphlet's history, for the simple reason that its author uses it to instruct posterity how the pamphlet is to be read. The instruction is unambiguous in its central point. The pamphlet, he explains, was a polemical work against economism, written to correct a specific deviation of a specific period. The Economists had bent the stick in one direction, toward the worship of spontaneity and the surrender of independent politics. To straighten a stick so bent, it is necessary to bend it in the other direction, and that, he says, is what the pamphlet did. Its formulations were therefore deliberately one sided, calibrated to the distortion they corrected, and the worst use a reader could make of them was to lift them out of that context and apply them as a general programme for circumstances the polemic never addressed. He returns to the theme from several sides in the same preface: the pamphlet should be read as a summary of the Iskra tactics of its moment, its strong phrases belong to the fight that produced them, and the argument about consciousness from outside, in the form the pamphlet gave it, he treats as a formula of the polemic rather than as a settled doctrine of the movement.

The image is exact and worth pausing on, because it concedes more than a mere change of emphasis. A bent stick argument is an argument whose author knows it overshoots. The overstatement is not an accident of temperament discovered afterwards; it is described, by the author, as the method of the original correction, chosen because a moderate statement would have left the deviation standing. That admission reframes the entire reception problem. The pamphlet cannot be refuted by demonstrating that its formulations are one sided, since its author registers the one sidedness himself, in advance of the demonstration, and gives the reason. Nor can it be canonised by quoting those same formulations as timeless doctrine, since the author explicitly forbids the operation. Both of the text's great armies of users, the canonisers and the prosecutors, are therefore in the position of using a document against its author's stated instructions for its use. That is the bent stick claim in its full form, and it is why this guide treats the 1907 preface not as a footnote to the pamphlet but as part of the text a serious reader is obliged to read, bound in the same covers by the author's own decision when he republished the work inside the collection the preface introduced.

The preface should not be inflated into a retraction, and the guide is careful on this point because both camps inflate it in opposite directions. Lenin did not withdraw the pamphlet, apologise for it or disown its argument against economism, which he continued to regard as a necessary victory. He did not abandon the conviction that theory must be elaborated and carried into the movement by an organised party, nor the demand for a trained and continuous cadre, both of which persist through his later writings in changing forms. What the preface retracts is a use: the use of a corrective as a constitution. The distinction between withdrawing a text and withdrawing a licence is fine but real, and the entire later argument turns on it. Admirers who treat the preface as a full self criticism concede a case their author did not make. Critics who treat it as an admission that the pamphlet's politics were disowned claim a surrender he never signed. The document supports a narrower and more interesting conclusion: that by 1907 its author had come to see his most famous book as a period piece of great local importance, dangerous when generalised, and that he said so in the plainest language available to him. Posterity's refusal to take the hint is a fact about posterity, documented in the next section.

## The standard reading and how it was assembled

If the pamphlet's own author marked it as a correction, how did it become a charter? The transformation happened in stages, and each stage had its agents and its reasons. The first stage was canonisation within the movement the pamphlet helped to found. After 1917 the text was republished as a founding document of the ruling party, and founding documents are not read, they are cited; the distinction is the whole of the matter. A cited text is mined for warrants. The second stage was codification: in the lectures published in 1924 as The Foundations of Leninism, Joseph Stalin systematised the legacy into a doctrine with the pamphlet near its root, and gave the tradition its durable label for the organisational model, the party of a new type. A corrective addressed to the Russian movement's infancy thus entered the catechism as the eternal form of the revolutionary party, its conditions silently dropped, its quotations silently naturalised as its author's inventions. The third stage was the mirror image. In the scholarship and journalism of the pamphlet's opponents, above all during the Cold War, the same decontextualised text served as the prosecution's exhibit A: here, in 1902, in the founder's own words, was the contempt for workers and the blueprint for the party state, needing only to be quoted to be condemned. Both readings required the same editorial operations, the removal of the Kautsky attribution, the removal of the conditions, the removal of the 1907 preface, and both performed them. The standard reading of the pamphlet is therefore best understood not as an interpretation that won an argument but as a residue left by two propagandas that found the same simplified text convenient.

Academic scholarship complicated the picture in waves, without dislodging the residue from the textbooks. Historians of the Russian movement established the pamphlet's context in detail: the smallness of the world it addressed, the extent of its borrowings from the German party's common stock, the degree to which its organisational proposals answered the Okhrana's pressure rather than a philosophy of rule. Biographers divided. Robert Service's portrait keeps the emphasis on the author's controlling temperament and treats the pamphlet as an early disclosure of it. Neil Harding's two volume study of Lenin's political thought, written decades before the reassessment made the argument famous, had already stressed the continuity between the pamphlet and the orthodox Marxism of its day, a reminder that the correction has a scholarly prehistory and was not invented in the 2000s. What the textbooks transmitted, meanwhile, was the compressed version, because compression is what textbooks do, and the compressed version had the decisive advantage of being useful to everyone except the reader. A student meeting the pamphlet through the standard paraphrase learns a Lenin who invented a doctrine of distrust, built a party on it, and held it unchanged, three propositions that the documents complicate respectively with a citation, a conditions clause and a preface. The guide's remaining business is to state the modern reassessment that pressed all three complications at once, to give its critics their full weight, and then to face the objection that survives every correction.

## The reassessment and its critics

The modern reassessment arrived as a book of formidable patience. Lars T. Lih's Lenin Rediscovered, published in 2006, worked through the pamphlet in its full Russian context, retranslating contested phrases, reconstructing the debate with the economists from their own texts, and arguing that what he calls the textbook interpretation of the pamphlet is substantially a construction of a later period. On Lih's account, the historical Lenin of 1902 was an orthodox follower of the German party's programme, an Erfurtian in his own later shorthand for the synthesis, whose burning concern was not anxiety about the workers but the merger of socialism with the workers' movement: the doctrine existed, the movement existed, and the Russian tragedy was that the two had not yet fused as they had in Germany. The famous passage about consciousness is read, in this account, as standard Kautskyite doctrine about how theory and class meet, the term translated as spontaneity is restored to its sense of elemental force rather than backwardness, and the pamphlet's author emerges as a man in a hurry to replicate a German success, not as a heresiarch inventing a doctrine of tutelage. The argument is documented at a length that forbids dismissal, and it changed the scholarly weather: reviewers across the spectrum treated it as a work any future account must answer.

### What does the modern reassessment claim about the standard reading?

It claims the standard reading is a later construction: that Lenin in 1902 understood himself as an orthodox disciple of the German party, that his notorious passage restates Kautsky, and that the portrait of an elitist innovator breaking with Marxism was assembled afterwards by disciples and enemies who needed the same simplified founder.

The critics of the reassessment are not a chorus of the refuted, and the brief for this guide requires them at full strength. Their first count is proportion: a study that restores the pamphlet's orthodoxy, they argue, risks underweighting what was genuinely sharp in the text, the absolutism of the two ideologies passage, the readiness to treat theoretical deviation as the seed of political treason, the organisational appetite that the subsequent decade displayed. Restoration can become its own distortion if it irons a jagged text flat. Their second count is consequence. Whatever the pamphlet meant in 1902, the tradition that ruled in its name was not a seminar, and a reading that confines itself to the author's intentions can become a way of declining to discuss what the intentions built; intellectual history, on this view, must answer for reception as well as for composition. Their third count is method: the reassessment leans heavily on the author's later self descriptions, the 1907 preface above all, and authors are interested witnesses to their own pasts; the preface of 1907 was itself a polemical document of the faction struggles of 1907, and treating it as a transparent window on 1902 repeats, at one remove, the error of treating the pamphlet as a transparent window on the movement. Each of these counts lands on something real, and the guide's own practice has been shaped by them: the reassessment is used here as the strongest available account of the text's composition and context, not as a verdict on the tradition, and the standard reading is treated as a historical artefact to be explained rather than a stupidity to be mocked. The large question the reassessment reopens, whether the doctrine that bears Lenin's name broke with the Marxism it inherited or developed within it, is the subject of the companion essay on [was leninism a break with marx](/2014/05/15/was-leninism-a-break-with-marx/), and the guide leaves the verdict there, where the evidence on both sides is assembled.

## The strongest objection: the practice followed the pamphlet

Every correction registered in this guide can be granted, and a formidable objection remains standing. It should be stated as its holders state it, without softening. Whatever Lenin wrote in a preface in 1907, the organisational model the pamphlet advocated in 1902, a disciplined party of trained cadres, led from a centre, intolerant of organised internal opposition, is recognisably the model he spent his life building, defending at the Second Congress, enforcing after it, and bequeathing to a state. An author's later nervousness about his text does not retract his practice, and history judges practices. The reassessment, on this objection, performs a genuine service to intellectual history and none at all to political judgement: it corrects who wrote a sentence while the sentence's institutional career continues uncorrected. Worse, the objection continues, the defence from conditions proves too much, because the conditions were never allowed to lapse by the beneficiaries of the forms; secrecy, discipline and the monopoly of theory, justified in 1902 by the Okhrana, were retained and extended in circumstances where the Okhrana no longer existed, which suggests the conditions were the occasion rather than the cause. A stick bent against economism, on this reading, stayed bent, and the straightening its author recommended was never carried out by the only hands with the power to carry it out, his own and his heirs'.

The reply begins with a concession, and the concession is total within its sphere: the correction this article defends is textual and historical, not political. Establishing that a famous sentence is Kautsky's does not acquit or convict any party. Establishing that the author called his book a corrective does not establish that the correction was wise, or that its instruments were safe, or that the regimes which quoted it were entitled to. If the reader came for a verdict on the organisational model and its career, the honest sources are the critics and defenders of the practice itself, several of whom this guide has quoted, and the guide's neutrality consists in refusing to smuggle a verdict in under cover of a bibliography lesson. What the textual correction does change is the shape of the political argument, in three ways its critics should welcome as much as its defenders. It removes a false premise, the lone innovator, from both the worship and the prosecution, and forces each to argue about a tradition and a set of conditions rather than about a temperament. It restores the conditions to the claims, which means any modern borrowing of the pamphlet's machinery must now argue for the borrowing under modern conditions instead of citing a founder's authority. And it recovers the author's own standard for his text, the standard of the concrete correction, by which the tradition's later use of the pamphlet as a timeless constitution can be judged and found, on the evidence of the preface, unauthorised by its author. To say that the heirs used the text against its instructions is not to acquit the text. It is to locate the argument where it can actually be settled: in the record of what was done, argued on its merits, without a forged signature at the bottom. That separation, between the text and the state record, is the discipline of this whole cluster of guides, and this pamphlet is where the separation does the most work.

## The words themselves: a short course in the pamphlet's vocabulary

Close reading of this text is impossible without pausing over four Russian words and the English coats they have been made to wear, because the standard quarrel is partly a quarrel of translation. The first is stikhinost, rendered in the standard English version as spontaneity. The Russian word carries the sense of the elemental, of forces of nature, of what sweeps along unmastered, and it sits on a spectrum: at one end, the elemental as raw power, the flood and the storm; at the other, the elemental as the unreasoning, the part of life that proceeds without a head. English spontaneity, by contrast, sounds wholesome and democratic, a word from the vocabulary of praise. A translation choice therefore tilts the argument before it begins. When the pamphlet's author warns against bowing to stikhinost, an English reader hears a warning against trusting the people's spontaneity, and bridles; a Russian reader of 1902 heard a warning against worshipping the unmastered element, a piece of revolutionary common sense with a pedigree running through the whole movement. The reassessment's philological work on this single word does more to change the text's temperature than any reinterpretation of its doctrines, and it illustrates why the guide insists on reading the pamphlet as a document in its own language's debates rather than in the language of its later reputation.

The second word is soznatelnost, consciousness, with its adjective soznatelny, conscious, which in the movement's usage had a social meaning English struggles to hold: a conscious worker was not merely a thinking worker but a worker who had joined the cause knowingly, a made Social Democrat, and the conscious elements of the movement were its convinced cadres as against its elemental mass. Whole sentences of the pamphlet turn on that social sense. When Lenin opposes the conscious to the spontaneous he is opposing, in the first instance, the organised and convinced to the unorganised and unformed, a commonplace distinction in any movement's housekeeping, before he is opposing anything metaphysical about minds. The third term is the phrase rendered from without, in Russian s vneshnei storony's cousins and more exactly the construction about bringing consciousness izvne, from outside; the pamphlet's own gloss, outside the sphere of the economic struggle, depends on a spatial metaphor that translation repeatedly collapses into a social one, outside the class. The fourth is kustarnichestvo, the primitiveness or handicraft character of the economists' methods, a word from the world of the artisan workshop: Lenin's organisational complaint is that the movement works like cottage industry when it needs a factory's division of labour, and the metaphor explains the fourth chapter's tone of craftsmanly disgust better than any theory of power does. Four words, four tilts. A reader equipped with them will find the pamphlet less sinister in some places and more rigorous in others, which is what usually happens when a translated polemic is given back its native grain.

## The German template behind the Russian argument

Nothing in the pamphlet is intelligible without the German party standing behind it like a schoolmaster behind a pupil's chair. The Social Democratic Party of Germany was the movement's existence proof: a Marxist party with a million voters, a daily press, a theoretical journal, trade unions in its orbit and a programme, adopted at Erfurt in 1891, whose theoretical part Kautsky had drafted. The Erfurt synthesis held that capitalism's development made socialism necessary, that the working class was its destined agent, and that the party's task was to bring the two together, to fill the movement with the consciousness of its historical role. Lenin's generation of Russian Marxists learned their doctrine from that synthesis and measured their stunted movement against it with an envy that saturates the pamphlet's pages. The organisational demand for professionals is partly a demand to replicate, under Russian illegality, what the German party achieved openly: continuity, expertise, a press, a memory. The consciousness doctrine is the Erfurt doctrine restated for a movement whose fusion of theory and class had not yet occurred. Lih's name for the pamphlet's author, an Erfurtian, is polemical in intent but descriptive in content: the text is what the German party's programme looks like when it is translated into the conditions of a police state by a man who believes the programme and resents the translation losses.

The merger image deserves its own paragraph because it is the pamphlet's positive pole, the thing the polemic is for. Kautsky's journalism had popularised the thought that socialism and the workers' movement, arising separately, must be merged, as two streams joining; Lenin took the image as the movement's whole task. In Germany the merger was history. In Russia it was a project, and the pamphlet is the project's engineering drawings. Read in that frame, the notorious passages change their emotional register. The insistence that consciousness comes to the economic struggle from a wider sphere is the merger doctrine's way of saying that the streams have not yet joined and must be joined deliberately. The impatience with economism is the impatience of a merger engineer watching the Russian stream being proudly dammed at the factory gate. None of this requires the reader to endorse the doctrine; German orthodoxy had its own critics then and has them now, and the doctrine's confidence in science and its carriers is a period confidence a modern reader will want to interrogate. But a reading of the pamphlet that never mentions Erfurt is like a reading of a sonnet that never mentions the form: the constraints that explain the choices are missing, and the choices are then attributed to the author's soul, which is the standard reading's method and its error.

## First readers: the argument begins at once

The pamphlet was not received as a neutral textbook by the people it was written for, and its first readers' objections are part of its meaning. Pavel Axelrod, the senior figure of Russian Marxism abroad and a co-founder of the movement's first Marxist group, read the organisational scheme with alarm and developed the charge that has followed it since: that the scheme carried a Jacobin logic, substituting a conspiratorial centre for the class in the manner of the old Russian revolutionary sects, and that a party so built would school its members in obedience rather than in initiative. Axelrod pressed the criticism in print across 1903 and 1904 as the split widened, arguing for a party built outward from the workers' own growing organisations rather than downward from a committee. Georgi Plekhanov, the movement's founding theorist, who had stood with Lenin through the Iskra campaign, broke with him after the congress and, resuming his seat on the paper's board, published in 1904 a celebrated article asking whether the majority's centralism was in fact Bonapartism, the rule of a centre over the movement it claimed to serve. The charge stung because it came from the tradition's father and used the tradition's own categories: a party that fears its members' initiative reproduces, in miniature, the political forms it exists to abolish.

These early responses matter to the close reader for two reasons. First, they show that the critical reading of the pamphlet is not a later invention of the Cold War but an original product of the movement itself, filed within months by men whose Marxist credentials were older than the author's. The debate about the pamphlet is therefore not a debate between the tradition and its enemies; it is one of the tradition's founding internal arguments, and it has never closed. Second, the first readers read the text with the conditions attached, as participants in the same illegality, and still divided: Axelrod and Plekhanov did not think the Okhrana's existence settled the organisational question, which disposes of any claim that only a reader ignorant of the conditions could object to the answers. Context disciplines interpretation; it does not dictate it. The guide has insisted throughout that the pamphlet's claims be read with their conditions restored, and the first readers are the proof that restoration leaves the argument open rather than closing it. What the conditions exclude is only the lazy version, in which the scheme is explained by a taste for domination that its author supposedly concealed from his contemporaries. His contemporaries saw the scheme whole, conditions and all, and some of them fought it on sight, in the open, in print. That is a healthier picture of the tradition than either its catechism or its demonology affords, and the pamphlet is best honoured by being read inside it.

## The hardest paragraph in the book, read slowly

Every long quarrel over the pamphlet eventually arrives at one paragraph of the second chapter, and a reader's guide should slow down and walk through it sentence by sentence rather than gesture at its reputation. The paragraph's burden is that in a society split into hostile classes there can be no ideology standing above the split: humanity has worked out two class ideologies relevant to the argument, the bourgeois and the socialist, and no third. From that premise the paragraph draws its conclusion with a logic that is nearly mechanical. Any weakening of the socialist ideology, any failure to elaborate and spread it, does not leave a vacuum; it leaves the field to the bourgeois ideology, which is older, more various in form and carried by every institution of the existing order. Hence the celebrated severity: to belittle the theory is, in effect and whatever the intention, to strengthen the adversary. The paragraph is the theoretical root of the pamphlet's intolerance of theoretical looseness, and of much that later grew from that intolerance, and no reading that skips it has read the book.

Three observations keep the paragraph in its actual dimensions. The first is that its claim is about ideologies as social products, not about the worth of persons: the bourgeois ideology's advantage is institutional, a matter of age, elaboration and means of dissemination, and the paragraph reads at times like a media analysis conducted half a century before the discipline existed. The second is that its binary is presented as a fact of a class society's structure, and it stands or falls with that structural claim; a reader who believes that genuinely independent standpoints can be sustained outside the two great class projects has a philosophical disagreement with the paragraph, which should be conducted as philosophy rather than as biography. The third observation is the one the reassessment forces and the critics resist: the paragraph's severity is directed at socialists, at the movement's own intellectuals, whom it accuses of deserting their post. Its addressee is the wavering theorist, not the worker, and its mood is not contempt for the class but fury at the class's self appointed teachers for abandoning their subject. That does not make the paragraph benign; doctrines of no third way have a record, and the record is part of what the objection section weighs. But its aim is inward, at the movement's own ranks, and a reading that turns it into a doctrine of the worker's empty head has again mistaken the target. The paragraph is best read as the pamphlet's theory of cultural power, stated with the absoluteness of a man who believed ideas were weapons and that his side was being disarmed by its own armourers.

## The pamphlet among its companion writings

No text reads itself, and this one was issued as the centrepiece of a small shelf of Iskra writings that correct and qualify one another in ways the scholarly argument uses constantly. Before it stands the article Where to Begin? of May 1901, in which the newspaper plan and the attack on the economists' terrorism debate debut in compressed form; the pamphlet is that article expanded into a system. Beside it stands the Letter to a Comrade on Our Organisational Tasks, written in the autumn of 1902 for a St Petersburg activist and circulated in manuscript: a practical document, spelling out committees, districts, factory groups and the division of functions, and remarkable to later scholars because its tone is more layered and less absolutist than the pamphlet's, proposing broad workers' committees alongside the professional core and worrying at the same problems from the workbench rather than the rostrum. After it stands One Step Forward, Two Steps Back, the 1904 anatomy of the congress split, in which the organisational argument hardens under the pressure of defeat and the author defends the majority's discipline against the minority's boycott. Read as a shelf, the three years show a mind arguing with events: the pamphlet's certainties are real but they are the certainties of the campaign of 1902, and the shelf preserves both the campaign and its sequels. The standard reading's characteristic operation, once again, is to take one volume off the shelf, the loudest, and read the shelf through it. The reassessment's counter operation is to insist on the shelf. A general reader need not master all three companions, but should know they exist and should distrust any account of the pamphlet that shows no awareness of them, since the pamphlet's author wrote them too, in the same years, for the same movement.

## What the pamphlet does not contain

A close reading owes the reader an inventory of absences, because this pamphlet is routinely blamed and credited for doctrines it never states. It contains no theory of the state: the state appears as the pole toward which political consciousness is directed, the committee of the ruling class in the inherited formula, but there is no analysis here of how a revolution is to treat the existing machinery, a question its author addressed only in 1917 in a different book written under different stars. It contains no account of the peasantry as an ally, beyond the tribune's duty to take up rural grievances; the agrarian strategy that later became central is absent. It contains no theory of the soviets, which had not been invented, and no instructions for insurrection, whose timing and technique belong to later writings and later years. It contains no economics: the critique of political economy is presupposed, borrowed from Marx as finished science, and the pamphlet's business is exclusively the movement's organisation and ideology. It contains, perhaps most surprisingly given its afterlife, no general theory of the party as an eternal form; its party is specified against Russian conditions so persistently that the extraction of a universal model requires the editorial work the codifiers later performed. And it contains no prophecy. The famous sentence about overturning Russia is a wager about organisation, not a prediction of events, and the book foretells neither 1905 nor 1917, neither of which its author foresaw in the forms they took. The inventory matters because the tradition's argument about the pamphlet has often been an argument about a phantom: a complete Leninism, state theory, insurrection manual and party constitution bound in one volume of 1902. The real volume is narrower, stranger and more interesting, a treatise on how ideas and organisations meet, written before its author had seen a revolution, by a man who would spend the rest of his life being surprised by them along with everyone else.

## Before the pamphlet: the Credo and the protest

The pamphlet of 1902 was the second act of a quarrel that had opened three years earlier, and its first readers knew the opening act by heart. In 1899 a short document began circulating in the movement's exile colonies and underground circles: the Credo, drafted by Yekaterina Kuskova, a respected activist of the movement's practical wing. Its argument was candid. The Western workers' movement, it held, was passing into reformism, and the Russian movement, young and weak, should learn from that trajectory rather than repeat its illusions: the economic struggle of the workers was the real substance, political struggle in the liberal sense was the task of the liberal opposition, and Marxism's dogmatic phase was giving way to a critical one. The document was not published by its author as a programme, but it was read as one, and it detonated. In Siberian exile, a twenty nine year old Lenin drafted a reply, the Protest of the Russian Social Democrats, which the exile colonies adopted and which treated the Credo as the Russian form of Bernstein's revisionism: a surrender dressed as maturity. He followed it the same year with an article dissecting Rabochaya Mysl's editorial line, which he titled, with a pamphleteer's instinct for the wounding phrase, a retrograde direction in Russian Social Democracy.

The prehistory matters for three reasons. It shows that economism was answered by its critic for years before the famous book, and that the book is a summation of a running fight rather than a sudden thunderbolt. It shows the critic working inside the movement's collective forms, drafting protests for colonies of exiles to sign, which sits oddly with the portrait of a solitary legislator handing down doctrine. And it fixes the real stake of the quarrel in the participants' minds: not the metaphysics of consciousness but the movement's direction, whether Russian social democracy would grow into an independent political party or dissolve into a trade union secretariat with a liberal tail. Kuskova herself is worth a sentence of justice. She was no police agent of sloth, as later caricature implied, but an organiser of real courage whose later career ran through the cooperative movement and the doomed democratic politics of 1917; her husband and collaborator Sergei Prokopovich became a minister in the Provisional Government the Bolsheviks overthrew. The Credo's authors lost the argument of 1902 and lived to see where the winning argument led, which gives the old quarrel a human weight its textbook summaries never carry. A reader who wants the pamphlet's temperature should begin here: it was written by a man who had been fighting the same document for three years and intended, this time, to finish the argument.

## The tribune's Russia: what the exposures were for

The third chapter's positive doctrine, the politics of exposure, deserves a fuller walk than summaries give it, because it is the pamphlet's most concrete piece of political imagination and its least remembered. The Russia of the exposures is a gallery: the peasant flogged by order of a district chief under statutes that preserved corporal punishment for the rural estate; the religious sectarian, the Dukhobor or the Baptist, persecuted by the Orthodox establishment and the police together; the university student expelled for a protest meeting and conscripted as punishment; the zemstvo statistician and the country doctor, the so called third element of rural employees, harassed for reporting famine honestly; the factory inspector bribed, the journalist censored, the worker fined by deductions that consumed the wage. The Social Democrat as tribune is to take up every item in the gallery, in the party press and in leaflets addressed to the classes concerned, and to teach a single lesson from the accumulation: that these are not accidents or the faults of individual officials but the ordinary operation of a political system that must answer for all of them together. Politics, in this doctrine, is the art of connection. The worker who learns to be indignant at the sectarian's persecution has learned to see the state whole, and that seeing is precisely the political consciousness the second chapter theorised. Theory and practice lock together in the chapter with a neatness the pamphlet's critics rarely acknowledge: the from without of chapter two is cashed out, in chapter three, as a curriculum of exposures gathered from every corner of the empire's life.

The doctrine also contains the pamphlet's implicit theory of the party's audience, which is far wider than the factory. The tribune addresses all strata, and the party of the pamphlet aspires to be the empire's general accuser, the one organisation that keeps the whole regime's file. Later practice gave that aspiration a complicated history, and the historian's duty is to record the aspiration's original breadth whatever became of it: in the text, the party that confines itself to the workers' economic demands is the narrow thing, and the party that ranges over every injustice in the society is the broad one. The inversion surprises readers formed by the standard reading, in which breadth belongs to the pamphlet's opponents. The economists' movement was broad in its social sympathies and narrow in its political programme; the Iskra movement proposed the reverse. Both sides claimed the mantle of the class's real interests, and the quarrel was in part a quarrel about what breadth means, a quarrel the modern reader will recognise in many later movements that have had to choose between depth in one constituency and range across the whole society. The pamphlet chose range armed with doctrine. Its opponents chose depth armed with patience. The century argued the choice in a dozen languages, and the argument is recognisably the same one each time, which is a measure of how well the pamphlet posed it.

## The Jacobin question

No charge against the pamphlet has a longer pedigree than the charge of Jacobinism, and none repays slower handling. The word was thrown at Lenin by Axelrod and others from 1903 onward, and it carried a precise cargo in the movement's vocabulary: the Jacobin was the revolutionary of the small conspiratorial organisation, the heir of Blanqui rather than of Marx, who proposed to make the revolution by the action of a disciplined minority and to school the class afterwards from the height of power. Behind the charge stood the older Russian tradition of Narodnaya Volya, the People's Will, whose executive committee had assassinated a tsar and whose organisational virtuosity Lenin openly admired while rejecting its politics root and branch. The pamphlet's enemies argued that the admiration had eaten the rejection: that the scheme of professionals was the People's Will with a Marxist vocabulary, and that the theory of consciousness from outside supplied the missing premise, a class that must be led because it cannot lead itself.

The documentary record supports a more interesting exchange than the charge's later career suggests, because Lenin answered it by embracing the word and reversing its charge. In One Step Forward, Two Steps Back he wrote the sentence his critics never stopped quoting and never quite finished: a Jacobin who wholly identifies himself with the organisation of the proletariat, the class conscious of its interests, is a revolutionary Social Democrat. The move is characteristic and worth understanding rather than merely deploring or applauding. Lenin's claim is that organisational form takes its class character from the body it organises: conspiracy in the service of a sect is one thing, discipline in the service of a mass class movement is another, and the People's Will failed, on his analysis, not because it was organised but because it was organised around a substitution, heroes in place of a class. Whether the distinction held in the practice of the following decades is the political argument, and it is exactly the argument stated in the objection section of this guide. What can be settled textually is narrower: the pamphlet's author did not regard tight organisation and mass class politics as opposites, he regarded the first as the second's instrument under Russian conditions, and the Jacobin charge, in its original form, was an argument within that shared framework about whether the instrument would master the purpose. Axelrod, who coined the charge's canonical form, had himself been a populist in his youth and knew both traditions from inside; his warning came from a man who had watched one revolutionary generation's methods outlive its politics. The warning deserves its longevity. So does the answer. A reader's guide serves neither by pretending the exchange was a misunderstanding. It was the first round of the argument the whole century would have about organisation and emancipation, conducted by the two men best qualified to have it, and the pamphlet is its opening brief.

## The worker carriers: Babushkin and the movement's real sociology

The doctrine of consciousness from outside describes a traffic between intellectuals and workers, and the movement's actual sociology, visible in its own records, was more tangled than the doctrine's diagram. The Iskra network's indispensable people inside Russia were very often workers who had crossed, by their own efforts, into the ranks of the convinced: organisers who ran illegal print shops, kept the codes, taught in the study circles and wrote the paper's factory correspondence. The emblematic figure is Ivan Babushkin, a metalworker from the Sestroretsk and Bryansk plants who became one of Iskra's first agents, reporting factory conditions, distributing the paper and building circles, until his arrest and exile; he was shot by a punitive expedition during the 1905 revolution's Siberian aftermath while escorting a transport of weapons. Lenin, writing his obituary in 1910, held him up as the type of the worker the movement existed to multiply: a man who had begun in the economic struggle, educated himself into politics through the movement's literature and organisation, and become a leader whose authority among workers rested on being one of them. The obituary is an inconvenient document for the crude version of the from without doctrine, since its hero carries consciousness to his class from inside it, and its author knew the difference and honoured it.

The complication is not a gotcha; it is the doctrine's own qualification, supplied by the practice the doctrine served. The pamphlet's professionals were always supposed to be recruited substantially from the class, trained up from the strike committees and the circles, and the 1905 writings make the recruitment drive explicit. What the doctrine reserved was not leadership to intellectuals but the production of theory to the movement's collective science, which workers entered as they became professionals. Critics answer that the distinction, however sincere, institutionalised a hierarchy of knowledge that no recruitment drive could dissolve, since the gate to the profession was guarded by those already inside it and the doctrine defined in advance what the recruits were to learn. Defenders answer that every serious movement distinguishes between experience and theory without thereby insulting experience, and that the Babushkins of the record refute the picture of a sealed caste. The guide takes no side in that exchange, which is the political argument in one of its sharpest forms. It insists only that the exchange be conducted with the movement's real sociology in view: a traffic in both directions, imperfect, contested and human, rather than the one way pipeline of the paraphrase. The pamphlet's Russia had worker intellectuals before it had a party, and its author, whatever his formulas, built his organisation out of them.

## The rhetoric of the stick: how the pamphlet argues

A close reading should attend to how the book persuades, because its afterlife cannot be separated from its style. The pamphlet's signature device is the borrowed metaphor promoted to an organising image. The stick, which the Economists had bent and the author bends back, governs the theory of polemic. The scaffolding governs the theory of organisation. The contrast of handicraft and factory governs the theory of method: the movement's localism is cottage industry, its remedy industrial. Around these load bearing images swarm the lesser figures of a famously savage polemicist: opponents are diagnosed like illnesses, their phrases are weighed like counterfeit coin, their programmes are followed into reductio with a delight the footnotes barely contain. The footnotes themselves are a parallel text, a running second voice that corrects, mocks and cross examines while the main voice expounds, and readers who skip them miss half the book and most of its comedy. The style is not decoration on the argument. It is the argument's theory of itself, that ideas are material forces wielded by organised people, enacted at the level of the sentence: every metaphor converts an abstraction into a task, a tool or a building, something that can be gripped.

The style also explains the text's vulnerability to its transmitters. A book that argues in images invites quotation by image, and the tradition duly quoted the stick, the scaffolding and the overturning of Russia while dropping the analyses the images summarised. Polemical compression is a wager: the author bets that the vivid formula will carry the complex argument into memories that would not hold the argument uncompressed. Posterity collected the formulas and discarded the arguments, which is the wager lost, and the author's own 1907 preface can be read as his recognition of the loss, an attempt to reattach the formulas to their contexts by main force. Writers of the guide's acquaintance in every tradition will recognise the pattern, since it is the common fate of polemics that succeed. The pamphlet's distinction is only that its author lived to file the correction himself and that the correction is so quotable, the bent stick, that it too circulates detached, deployed by readers who have never asked which stick, bent by whom, in which direction, in what year. This guide has tried to be the correction's scaffolding: unglamorous, structural, and removable once the building of an accurate reading stands.

## The pamphlet abroad: training manual of a century

Exported, the pamphlet had a second career that no account of it may omit, because for most of the twentieth century more people met it as a manual than as a document. In the Communist International's years the text became a standard of cadre education from Berlin to Shanghai: the new parties of the International were instructed to build themselves on its pattern, and its categories, the professional core, the central press, the war on economism in its local forms, supplied the vocabulary in which party building on several continents was discussed. In China its influence is a commonplace of the scholarship on the Communist Party's organisational formation, where the model of a disciplined party carrying doctrine to a vast peasant country was adapted with a thoroughness its author, who wrote for an empire's factory districts, could not have foreseen. In the Western universities after 1945 it became the set text through which Leninism was taught, usually in the standard reading's extracts; after 1968 it enjoyed a revival among student movements who read it, often against the Communist parties of their own countries, as a warrant for building new organisations, an appropriation its conditions clauses would have puzzled. Each export performed the same operation as the codifiers at home: the conditions were dropped at the border, because a manual must travel light, and the claims travelled as universals.

That career is evidence in the argument about the text, and both sides cite it. For the critics, the export record demonstrates what the text contains in embryo: a form so portable because so indifferent to circumstance could only have been, at root, a technology of control, and its worldwide replication by parties that reproduced the same internal regime is the experiment that settles the question. For the defenders, the export record demonstrates the opposite error: a text cannot be indicted for the editorial decisions of its exporters, and the parties that dropped the conditions were disobeying the document they claimed to follow, which is a fact about them. The guide's own position has been stated and is not moved by the export evidence in either direction, because the guide's claim is about what the text says and what its author said about it, and on those questions the export record is silent. What the record does establish, and what a reader in any country should carry away, is the mechanism of transmission this whole article documents: texts become charters by losing their contexts, and the loss is not an accident of careless reading but the normal physics of institutions, which need portable warrants more than they need accurate ones. Restoring the context is therefore not antiquarian work. It is the one operation that returns the text from the institutions to the reader, which is where, on the evidence of its author's preface, it was always supposed to end up.

## The table at work: one textbook paragraph, audited

The claims and attributions table earns its keep only in use, so let it be used once, on the sort of paragraph a student is most likely to meet. The paragraph, a fair composite of formulations found across introductory texts, runs as follows in substance. In 1902 Lenin published What Is to Be Done?, in which he argued that workers by themselves can develop only trade union consciousness, so socialist ideas must be brought to them from outside by a vanguard of professional revolutionaries. This doctrine, which broke with Marx's faith in the working class, became the theoretical basis of the Bolshevik party and of the one party state. Run the composite through the table's four questions and watch it come apart joint by joint. What does the text say: that trade union consciousness is the limit of the economic struggle left to itself, a claim about a struggle's products, not about a class's capacities. Whose words: the from outside formula is Kautsky's, quoted as orthodoxy, so the sentence that breaks with Marx was written by the guardian of Marxist orthodoxy to describe the normal relation of theory and class in the movement Marx founded; the break thesis survives the attribution only in a heavily qualified form. Conditions: the professional revolutionary is justified in the text by illegality, a condition the composite never mentions, and the party the pamphlet built its argument around was a faction in an illegal movement of a few thousand, not a state. Maintained afterwards: the author redescribed the book within five years as a one sided correction and urged the party open to mass worker recruitment within three, facts the composite's timeless present tense erases. Nothing in the composite is a fabrication; every phrase in it can be found in respectable print. It is a tissue of true words arranged into a falsehood by the omission of exactly the four things the table records: attribution, condition, gloss and sequel. That is why the guide built the table before building anything else, and why the reader who keeps it will find that most public argument about this pamphlet, on every side, fails the same audit in the same four places.

## Five misreadings, gathered in one place

The corrections scattered through this guide can be collected into the five misreadings the brief for it required, stated as a reader's checklist. The first misreading takes the from without argument for Lenin's innovation; the record shows a quotation from Kautsky, framed as orthodoxy, with the author's own gloss confining it to the outside of the economic struggle. The second takes the pamphlet for the blueprint of the party its author eventually led; the record shows a campaign document for a faction in an illegal movement, whose organisational maxims carry their police state conditions in the text and whose author broadened the gates at the first change of weather. The third takes the strong formulations for the author's permanent doctrine; the record shows the 1905 softening and the 1907 preface, the author's own instruction that the book is a correction to be straightened before use. The fourth lays the 1903 split at the pamphlet's door; the record shows a quarrel over a membership clause between co-editors, with the faction names minted by a later vote's arithmetic. The fifth treats economism as a swear word; the record shows a real tendency with serious proponents, a serious argument and a document, the Credo, that the young movement had to answer or dissolve. Each misreading is held in place by the same mechanism, the stripping of context for portability, and each correction is available in documents no scholarship disputes. A reader who can recite the five pairs, misreading and record, owns the pamphlet more securely than one who can recite the pamphlet.

## Reading the pamphlet well: editions, order and traps

The pamphlet is short enough to be read whole and famous enough to be ruined by extracts, so the first rule of reading it well is to read all of it, in order, including the preface and the footnotes. The standard English text is the fifth volume of the Collected Works, in the translation prepared for the Progress Publishers edition, and it is the version the scholarship cites; the reassessment's discussions of contested phrases should send the careful reader to its retranslations for the handful of passages where the argument turns on a word. Read it with its companions: the 1907 preface, without which the book is unfinished in the author's own judgement, and at least the Credo it answers, so that the enemy is a voice and not a ventriloquism. Keep a passage file as you go: the verified wording of each contested formulation, its attribution and its conditions, kept together in one place, is the reader's private version of this guide's table, and a local store for such working notes is the purpose of the study companion at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), where the passage cards for a text like this one can be kept beside the cross references they need. The traps are the five misreadings wearing reading clothes: the extract that begins at the famous paragraph, the summary that assigns the quotation to the quoter, the edition whose introduction settles the argument before the text can speak, the seminar that asks what the book proves about its author's soul before asking what it says. Against each trap the remedy is the same unglamorous discipline this guide has practised throughout, attribution, condition, gloss, sequel, applied in that order to every claim.

### Which edition or translation should a first reader choose?

The fifth volume of the Collected Works, whose English text is the scholarly standard and whose notes identify the pamphlet's targets and occasions. Read the whole volume's pamphlet in sequence rather than in extracts, and consult the reassessment's retranslations for the contested phrases where wording carries the argument.

One further trap deserves naming because it catches the guide's own likely readers. The pamphlet is a set text, and set texts are read for the examination's sake in a posture of extraction: the candidate hunts the quotable doctrine, the examiner's expected objection, the paragraph that will fill the essay's middle. That posture is half right about this book and wholly wrong about its interest. The pamphlet rewards being read as its first readers read it, as an intervention in a live quarrel whose other voices are recoverable and whose stakes were practical: whether a movement exists in six months' time. Read at that temperature, its certainties become intelligible without becoming contagious, its exaggerations declare their targets, and its author becomes a historical figure rather than a ventriloquist's dummy for the reader's prior politics, which is the transformation this whole guide exists to perform.

## The teacher behind the pupil: Plekhanov and the older orthodoxy

The pamphlet's posture of orthodoxy becomes fully intelligible only when the genealogy is extended one generation further back, to Georgi Plekhanov and the Emancipation of Labour group he founded with Vera Zasulich, Pavel Axelrod and others at Geneva in 1883. That group performed the founding labour of Russian Marxism: translating the texts, fighting the populist doctrine that Russia could leap to socialism through the peasant commune, and insisting that the Russian revolution would be made by a working class educated into politics by a Marxist party. Plekhanov's writings of the eighteen eighties and nineties, above all his 1883 pamphlet Socialism and the Political Struggle and his long essay on the development of the monist view of history, supplied the Russian movement with its theoretical grammar: the two stage revolution, the leading role of the workers, the necessity of political struggle against tsarism, and the conception of the party as the class's conscious vanguard, a word and a role present in the tradition well before the pamphleteer of 1902 was out of the gymnasium. The young Lenin revered Plekhanov with a completeness he granted no other living writer, and the pamphlet's author presented himself, accurately, as the old man's pupil in theory even where he was becoming his rival in organisation.

The genealogy cuts the standard reading at its root in a second place. If the pamphlet's central conceptions, the party as the bearer of consciousness, the intelligentsia as theory's first carriers, the suspicion of the elemental, were the common property of the Russian Marxist school since the eighteen eighties, then the search for the moment of innovation must be relocated, and with it the argument about responsibility. The reassessment's scholarly weight rests substantially on this relocation: the text's ideas have a documented prehistory in the movement's founding documents, and what distinguishes the pamphlet within that prehistory is not its doctrines but its engineering, the conversion of a shared outlook into an organisational plan with a newspaper for its engine. Plekhanov himself, reading the plan, supplied the counterpoint from inside the same orthodoxy: his break with its author after the congress, and his Bonapartism charge, were the complaints of the school's founder that the engineering had consumed the outlook. Teacher and pupil thus divide the tradition's two anxieties between them, the pupil fearing that without the machine the doctrine would remain literature, the teacher fearing that with it the doctrine would become the machine's property. The pamphlet is the pupil's brief, and it should be shelved, in the reader's mind, beside the teacher's dissent, which is not a refutation from outside the faith but the faith's own second thoughts. No account of the text that lacks the genealogy can explain either the text's confidence or its critics' standing, since both drew on the same inheritance.

## What the word science carried in that era

One more piece of period equipment is needed before the famous passage can be heard at its original pitch: the word science. In the German of the pamphlet's world, Wissenschaft meant systematic knowledge in general, and Marxism's claim to be scientific socialism was its proudest boast and its recruiting banner, the promise that the movement rested not on moral wishes but on a demonstrated knowledge of society's laws, comparable in dignity to the natural knowledge that was transforming industry. Kautsky's articles in Die Neue Zeit were the claim's weekly exhibition. To call the bourgeois intelligentsia the vehicle of that science was, in the idiom of the day, almost a sociological truism: the universities and the professions were staffed by the sons of the possessing classes, the workers were worked too long and schooled too little to staff them, and the socialist scholars of the movement, Kautsky and Bernstein and Plekhanov himself, were in biographical fact intellectuals who had carried their science into the workers' parties from the educated world. The passage that scandalises the modern ear was, to its first audience, a description of the movement's own personnel files, elevated into a doctrine of how the merger of theory and class begins.

Hearing the pitch correctly does not require the modern reader to share the period's confidence, and the guide flags the confidence as a period feature to be examined rather than absorbed. The doctrine that a science of society exists, finished in its essentials and carried by a definable stratum, is the pamphlet's deepest assumption and the one least argued for inside its pages, because its audience already held it. Later generations, including many inside the tradition, came to doubt both the finishedness and the carriers, and the doubts are part of the serious criticism the text must face: a movement armed with a science it believes complete will treat disagreement as error and error as heresy, and the pamphlet's polemical register, its certainty that the economists were not mistaken but deviant, breathes that air. Against this the reassessment's context cuts only so far: it can show that the certainty was the age's common property, shared by the pamphlet's opponents in their own publications, and it cannot show that the certainty was harmless. The reader should therefore hold two truths about the famous passage in a single grip. As a piece of sociology about who staffed the movement's theory in 1901, it was banal. As a charter for the permanent government of a movement by its science's licensed carriers, it contained, in embryo, the danger its first critics named. The text supports the first reading on its face. The second reading is the work of the century that used it, and the argument between the readings is not closable by philology, which is why this guide keeps the textual and the political accounts in separate ledgers and asks the reader to do the same.

## Luxemburg's answer, given its full weight

Among the first critics, one voice has outlasted the others and must be given a section of its own, because her 1904 essay on the organisational questions of Russian social democracy is the counter text to the pamphlet inside the tradition, written by the only contemporary whose theoretical stature matched the principals. Rosa Luxemburg's argument, made against the centralism she saw hardening in the majority's practice, is not the liberal argument that organisation is oppression. It is a revolutionary's argument about how a class learns. The working class, she held, educates itself in struggle and only in struggle; its errors are not waste to be engineered away by a committee's supervision but the very curriculum of its political maturity. Her sentence on the point has become the counter quotation to set against the pamphlet's: historically, she wrote, "the errors committed by a truly revolutionary movement are infinitely more fruitful" than the infallibility of the cleverest central committee. A centralism that distrusts the class's own movement, that treats initiative from below as a danger to be licensed, will produce a movement of clerks executing a centre's decisions and will discover, at the moment of crisis, that it has trained obedience where it needed judgement. The party's task is not to replace the class's self activity but to accelerate it, to be the most conscious expression of a movement whose life is its own.

The essay's authority in the argument rests on its author's standing and on its sequel. Luxemburg was no economist of the tendency the pamphlet opposed; she was a founder of a revolutionary party in Poland, a theorist of the first rank in the German party's left, and a practitioner of the mass strike politics the pamphlet's categories struggled to house. Her 1918 manuscript on the Russian revolution, written in a German prison while the state founded by the pamphlet's heirs was being built, extended the 1904 warning into a judgement on the practice: she defended the revolution against its enemies and attacked, from inside the defence, the suppression of the general suffrage, the dispersal of the elected assembly and the narrowing of public life, arguing that freedom restricted to the government's supporters is no freedom, since "freedom is always the freedom of the one who thinks differently". The manuscript, published after her murder in 1919, became the tradition's internal bill of rights, quoted by every subsequent generation of the movement's dissidents. Set beside the pamphlet, the two texts of 1904 and 1918 mark the poles of the tradition's permanent argument about itself: whether consciousness flows into the class through its organisation or rises out of the class through its experience, whether the party is the movement's teacher or its pupil, whether error is the enemy or the tuition. The guide's discipline forbids it a verdict in that argument, which is alive in every movement that inherits these texts. What the guide can do, and has tried to do, is to prevent the argument being settled by a misquotation: Luxemburg read the pamphlet's author closely enough to answer his real positions, and the least a modern reader owes both of them is the same courtesy.

## The renegade teacher: the quotation's revenge

The Kautsky borrowing has an epilogue too perfect for a novelist to risk, and a reader's guide should record it because it disciplines every party to the argument. In 1914 the German party's leadership voted the war credits, and the orthodoxy of which Kautsky was pope split along the century's great fault line. In 1918 Kautsky published The Dictatorship of the Proletariat, a systematic indictment of the Bolshevik regime's suppression of democracy, written with all the authority of the movement's former chief theorist. Lenin answered with The Proletarian Revolution and the Renegade Kautsky, among the most vitriolic texts he ever wrote, in which the pupil excommunicated the teacher from the doctrine both had shared. The man whose 1901 sentences on consciousness had been quoted as settling the matter in 1902 was, by 1918, the renegade; and the pamphlet's author, who had built his youthful authority on the teacher's, now policed the doctrine against him. Neither man, in the exchange, showed much interest in the youthful text that bound them, but a historian should: the episode demonstrates that the orthodoxy of 1902 was a living thing that its own carriers would soon wield against each other, which is what living doctrines do, and that the pamphlet's appeal to a settled science was an appeal to a settlement already beginning to crack. The standard reading, which requires the pamphlet to be the charter of a monolith, has no room for a story in which the charter's quoted authority is excommunicated by the charter's author within sixteen years. The documentary reading has room for it, because the documentary reading treats the pamphlet as an event in a tradition's life rather than as the tradition's genetic code. The companion essay on the [lenin vs kautsky debate](/2014/07/01/lenin-vs-kautsky-debate/) follows the quarrel into its later texts; this guide notes only its bearing on the passage with which the whole modern argument began.

## Checking the pamphlet's own evidence

The pamphlet argues from documents, its opponents' publications, congress reports, strike accounts and the movement's press, and it argues about them with a controversialist's selectivity. A reader who wants to test the text rather than merely absorb it has a defined set of checks, and performing two or three of them teaches more about the period than a shelf of summaries. The first check is the Credo itself, which survives and can be read entire in a few minutes: does it say what the pamphlet's tradition says it says, and is its tone that of a capitulation or of a strategy? Readers regularly find it more moderate and more interesting than its reputation, which recalibrates their sense of the polemic's temperature without reversing its verdict. The second check is Rabocheye Dyelo's own pages, particularly the editorial that supplied the pamphlet's first chapter with its target phrases, where the slogan of freedom of criticism can be seen doing its local work rather than its alleged German work. The third check is the strike record of the eighteen nineties from the historians rather than from the pamphlet, against which the embryonic consciousness judgement can be weighed as the historical claim it is. The fourth check is the Kautsky passage in its journal, Die Neue Zeit, where the reader can confirm with their own eyes the quotation marks the paraphrase forgot, and can read the Austrian programme debate around it to see how unremarkable the doctrine was in its home. The fifth check is the pamphlet's own footnotes against the texts they cite, the oldest discipline of controversial scholarship, which in this case yields the usual mixed harvest: a controversialist who quotes accurately and interprets ferociously, whose citations survive audit better than his conclusions do. None of these checks requires Russian beyond what translations supply, and all five together model the habit this series exists to teach: a famous text is a claim about other texts, and the claim can be audited.

## The congress in its own arithmetic

Because the split's legend does so much work in the pamphlet's reputation, the congress that minted the faction names deserves one section of unglamorous arithmetic. The Second Congress met with a mandate to found the party in fact: adopt the programme, adopt the rules, elect the centre. Its Iskra majority was real but constructed, resting on the paper's network and on alliances with groups whose loyalty was to the programme rather than to any editorial board. The programme passed with the unity of a movement ratifying its Erfurt inheritance. The rules divided it, first on the membership clause, where Martov's broader wording actually carried the vote against Lenin's, and then, after the congress had been thinned by the walkout of the Jewish Bund's delegates over the federation question and of two economist delegates over a point of recognition, on the election of the paper's editorial board, where the reduced Iskra slate won and the words majority and minority, bolshevik and menshevik in Russian, entered history as the names of the victors and the vanquished of that single ballot. The labels then wandered: majorities reversed themselves in committee after committee across the following decade, the factions reunified on paper and re-split in fact, and by 1912 the majority of 1903 had become a separate party by a route no clause of 1902 describes. The arithmetic teaches the same lesson as the attribution and the preface. A tradition that knows its own legends loosely, that lets a ballot's accident harden into an essence and a polemic's metaphors into a constitution, will misread its founding text in the same direction every time, by substituting the sequel for the document. The pamphlet belongs to 1902, the split to 1903, the party of the legend to a decade of subsequent construction, and a reader who keeps the three dates in separate columns of the mind has mastered the elementary discipline from which all the corrections in this guide follow.

## The other bowers: economists, terrorists and the symmetry of spontaneity

One of the pamphlet's framing insights is easiest to see in the article that preceded it, and it deserves extraction because it proves the central category was an analytical instrument rather than a factional cudgel. In Where to Begin?, the 1901 Iskra article that drafted the pamphlet's scheme, Lenin surveyed the movement's two fashionable deviations and pronounced them twins. The economists, he argued, bowed to the spontaneity of the workers' movement in its elemental form. The terrorists of the reviving populist tradition, whose Socialist Revolutionary Party was then coalescing around the assassination of ministers, bowed to the spontaneity of outraged intellectuals, substituting the elemental indignation of the heroic individual for the movement of the class. Both, in the formula's elegant cruelty, worshipped the elemental and called the worship realism; both thereby renounced the deliberate work, theoretical and organisational, that converts force into politics. The symmetry matters because it locates the pamphlet's target precisely. The enemy is not a class of persons, workers in one case or intellectuals in the other, but a posture toward unorganised force, and the posture is attributed to the author's opponents and temptations alike. A doctrine whose central sin is the abdication of deliberate organisation can be criticised, as this guide's critics do, for the uses of the organisation it enthrones; it cannot accurately be described as a doctrine of contempt for whichever group the critic prefers to defend, since its scorn falls on intellectuals who assassinate ministers as readily as on intellectuals who trail behind strikes. The pamphlet's first chapter and its prehistory in the terror debate restore that symmetry to view, and with it the coherence of the category of spontaneity across the whole Iskra campaign: one concept, wielded against every shortcut, in the name of a slow and unfashionable proposition, that movements are built.

## The German party reads its pupil

The pamphlet's Russian quarrel was refereed, in its first years, from Berlin, and the referee's rulings are part of the documentary record the standard reading omits. Kautsky's Die Neue Zeit followed the Russian split with the attention due to a family matter, and its editor's sympathies in 1903 and 1904 lay substantially with the Iskra majority's heirs: Kautsky regarded the new party's organisational seriousness as the Russian movement's coming of age and said so in the exchanges of those years, while also giving the columns of his journal to Luxemburg's organisational critique, an editorial hospitality that tells its own story about the orthodoxy's internal pluralism before the war destroyed it. The relationship ripened in 1906, when Kautsky published his analysis of the driving forces and prospects of the Russian revolution, a text that assigned the revolution's leadership to the proletariat in alliance with the peasantry and treated the liberal bourgeoisie as a spent force. Lenin translated the pamphlet into Russian himself and prefaced it with an endorsement so warm it startles readers formed by the later renegade quarrel: the teacher's analysis, he wrote, had brilliantly confirmed the tactics the pupil's faction had pursued. For the historian of the 1902 text the episode is a control experiment. A decade's mythology requires the pamphlet's author to have broken with German orthodoxy at birth; the record shows him, four years after the pamphlet, performing the labour of translation to bring the orthodoxy's chief into Russian print as his own vindication. Whatever broke between them broke later, over the war and the seizure of power, and an account that needs the break in 1902 must explain away the translation of 1906. The guide leaves that labour to the standard reading's defenders, with the observation that they rarely attempt it.

## The category travels: trade union consciousness after the pamphlet

Concepts that work do not stay home, and the pamphlet's central distinction had a long emigration into the social sciences, where it was naturalised under other flags. The Italian Marxist Antonio Gramsci, writing his Prison Notebooks in a Fascist cell a generation later, built his theory of hegemony on a cognate distinction between the economic corporate phase of a class's life, in which it knows its occupational interests, and the ethico political phase, in which it learns to pose its interests as the interests of the whole society; the parallel with the pamphlet's trade union and political consciousness is structural, and scholars of Gramsci have long noted the family resemblance, whatever the exact route of transmission. In postwar labour sociology the pamphlet's category survived in secular dress, in studies of workers' orientations that distinguished instrumental collectivism, the union as a wage machine, from solidaristic and political orientations, and argued about which the evidence supported; the argument is the pamphlet's argument with the metaphysics removed and survey data admitted, and it has never reached a settled verdict, which the pamphlet's ghost is entitled to find familiar. Even the category's critics inside the labour movement worked with its tools: the syndicalist and council traditions defined themselves by inverting its valuation, treating the economic struggle's own organs as the school and embryo of the new society rather than as its kindergarten, and the inversion is unintelligible without the original. A concept's travels are the final audit of its quality. The distinction between what a class generates in its daily bargaining and what it must construct to rule is one of the durable instruments of political analysis, used by scholars who would not cite its source in a footnote, and the pamphlet's authorship of its canonical form, whatever the quarrels over the use to which he put it, is the part of the standard reading that the documents fully confirm.

## The pamphlet's library: the authorities between its lines

A polemic is a web of citations wearing the dress of an argument, and this one's web maps the movement's intellectual world with unusual completeness. At the web's centre stands Engels, whose authority the first chapter borrows for its foundation stone: the pamphlet quotes his observation, from the journalism of the eighteen seventies on the German movement's refugee controversies, that the German workers' movement fought on three coordinated fronts, the theoretical, the political and the practical economic, and that this coordination was the secret of its strength. The quotation is chosen with a strategist's care. It establishes, from the tradition's second founder, that theory is not a garnish on the struggle but one of its three fronts, which is the first chapter's entire case for refusing the slogan of free criticism. Beside Engels stands the Manifesto, present everywhere and quoted sparingly, its account of the communists as the section of the class that pushes forward the common interests of the whole supplying the template the pamphlet's party is meant to fill. Kautsky's presence has been documented at the decisive passage. Around these stand the Russians: Chernyshevsky in the title, Pisarev in the dreaming passage, and a dense undergrowth of citations from the movement's own press, the economists' editorials quoted against themselves, the congress reports of foreign parties, the factory inspectors' published returns. The web's lesson for the close reader is that the pamphlet performs the very activity it preaches. It is a work of carried consciousness in its own form, an assembly of the movement's accumulated theory, German, Russian and classical, transported into a local quarrel by an editor who believed that was what theory was for. Summaries that present its author as a solitary legislator have not looked at his footnotes, where the legislature sits in permanent session.

The citation web also dates the text in a way its doctrines try not to be dated. The authorities are those of a specific settlement, the Erfurt settlement, in which Engels was the living arbiter, Kautsky his heir, and the Russian founders the local franchise. Within fifteen years every one of those relations had collapsed: Engels dead, Kautsky excommunicated, Plekhanov estranged, the German party split by the war. A text that argues by authority is hostage to its authorities' fortunes, and part of the pamphlet's strangeness for later readers is that its proofs kept resigning. The reassessment's deepest claim can be restated in these terms: the pamphlet is the Erfurt settlement's Russian application, and it should be read as a period document of that settlement, with the settlement's strengths and its shelf life, rather than as the founding act of something that imagined itself eternal. The codifiers who later froze it were, on this showing, freezing a moment their own tradition had already outlived, which is why the freezing required the editorial violence this guide has catalogued, the dropped attributions and amputated conditions. A living text resists its reliquary. This one's resistance is documented in its own apparatus, in the preface its author attached when the freezing began, and the resistance is available to any reader who reads the apparatus rather than skipping to the relics.

## The programme behind the pamphlet: minimum and maximum

The pamphlet presupposes a document it does not discuss, and a reader who supplies the document understands the pamphlet's political horizon for the first time. The Russian party, like its German model, was to have a two part programme. The maximum programme stated the final aim, the socialist transformation of society. The minimum programme stated the demands for the revolution immediately at hand: the overthrow of the autocracy and a democratic republic, universal suffrage, freedom of press and assembly, the eight hour day, the confiscation of the landed estates' remnants and the rest of the democratic inventory. Plekhanov drafted the programme for the Iskra board; Lenin's amendments pressed its agrarian clauses toward the peasantry; the Second Congress adopted it in the form the board proposed. Every political argument in the pamphlet operates inside the minimum programme's horizon. The tribune's exposures are the minimum programme's demands being taught as a curriculum. The war on economism is a war for the minimum programme's independence against those who would dissolve it into trade unionism or liberalism. The professional organisation is the instrument for a struggle whose stated goal is a democratic republic, within which, on the orthodox theory, the movement for the maximum would then proceed under legal conditions for the first time.

Recovering the horizon corrects a persistent foreshortening in the pamphlet's reputation. Read without the programme, the organisational maxims float free and can be mistaken for a technology of power in the abstract, equally suited to any aim. Read with it, they are the instruments of a specified campaign, the campaign for the democratic republic against the tsar, and their author's later conduct has at least to be argued about in the light of the specification: the same man spent the spring of 1917 insisting that the revolution not stop at the minimum, which his orthodox critics called heresy and his supporters called development, but neither camp can pretend the minimum was not the frame within which the pamphlet's politics were conceived. The frame also explains the pamphlet's otherwise odd silences, catalogued earlier in this guide. There is no theory of the socialist state in the pamphlet because the socialist state belongs to the maximum's territory, and the pamphlet is a minimum programme text from title page to colophon. There is no insurrection manual because insurrection belonged to a stage the minimum campaign had not reached. The codifiers' extraction of an eternal party doctrine required detaching the instruments from the campaign, and the historian's restoration of the campaign is therefore not pedantry but the recovery of the text's politics. Judge the instruments as campaign instruments and the argument about them becomes concrete, about fitness for a named purpose under named conditions, which is the only form in which it can be honestly settled.

## The exile university: where the professionals were actually made

The pamphlet demands a stratum of trained revolutionaries, and a natural question follows: trained where? The empire provided the answer in its own despite itself, in the system of Siberian exile, which functioned for two generations as the movement's involuntary university. Exile colonies gathered the movement's readers in one place with time, books and each other; the young Lenin spent his Siberian years at Shushenskoye writing The Development of Capitalism in Russia, translating, corresponding with the movement abroad and marrying into the cause, and he emerged with the habits the pamphlet demands of its professionals already formed. The pattern was general. The prisons and the exile settlements were where the scattered students and workers of the circles became a cadre: they read the library, learned the languages, argued the programmes out to their foundations and formed the personal loyalties on which the underground's trust, its scarcest resource, depended. The pamphlet's professional is unintelligible as a job description until this infrastructure is seen beneath him: he is the exile system's graduate, and the organisation is proposed as the means of making the system's accidental product deliberate, of training in freedom abroad and in the underground at home what Siberia had trained by accident.

The observation carries a moral the guide records without pressing. A movement's virtues are frequently the transfiguration of its persecutions, and the qualities the pamphlet celebrates, continuity, memory, theoretical seriousness, personal reliability under interrogation, were the qualities the repression selected for. The same selection also chose for conspiracy's vices, suspicion, factional memory, the habit of treating disagreement as a security matter, and the tradition carried both inheritances out of Siberia together. Later arguments about the party the pamphlet founded often proceed as if its character were deducible from its founding text alone. The exile university is a standing reminder that institutions are made by their material conditions before they are made by their documents, and that the pamphlet, for all its fame, was one input among several, competing with the police file, the transport list and the Siberian winter. A historian asked to explain the party that emerged will cite the pamphlet early and often, and will then have to cite the conditions that produced its readers, its author and its audience, which is the deepest sense in which this guide's recurring formula holds: the conditions are not the text's excuse. They are most of its explanation.

## The catechism years: the pamphlet in the party school

The pamphlet's final transformation, from document to catechism, has a datable instrument, and naming it completes the reception history this guide has traced in stages. In 1938 the party published the History of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (Bolsheviks): Short Course, the authorised narrative through which a generation of cadres, in the Soviet Union and in the parties abroad, learned the movement's past. The Short Course's treatment of the pamphlet is the standard reading in its perfected form: the from without doctrine presented as the founder's own theory of the party, the economists as a cautionary exhibit, the conditions nowhere, the 1907 preface unmentioned. The book was studied in circles with examinations, translated by the state's foreign languages press into the languages of every continent, and installed as the lens through which the pamphlet itself was thereafter read by the very organisation that claimed descent from it. To read the pamphlet in the middle of the century, inside the tradition, was to read it through the Short Course's summary; outside the tradition, it was to read it through the Cold War's mirror summary, which agreed with the Short Course on every fact and differed only in the moral. The two summaries' agreement is the reception's central datum, and it explains the reassessment's explosive effect when it finally arrived: Lih's book was not proposing a new nuance but withdrawing consent from a settlement that had held, across the barricades, for seventy years.

The catechism years also supply the guide's most portable lesson, and it is fitting that the lesson close the historical sections. Institutions that teach a text as a warrant will always simplify it in the direction of their needs, and the simplifications will always be the same four: drop the attribution, drop the conditions, drop the author's later qualifications, drop the enemy's case. The pamphlet suffered all four at the hands of its own church before its enemies bothered. Against that physics there is exactly one counterweight, the unglamorous one this guide has applied on every page: readers who go back to the documents, in order, with the apparatus attached. The pamphlet's author, whatever else is argued about him, left the counterweight's instructions in his own preface, in plain sight, where any party school could have read them. That the school did not is the tradition's judgement on itself, delivered in advance by its founder, and a reader who has followed the argument this far is equipped to deliver the same judgement independently, on evidence, which is the only kind this series recognises.

## The secretary of the revolution: the labour the plan presupposed

The pamphlet's plan required, as its first physical input, a correspondence network: hundreds of letters in cipher, addresses in invisible ink, reports from factory towns, manuscripts smuggled in luggage linings. That network had a secretary, and her near invisibility in the pamphlet's legend is a small model of the reception problem this guide exists to correct. Nadezhda Krupskaya, the author's wife and collaborator, ran the Iskra correspondence from the editorial side, holding the codes, the addresses and the threads of the agent web in her memory and her notebooks, performing the unglamorous labour on which the collective organiser's organising actually depended. Vera Zasulich sat on the paper's board, a founder of Russian Marxism whose attempt on a tsarist official's life a generation earlier had made her the movement's living link to its populist prehistory. The movement the pamphlet addressed was not, in its real composition, a pyramid of male theoreticians instructing a male class; it was a web of exiles, couriers, printers' assistants, teachers and worker correspondents of both sexes, held together by exactly the clerical and conspiratorial labour the plan theorised least and used most. Restoring that labour to the picture does the same work as restoring the Kautsky attribution: it replaces a legend of solitary authorship, doctrinal or organisational, with the record of a collective operating under constraints, which is the record's consistent shape wherever the guide has been able to check it. The pamphlet's own theory, that movements are made by organised people performing unglamorous tasks on schedule, is nowhere better illustrated than by the secretary its legend forgot, and a reader who wants to know what a professional revolutionary's working week looked like in 1902 could do worse than start with her correspondence desk.

## The author argues with himself: the later shelf

The surest antidote to the frozen Lenin of the standard reading is the shelf of his own later writings that argue, in effect, with the pamphlet, and a reader's guide should walk the shelf once before closing. Two Tactics of Social Democracy, written in the summer of 1905, fights the Mensheviks over the revolution then unfolding and breathes a different air from 1902 throughout: the emphasis falls on the masses in motion, on the party's duty to lead a democratic revolution it did not script, on boldness in alliance and improvisation, and the pamphlet's careful machinery appears as the scaffolding of a building now visibly rising on its own energies. The State and Revolution, written in hiding in 1917, imagines the administration of the new order in terms drawn from the Paris Commune, with officials on workmen's wages and the machinery of the old state smashed rather than inherited, a text whose libertarian temperature has embarrassed the tradition's administrators ever since and whose existence refutes any simple line from the pamphlet's centralism to a settled doctrine of rule. Left Wing Communism: An Infantile Disorder, of 1920, the author's last organisational testament, instructs the young parties of the West to work inside reactionary trade unions, to use bourgeois parliaments, to compromise and manoeuvre and retreat when retreat is required, a manual of flexibility whose author mocks the purists who mistake the Russian forms for universal sacraments. Read as a sequence, the shelf shows a political intelligence in permanent negotiation with circumstance, of which the pamphlet is the most rigid surviving statement precisely because it was written for the movement's most rigid circumstance, total illegality before any revolution. The standard reading's method is to take the shelf's most rigid volume as the whole mind. The documentary method is to read the shelf, and to let the author be as inconsistent, as adaptive and as argumentative with himself as the record shows him to have been. Neither method acquits or convicts the politics. Only one of them describes the evidence.

## The wager, costed on both sides

A guide that refuses a verdict still owes its reader a costing, and the costing can be done honestly if each side's ledger is kept in its own terms and neither is totalled by the house. On the credit side of the organisational wager, as its defenders state it: survival. The party form the pamphlet proposed carried a revolutionary organisation through two decades of the most efficient political policing in Europe, preserving continuity of programme, press and personnel across arrests that dissolved every rival formation at least once. When the autocracy collapsed in 1917, the form's beneficiary was the one organisation in the empire with the cadres, the communications and the habits of decision to act at the speed events required; historians who deplore the sequel routinely concede the organisational fact, since it is the premise of their explanations. On the debit side, as its critics state it: the habits. A form selected for survival under conspiracy carried conspiracy's reflexes into power, the treatment of dissent as a security question, the equation of faction with treason, the subordination of the movement's own pluralism to the centre's continuity, and the critics' ledger runs from Luxemburg's warnings through the faction ban of 1921 to the machinery of the following decades, each entry attributed by them to the logic the pamphlet first codified. The two ledgers are not reconcilable by addition, because they measure different currencies, survival against liberty, and the argument between them is the argument of the tradition's whole subsequent history, which this cluster of guides treats in its own volumes with the evidence assembled case by case. What the costing establishes for the reader of the pamphlet is narrower and sufficient: the text proposed a wager, that organisational continuity was worth its price in internal freedom, under conditions where the alternative appeared to be extinction. Wagers are judged by their prices and their alternatives together. The pamphlet's readers have usually been offered the price without the alternative or the alternative without the price. This guide's last service is to refuse both courtesies.

## A theory of the press before its time

Set the polemic aside and the fifth chapter contains something rarer than a party plan: an early and serious theory of what a periodical does to the people who produce and distribute it. The pamphlet's insight is that a newspaper is not primarily a container of opinions. Opinions, it argues by its whole design, are the least of it. A regular paper is a rhythm imposed on a scattered population: deadlines that synchronise strangers, a distribution round that maps the movement's real geography, a correspondence column that converts readers into reporters and reporters into cadres, a common text that gives a hundred isolated circles the same sentences to argue with in the same week. The organisation is the sediment of these repeated acts. Later ages would build theories of media around less, and would usually credit the discovery to the age of broadcasting or of the network; the pamphlet's version, framed for smuggled newsprint and false bottomed suitcases, grasps the essential mechanism with a clarity the later theories rarely match, that a medium organises its producers before it persuades its audience. The German party's press empire stood behind the insight as its proof: hundreds of papers, an army of functionaries formed by producing them, a movement whose nervous system was print. The Russian application required the same system to be run as a conspiracy, which is the pamphlet's distinctive twist on its model, the scaffolding metaphor doing duty for a sociology of communication that its century had not yet named.

The insight has a dark twin, which the guide's even handedness requires to be stated with the same clarity. A medium that organises its producers also disciplines them: the common text is a common line, and the machinery that synchronises a movement can be used to silence its internal variety, as the tradition's later history of its own press abundantly shows. The pamphlet knows the first half of this and cannot be expected to know the second, since in 1902 the problem was existence, not excess. The reader who extracts the media theory should therefore extract it whole, mechanism and risk together, and should notice that the extraction vindicates the guide's method once more. The fifth chapter, the least quoted part of the book, turns out to contain its most durable idea, invisible to a reception that strip mined the second chapter for doctrine. Reading whole books, in order, with their unfashionable chapters intact, is not a pedant's preference. It is how the durable ideas are found, in this text and generally.

## How the defeated remembered it

The economists lost the argument of 1902 so completely that their side of it survives mainly in their opponent's quotations, and a reader's guide should end its historical work by noting what the defeated themselves later made of the defeat. The survivors dispersed along the century's fault lines. Kuskova and Prokopovich passed through the cooperative movement and the Provisional Government into emigration, where she wrote with undiminished sharpness about the regime her old antagonist founded, regarding the Credo affair, in retrospect, as the overture to a tragedy whose authors included her opponents' certainty as much as her own side's evasions. Martynov, the pamphlet's named sparring partner, became a leading Menshevik publicist and, in a turn the pamphlet's author did not live to enjoy, ended his career inside the Communist Party's own historical institutes, having concluded in the nineteen twenties that the Bolsheviks had been right about the movement's direction in the years of the great quarrel; his memoirs concede the economists' theoretical surrender with a frankness that complicates every camp's caricature of him. Others of the tendency vanished into the professions, the unions and the prisons, leaving the record to be written by the winners. The pattern is the usual one, and naming it is the guide's final act of fairness: the archive of a polemic is curated by its victor, and the reader who wants the economists whole must go to the memoirs, the minor papers and the footnotes of the scholarship, where the defeated are still arguing. Their argument, recovered, does not reverse the verdict of 1902 on the evidence this guide has weighed. It restores the quarrel's dignity, which the catechism version, with its cardboard deviationists, had abolished, and a quarrel with its dignity restored is the only kind from which a reader learns anything transferable. The pamphlet's deepest subject, announced in its title and pursued through every section here, is what is to be done when a movement must think and build at once. Its defeated opponents were asking the same question with a different answer, and the century that judged between them did so, as centuries do, partly on the merits and partly on the events. The merits are in the documents. The documents are open. That is where this guide leaves its reader, deliberately.

## The second chapter with a pencil: its logic in sequence

Students are often told to master the second chapter and are rarely shown its skeleton, so let the guide perform the unfashionable service of reducing it to its moving parts, in order, before restoring the flesh. The chapter begins with a concession: the spontaneous awakening of the masses in the strike wave was real, heroic and formative, the class's first school. It proceeds to a measurement: what that school taught was the art of combination and the demand for economic redress, trade union consciousness, valuable and bounded. It introduces, by quotation, the doctrine that explains the bound: knowledge of society as a whole, the science, is produced under conditions the economic struggle does not supply, and reaches the struggle from the wider sphere. It draws the organisational corollary: an agency must exist whose business is the carrying, and the corollary's edge is sharpened by the two ideologies passage into a law of cultural gravity, that unelaborated theory defaults to the ruling ideas. It closes by turning the whole sequence against the economists' practice: to trail behind the spontaneous movement is to leave its ideas to the gravity, therefore to serve, in effect, the ideology the movement exists to oppose. Every step is contestable and has been contested, on the measurement by historians of the strikes, on the doctrine by philosophers of knowledge, on the corollary by the tradition's own dissidents. But the sequence is an argument, with premises that can be isolated and tested one by one, not a mood to be absorbed or denounced whole. The pencil work matters because both the catechism and the demonology require the chapter to be a mood: a catechism cannot be cross examined, and a mood cannot be refuted. Restore the skeleton and the chapter becomes what its author, at his most careful, intended, a chain of claims about knowledge, organisation and power that a serious opponent can break at a chosen link. Luxemburg chose the corollary's link. The historians chose the measurement's. The philosophers chose the doctrine's. The argument's map is the chapter's real bequest, and it is a better one than any of its slogans.

## What the corrected reading changes, and what it leaves standing

The guide has accumulated corrections for many pages, and it owes the reader a final accounting of their sum, stated as precisely as the evidence allows. The corrected reading changes the pamphlet's genre, from constitution to correction: a campaign document of 1902, aimed at a named deviation, angled by its author's admitted method, and recalled by that author to its context when others began to freeze it. It changes the pamphlet's lineage, from solitary invention to orthodox application: Erfurt's doctrine, Plekhanov's school, Kautsky's sentences, Chernyshevsky's title, a collective inheritance assembled by an editor of genius. It changes the pamphlet's temperature, restoring the conditions, illegality, dispersion, a hunted movement of circles, that its maxims carry in their own text. And it changes the location of the argument about the tradition the pamphlet fathered, moving that argument out of the founder's supposed psychology and into the record of choices, by the author and by his heirs, that the documents actually preserve. What the corrected reading leaves standing should be stated with equal precision, since a correction that claims everything claims nothing. It leaves standing the pamphlet's organisational proposal in its full rigour, proposed by its author and built by him. It leaves standing the severity of the two ideologies doctrine and every hard question that severity raises about movements armed with certainties. It leaves standing the critics' central objection, that the practice followed the pamphlet and that texts are finally judged in the company of their consequences. And it leaves standing the text itself, stranger and more interesting than its legend, available to any reader willing to do what its author finally asked: to straighten the stick before judging the hand that bent it.

## Paper across borders: the plan's logistics in practice

The pamphlet's scheme is usually discussed as doctrine, but it was first of all a logistics, and the logistics explain the doctrine's shape better than any commentary. A newspaper printed abroad, in Munich first, then London, then Geneva, had to reach readers in the empire's factory districts through borders watched by a professional political police. The solutions the Iskra organisation built became the party's skeleton in fact: transport routes through Scandinavia and Finland, through Persia and the Caucasus, through the Black Sea ports; literature sewn into bindings, packed in double bottomed trunks, carried by sailors, commercial travellers and students; and, in time, illegal printshops inside Russia itself, at Kishinev and Baku among other places, where issues were reprinted from matrices smuggled past the frontier. Every stage of the chain required people with specialised skills and absolute reliability: the courier who memorised the addresses, the agent who maintained the safe house, the compositor who set type in a cellar, the correspondent who wrote the factory reports that filled the paper's columns and proved to its readers that the network reached their own street. The organisation the pamphlet theorises is this chain, reflected on by its own builders. Its centralism is the chain's requirement that a compromised link be cut before it burns the whole; its professionalism is the chain's division of labour; its famous discipline is the timetable of a publication that must appear whether or not the police have had a good month. Readers who grasp the logistics stop asking why the pamphlet's tone is so unlike a seminar's. It is the tone of people describing a supply line under fire, and its severities are the severities of the supply officer rather than the metaphysician. The guide has argued throughout that conditions explain the text without acquitting it, and the logistics are the conditions at their most concrete: paper, ink, borders and jail. Any judgement of the pamphlet that cannot say how the paper crossed the frontier has not yet met the pamphlet's world.

## The crafts of conspiracy: what professionalism comprised

Professionalism sounds like an abstraction until its curriculum is listed, and the curriculum of the underground was a set of learnable crafts, each with its manuals, its masters and its failures paid for in arrests. There was the craft of the cipher, chemical inks and book codes for the correspondence on which the network's memory depended, the secretary's desk its academy. There was the craft of the alias, the managed second identity with its papers, its biography and its discipline of never being breached in a careless letter home, the craft in which the pamphlet's author, who signed himself with a river's name and a shelf of other signatures, was a lifelong practitioner. There was the craft of the safe house and the meeting, the rotation of addresses, the watch kept on the watchers, the rule that no member know more of the network than his task required. And there was the master craft that contained the rest, the assessment of people: the judgement of who would hold under interrogation, who talked too much in safety, who could be trusted with the border crossing and who only with the leaflets. The pamphlet's demand for training is a demand that these crafts be taught deliberately rather than acquired by catastrophe, and its contempt for amateurishness is the craftsman's contempt, earned in a world where the amateur's mistake was measured in other people's sentences. Restoring the curriculum to the argument performs the guide's usual restoration in miniature: a doctrine that sounds like a theory of human hierarchy resolves, at ground level, into a syllabus of skills under conditions of persecution. The hierarchy questions remain, and this guide has not dodged them. But they are questions about a real syllabus and its real dangers, not about an essence, and they are the better argued for being asked in the concrete. The cellar, the cipher and the false bottomed trunk are not the pamphlet's metaphors. They are its footnotes, written in the empire's police registers, and a reader who keeps them in view reads the famous sentences at their true weight.

## One word, two Englishes: the elemental and the spontaneous

A last lesson in reading belongs to a single Russian word, because the pamphlet's most famous antithesis changes its colour with its translation. Stikhinost, the quality of the stikhiiny, is rendered in the standard English edition as spontaneity, and the choice is defensible and consequential. The Russian root carries the Greek stoicheion, the element, and its native weather: the стихийный process is the elemental one, a force of nature, blind, powerful and unreasoning, as a flood or a fire is powerful. To call the strike wave's consciousness elemental is to make a claim about its nature, that it moves as weather moves, magnificent and unguided. To call it spontaneous is to make a claim about its origin, that it arises unbidden, which invites the reader to ask the political question, unbidden by whom, and to hear a reproach where the Russian hears a diagnosis. English language polemics about the pamphlet have been conducted for a century across this small crack in the translation, with one tradition's elemental Lenin, the theorist of nature's forces requiring a channel, shadowed by another tradition's spontaneity Lenin, the commissar reproaching the class for its own initiative. The reassessment's philological care consisted partly in restoring the weather to the word. The first reader cannot learn Russian for one noun, but can learn to ask, whenever a translated text is quoted at them with an air of finality, which of its loads the rendering has chosen to carry. It is the smallest possible version of this guide's whole method, and the one most easily carried to every other text in the series.

## For students and exam candidates

This pamphlet generates the most predictable essay question in the Lenin syllabus, some form of asking whether it shows its author distrusting the working class, and it is lost by candidates who answer it from the paraphrase. The answer that scores is the audit this guide performs: name the famous claim, attribute it correctly to Kautsky's quoted passage, state the author's own gloss confining outside to the sphere beyond the economic struggle, produce the conditions clause for the organisational demands, and close with the 1907 preface and the bent stick. Each of those moves is a document, not an opinion, and markers reward documents. In evaluation, state the strongest objection in its owners' words, that the practice followed the pamphlet whatever the preface said, before offering the separation of textual and political questions as your adjudication. Never write that the pamphlet caused the 1903 split, never attribute the from without formula to its quoter without noting the quotation, and never present the strong formulations as the author's final position without the sequel. Candidates who conclude against the pamphlet's politics score full marks when their exposition would satisfy its defenders; candidates who defend it from the paraphrase score poorly against markers who have read the preface.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

Teach this text as a pair, never alone: the pamphlet and its 1907 preface in the same week, with the Credo distributed beside them so the enemy speaks. The seminar exercise that transforms the room is the claims table performed live: give small groups one famous formulation each and require them to fill the four columns, attribution, wording, conditions, sequel, from the text before any discussion of merits is allowed. Expect the Kautsky attribution to land as a small scandal and use the scandal: ask why three generations of summaries deleted the quotation marks, and let the codification story, catechism and Cold War alike, emerge from the students' own answers. Set Luxemburg's 1904 essay as the counter text and Axelrod for the prosecution's opening, and forbid, for the first hour, all reference to events after 1917, so the class learns the discipline of the document before the licence of hindsight. The predictable failure modes are biographical reduction, the pamphlet as a window on a temperament, and its mirror, the acquittal by context; name both in advance and mark against both.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The live research frontier on this text is documentary, and three files remain genuinely open. The first is philological: the contested phrases of the standard translation, above all the renderings of the spontaneity vocabulary and of the from without construction, where the reassessment's retranslations have shifted scholarly usage without yet displacing the classroom text, so that citation practice now varies by camp and a footnote's translation choice has become a position. The second file is the draft's own history, the circulation of the manuscript among the Iskra editors and the record of their marginal responses, which bears directly on how far the pamphlet represented a board's collective line against how far it was one editor's maximal statement later trimmed by events. The third is reception in the strict sense: the printing, excerpting and translation history by which a campaign document became a constitution, including the editorial apparatus of the successive collected works, whose footnotes quietly reframed targets and softened or sharpened phrases as the tradition's needs changed. Journalists covering the text's periodic returns to the news, usually as a shorthand in arguments about quite different movements, have one professional obligation this guide exists to make easy: the most quoted sentence in the pamphlet is a quotation, and printing it as its quoter's coinage is a factual error of the same order as misnaming a source. Historians of the book will find the pamphlet an unusually pure case of a general law their discipline keeps rediscovering, that institutions preserve the formulas of their founding texts by amputating the contexts that made the formulas true, and that the amputation is visible, datable and reversible in the archive.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is What Is to Be Done about?

It is a polemic of 1902 in which Lenin argues against the tendency he calls economism and for a centralised party of professional revolutionaries, built around a common newspaper, under the conditions of Russian illegality. Its theoretical core distinguishes trade union consciousness, which the economic struggle generates unaided, from socialist political consciousness, which must be constructed from knowledge of all classes' relations to the state. Its practical core is organisational: trained cadres, a stable leadership, and the Iskra newspaper network as the scaffolding of a party that did not yet exist. Read whole, it is a builder's manual for a movement, written as a theoretical treatise.

### Q: Did Lenin say workers cannot develop socialist consciousness on their own?

He took the argument over from Kautsky and presented it as the movement's settled orthodoxy: the celebrated passage in the second chapter quotes Kautsky's article of 1901 at length and endorses it in terms as profoundly true and important. The distinction his own gloss insists on separates the economic struggle, which acquaints the worker with the employer alone, from the wider sphere of all classes' relations to the state, the sphere from which political consciousness must be drawn. Five years on he was describing the whole pamphlet as a polemical corrective, angled against the economists' deviation rather than framed as a programme for general use, and in the revolution's year he wrote of the class as instinctively drawn toward socialism.

### Q: What was economism?

Economism was the tendency, strong in the Russian movement around 1900 and voiced by papers such as Rabocheye Dyelo and by the 1899 Credo, which held that the workers' economic struggle was the movement's real substance and that political aims should grow from it or be left to the liberals. Its proponents argued from the movement's youth and from the strike wave's spontaneous origins, and suspected intellectuals of appointing themselves the class's tutors. Lenin's counter was that a movement which renounces independent politics does not escape politics but receives the politics of others, and that the economic struggle alone yields demands the existing order can satisfy. It was a real position with serious carriers, not merely a term of abuse.

### Q: Did Lenin later disavow the pamphlet?

Not in the sense of retracting or apologising for it, and the distinction is the point. In the preface he wrote in 1907 for the Twelve Years collection he described the pamphlet as a polemical corrective against economism, said that it bent the stick as the Economists had bent it the other way, and warned against reading its formulations as a general programme for other conditions. He continued to defend its argument against economism and its demand for trained cadres. What he withdrew was the licence to use a corrective as a constitution. Readers who call the preface a disavowal overstate it; readers who ignore it misread the book against its author's explicit instructions.

### Q: What does bending the stick mean?

It is Lenin's image, from the 1907 preface, for deliberate polemical overcorrection. When a stick has been bent in one direction, his argument runs, straightening it requires bending it the other way past the vertical. The Economists had bent the movement's stick toward worship of spontaneity and surrender of independent politics; the pamphlet bent it back, with one sided formulations calibrated to the distortion they corrected. The image concedes that the pamphlet overshoots on purpose and instructs the reader to straighten it before use. It is this guide's organising claim, because a century of canonisation and prosecution alike proceeded by quoting the bent formulations as though they were the straightened ones.

### Q: Why did Lenin want professional revolutionaries?

Because the movement he addressed was illegal, hunted and perpetually decapitated by arrests. Trained, full time, continuous cadres, maintained by the organisation and schooled in both theory and the techniques of underground work, were his answer to the handicraft local circle whose members improvised every task and vanished at the first raid, taking the organisation's memory with them. The text justifies the demand by its conditions, the autocracy's surveillance and the absence of legal politics, and it expects workers to be promoted into the professional ranks rather than excluded from them. Whether the form outlived its conditions, and what that did to what it built, is the standing political argument the critics press.

### Q: Is the standard reading of the text wrong?

In its three load bearing propositions, substantially yes, on documents no scholarship disputes. It presents as Lenin's invention a formula he quotes from Kautsky and flags as orthodoxy. It presents as a timeless party blueprint a set of organisational maxims whose justifying conditions, illegality under autocracy, stand in the text beside the maxims. And it presents as the author's settled doctrine formulations he publicly redescribed within five years as a one sided correction. The reassessment associated with Lars Lih's Lenin Rediscovered of 2006 argues the case at length. Its critics reply, with force, that the correction is about the text's meaning and lineage and does not touch the political criticism of the practice that followed it, a reply this guide accepts and builds into its own separation of the two questions.

### Q: How long is the pamphlet?

Short enough to be read whole, which is how it should be read. It has a preface and five chapters and occupies, in the fifth volume of the Collected Works, a little under two hundred pages of print, though much of that bulk is the savage footnotes, which are a parallel text and should not be skipped. A careful reader finishes it in a few sittings. Its brevity is part of its history: generations of students could be assigned the entire book, so its formulations entered the tradition at full strength, while its length made the later excerpting of the second chapter a choice rather than a necessity, a choice whose consequences this guide documents.

### Q: Did the pamphlet cause the party split?

No. The split came at the Second Congress of 1903, a year after publication, and it turned on the first clause of the party rules, the definition of membership, where Martov's broader formula in fact defeated Lenin's narrower one in the vote. The faction names, majority and minority, derive from a subsequent ballot on the editorial board, held after walkouts had changed the congress's arithmetic. The pamphlet's author and his chief opponents at the congress had co-edited the same newspaper and co-drafted the same rules. Loading the pamphlet with responsibility for the schism reads history backwards from the schism's consequences, and confuses a campaign document of 1902 with the quarrels of 1903.

### Q: What should you read alongside What Is to Be Done?

Four companions, in this order. The Credo of 1899, so the enemy is a voice rather than a ventriloquism. The 1907 preface to Twelve Years, the author's own instruction for the book's use, without which the book is unfinished in his judgement. Luxemburg's essay of 1904 against the majority's centralism, the tradition's great internal counter text. And a modern reassessment, Lih's Lenin Rediscovered above all, read with its critics, so that the student sees both the correction and the case against overcorrecting. Add the companion Iskra writings, Where to Begin? and the Letter to a Comrade, when time allows, since the shelf corrects the single volume.

### Q: How does the bent stick image explain Lenin's polemical method?

It presents overstatement as an instrument chosen in advance: identify the direction in which the opponent has bent the common position, then bend deliberately past the vertical the other way, so that the argument's landing point, once the opponent's resistance is spent, approximates the truth. The method explains the pamphlet's register throughout, its absolutisms about ideology, its scorn for the slogan of the hour, its refusal of the moderate formulation even in passages where the moderate formulation is what the author, on his own later showing, believed. It also explains the method's danger, visible in the reception: instruments calibrated to an opponent's distortion become distortions in their own right when the opponent leaves the field, and the author's preface is the record of the calibrator trying, too late for the tradition's habits, to recall the instrument.

### Q: What should a reader check in the pamphlet's account of the economists?

Four things, all checkable in an afternoon. Read the Credo entire and compare its tone and content with the pamphlet's tradition of it. Read Rabocheye Dyelo's editorials for the slogan of freedom of criticism in its local setting. Compare the pamphlet's quotations from its opponents with their originals, the oldest audit in controversial scholarship, and note the usual controversialist's harvest: accurate quotation, ferocious interpretation. And check the strike record of the eighteen nineties from the historians, so that the judgement of embryonic consciousness can be weighed as the historical claim it is rather than absorbed as a slur or a compliment. The pamphlet argues from documents and invites, on its best pages, exactly this audit.

### Q: Why did Lenin later call parts of the pamphlet overstated?

Because he understood his own method and said so. The pamphlet was written to correct economism at the moment of its maximum influence, and its formulations were set, by the logic of the bent stick, at the angle the correction required rather than at the angle of his settled view. By 1905 a real revolution had changed the conditions and displayed the class's capacities on a scale that made several of the strong formulations unusable without qualification, and by 1907 he was preparing the text for republication inside a collected volume, where unqualified formulations would harden into doctrine. The preface is his attempt to prevent that hardening. He did not think the pamphlet false; he thought it angled, and he wanted the angle marked on the instrument.

### Q: How does the claims table separate the text from its later uses?

By forcing every claim through four questions the later uses cannot answer. What exactly does the text say, which dissolves paraphrase into wording. Whose words are they, which restores the Kautsky quotation to its author. By what conditions does the text justify the claim, which reattaches the police state to the organisational maxims. And did the author maintain it afterwards, which brings the 1905 softening and the 1907 preface into the same row as the claim they qualify. A later use that survives all four columns is an interpretation; one that requires a column to be left blank is a conscription. The table is the guide's discipline made portable, and it works on any summary of the pamphlet from any camp, including the summaries in the pamphlet's own tradition.

### Q: What separates consciousness in the pamphlet from its meaning in Marx?

Emphasis and machinery, more than substance. In Marx the consciousness of the class arises within its practical struggle and its organisation, with the communists as the section that articulates the movement's general interest and pushes it forward; the knowing and the doing grow together. The pamphlet inherits that frame and adds a stress: theory, as a finished science elaborated by intellectuals, must be carried into the struggle by an organised party if the class's knowledge is not to stall at the bargaining table. The stress creates the tension the whole literature argues about, between consciousness as the class's self education and consciousness as an import with the party as its carrier. Later thinkers inside the tradition, Lukács with his account of imputed class consciousness above all, tried to rebuild the bridge the pamphlet's polemic had strained.
