## Why British India needed one penal code

### Why did British India need a single penal code in the 1830s?
British India needed the Indian Penal Code because criminal law varied by place and community, leaving punishment uncertain and unequal. A single code promised clear definitions, consistent penalties, and rules that officials across distant districts could apply in the same way to the same act.

![The Indian Penal Code as a designed code: Macaulay's architecture of definitions, exceptions and graded offences - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-16.webp)

Before codification, a person accused of wrongdoing in British India did not face a single body of criminal law that applied in the same terms across the territory. The law that governed the case depended heavily on where the act had taken place, which court heard the matter, and which source of law that court treated as authoritative for the question before it. In the presidency towns, English criminal law occupied a prominent place, carried into India through the institutions that served those towns and applied by judges whose training and habits of reasoning were shaped by English practice. Outside those towns, in the mofussil, the position was different. Regulations issued for the administration of the provinces supplied much of the criminal law, and those regulations did not always reproduce the English rules that applied in the presidency towns. Alongside both stood uncodified Hindu and Islamic criminal law, still relied upon for many questions and administered unevenly from place to place. The result was not a system in any coherent sense. It was an accumulation of sources, each with its own history, its own language, and its own method of interpretation, brought together by administrative necessity rather than by design.

The practical consequence of that accumulation was uncertainty at every stage of a criminal case. An official who had to decide whether conduct amounted to an offence first had to settle which body of law governed the conduct at all. That preliminary question could consume the attention that ought to have gone to the facts of the case, to whether the act had been done, and to whether the person accused had done it with the state of mind that made it punishable. Where sources pointed in different directions, the official had to choose between them, and a choice made in one district might not be made in the next. Two persons who had done the same thing in materially identical circumstances could therefore stand in very different legal positions for no reason that had anything to do with the seriousness of what they had done or the harm it had caused. One might face prosecution for a serious crime, with a severe punishment attached. The other might face a lesser charge, a different procedure, or no criminal liability at all. A legal order that produces such differences by accident of geography teaches those who live under it that law is a matter of location and local habit, not a set of rules that can be known in advance and relied upon.

That uncertainty bore hardest on the ordinary administration of justice, because criminal law is the branch of law the state uses most directly against the individual. A defect in a rule about property or contract may cause loss and confusion, but a defect in the criminal law can lead to imprisonment, to corporal suffering under older forms of punishment, and to the lasting disgrace that follows conviction. For such law to command even minimal respect, the person subject to it needs a fair chance of knowing beforehand what is forbidden and what will follow if the prohibition is broken. Uncodified law, known principally to those who administered it and expressed through texts and commentaries that were not readily accessible to the wider population, could not supply that chance across a vast territory. Nor could regulations scattered through administrative collections, amended and supplemented over time without being gathered into a single ordered statement. The problem was not simply that the law was diverse. Diversity by itself might be defended where different communities govern their own internal affairs by their own rules. The problem was that the diversity governed the coercive power of the same state, exercised in the name of the same sovereign authority, yet varied in substance from one court and one district to another.

The position also placed an unreasonable burden on the officials who had to administer the law. Many of those officials were not trained lawyers, and even those with legal training in England found that their training prepared them only imperfectly for the mixture of sources they encountered in the mofussil. An administrator expected to keep order, collect revenue, and decide criminal matters needed law that could be consulted directly, in one place, in language that answered the question before him without requiring a search through competing authorities. Where the law had to be reconstructed from regulations, English authorities, and religious legal traditions on each occasion, consistency depended on the learning and diligence of the individual official. The defect, in other words, was institutional rather than personal. It could not be cured by exhorting officials to work harder or by replacing one set of administrators with another. It could be cured only by changing the form in which the law itself was presented to them, so that the answer to an ordinary criminal question lay in a text that any competent official could read and apply.

For the government of British India, there was a further difficulty of legitimacy and control. A central authority that cannot state, in a single document, what conduct it treats as criminal within its own territory has only an imperfect grip on the power exercised in its name by distant subordinates. Local variation may begin as adaptation to local circumstance, but over time it hardens into local discretion, and discretion exercised without a common standard is difficult to supervise, difficult to appeal against, and difficult to correct. A uniform penal code offered the central government a means of discipline over its own machinery as much as over the population. It would define the offences that local officials could prosecute, fix the punishments they could seek, and supply the standard by which a superior court or a superior administrator could judge whether a conviction was sound. Uniformity, seen in this light, was not an abstract preference for tidiness. It was a technique of government for a territory too large to be supervised by personal attention, where written rules had to do the work that direct oversight could not. The same code that told the subject what was forbidden also told the magistrate what he was authorised to punish, and told both of them where the boundary lay.

It is against that background that the Charter Act of 1833 has to be understood. The Act remade the constitutional arrangements for British India and provided for a legislature capable of making law for the whole of the territory, rather than leaving lawmaking divided among separate local authorities whose enactments stopped at their own boundaries. That change mattered for criminal law because a single penal code is only possible where a single lawmaking authority exists to enact it and to maintain it. Without such an authority, even a well drafted code would remain a proposal, dependent on adoption piecemeal by bodies that might amend it in incompatible ways and reproduce the very fragmentation it was meant to end. With such an authority, codification became a practical project rather than a scholarly aspiration. The Act also created the office and the institutional setting within which systematic law reform could be undertaken as a deliberate exercise, with a commission charged to inquire, to draft, and to submit its work for enactment. The connection between the constitutional change of 1833 and the penal project that followed is therefore direct. First came the power to make one law for all India. Then came the machinery to prepare that law, and only after that preparation could a draft be placed before the legislature.

A penal code, properly understood, performs a limited and precise task within that larger legal order, and the limits are part of its value. The Penal Code states what counts as an offence and what it is punished with, while investigation, arrest and trial belong to the procedural code, treated in [the complete guide to the Code of Criminal Procedure 1973](/2010/11/15/crpc-1973-complete-guide/). Keeping those questions apart clarifies the responsibility of each part of the system. The substantive code answers the question of liability: what act or omission, done with what intention or knowledge, the law treats as criminal, and within what range it may be punished. The procedural law answers the question of process: how a complaint is received, how an accused person is brought before a court, how evidence is heard, and how a judgment is reached and reviewed. Confusing the two produces law that is hard to use, because a reader searching for the definition of an offence should not have to work through rules of arrest to find it, and a reader searching for the rules of trial should not have to reconstruct them from the chapters that define crimes. The decision to codify the substantive criminal law as a distinct work, with its own definitions and its own structure, reflected that separation and made each part easier to state completely and to amend without disturbing the other.

## Macaulay and the making of the draft

The First Law Commission was constituted in 1834 under the Charter Act of 1833, and its chairman was Thomas Babington Macaulay, who held the position of first Law Member in the government of India. The pairing of the man and the moment deserves attention, because the draft that emerged bore the marks of both. Macaulay arrived with a reputation formed in Parliament and in letters, but the task before the Commission was not literary. It was to reduce the criminal law of a subcontinent to a single ordered text that the new all India legislature could enact and that officials across the territory could use. That required a particular kind of ability. The drafter had to hold the whole field of criminal liability in view at once, to decide what belonged in the code and what could be left out, to arrange the material so that general principles were not repeated under every offence, and to write each rule in language plain enough to survive translation, distance, and inexperienced administration. Macaulay, as chairman, gave the project its intellectual direction and its confidence. The draft Penal Code submitted in 1837, only a few years after the Commission began its work, was the product of that direction, prepared from 1834 to 1837 as a complete proposal rather than as a series of disconnected amendments to existing law.

The most consequential decision taken by Macaulay and his colleagues was to write a complete and self contained code instead of a digest of the law as they found it. A digest gathers existing rules, arranges them, and leaves their authority where it was, in the older sources from which they were taken. A code does something more demanding. It states the law itself, in its own words, and invites the reader to look to the code as the governing text. Anyone who has worked through the criminal statutes of England in that period will appreciate the difference. English criminal law had grown by accumulation over centuries, in statutes passed for particular occasions, in language that assumed a reader already familiar with earlier enactments and with the common law that surrounded them. To transplant that accumulation into India, tidied and indexed, would have preserved its obscurities while removing the professional culture that made those obscurities manageable in England. Macaulay chose the harder course. The draft would cover the field as a whole, define its own terms, state its own exceptions, and leave as little as possible to be supplied from outside. Completeness of that kind is an ambition that exposes a drafter to criticism from every direction, because any gap can be pointed to as a failure and any inclusion can be questioned as unnecessary. It is nevertheless the only ambition equal to the Indian problem as the Commission had diagnosed it, since a partial code would have sent officials back to the old mixture of sources for everything the code omitted, and the mixture was the disease the code was meant to cure.

The method by which that completeness was made workable can be seen in the treatment of definitions. In the draft, a term that carries legal weight is defined once, in general provisions, and that single definition then governs wherever the term appears throughout the code. The alternative, defining the same expression afresh in each chapter or allowing its meaning to shift with context, multiplies words without adding certainty and creates opportunities for inconsistency that a court will later be asked to resolve. A single definition, carefully framed, does the opposite work. It forces the drafter to decide at the outset what the term must cover and what it must exclude, and it gives the reader a fixed point of reference against which every later use can be tested. The same economy governs the arrangement of mental elements in criminal liability. Where an offence turns on intention, knowledge, or dishonesty, the code states the relevance of those states of mind in the definition of the offence itself, using expressions whose meaning has already been settled in the general part. The reader therefore moves through the code in an order the code itself supplies, from the general to the particular, and each new provision builds on language already learned instead of introducing a fresh vocabulary for familiar ideas.

Still more distinctive was the use of illustrations placed after sections to show how a rule works on facts. An illustration takes the abstract statement of a section and applies it to a short imagined case, stating whether the conduct described falls within the rule or outside it. The device answers a weakness that every general rule, however carefully drafted, retains. Words that seem clear to the drafter may divide at the edges, and the first difficult case will often arise in a district far from any superior court, before an official who cannot postpone the decision until guidance arrives. An illustration narrows that area of doubt in advance. It shows the kind of case the drafter had in mind, marks a boundary by example where language alone might leave the boundary uncertain, and teaches the method of the code at the same time as it clarifies the particular section. For a jurisdiction expected to have few trained lawyers, illustrations performed work that in England was performed by a large profession, by reported decisions circulating among practitioners, and by the shared habits of a small legal world. India could not be assumed to possess any of that apparatus in sufficient measure. The code therefore had to carry its own teaching within its own pages, and the illustrations were the means by which it did so without swelling into a treatise.

A similar economy appears in the treatment of the general exceptions. Every system of criminal law recognises circumstances in which an act that matches the definition of an offence is nevertheless not punishable, because it was done by a person who lacked the capacity to understand it, or under a compulsion the law regards as excusing it, or in the exercise of a right of defence that the law itself protects. In uncodified law those excusing circumstances lie scattered across the law of each separate offence, repeated in varied language, with the constant risk that an exception recognised for one offence will be overlooked for another to which it ought equally to apply. The draft gathered those circumstances into general exceptions stated once for all offences. The gain was twofold. The code became shorter and more consistent, since a principle stated once could be stated carefully and applied uniformly. The law also became fairer in its structure, because exculpation no longer depended on whether the drafter of a particular provision had remembered to insert the saving words. A person entitled to the benefit of an exception held that benefit across the code as a whole, and the court considering the case began from the same set of general principles whatever the charge happened to be.

The celebrated clarity of the draft, often contrasted with the English criminal statutes of the era, was therefore not a stylistic accident or a product of Macaulay's literary gifts alone, welcome as those gifts were to the enterprise. It was a deliberate response to the conditions under which the code would be used. English statutes could afford a degree of technicality because they were addressed to a compact profession, working in one language, within reach of superior courts whose decisions were reported and read. A code for India was addressed to a far more dispersed audience. The draft accordingly favoured short and direct statements of rule, ordinary words used in a settled sense, and a structure that showed the reader where a provision stood in relation to the provisions around it. Clarity on this view is not the opposite of precision. It is precision made usable, the discipline of saying exactly what the rule is in words that the person who must act on the rule can grasp without an intermediary.

No drafter works in isolation from the materials of his time, and Macaulay drew on more than one tradition in shaping the draft. English criminal law supplied the largest body of substance, as might be expected in a code prepared for a British administered territory by a commission working within British institutions, and many of the offences and distinctions in the draft have English antecedents that a contemporary English lawyer would have recognised. But the draft was not a copy of English law, and its makers looked elsewhere for both substance and method. The French penal code offered an example of criminal law arranged as a systematic code, with offences classified and penalties stated in an ordered scheme, demonstrating that the field could be presented as a coherent whole rather than as a chronological pile of enactments. From Edward Livingston's code prepared for Louisiana came a further example of codification undertaken for a jurisdiction whose law drew on more than one tradition, and where the drafter had therefore been compelled to state principles generally and plainly for readers who could not be assumed to share a single legal education. The value of those influences lay less in any individual provision borrowed from them than in the proof they supplied that codification itself was possible. Macaulay could point, in effect, to work already done elsewhere and argue that a complete penal code for India was a practical undertaking with precedents, not a theorist's fantasy imposed on an unwilling administration.


## From draft to statute: the long gap

A draft submitted in 1837 might have been expected to reach the statute book within a few years, and the history of the Indian Penal Code is in large part the history of why it did not. The draft slept for over two decades. That long interval is sometimes treated as a simple story of official neglect, and neglect no doubt played its part in a government whose attention was claimed by revenue, by war, by administration, and by the ordinary pressure of business that could not wait. But delay in the case of a penal code has causes that go deeper than inattention. A code differs from an ordinary statute in the completeness of its claim. An ordinary statute can be enacted, tested, and amended without disturbing the surrounding law very greatly. A code that purports to state the whole of the criminal law asks the legislature to commit itself at once on hundreds of connected questions, each of which will govern prosecutions from the day of commencement. The very qualities that made the draft impressive, its completeness, its systematic arrangement, and its departures from familiar English forms, therefore also made it formidable to enact. Officials and lawyers who agreed that India needed a code could still disagree, in good faith and at length, about whether this code said the right thing in the right words on the questions it covered.

Revision, when it came, was placed in the hands of Barnes Peacock as Law Member, and the character of that revision tells its own story about the draft it examined. Peacock did not discard the work of Macaulay and the First Law Commission and begin again from an empty page. The draft was revised, considered, and prepared for enactment on the foundation that had been laid in the work submitted in 1837. That continuity matters for any judgment about the respective shares of the two men in the finished code. Macaulay supplied the design, the method, and the greater part of the substance. Peacock and those who worked in the revising stage supplied the scrutiny of a later generation, testing the draft against administrative experience, removing or adjusting what further reflection showed to be defective, and bringing the text to the point at which a legislature could responsibly enact it. A code that survives such revision with its structure intact has passed a severe test. It has shown that its arrangement was not a personal idiosyncrasy of its first drafter, intelligible only to him, but a framework within which other lawyers could work, argue, and improve the detail while accepting the plan. The long gap, seen in that light, was not wholly wasted time. It allowed the draft to be read by men who had not written it, in the light of years of governing the territory it was meant to serve, and it left the code stronger at enactment than a hastier passage would have done, even though the population of India lived under the old patchwork for a generation longer than the Commission had intended.

What finally converted a revised draft into a statute was a change in the political conditions surrounding the project, and the rebellion of 1857 stands at the centre of that change. The rebellion shook the foundations of British rule in India and forced a searching reconsideration of the instruments by which that rule was exercised. Among the lessons the state drew from the crisis was the value, and indeed the necessity, of a uniform criminal law administered consistently across its territory. A government that had experienced how quickly disorder could spread from district to district had a sharpened interest in knowing precisely what conduct it could punish, under what definitions, and with what penalties, without depending on the accidents of local law that the old system had preserved. Uniformity after 1857 was therefore not only a reformer's ideal inherited from the work of the 1830s. It had become a practical concern of a state examining its own security and its own coherence. That shift in official interest did not by itself draft a single section, and the intellectual work remained the work done from 1834 to 1837 and in the revision that followed. But enactment is a political act as well as a legal one, and a legislature enacts a large and difficult measure when those who govern feel the cost of leaving the matter undone. After 1857, the cost of the patchwork was felt in a new way, and the balance of argument moved decisively in favour of completing the project.

The completion came on 6 October 1860, when the code was enacted as Act 45 of 1860. Commencement followed after an interval, and the code came into force on 1 January 1862, so that administrators and courts had time to prepare for the change from the old sources to the new text. In its enacted form the code contained 511 sections arranged in 23 chapters, a structure that reflects the design choices made at the drafting stage. The general provisions and definitions come first, so that the reader learns the language of the code before meeting the offences expressed in that language. The general exceptions stand in their own place, applicable across the offences that follow. The substantive offences are then grouped by the interest they protect, allowing a reader who knows the nature of the harm alleged to find the relevant provisions without searching the whole text. This was not a statute about one offence or one class of offences. It was the criminal law of a subcontinent, stated between the covers of one Act, intended to be consulted as a whole and amended as a whole, and its size is the measure of the fragmentation it replaced.

The career of the code after enactment supplies the strongest evidence of the quality of its design, because the code travelled. It was carried across the empire to jurisdictions whose conditions differed considerably from those of India, and codes modelled on it were adopted in places such as the Straits Settlements and Ceylon. A legal text does not travel in that way merely because an imperial government wishes it to. Officials in a receiving jurisdiction must be able to read it, to train others in its use, and to apply it to local facts without finding that its provisions assume institutions or habits that exist only in the country of its origin. The Indian code travelled well precisely because of the features that had been built into it for Indian conditions. Its definitions were stated within the text rather than assumed from a surrounding common law tradition. Its illustrations showed administrators how its rules bore on concrete cases. Its general exceptions and general principles were gathered in one place instead of being left to inference from decided cases that a distant jurisdiction would never receive. A territory with few trained lawyers, which described a great many places to which the code or its models were carried, could take the text and begin work with it. Portability, in short, was not an accidental byproduct of the design. It was the design, tested in a new setting each time the code was adopted elsewhere.

The same design explains the treatment of the code at the end of empire. When British rule ended, the successor states were free to discard the penal code as an instrument of a departed power, and the decision each of them took is instructive. Pakistan and Bangladesh retained the code after independence, as India itself did, rather than treating its origin as a reason to sweep it away and begin criminal law afresh. Retention on that scale, across states whose politics diverged sharply in other respects, points to a distinction that lawyers and legislators in each country were able to draw between the authority that had enacted the code and the usefulness of the text itself. By the time of independence, generations of judges, lawyers, and administrators had been trained in its language, its categories structured the criminal business of the courts, and its provisions had been tested against an enormous variety of facts. The code endured because it worked, and it worked because the decisions taken by Macaulay in the draft submitted in 1837, on completeness, on definitions stated once, on illustrations, and on general exceptions, had produced a text that officials could actually use. The long history from the Charter Act of 1833 to enactment in 1860, and the longer history of retention and imitation that followed, is therefore a single story about the value of stating criminal law clearly, completely, and in one place, for a territory whose earlier law had been none of those things.

## The architecture of the Indian Penal Code

The Indian Penal Code is built like a machine whose parts are meant to be read in a fixed order, and much of its durability comes from that order. The reader does not begin with murder or theft. The reader begins with territory and jurisdiction, moves next to a controlled vocabulary in which the recurring words of the Code are defined once, passes then to the punishments the courts may award and to the defences that answer every charge, and only after that groundwork reaches the substantive offences. The sequence explains why a definition near the front can decide a trial about an act described hundreds of sections later. The architecture is not decoration.

Chapter I, the preliminary chapter in Sections 1 to 5, fixes the field on which everything else operates. It gives the Code its title and extent, states that acts and omissions within India are punishable under it, addresses offences committed outside India by persons nevertheless liable under Indian law, extends the Code to certain extra territorial offences, and saves special and local laws from displacement. Jurisdiction is the first question in any prosecution, and the saving explains why the Code, for all its breadth, was never the whole of Indian criminal law, since Parliament and the state legislatures remained free to create offences elsewhere.

Chapter II, the general explanations in Sections 6 to 52A, is the dictionary and grammar of the Code. Definitions given here carry the sense assigned to them wherever they afterwards appear, and the general explanations direct that expressions in the Code be understood subject to the general exceptions. The chapter settles, among much else, that person includes a company or association or body of persons, whether incorporated or not, and it defines document and valuable security and explains counterfeit. Injury denotes any harm whatever illegally caused to a person in body, mind, reputation, or property. Dishonestly describes an intention to cause wrongful gain to one person or wrongful loss to another, and fraudulently an intention to defraud. Reason to believe is pitched below knowledge, requiring sufficient cause to believe without certainty, and good faith is defined through its absence, since nothing is done or believed in good faith which is done or believed without due care and attention.

The decision to define these words once and let the definitions travel silently through all 511 sections is the most consequential piece of drafting economy in the Code. It removes repetition on a grand scale, because the framers did not have to explain in the theft sections, the cheating sections, the forgery sections, and the mischief sections, over and over again, what dishonesty means each time the word appears. It also fixes meaning across offences, so that the same word cannot drift into one sense for property crime and another for offences relating to documents unless the context or a special provision displaces the general explanation. It makes the Code teachable as well, because a learner who has mastered the general explanations, the punishments, and the exceptions already holds the keys to offences not yet read.

Chapter III, in Sections 53 to 75, turns from meaning to consequence and collects the punishments to which offenders are liable, together with rules on how sentences are computed, limited, and in defined circumstances altered. The placing matters, because it signals that sentencing is not improvised crime by crime but is a system with its own principles, covering commutation, fractions of terms, the limit of punishment where one act falls within several definitions, solitary confinement and its limits, and enhanced punishment after certain previous convictions.

### How were the punishments in the Indian Penal Code graded?

The Code graded punishments by attaching a stated maximum, and often a stated kind of imprisonment, to each offence rather than by publishing one tariff for all crime, leaving judges to choose within the range Parliament set for that offence, with death and life imprisonment reserved for the gravest categories of wrongdoing within the scheme.

Section 53 named the punishments, and the list records both the penal imagination of the nineteenth century and the changes worked upon it afterwards. The punishments were death, imprisonment for life, imprisonment of either description for a term, being rigorous imprisonment, with hard labour, or simple imprisonment, without it, forfeiture of property, and fine. The original list also carried transportation, a punishment rooted in the removal of convicts across the sea to penal settlements, which is why older judgments speak of transportation for life. Transportation was replaced by imprisonment for life in 1955, by Act 26 of 1955.

The grading logic is offence by offence rather than tariff by tariff, and that choice shapes everything about sentencing under the Code. Parliament attached to each offence its own punishment, or its own choice between punishments, so the gravity judgment is embedded in the individual section and must be read there. One offence may be punishable with imprisonment of either description for a term extending to a stated number of years, or with fine, or with both. Another may carry imprisonment for life or a term of years, and a third may be punishable with death in the alternative, reserving the extreme sentence for the offences treated as gravest while leaving the court a lesser option on the facts.

Sections 54 and 55 qualify that structure from above. They empower the appropriate government, in defined circumstances and without the consent of the offender, to commute a sentence of death for another punishment provided by the Code, and to commute a sentence of imprisonment for life for imprisonment of either description for a term not exceeding fourteen years. The general structure also presumes judicial choice between alternatives wherever Parliament left alternatives open, and the presumption holds except where Parliament removed the choice by prescribing a single course. Sentencing discretion under the Code is therefore neither free nor absent. It is discretion inside a graded frame, exercised offence by offence.

After the general chapters, the Code completes its machinery of defence and secondary liability before the catalogue of offences begins, since Chapter IV states the general exceptions in Sections 76 to 106, Chapter V deals with abetment in Sections 107 to 120, and Chapter VA contains criminal conspiracy in Sections 120A and 120B. The substantive chapters then move from the collective to the individual. Chapter VI contains offences against the State, and Chapter VIII offences against the public tranquillity. Chapters on offences by or relating to public servants follow, together with contempts of the lawful authority of public servants, and Chapter XI takes up false evidence and offences against public justice. Offences relating to coin and government stamps, offences relating to weights and measures, and offences affecting public health, safety, convenience, decency, and morals extend protection to the shared instruments and conditions of common life, and Chapter XV addresses offences relating to religion. Only after this public prelude does Chapter XVI, in Sections 299 to 377, reach offences affecting the human body, covering culpable homicide, murder, hurt, wrongful restraint and confinement, criminal force and assault, kidnapping and abduction, and the sexual offences. Chapter XVII, in Sections 378 to 462, protects property through theft, extortion, robbery and dacoity, criminal misappropriation, criminal breach of trust, receiving stolen property, cheating, fraudulent deeds, and mischief. Later chapters deal with offences relating to documents and property marks, the criminal breach of contracts of service, offences relating to marriage in Chapter XX, cruelty by a husband or the relatives of a husband in Chapter XXA, defamation in Chapter XXI, and criminal intimidation in Chapter XXII, before Chapter XXIII closes the Code with attempts, in the single Section 511.

## The general exceptions: the code inside the code

If the architecture of the Code has a centre of gravity, it lies in Chapter IV, and the decisive design choice for which Macaulay and the framers deserve their reputation is the choice to state the defences once, in general terms, so that they operate against every offence in the Code. A lesser draft might have repeated the excuses offence by offence, writing into the homicide sections that killing in self defence is no offence and into the hurt sections that harm caused by accident is not hurt, and the result would have been a code three times the length and a fraction of the coherence. The framers instead enacted a body of general exceptions, so that an act falling within an exception is not an offence at all, whichever chapter the charge is drawn from. The exceptions are a code inside the code, a second statement of the conditions under which conduct matching the words of an offence definition nevertheless attracts no liability.

The first family of exceptions protects acts done under legal duty or under a mistake of fact. Section 76 covers the act of a person who is, or who by reason of a mistake of fact and not by reason of a mistake of law in good faith believes himself to be, bound by law to do it, and Section 79 covers the act of a person who is justified by law, or who by reason of a mistake of fact in good faith believes himself to be justified by law, in doing it. The line drawn between mistake of fact and mistake of law is one of the most practical distinctions in criminal law. A mistake of fact, honestly made, may excuse, because it negates the guilty mind the offence requires or because the actor stood, as he supposed, within the law's own command. A mistake of law does not excuse, because the law cannot allow every person to plead a private misunderstanding of rules that bind everyone.

Sections 77 and 78 carry the same logic into the courtroom. Nothing is an offence which is done by a judge when acting judicially in the exercise of a power which is, or which in good faith the judge believes to be, given by law, and nothing is an offence which is done in pursuance of the judgment or order of a court whilst that judgment or order remains in force, even where the court had no jurisdiction to pass it, provided the person doing the act believes in good faith that the court had such jurisdiction. A judge who could be prosecuted for a judicial act, or an officer who could be prosecuted for executing an order later held invalid, would act under a paralysing fear, and the administration of justice depends on protecting acts done under judicial authority while appeal and review correct the error. Section 80 then states the defence of accident, under which nothing is an offence which is done by accident or misfortune, and without any criminal intention or knowledge, in the doing of a lawful act in a lawful manner by lawful means and with proper care and caution.

Section 81 states the principle of necessity, providing that nothing is an offence merely by reason of its being done with the knowledge that it is likely to cause harm, if it is done without any criminal intention to cause harm and in good faith for the purpose of preventing or avoiding other harm to person or property. Infancy is dealt with in two stages reflecting a graduated view of childhood responsibility. Section 82 gives absolute immunity to a child under seven years of age, treating such a child as incapable of crime, while Section 83 extends a qualified immunity to a child above seven and under twelve who has not attained sufficient maturity of understanding to judge the nature and consequences of the conduct. Liability in the borderline years therefore turns on the individual child's understanding rather than on age alone.

Section 84, the insanity exception, provides that nothing is an offence which is done by a person who, at the time of doing it, by reason of unsoundness of mind, is incapable of knowing the nature of the act, or that the act is either wrong or contrary to law. The section is built on the McNaghten rules, and its language has been read as enacting legal insanity rather than medical insanity. The distinction governs practice, because a medical witness may describe a condition in clinical terms and regard the accused as plainly unwell, yet the legal question remains the narrower one the section poses, whether unsoundness of mind at the moment of the act destroyed the capacity to know what was being done or to know that it was wrong or contrary to law. An accused who knew the nature of the act and knew that the law forbade it will therefore fail under the exception however disturbed the medical evidence shows him to have been.

Intoxication receives a divided treatment in Sections 85 and 86, reflecting the unwillingness of the law to let a person manufacture a defence from his own indulgence. Section 85 protects the person incapable of knowing the nature of the act, or that it is wrong or contrary to law, by reason of intoxication caused by something administered without his knowledge or against his will, so involuntary intoxication stands on a footing close to insanity. Section 86 addresses acts that are offences only when done with a particular knowledge or intent, and provides that where intoxication was voluntary, the accused is dealt with as if he had the knowledge he would have had if sober. In Basdev v State of PEPSU, the Supreme Court explained the working of Section 86 and drew the line that organised the later cases, holding that voluntary drunkenness is no excuse for the knowledge the law imputes to the sober person, but that evidence of intoxication may still be relevant when the prosecution must prove a specific intent.

Consent occupies Sections 87 to 91 and rests on the principle that the law does not treat as an offence certain harms to which the person affected has genuinely agreed. Section 87 covers an act not intended and not known to be likely to cause death or grievous hurt, done with the consent of a person above eighteen who has consented to suffer the harm or to take its risk. Section 88 protects an act not intended to cause death, done by consent in good faith for the person's benefit, the ground on which surgery and medical treatment stand, and Section 89 extends a parallel protection to acts done in good faith for the benefit of a child or a person of unsound mind by or with the consent of a guardian, hedged with conditions withholding protection from the intentional causing of death. Section 90 explains when consent is no consent in law, where it is given under fear of injury or under a misconception of fact known to the actor, or by a person who from unsoundness of mind or intoxication cannot understand the nature and consequence of the thing consented to, or by a person under twelve years of age. Section 91 provides that these exceptions do not extend to acts which are offences independently of any harm they cause, so consent cannot license conduct the law forbids for reasons of its own.

Section 92 generalises the benefit principle for cases in which consent cannot be obtained, protecting an act done in good faith for the benefit of a person without that person's consent where circumstances make consent impossible to signify or the person is incapable of giving it and has no guardian from whom consent can be obtained in time, again subject to provisos excluding the intentional causing of death. Section 93 provides that no communication made in good faith is an offence by reason of any harm to the person to whom it is made if it is made for the benefit of that person, the provision under which a doctor may break grave news to a patient. Section 94 deals with compulsion by threats, protecting an act done by a person compelled by threats which reasonably cause the apprehension that instant death will otherwise be the consequence, but the protection is withheld from murder and from offences against the State punishable with death, and from the person who placed himself in the situation of compulsion by his own choice. Section 95 enacts the maxim that the law does not concern itself with trifles, providing that nothing is an offence by reason of harm so slight that no person of ordinary sense and temper would complain of it.

The largest and most litigated part of Chapter IV is the right of private defence, stated in Sections 96 to 106, under which nothing is an offence which is done in the exercise of that right. The right extends to defence of the body and of property, whether one's own or another's, while Section 99 imposes the governing restrictions, that there is no right against an act which does not reasonably cause the apprehension of death or grievous hurt when done by a public servant acting in good faith under colour of office, that there is no right where there is time to have recourse to the protection of the public authorities, and that the right never extends to inflicting more harm than is necessary for the purpose of defence. Sections 100 and 103 mark the points at which the right extends even to causing death, Section 100 for assaults reasonably causing the apprehension of death or grievous hurt and for the other grave assaults the section enumerates against the body, and Section 103 for robbery, house breaking by night, mischief by fire, and theft, mischief, or house trespass in circumstances reasonably causing the apprehension that death or grievous hurt will otherwise follow, against property.

The burden of proof gives the exceptions their procedural force. The prosecution must prove the offence it charges, and that burden does not shift. But an accused who claims the benefit of an exception must establish it, and Section 105 of the Indian Evidence Act 1872 places the burden of proving the existence of circumstances bringing the case within an exception on the accused, with the court directed to presume the absence of such circumstances. The standard the accused must meet is a preponderance of probabilities, the standard of the civil law, not proof beyond reasonable doubt, so an accused who shows that his account of accident, private defence, or unsoundness of mind is more probable than not is entitled to the benefit of the exception even though doubts remain. Courts also read the exceptions with the offence definition rather than as afterthoughts, so the prosecution case is tested from the beginning against the possibility that a general exception answers it. This is why the exceptions function as part of the real definition of every offence.

## Liability in company: abetment, conspiracy, common intention and common object

Criminal law is written mostly in the singular, as if offences were committed by one person acting alone, but crime in the world is very often a group activity, and the Code meets that fact with a connected set of doctrines attaching liability to persons who did not themselves perform the final act. Chapter V, on abetment, makes the encourager and the helper liable before and alongside the principal. Chapter VA makes the agreement itself an offence in the shape of criminal conspiracy. Section 34 fastens on the group that acts in furtherance of a common intention, and Section 149, working through the definition of unlawful assembly in Section 141, fastens on the member of an assembly for offences committed in prosecution of its common object. The provisions overlap, but they are not interchangeable, because each rests on a different foundation.

Section 107 defines abetment through three modes. A person abets the doing of a thing who instigates any person to do that thing, or who engages with one or more other persons in any conspiracy for the doing of that thing if an act or illegal omission takes place in pursuance of that conspiracy and in order to the doing of that thing, or who intentionally aids, by any act or illegal omission, the doing of that thing. Intentional aiding is the quieter mode, covering the person who supplies the weapon, keeps watch, or drives the vehicle, provided the help is given with intention and not by mere presence or accident. The conspiracy mode of abetment, which requires an act in pursuance of the engagement, must be held apart from the separate offence of criminal conspiracy, because abetment by conspiracy is a way of participating in the thing done, while conspiracy as an offence is complete in the agreement itself.

### How did criminal conspiracy enter the Indian Penal Code?

Criminal conspiracy entered the Code in 1913, when a new Chapter VA inserted Sections 120A and 120B and made the agreement to commit an offence, or to do an illegal act by illegal means, punishable in itself, separate from abetment and separate from the offence the conspirators planned together.

Agreement was not absent from the original scheme, since Section 107 already treated engagement in a conspiracy as one mode of abetting a thing, but abetment by conspiracy remained tethered to the principal act, requiring something done in pursuance of the engagement and answering for the thing abetted rather than for the agreement as such. The insertion of Chapter VA in 1913 cut that tether. Section 120A defines criminal conspiracy as the agreement of two or more persons to do, or cause to be done, an illegal act, or an act which is not illegal by illegal means, with a proviso that an agreement merely to commit an offence needs nothing beyond the agreement itself, while an agreement falling outside that category requires some act besides the agreement by one of the parties in pursuance of it. Section 120B supplies the punishment, treating conspiracy to commit the gravest offences alongside the punishment provided for abetment of those offences, and conspiracy directed at lesser offences and illegal acts on a lesser scale. The agreement becomes the gist of the offence, so the crime is complete before the planned act begins and even where it never begins, and the prosecution proves it by proving the meeting of minds, usually from conduct and circumstance.

### What is the difference between abetment and criminal conspiracy under the Indian Penal Code?

Abetment under Section 107 is conduct that instigates, conspires in, or intentionally aids an offence, while criminal conspiracy under Section 120A is the agreement itself; abetment looks to encouragement or help given toward an act, conspiracy is complete once two or more persons agree to pursue the unlawful object together in concert.

The difference matters in pleading and in proof, and the two charges are frequently framed together precisely because they fail in different ways. An abetment charge asks what the accused did to bring the offence about, whether he urged it, engaged in a design that produced an act in pursuance, or intentionally helped it along, and the liability is secondary in character, measured by its connection to the thing abetted and to what was actually done in consequence. A conspiracy charge asks the anterior question whether the accused agreed with at least one other person upon the illegal act or the illegal means, and it can succeed where the substantive offence was never attempted and where no identifiable act of instigation or aid can be pinned on the individual accused, provided the agreement itself is proved against him. The overlap sits inside Section 107, where engagement in a conspiracy is one form of abetment, but that form requires an act or illegal omission in pursuance of the conspiracy and in order to the doing of the thing, which is the element the standalone offence discards for agreements to commit an offence.

Section 34 states the doctrine of common intention in words of famous economy. When a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all, each of such persons is liable for that act in the same manner as if it were done by him alone. The provision is not itself an offence and creates no punishment of its own. There must be a criminal act done by several persons, a common intention shared by all of them, and an act done in furtherance of that intention, so the person who happened to be present, or who shared only the occasion and not the purpose, stands outside the section. Where the conditions are met, the participant who stood at the door is liable for the killing inside the room as if he had struck the blow himself, because a group that acts on a shared intention has pooled its agency and Section 34 makes the pool answer as a single actor.

The Privy Council gave the doctrine its enduring image in Barendra Kumar Ghosh, decided in 1925, where the accused had stood outside a post office while his companions went in and shot the postmaster during a robbery, and where the argument was pressed that the man who had not fired could not be liable for the murder as his own act. The Board rejected the argument on the footing of Section 34, holding that the criminal act was the shooting done in furtherance of the common intention of all who took part in the enterprise, and it captured the position of the waiting participant in the line that they also serve who only stand and wait. The sentence has survived because it states the precise distinction the section enforces, between service to a common design, which attracts full liability, and mere presence in the vicinity of a crime, which does not.

Mahbub Shah, decided by the Privy Council in 1945, supplies the counterweight that keeps Section 34 within its proper bounds. The Board insisted that common intention implies a prior meeting of minds, a prearranged plan formed before the act, even if formed shortly before it, and that the intention of each participant must be known to and shared by the rest. The judgment drew the separation that has governed the doctrine since, between common intention and a merely similar intention, where two or more persons happen to entertain the same criminal purpose independently and act on it at the same time without prior concert. Similar intention is a coincidence of minds. Common intention is a meeting of minds, and only the meeting attracts Section 34.

Sections 141 and 149 construct the neighbouring doctrine of common object on a different and broader base. Section 141 defines an unlawful assembly as an assembly of five or more persons whose common object falls within the objects the section states, which include overawing by criminal force, or show of criminal force, the government or any public servant in the exercise of lawful power, resisting the execution of any law or legal process, committing mischief or criminal trespass or another offence, and obtaining possession of property, enforcing a supposed right, or compelling another to act by means of criminal force or its show. Section 149 then provides that if an offence is committed by any member of an unlawful assembly in prosecution of the common object of that assembly, or such as the members of that assembly knew to be likely to be committed in prosecution of that object, every person who at the time of the committing of that offence is a member of the same assembly is guilty of that offence. The contrasts with Section 34 are systematic. Section 34 has no minimum number and requires a common intention shared by all, proved as a prior meeting of minds directed at the act done. Section 149 requires five or more persons and an unlawful assembly, but the liability of the individual member rests on membership with knowledge, extending to offences the members knew to be likely in prosecution of the object, and it does not require proof that the individual member shared the intention to commit the particular offence or did any act toward it.

The reason Sections 34 and 149 carry so much of the real litigation under the Code is evidential before it is doctrinal. Group crime rarely reveals who struck which blow. A victim falls in a melee, a shop is looted by a crowd that arrived together and dispersed together, a killing follows a quarrel in which several persons were armed and moving at once, and witnesses who are frightened, partisan, or simply unable to see through the confusion can describe the event as a whole without assigning each injury to a hand. A criminal law that insisted in such cases on proof of the individual act of each accused would acquit the group as a group, because the very method of the crime dissolves individual attribution, and the doctrines of common intention and common object exist to prevent combination from becoming a technique of immunity. They decide cases because they convert the question from the unanswerable one of whose blow killed into the answerable ones of what design the group shared, what assembly it formed, and what its members knew was likely to happen.

## The offence chapters: from the state to reputation

The offence chapters are best read not as a catalogue but as an argument about what a criminal code is for, made in the order in which the harms are taken up. The movement is from the state outward and then inward, from war against the government to violence in a street, from the integrity of a courtroom to the integrity of a body, from property to paper, from marriage to reputation, and at the end to the attempt that never ripened into any of those harms. Grouped by function, the chapters protect distinct things: the state, public peace, the process by which disputes are decided and offices are filled, the body, possession, documents, marriage, reputation and peace of mind. The drafting habit is consistent. A term is defined with precision, the offence is stated, aggravated and mitigated forms are distinguished, and exceptions carve back what the definition, left alone, would have caught too widely.

Offences against the State, in Chapter VI, Sections 121 to 130, stand first because the harm they describe is prior to every other harm the Code punishes. A legal order cannot protect bodies or property if the order itself can be overthrown by force, and the chapter accordingly begins with waging war against the government, with conspiracy and preparation directed to that end, and with assaults upon the highest offices of the state. Their definitions turn less on private motive than on the public character of the act, on whether force is directed against the state as a state. Sedition had its place within this chapter, and its placement is instructive, since the Code treated words and representations that excited disaffection toward the government as an offence against the state rather than as a mere breach of the peace. The full history of that provision belongs to a later section of this article, and it is enough here to notice the logic of its home: placed in Chapter VI, sedition was classified by the interest it was said to injure.

Offences against public tranquillity, in Chapter VIII, move the focus from the existence of the state to the condition of public space. Unlawful assembly, rioting and affray are offences of combination and of place, not simply lesser versions of offences against the body. An assembly becomes unlawful by reference to its common object, and that shift from individual act to shared object is the analytical centre of the chapter. A crowd is a distinct source of danger because numbers change what participants will do, what victims can resist and what bystanders must fear. Rioting takes the unlawful assembly and adds force or violence in prosecution of the common object, so that the offence is complete through the collective act even before any particular injury is separated out under another chapter. Affray captures fighting in a public place that disturbs the public peace, a smaller disturbance that needs no elaborate common object. The logic is completed by Section 149, which provides that every member of an unlawful assembly is guilty of an offence committed by any member in prosecution of the common object, or such as the members knew to be likely to be committed in prosecution of that object. Membership, knowledge and common object do the work, on the justification that in concerted violence encouragement, presence and numbers are themselves forms of participation.

Two further families protect the machinery through which authority is exercised and contested. Offences relating to public servants address disobedience, obstruction, threats and impersonation connected with those who carry public functions, while offences relating to elections, in Chapter IXA, Sections 171A to 171I, inserted in 1920, give criminal form to a democratic process the original Code could not have described in the same terms. Chapter IXA supplied definitions and offences for bribery, undue influence, personation and related misconduct at elections, and its late arrival shows a framework into which new protected interests could be fitted without disturbing the chapters around it. Beside these stands Chapter XI, false evidence and offences against public justice, whose protected interest is neither a person nor a thing but a process. Perjury is not punished simply because a lie is wrongful. It is punished because adjudication depends upon testimony it cannot independently verify in every instance, and a false statement in a judicial proceeding corrodes the mechanism by which all other offences are tried. Its design, with attention to the stage of the proceeding, to materiality and to intention, shows the Code protecting its own conditions of operation, a concern that extends to screening offenders, destroying evidence, bringing false charges and resisting lawful apprehension. Where Chapter VI protects the state as sovereign, Chapter XI protects the state as judge.

Offences affecting the human body, in Chapter XVI, Sections 299 to 377, receive the deepest treatment in the Code and deserve the deepest treatment here, because it is in this chapter that the grading of intention and knowledge is worked out with the greatest refinement. Section 299 defines culpable homicide through three routes. A person commits culpable homicide by causing death by an act done with the intention of causing death, or with the intention of causing such bodily injury as is likely to cause death, or with the knowledge that the act is likely to cause death. The limbs are not interchangeable. Intention directed at death is the clearest case. Intention directed at bodily injury likely to cause death moves the inquiry from the result desired to the character of the injury chosen. Knowledge that death is likely moves it further, to foresight of a probable consequence without death being the object of the act. Section 299 therefore establishes a field broader than murder, within which Section 300 performs the work of selection. Culpable homicide is the genus, murder a species within it.

Section 300 states when culpable homicide is murder in four clauses. The first covers an act done with the intention of causing death. The second reaches bodily injury that the offender knows to be likely to cause the death of the particular person harmed, importing knowledge of that person and that person vulnerability. The third covers bodily injury intended to be inflicted that is sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death, shifting attention to the objective sufficiency of the intended injury. The fourth covers an act known to be so imminently dangerous that it must in all probability cause death, or such bodily injury as is likely to cause death, committed without excuse for incurring that risk. Together, the clauses treat intention, particular knowledge, objective sufficiency and extreme risk as each, in defined circumstances, equivalent in culpability to an intention to kill. That equivalence is a legislative judgment, not a natural fact, and much later case law is argument about where its boundaries fall. The section then carves back five exceptions, in which culpable homicide that would otherwise be murder is not murder, because provocation, excess in the exercise of a right of private defence, excess by a public servant acting in good faith, a sudden fight, or consent in the terms allowed changes the quality of the act. The exceptions are part of the definition of murder itself.

The classic explanation of the border between Section 299 and Section 300 remains the analysis of Melvill J in Reg v Govinda, decided in 1876. The question was what separated culpable homicide that was murder from culpable homicide that was not, when both provisions spoke in the language of likelihood. The answer was that the difference is one of degree. Under Section 299, the relevant injury or act is likely to cause death. Under the corresponding provisions of Section 300, the probability rises from likely to most probable. Melvill J worked through the sections clause by clause, comparing their language rather than treating murder as a moral category detached from the text, and that method is the enduring value of the judgment.

The third clause of Section 300 received its authoritative treatment in Virsa Singh v State of Punjab, decided in 1958, which reduced a clause capable of loose use to a sequence of inquiries. The clause applies where the act is done with the intention of causing bodily injury and the bodily injury intended to be inflicted is sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death. The prosecution must show that a bodily injury is present, that the nature of that injury is proved, that the accused intended to inflict that particular injury rather than causing it by accident or inflicting a different injury altogether, and that the injury so intended was sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death. The final element is objective. Its sufficiency is assessed by reference to what such an injury does in the ordinary course of nature, drawing where necessary on medical evidence, and not by asking whether the accused personally knew or desired that death would follow.

Exception 1 to Section 300, grave and sudden provocation, asks not what probability attended the act but what had happened to the self control of the person who acted. Culpable homicide is not murder where the offender, whilst deprived of the power of self control by grave and sudden provocation, causes the death of the person who gave the provocation, or of another person by mistake or accident. The provocation must be grave, judged by whether a reasonable person of the class to which the accused belongs would have been deprived of self control, and it must be sudden, so that the act follows upon provocation that has not had time to lose its force. The trial of K M Nanavati, arising from events between 1959 and 1961 and decided by the Supreme Court in 1962, turned this exception into the most widely discussed provocation case in Indian law. The accused, a naval officer, had learned from his wife of her intimacy with another man, had driven his family to a cinema, had gone to his ship and obtained a revolver, had driven to the other man flat and shot him, and had then given himself up. The Supreme Court treated the time that elapsed between provocation and killing as the space in which passion could cool, reasoning that the intervening conduct showed a mind that had had opportunity to regain self control rather than a mind still deprived of it. That reading still governs Exception 1, not because an interval automatically defeats the exception, but because Nanavati fixed the method: courts look at what the accused did in the interval, and at whether the acts were consistent with continuing loss of self control or with deliberation.

Punishment follows definition, and Section 302 supplies the punishment for murder as death or imprisonment for life, together with liability to fine. The text offers an alternative without stating the principle of choice, a silence that became consequential once the constitutionality of the death penalty was challenged. In Bachan Singh v State of Punjab, decided in 1980, the Supreme Court upheld the penalty but confined it to the rarest of rare cases, when the alternative option is unquestionably foreclosed. Sentencing courts were directed to weigh aggravating and mitigating circumstances relating both to the crime and to the criminal, so that death is not the ordinary consequence of falling within Section 300. Below homicide, Sections 319 to 338, covering hurt and grievous hurt, show the Code working as a scale. Hurt is bodily pain, disease or infirmity caused to another, and grievous hurt selects specified kinds of injury for heavier punishment by reference to permanence and seriousness, to destruction or impairment of organs and members, to fracture, and to hurt that endangers life or causes severe bodily pain or inability to follow ordinary pursuits for the period the section specifies. Distinctions follow from means used, dangerous weapons, provocation, intention and knowledge. The rest of Chapter XVI fills out bodily autonomy. Wrongful restraint and wrongful confinement punish interference with freedom to proceed where a person has a right to proceed, confinement extending the wrong by total restraint within circumscribed limits. Assault and criminal force address apprehension and application of force short of hurt as defined. Kidnapping and abduction, defined by taking or enticing from lawful guardianship and by compelling movement by force or deceitful means, protect custody and liberty together.

Property offences, in Chapter XVII, Sections 378 to 462, reward close definition, because ordinary speech merges conduct that the Code separates, with consequences for charge, trial and punishment. Theft is defined through dishonest intention and through movable property taken out of the possession of another without consent. Dishonest intention must accompany the taking, and that emphasis becomes an organising principle for the whole chapter. Extortion places a person in fear of injury and thereby dishonestly induces delivery of property, so that consent is produced by threat rather than removed by stealth or force. Robbery is an aggravated form in which theft or extortion meets violence or the threat of immediate violence, and dacoity aggravates robbery further by the number of persons conjointly committing or attempting it. Criminal misappropriation addresses dishonest conversion of property that has come into a person possession without the initial taking being theft, while criminal breach of trust adds entrustment or dominion over property and dishonest misappropriation or use in violation of a direction of law or a legal contract. Cheating, defined from Section 415 onwards, turns on deception that dishonestly or fraudulently induces delivery of property or the doing or omission of an act likely to cause damage or harm. The distinction between criminal breach of trust and cheating turns on when dishonest intention arises. In cheating, it is present at the inception, when the representation is made and the other party is induced to part with property. In breach of trust, property is received without that initial dishonest inducement, entrustment follows, and dishonesty arises afterwards in conversion or misuse.

The remaining property offences protect possession and enjoyment as much as ownership in the abstract. Receiving stolen property punishes the market and the shelter that make theft profitable, requiring knowledge or reason to believe that the property is stolen. Mischief causes wrongful loss or damage by destroying property or diminishing its value or utility, an offence framed around effect on property rather than around taking it. Criminal trespass enters or remains upon property in the possession of another with intent to commit an offence or to intimidate, insult or annoy the person in possession, and its aggravated forms, including house trespass and house breaking, reflect the special protection given to dwellings. The chapters therefore distinguish taking from receiving, deception from conversion, damage from entry, and fear from force, and grade each by intention and circumstance.

Documents and property marks, in Chapter XVIII, extend protection into the world of paper and signs, and forgery is the centre of the family. A false document is defined through the making of a document or part of a document with specified intentions, and forgery is built upon that foundation by the intent that the false document be believed to be genuine or be acted upon as genuine, with the further intentions the section requires in relation to deception, damage, support of a claim, parting with property or entry into a contract. The Code does not punish falsity in the abstract. It punishes making a false document with the intention that reliance be placed upon it, because reliance is the mechanism by which documents move property, establish rights and persuade institutions to act.

Marriage offences, in Chapter XX, Sections 493 to 498, cover deceitful inducement of a belief in lawful marriage, bigamy and its aggravated forms, fraudulent marriage ceremony, adultery as the Code then framed it, and enticing or taking away or detaining a married woman with the criminal intention the section specifies. Chapter XXA added protection within marriage, directed at conduct inside the household rather than at the validity of the marriage itself. Section 498A, inserted in 1983, made cruelty by a husband or by the relatives of a husband a specific offence, cruelty being defined to include wilful conduct likely to drive a woman to suicide or to cause grave injury or danger to her life, limb or health, and harassment connected with an unlawful demand for property or valuable security. Section 304B, inserted in 1986, created dowry death, where the death of a woman is caused by burns or bodily injury or occurs otherwise than under normal circumstances within the period and in the conditions the section lays down, and it is shown that soon before her death she was subjected to cruelty or harassment in connection with a demand for dowry. The provision deems the husband or relative who subjected the woman to that cruelty or harassment to have caused her death when the other elements are established, a strong response to deaths in the home where direct evidence of the fatal act was peculiarly difficult to obtain. The misuse argument around Section 498A, that a provision enacted for protection has in some cases been invoked in matrimonial conflict in ways its critics describe as oppressive and its defenders describe as the ordinary contest that surrounds any effective remedy, is a live debate treated in depth elsewhere in this series, and it is recorded here in that balanced form without resolution.

Defamation, in Chapter XXI, Sections 499 and 500, protects reputation, an interest less tangible than the body and less easily measured than property. Section 499 defines defamation through the making or publishing of an imputation concerning a person by words, signs or visible representations, with the intention of harming, or knowledge or reason to believe that the imputation will harm, the reputation of that person, and explanations work out imputations concerning the dead, concerning companies and collections of persons, concerning statements in the alternative and concerning irony. Section 500 supplies the punishment. The defining feature, however, is its ten exceptions in Section 499, under which imputations that would otherwise fall within the definition are not defamation. Truth published for the public good, public conduct of public servants, conduct in relation to public questions, reports of court proceedings, comment on cases and on public performances, censure by persons in authority, accusations made in good faith to authorised persons, and cautions intended for good together map the space left for public discussion, official duty, complaint and criticism. Chapter XXII, criminal intimidation, insult and annoyance, then addresses threats intended to cause alarm and insults intended to provoke a breach of the peace. Finally Section 511, the general attempt provision, closes the Code by punishing attempts to commit offences punishable with imprisonment where no express provision for the attempt has been made elsewhere and where an act has been done toward the commission of the offence. Its closing placement is itself a design statement about completeness: specific attempt provisions were enacted where thought necessary, and a residual rule then catches what the specific chapters had left out.

## Whom the Code bound, and where

A code is defined as much by the persons and the territory it reaches as by the offences it creates. Section 1, as enacted, extended the Code to British India, the territory administered directly through the provinces and presidencies that made up that legal geography. That extent was never the whole of the subcontinent. After independence, the Code extended to the whole of India except the State of Jammu and Kashmir, a formula that preserved a single national penal code while recognising the special constitutional position that state occupied. Jammu and Kashmir kept its own Ranbir Penal Code, modelled on the Indian Penal Code and dating from 1932, similar in structure and in much of its substance but a separate enactment operating in that state in place of the Indian code. The position ended with the reorganisation of 2019, under which the Indian Penal Code was brought into force in Jammu and Kashmir from 31 October 2019. From that date the territorial formula described, for the first time since independence, a code extending across the whole of India without that exception.

Section 2 states the core of territorial liability, providing for the punishment of offences committed within India, so that every person who commits an act or omission contrary to the Code within India is liable under it. The place of the offence, rather than nationality or domicile, founds liability, and the foreigner who offends within India is bound in the same way as a resident. Sections 3 and 4 then reach outward. Section 3 provides that a person liable by any Indian law to be tried for an offence committed beyond India shall be dealt with according to the Code for an act committed beyond India in the same manner as if the act had been committed within India, operating where some other Indian law creates the liability. Section 4 extends the Code to offences committed by a citizen of India in any place without and beyond India, to offences on a ship or aircraft registered in India wherever it may be, and to offences targeting a computer resource located in India. Each limb rests on a different principle: nationality, the treatment of registered ships and aircraft as carrying the law of their registration, and the location of the target, since an act done abroad may produce its intended harm upon a system physically in India.

Section 5 qualifies everything that precedes it. It provides that nothing in the Code is intended to repeal, vary, suspend or affect any provision of any Act for punishing mutiny and desertion of officers, soldiers, sailors or airmen in the service of the government, or of any special or local law. The Code therefore never claimed to be the whole of criminal law in India, even within its territory. Military offences continued under their own disciplinary codes, special laws created offences of their own beside the Code, and local laws continued to operate where Section 5 saved them. A person reading the Code alone would therefore have an incomplete account of criminal liability in force at any given place and time, and that incompleteness was deliberate. The project had been a general penal code, not a consolidation of every penal provision in existence, and Section 5 preserves the boundary between the general and the special, a boundary whose importance grew as legislatures enacted more special statutes.

Within the territory of the Code, a further qualification arose from the federal distribution of legislative power, since criminal law sits in the Concurrent List, open to legislation by both Parliament and the state legislatures, with the constitutional rules on repugnancy and Presidential assent governing conflict between the two. State legislatures amended the Code for their own states across the decades, altering definitions, punishments and classification of offences in application to their territories, sometimes with Presidential assent obtained so that the state amendment would prevail within that state notwithstanding a repugnancy with the central enactment. The result was a single national code that did not read identically in every state, and a practitioner had to check state amendments before advising on the law in force in the place of the offence. This is the predictable consequence of placing criminal law in the Concurrent List, a choice that valued the ability of states to respond to local conditions and local patterns of offending within a common national framework. State amendments worked upon the shared architecture at particular points rather than replacing it, so that the law remained recognisably one code even where its details diverged.

The original map was incomplete for a different reason, namely the constitutional geography of India at commencement. The princely states stood outside British India for the purpose of Section 1, under their own rulers and their own legal arrangements, connected to the paramount power by treaty and political supervision but not governed by legislation enacted for British India. Many of those states worked with codes of their own modelled on Macaulay draft or on the enacted Code itself, or applied the Indian code in a modified form through their own adoption, so that the substance of the scheme travelled well beyond the territory to which the Code applied by its own force. A princely state might reproduce the definitions and punishments of the Indian Penal Code almost exactly and yet administer them as its own law, under its own courts, so that a map coloured only by legal force and a map coloured by intellectual influence would show two different pictures. That is why the Code map filled in by accession rather than by its own force. As princely states acceded to the Indian Union after independence and were integrated into its constitutional structure, the Code extended to their territories through that process of integration, not through any dormant provision in Section 1.

The persons bound form a wider class than a first reading of a territorial code might suggest. The Code binds the foreigner who offends within India, because Section 2 rests liability on the place of the act, and the citizen who offends abroad, because Section 4 rests liability on nationality for the offences it covers. It binds persons on Indian registered ships and aircraft far from Indian soil, and a person abroad whose offence targets a computer resource in India. It did not, by Section 5, displace military law or a special or local law that validly covered a matter, and its provisions might reach a resident of one state in a form amended for that state and a resident of another in the unamended form. None of this alters the central fact about everyday operation. Its everyday subject was and is the ordinary resident, whose disputes, injuries, takings, threats and acts of violence made up the overwhelming bulk of cases in which the Code was invoked, far removed from questions of war, elections or extraterritorial jurisdiction. Uniformity was always a uniformity with stated exceptions, for a state with its own code, for special and local laws, for military law, for state amendments and, at the beginning, for the princely states outside British India altogether. The achievement of the Code was not that it abolished those exceptions, but that it gave the territory it did cover a single, systematic, definitional criminal law where a patchwork had operated before.

## A long life in amendments

A code that survives for more than a century and a half does not survive by standing still, and the Indian Penal Code is best understood not as a fixed text handed down intact from 1860 but as a structure that Parliament, and before Parliament the colonial legislature, returned to again and again, inserting, replacing and recasting provisions while leaving the frame around them largely undisturbed. The Code that the Republic inherited in 1950 was already a heavily worked document, and the amendments made to it before independence show how early the habit of insertion began and how varied the pressures behind it were. Section 153A, the provision on promoting enmity between different groups, was inserted in 1898 amid communal tension, and its presence in the chapter concerned with offences against public tranquillity shows the legislature using the Code to answer a problem of public order that the original drafters had not framed in quite that form. Criminal conspiracy became a standalone offence in 1913, a change of real doctrinal weight, because it moved liability away from the completed act and towards agreement itself, allowing the law to reach combinations and preparations that older provisions could touch only once they had ripened into an attempt or an abetment. Election offences arrived as Chapter IXA in 1920, and their arrival marks another kind of expansion, the Code being asked to protect a political process that was itself still developing, so that the integrity of voting and candidature took its place alongside the protection of life, body and property. The punishment of transportation disappeared in 1955, when imprisonment for life replaced it, and that substitution closed a chapter of penal practice that belonged to an earlier imperial geography, in which removal across the sea had been a central instrument of punishment. Each of these changes altered what the Code did, and none of them required the Code to be rebuilt, and that combination of substantial change and structural continuity is the pattern that the whole amendment history repeats.

The character of amendment changed after independence, and the change is visible less in the frequency of intervention than in the reasons given for it. Colonial amendments had often served the administrative needs of the state that made them, extending control over combination, over elections and over communal feeling in ways that protected the governing order as much as they protected the public. Amendments in the Republic increasingly responded to identified social harms and to committee and court pressure, to harms named in public argument, examined by commissions and committees, and pressed upon the legislature by movements, by judgments and by events that made an existing gap impossible to defend. The direction of travel matters because it explains where the new provisions were placed and what work they were expected to do. Where the nineteenth century legislature had reached most readily for offences against the state and against public order, the later legislature reached into the household, into the workplace of the body, and into forms of violence that the original Code had either treated thinly or left to general provisions on hurt and assault that did not name the harm at all. The amendment record of the Code is therefore also a record of which harms the Republic learned to name, and in what order, and at what cost in delay.

No part of that record shows the shift more plainly than the dowry and cruelty amendments of the 1980s. Section 498A was inserted in 1983 and created the offence of cruelty by a husband or his relatives towards a married woman, and Section 304B followed in 1986 and created the offence of dowry death. Between them the two provisions moved the criminal law inside the household in a way that the code Macaulay drafted had not attempted, because the original architecture had treated the home largely as a private space whose internal relations were governed elsewhere, and had punished violence within it mainly through the same general offences that applied to violence between strangers. The new provisions rejected that neutrality of form. They identified a pattern in which economic demand, family pressure and physical or mental cruelty were bound together, and in which death could follow a course of conduct that a stranger model of assault described badly. Section 498A made cruelty itself, within a defined relationship, the substance of an offence, rather than a background circumstance to some other charge. Section 304B addressed the gravest end of the same pattern, the death of a woman in connection with dowry demand, and in doing so it recognised that certain deaths had to be read in the light of the demands that preceded them. The significance of the pair lies in their combined method. One provision reaches the continuing conduct, the other reaches the fatal outcome, and together they treat the household not as a refuge from the criminal law but as a place where its protection was most needed.

The sexual offences reforms of 2013 tested it most severely, because they arrived in the aftermath of a crime that had shaken public confidence in the adequacy of the existing law and because the process that produced them was unusually public and unusually rapid. The reforms were enacted after the Delhi gang rape of December 2012 and the report of the committee chaired by Justice J. S. Verma, and the resulting statute, examined in detail in [the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act 2013 and what it changed](/2011/02/15/criminal-law-amendment-act-2013/), rewrote the sexual offence provisions of the Code rather than merely raising their punishments. The definition in Section 375 was expanded, so that the offence of rape described a wider range of acts than the older language had captured, and the expansion mattered because definitions in a penal code decide the boundary of liability before any question of sentence arises. Around that rewritten centre the amendment inserted new offences, including acid attacks in Sections 326A and 326B, and sexual harassment, disrobing, voyeurism and stalking in Sections 354A to 354D, together with a recast trafficking provision in Section 370. The shape of that list repays attention. By naming each conduct separately, the amendment graded harms that a single general provision would have flattened, and it allowed punishment, procedure and public understanding to follow the description. The Verma Committee connection is part of the explanation for that method, since a committee asked to review the law as a whole is more likely to propose a scheme than a single clause, and the legislature, acting under intense pressure, adopted much of the scheme while also moving beyond it in matters of punishment. The result was the most extensive single rewriting of a part of the Code in its independent history, and it was still, in form, an amendment, a set of substitutions and insertions fitted into chapters that already existed.

The amendment of 2018 moved in the other direction along the same axis, changing severity more than definition. It raised punishments for rape, including death for the rape of a girl below twelve years of age, and in that provision the legislature placed its emphasis on the symbolic and deterrent weight of the maximum sentence. Read beside the reforms of 2013, the later amendment shows the two levers that amendment of the Code made available and the difference between them. The first lever is descriptive, widening a definition or naming a new offence, and it changes what the law can reach. The second lever is punitive, raising a minimum or a maximum, and it changes what the law threatens once its reach is settled. The reforms of 2013 pulled the first lever hardest, though they adjusted punishments as well, while the amendment of 2018 pulled the second lever almost alone. That contrast also exposes a recurring question in the Code's long life, which is whether the failures attributed to the sexual offence provisions lay in their text or in investigation, prosecution and trial, matters that amendment of the Code could influence only indirectly.

Set against this long record, the analytical point is that the Code absorbed amendment without structural collapse because its architecture made insertion cheap. Its method, repeated across its chapters, was to define once, to except once, and to grade by chapter, and each element of that method lowered the cost of adding something new. A definition given in one place could carry a new offence without fresh explanation of every term it used, because words defined for the Code as a whole travelled with the inserted section. Exceptions stated in a general form could qualify a new provision without being rewritten for it, so that questions of lawful authority, of private defence and of other general defences did not have to be settled section by section every time Parliament acted. Grading by chapter meant that a new offence arrived with its seriousness already signalled by its neighbours, placed among offences against the body, or against property, or against public tranquillity, so that courts and readers could locate its weight by position as well as by the punishment it carried. Insertion was therefore a local operation with system wide support, and that is why a provision on cruelty within marriage in 1983 and provisions on stalking and voyeurism in 2013 could enter a Victorian code and function there without requiring the Victorian code to be abandoned. The amendment record is accordingly evidence about the design, and not only about politics. Politics explains the timing of each change, the event or the report or the campaign that forced a subject onto the agenda, but design explains why the chosen instrument was so often an insertion into this Code rather than a free standing statute, and why the insertions accumulated into a workable whole instead of a pile of unrelated Acts. A badly organised code would have punished that habit, forcing consolidation or collapse, and the fact that neither became unavoidable for so long is a judgment on the original architecture delivered by the practice of successive legislatures.

The same record carries a negative fact that is just as revealing, which is what amendment never did. No Parliament rewrote the general part of the Code. The definitions, the exceptions, the doctrines of liability and the scheme of punishments that organised the whole were adjusted at the edges and supplemented in places, but they were never taken up together and refounded, and the special part grew by accretion around a general part that retained its nineteenth century shape. That restraint had practical causes, since reopening the general part would have unsettled every offence that depended on it and would have converted each amending bill into a codification project, but it also had an intellectual cause, which is that the general part continued to work well enough that its replacement never became the urgent question. The consequence is the defining oddity of the Code's later life. A text whose general doctrines were drafted in the 1830s could still organise twenty first century offences, could still supply the language in which acid attacks, stalking and trafficking were described, because the work of organisation had been done at a level of abstraction that did not depend on the particular offences the drafters had in view. The new offences were new in their social detail and often new in the harms they named, but they were old in their legal grammar, framed in definitions, exceptions and graded punishments that the Code had carried from the beginning. Long life in amendments thus means two things held together. The Code changed constantly at the level of the offence, responding to communal tension in one century and to dowry death and sexual violence in another, and it changed barely at all at the level of the system, so that its history is one of a stable grammar learning new sentences. When replacement finally came onto the agenda, it came not because the grammar had failed in a single case but because the accumulation of sentences, and the age of the book that held them, had themselves become an argument.

## Sedition: the provision that outlived its empire

Among all the provisions of the Code, Section 124A has the strangest biography, because it was absent at the birth of the book it came to symbolise, it served the empire that inserted it as an instrument against the movement that ended that empire, and it then survived the empire, the founding of the Republic, the framing of a constitution that refused its name, and repeated judicial and political challenge, until its continued presence became a question that each generation of Indian public life had to answer again. The provision was not in the Code as enacted in 1860. A clause on exciting disaffection appeared in Macaulay's draft and was omitted when the Code was passed, so that the enacted Code began its life without a sedition offence of this kind, and sedition entered only in 1870, as Section 124A. The enacted text punished whoever brings or attempts to bring into hatred or contempt, or excites or attempts to excite disaffection towards, the Government established by law, with imprisonment for life or imprisonment up to three years, and fine. Every element of that formula deserves slow reading. The verbs reach attempts as well as completed results, the objects of the offence are feelings, hatred, contempt and disaffection, rather than acts of violence, and the protected object is the Government established by law, an institution, rather than the physical safety of any person. A provision framed in those terms criminalises a relationship of the mind between citizen and government, and its breadth is built into its language rather than imposed on it by unsympathetic interpretation. At the same time the explanations attached to the section protected comments expressing disapprobation that stopped short of exciting hatred, contempt or disaffection, and that protection shows that the drafters of the insertion knew a line had to be drawn somewhere between criticism and sedition, even if the place they drew it left the citizen to guess where vigorous condemnation ended and punishable disaffection began.

That uncertainty was not an accident of the provision but the source of its usefulness to the state that enacted it, and the prosecutions under the Raj made the point in practice. Section 124A became the signature political offence of the Raj, the charge reached for when speech itself was the conduct the government wished to punish, and its history in that period is written in the names of those it was used against. Bal Gangadhar Tilak was prosecuted under it in 1897 and again in 1908, and the repetition across more than a decade shows the provision returning to the same target as nationalist argument developed, each prosecution treating published words as a danger to the established government. In 1922 Mohandas Gandhi pleaded guilty to sedition charges and used the trial itself as a forum, telling the court in words that have followed the section ever since that Section 124A was the prince among the political sections of the Indian Penal Code designed to suppress the liberty of the citizen. The guilty plea is central to the meaning of that moment, because Gandhi did not contest the facts the section made relevant and instead accepted its penalty while denying its legitimacy, turning a prosecution for disaffection into a demonstration that disaffection, honestly avowed, could be a moral position rather than a crime. A provision that began as an administrative insertion in 1870 had by 1922 acquired a place in the moral argument about empire itself, and that acquisition explains why its fate after independence could never be a merely technical question.

The survival question has to be answered from the record, and the record begins with a deliberate constitutional refusal. The Constituent Assembly dropped the word sedition from the permissible restrictions on free speech in what became Article 19(2), after K. M. Munshi argued that it had no place in a democratic constitution. That decision was a considered one, made by an Assembly that knew the provision from the receiving end, and its effect was to deny sedition the constitutional shelter that an express listing would have given it, leaving any defence of Section 124A to be made under other heads of restriction, principally public order. Jawaharlal Nehru, speaking in Parliament in 1951 during the first amendment debates, called the provision obnoxious and said it ought to have no place in the law. Coming from the Prime Minister of the new Republic, in the very season when the Constitution's speech provisions were first being amended, those words might have been expected to end the matter. They did not, and the reason the record gives is uncomfortable in its simplicity. The same amendment season saw governments unwilling to surrender a public order instrument, and the provision stayed. Between the constitutional refusal to name sedition and the executive refusal to surrender it, the section lived on in the gap, defended in practice as a tool of order while disowned in principle as a relic of subjection. The Law Commission in later decades and the courts pulled in opposite directions, and repeal bills never gathered a majority, so that the provision's survival was nobody's single decision and everybody's accumulated one. Each institution could point to another as the proper place for the question to be settled, the legislature to the courts' power of construction, the courts to the legislature's power of repeal, and the executive to the necessities of order, and in that circulation of responsibility a section drafted for an empire outlived the empire by three quarters of a century.

The courts, when the question reached them in a form they could not pass on, answered it by narrowing the provision rather than by ending it, and the two great cases in that story point in opposite directions before the second settles the law. The Allahabad High Court in Ram Nandan, in 1959, held the section unconstitutional, taking the view that its language could not be reconciled with the freedom of speech the Constitution guaranteed. That judgment had the merit of reading Section 124A as it was written, as a punishment for exciting feelings against the government, and of asking whether a free constitution could permit feelings to be criminalised in that way. Three years later a Constitution Bench in Kedar Nath Singh v State of Bihar, in 1962, saved the section by construction, holding that it applies only to acts involving intention or tendency to create disorder, or disturbance of law and order, or incitement to violence, so that strong criticism of government, however vigorous, is not sedition. The construction transformed the provision's legal meaning while leaving its text untouched, and that technique had lasting consequences. On one side, it gave citizens and courts a test that protected vigorous criticism and confined the offence to speech bound up with disorder and violence, and generations of argument about sedition have been conducted in its terms. On the other side, it left on the statute book a text that said one thing and meant, in law, a narrower thing, so that a person reading Section 124A without knowledge of the judgment would find an offence far wider than the one the Constitution Bench had permitted to survive. The gap between text and construction became the natural habitat of misuse, because a police officer registering a case works from the text, while a citizen resisting the case must invoke the construction, and the labour of narrowing falls on the accused long after arrest and process have done their work. The provision also reached well beyond politicians and public speakers in its application, since journalists and commentators were among those prosecuted under the section, and those who document such prosecutions keep their source material private, as described in [this account of how journalists use VaultBook to protect their sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/). Kedar Nath Singh thus stands as both a rescue and an indictment, a judgment that saved free criticism by declaring that the section had never lawfully covered it, and that left standing the words under which criticism continued to be charged.

### What did the Law Commission recommend about sedition in 2023?

The Law Commission, in its 279th Report of 2023, recommended retaining Section 124A rather than repealing it, while adding procedural safeguards around registration, investigation and sanction, on the reasoning that the provision addressed internal security threats that other offences did not fully cover.

To understand the place of that recommendation in the long argument, the state of the provision in the years immediately before it has to be set out, and that state was one of suspension rather than operation. In S. G. Vombatkere v Union of India, by an interim order dated 11 May 2022, a bench led by Chief Justice N. V. Ramana recorded the Union's decision to re-examine the provision and urged the Centre and the states to refrain from registering fresh cases, continuing investigations, or taking coercive steps under Section 124A while that reconsideration lasted. The order directed that all pending trials, appeals and proceedings under the section be kept in abeyance, and it left persons facing fresh charges free to approach the courts for relief. Structurally, this was an order that held the provision in suspension pending executive reconsideration, and its form is as striking as its content. The Court did not strike the section down, as the Allahabad High Court had done in Ram Nandan, and it did not restate the narrowing construction, as the Constitution Bench had done in Kedar Nath Singh. Instead it recorded that the executive itself had undertaken to re-examine the provision, and it organised the law's pause around that undertaking, so that for the duration of the reconsideration the section existed in the Code but was not to be set in motion. A penal provision in that condition occupies a singular position in a legal system. It has not been repealed, so it continues to declare certain conduct criminal, and it has not been invalidated, so no court has held that it cannot lawfully do so, and yet by judicial direction its machinery is stopped, its trials frozen and its investigations halted.

The 279th Report answered the reconsideration from one side of that question. The Law Commission recommended retaining Section 124A with procedural safeguards, reasoning from the internal security threats the provision addresses, and the structure of the recommendation follows from that reasoning. If the mischief is understood as the misuse of a necessary power, then the remedy lies in the procedure that surrounds the power, in safeguards governing how a case is begun and by whom it is sanctioned, rather than in the abolition of the power itself. Retention with safeguards thus accepts the long official argument that the state requires an offence addressed to threats against its own existence and integrity, threats that ordinary offences against person and property describe imperfectly, while conceding that the provision's history in application requires new controls on its invocation. Set against the record traced in this section, the recommendation is a defence of continuity. It treats the section's survival through the Constituent Assembly's refusal, through Nehru's condemnation, through Ram Nandan and Kedar Nath Singh, as evidence that the provision answers a permanent need of the state, and it proposes to discipline the provision rather than to end it. Again and again the Indian legal system, confronted with Section 124A, has chosen to qualify it, to construe it, to suspend it, or to surround it with safeguards, and has declined the simpler and more final course of removing it from the book.

The critics' answer, associated in public debate with retired judges and civil liberties organisations, meets the recommendation at exactly that point and draws the opposite conclusion from the same record. On that view, a provision saved only by a narrowing construction and suspended by the Court has already failed the test of a working law, because a working law is one whose text can be trusted by the citizen who reads it and by the officer who applies it, and Section 124A fails that test at both ends. The citizen reading the section finds feelings criminalised, hatred, contempt and disaffection named as the objects of punishment, and must be told by a lawyer that a judgment of 1962 confines those words to incitement to violence and disorder. The officer applying the section works from a text whose plain breadth invites the very charges that the construction forbids, and the history of prosecutions, from Tilak and Gandhi under the Raj to writers and reporters in the Republic, shows how consistently the invitation has been accepted at the point of registration, whatever later courts have said at the point of trial. Procedural safeguards, on this view, address the manner of a misuse whose possibility is written into the offence itself, and a provision that requires a judicial rescue in one generation and a judicial suspension in another has demonstrated, by the system's own handling of it, that it cannot be left to operate as an ordinary part of the criminal law. The two positions are not easily reconciled because they read the same survival differently. For the Law Commission's reasoning, survival through repeated challenge shows a need repeatedly recognised. For the critics, survival through repeated rescue shows a defect repeatedly confessed. Between those readings the section remained where the interim order had left it, in the Code and out of use, its future referred from the courts to the executive and from the executive, in time, to the legislature that would replace the Code itself.

The end of the story, so far as the Code is concerned, came by replacement rather than by repeal. The successor code dropped the word sedition while enacting an offence of acts endangering the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India, and with that enactment the question that had run from 1870 through the Constituent Assembly, the courts and the Law Commission changed its form. Whether the new offence reproduces the old provision under another name, or confines itself to the ground that Kedar Nath Singh marked out, or travels beyond it, is a comparison of two texts that belongs to the articles which own that comparison, and it is enough for the history of the Penal Code to record the manner of the departure. Section 124A was never repealed by a Parliament persuaded that it should not exist, and it was never struck down by a court prepared to finish what Ram Nandan had begun. It was construed into a narrower shape, suspended while the executive reconsidered it, defended by the Law Commission with safeguards proposed around it, and finally left behind when the book that contained it was replaced as a whole. A provision inserted to protect an empire from disaffection thus ended not with a verdict but with a succession, and its long life stands as the clearest case in the Code's history of the difference between a law that endures because it is accepted and a law that endures because no institution, at any single moment, takes upon itself the act of ending it.

## What the courts removed before Parliament did

Parliament repealed the Indian Penal Code in 2023, with effect in 2024, but by then parts of the Code had already gone by a different route. Indian courts had examined individual provisions against the Constitution, measured them against rights that the colonial legislature had never recognised as limits on its power, and struck them down, read them down or emptied them of practical force one at a time. The repeal therefore closed a process the judiciary had begun decades earlier. Parliament did not, in the end, remove most of the provisions that had become hardest to defend in constitutional terms. The courts had done that work first, leaving the successor code to decide whether to restore, replace or omit what the courts had taken away. No court audited the whole statute at once. The dismantling arrived case by case, as challenges forced narrow questions. Could the state make death the only sentence for one class of murder. Could it punish a person for trying to end his or her own life. Could it criminalise private sexual conduct between adults. Could it punish adultery by punishing one participant and treating the other as incapable of wrongdoing. The answers accumulated into a transformation larger than any single judgment.

Section 303 made death the mandatory punishment for murder committed by a person under sentence of imprisonment for life. The convicting court had no discretion to impose a lesser sentence, however different the circumstances of one case might be from another falling under the same section. In Mithu v State of Punjab in 1983 a five judge bench struck the section down as violating Articles 14 and 21. The reasoning went to the structure of fair sentencing. A mandatory sentence abolishes the judicial duty to weigh the circumstances of the crime and the criminal, and treats materially different cases as identical for the one purpose where difference matters most, the choice between life and death. The Court created no new offence and no new penalty. It returned murder by a life convict to the ordinary framework of Section 302, under which the court retained the discretion Section 303 had removed. Mithu also carried a wider lesson. A provision could stand in the Code for generations and still fail when its method, rather than its subject matter, denied equality and fair procedure.

Section 309 punished attempt to commit suicide, and its history moved in three stages. In P. Rathinam in 1994 the Supreme Court struck the section down. In Gian Kaur in 1996 a Constitution Bench restored it, holding against the challenge that had succeeded two years earlier, so the formal position thereafter was that Section 309 remained valid law. Formal validity proved to be only part of the story. The Mental Healthcare Act 2017 emptied the section in practice without repealing it in terms. Section 115 of that Act presumes that a person who attempts suicide suffers severe stress and provides that such a person shall not be tried and punished under the Penal Code unless the prosecution proves the absence of severe stress. The mechanism placed a presumption between the offence and any prosecution and a burden on the state that ran opposite to the assumption on which the old offence had rested. The old law treated the attempt principally as a wrong to be answered by the criminal process. The newer law treated it principally as evidence of distress, and prosecutions fell away once the prosecution had to displace that starting point. Section 309 thus survived on paper while losing its ordinary life as a working offence, and the successor drafter had to decide whether a paper survival deserved a place in a new text at all.

Section 377, the unnatural offences provision, reversed direction more than once. It punished carnal intercourse against the order of nature, language broad enough in application to reach consensual sexual conduct between adults in private. The Delhi High Court read it down in Naz Foundation in 2009. The Supreme Court restored it in Suresh Kumar Koushal in 2013, leaving change at that stage to Parliament. The reasoning is traced in [the Navtej Singh Johar judgment and the reading down of Section 377](/2011/03/01/section-377-navtej-johar-judgment/), where in 2018 a five judge bench read the section down again, definitively for the remaining life of the Code, and held it unconstitutional so far as it criminalised consensual sexual conduct between adults. The reading down was not a repeal. Section 377 remained standing for non consensual acts, for acts with minors and for acts with animals, where criminal liability raised entirely different considerations. What changed was its reach over adults who had consented, and the provision that survived was narrower and shaped by constitutional adjudication rather than by the language its drafters had chosen.

Section 497 punished adultery, and punished only the man, treating the wife as incapable of the offence and not liable even as an abettor. That asymmetry rested on a view of marriage in which the husband held a proprietary claim over his wife and the criminal law intervened to protect that claim by punishing the outsider. In Joseph Shine in 2018 a unanimous five judge bench struck Section 497 down, with the connected part of Section 198 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, as violating Articles 14, 19 and 21. The Court held that a provision built on the husband's proprietary claim over his wife could not survive a constitution of equals. A law that defined wrongdoing through the status of the participants and exempted one sex by deeming it incapable could not be cured by better enforcement. The bench preserved a distinction often lost in summary accounts. Striking down the crime did not make adultery legally irrelevant for all purposes. It could remain relevant as a civil wrong and a ground in matrimonial proceedings, where the question was the future of a marriage rather than imprisonment imposed by the state.

Section 124A was narrowed by construction in 1962 and held in abeyance by interim order in 2022, and its fuller treatment belongs to the discussion of sedition elsewhere in this article.

A provision read down in Navtej Singh Johar continued only in the narrower shape the judgment left it, so the successor inherited a reduced offence rather than the original breadth. A provision neutralised by another statute occupied the most ambiguous position, valid in form and weak in operation. A provision held in abeyance remained valid yet unusable while the interim order lasted. Parliament therefore repealed a code whose contents constitutional litigation had already sorted into provisions that could be carried forward, provisions that could be carried forward only in altered form and provisions that had no constitutional future at all.

## The colonial charge, answered both ways

The charge that the Penal Code was a colonial code rests on a fact nobody seriously disputes. It was drafted by a colonial power for a subject population that had not chosen that power. In its weak form the charge is merely chronological, as if the date of a law settled its character forever. In its strong form it is institutional and moral. It says a code designed by rulers for subjects carries that relationship inside its provisions, in the offences it emphasises, in the authority it assumes and in the obedience it expects, and cannot become the criminal law of a free republic merely by surviving independence. That strong form deserves an answer at full strength, because if it is right it condemns not only the origin of the Code but the decision of independent India to retain it for more than seven decades, and presents repeal as a necessary act of decolonisation rather than ordinary legislative reform.

The prosecution case begins with authorship and purpose. A colonial legislature drafts criminal law for order in a territory it intends to hold, for officials it intends to protect and for resistance it intends to suppress. On this account the Code belonged to that project whatever its technical merits, and its very completeness can be turned against it. A single code imposed across a vast and varied society made administration easier for a state governing from above, standardised the language of wrongdoing and gave officers and magistrates a portable instrument of control. The subject population appeared in the scheme principally as the object of regulation. Later amendment by Indian legislatures does not, on the prosecution view, cure the point, because amendment works within an architecture. If the architecture was built to secure a ruling power, amendments can soften edges without changing the distribution of authority the original design embodied.

Sedition supplies the prosecution with its most concrete evidence. The sedition provision was used against Tilak and against Gandhi, prosecutions that entered the political memory of the freedom movement precisely because the law treated criticism and mobilisation against the government as threats to the state itself. The offence did not require a completed rebellion. It reached expression that the government construed as disaffection, in courtrooms controlled by the imperial system. For critics, the later narrowing of sedition by constitutional interpretation confirms rather than erases that history. A provision that needs to be read down by courts of a republic to coexist with free speech is, on this view, a provision whose natural home was a polity where speech against the government was a danger to be managed. The prosecution extends the same reading to the chapters on policing and public order. Those provisions, it is said, assumed a state that feared its subjects, priced public order high and the liberty of the subject low, and gave wide authority over assembly and collective action to a minority government that ruled without consent and depended on police and magistracy. Retaining such provisions after independence, the argument runs, retained the habits embedded in them.

That case acquired a political voice at the moment of replacement. Home Minister Amit Shah, introducing the successor bills in 2023, argued that laws bearing the stamp of slavery had to go and that the new codes would deliver justice rather than punishment as their organising idea. Supporters of replacement joined a symbolic claim to a programmatic one. The provenance of the colonial codes disqualified them, and a new set of codes could place the citizen and the delivery of justice at the centre in place of penal control. On this view, keeping the old code merely because it worked was an inadequate answer. A republic should write its own criminal law in its own name and should not ask its citizens to live indefinitely under a code composed for their subjection.

The defence begins with the character of the drafting. The drafter was a utilitarian reformer, and the Code was in several respects more liberal than the English criminal law of its day, which it declined to copy wholesale. A mere transplantation would have imported technical distinctions and severities that reformers in England itself were attacking. Instead the Code attempted a systematic statement, with definitions, general exceptions and graded punishments, and it contained restraints a purely repressive instrument would not have needed. It recognised mistake, necessity, infancy, insanity and private defence, and it built a guilty mind into definitions across the special part. The defence next points to uniformity. Within its territory the Code imposed one law on ruler and ruled alike, in place of personal and religious criminal laws of unequal severity that allocated liability by the identity of the parties. Formal equality did not make the empire just, and police discretion and political subordination remained, but the question for a court became what the accused had done under a rule stated in advance, not which personal law governed the parties. That generality gave the republic a foundation it could constitutionalise and amend, rather than fragmented regimes it would first have had to dismantle.

The strongest defence is the conduct of free India itself. Governed over the decades by every major political formation, the republic kept the Code, amended it scores of times and never found its general part wanting. Successive legislatures added offences, revised punishments and reworked chapters when politics or conscience demanded it. They were sovereign and could have replaced the whole text at any point. Retention across changes of government is not conclusive proof of merit, since institutions survive through inertia as well as approval, but repeated amendment is harder to dismiss. Legislatures that amended the Code so often treated it as theirs to alter, and progressively made it the criminal law of the republic in practice as well as in form. The final defence point turns the prosecution examples back upon themselves. The provisions most identified with repression were removed by Indian courts applying the Indian Constitution before Parliament repealed the Code. The mandatory death provision fell in Mithu, the reach of Section 377 over consenting adults fell in Navtej Singh Johar, adultery as a crime fell in Joseph Shine, and Section 309 was emptied by an Indian statute on mental healthcare. The worst provisions proved severable, and the general part survived their removal without collapse, which suggests they were survivals within an architecture that did not depend on them for coherence.

The verdict this article takes is discriminating. The colonial origin of the Penal Code is a fact about its birth, not a measurement of the law it became. Origin explains how the Code arrived, why certain offences were present and why the enacting state held the assumptions it did. It does not tell a reader whether a definition is clear, whether an exception is just or whether a general part served a constitutional republic well. By the same reasoning, a successor is not better because its title is Indian, any more than the old code was worse in every particular because its first enactment was colonial. The honest test of the replacement is whether it preserved the architecture that had proved worth preserving while discarding the provisions that had failed, a question the final verdict answers.

## Repeal, savings and the afterlife of a code

A code does not end completely on the day its successor commences, because criminal liability attaches to acts at the time they are done, and the calendar of an offence, not the calendar of its trial, decides which code supplies the charge. The successor is [the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023 explained](/2010/12/15/bharatiya-nyaya-sanhita-2023-explained/), which received Presidential assent on 25 December 2023 and commenced on 1 July 2024, repealing the Penal Code through its repeal and savings provision, Section 358. From commencement the Sanhita stood as the general penal law for new facts. Section 358 did two things at once. It closed the old code as a source of liability for future conduct, and it preserved the old code's operation for the past to the extent its savings language and the Constitution required. To treat repeal as a simple substitution of one book for another misses the mechanism that will govern real cases for years.

Article 20(1) of the Constitution supplies the foundation. It forbids conviction under a law that was not in force when the act was done and forbids a greater penalty than the one then applicable. The first limb bars retroactive criminalisation, so conduct that was not an offence when it occurred cannot be made an offence by a later code. The second limb bars a later law from increasing the punishment for past conduct. Together they express a principle older than either code, that a person is entitled to know at the moment of acting whether the act is criminal and what punishment it risks. The Sanhita could rename offences, reorganise chapters and revise punishments for the future. It could not reach backwards to recharacterise past conduct or enlarge its penalty, and the changeover was therefore legislation performed inside a constitutional limit, not an exercise of legislative will alone.

The practical consequence is that offences committed before 1 July 2024 are investigated, tried and punished under the Penal Code, even if investigation, charge or trial follows long after that date. The savings provision preserves pending proceedings and liabilities incurred under the repealed code, together with rights and obligations acquired under it, so repeal does not extinguish a liability already arisen or end a proceeding already begun. Trial courts will therefore run both codes in parallel for years, the old code for the old facts and the new code for the new. A magistrate may deal in a single list with charges under each code, and police, prosecutors and defence counsel alike must begin with chronology. When did the alleged act occur, which code was in force, and does the savings provision preserve the proceeding. An error at that threshold can misstate the ingredients of the offence, the maximum punishment or the availability of a defence, so the transition demands more care, not less, from institutions whose familiarity with the old code was once an argument for keeping it.

How the two codes compare provision by provision can be followed in [the IPC and BNS compared provision by provision](/2011/02/01/ipc-vs-bns-comparison/), where familiar provisions reappear in a new order under new numbers. Section 34 of the Penal Code, the common intention rule, is carried to Section 3(5) of the Sanhita. Section 149 appears as Section 190, Section 302 as Section 103(1), and Section 498A as Section 85, with cruelty defined in Section 86. The Sanhita contains 358 sections in 20 chapters, a structure that reorganises much familiar material rather than abandoning it. Community service was added as a punishment, a sanction the old list did not include, yet the addition operates within a familiar framework of punishments, offences and exceptions. Renumbering should not be confused with reinvention. A practitioner moving between the codes is often translating a known provision into a new location, not learning a new offence from nothing.

That continuity explains the continuing authority of Penal Code precedent. Where the Sanhita re-enacts a Penal Code provision, or re-enacts its substance in materially the same words, courts read the new provision through the case law the old provision carried with it, because a legislature that repeats language bearing a settled judicial construction is taken to legislate with that construction in view. Judgments on common intention, unlawful assembly, private defence and the mental element in definitions therefore remain the working commentary on the many Sanhita provisions that re-enact Penal Code text. The old code lives on as an interpretive presence inside its successor, consulted not from nostalgia but because part of the meaning of the new text is stored in decisions on the old.

This account is general information about a repealed code and its successor, and anyone facing a charge arising from facts before the changeover needs advice on the savings and transition rules as they apply to that case.

The strongest evidence of continuity lies in the general exceptions. The architecture of justification and excuse that answered when an act is not an offence, although it matches the words of a prohibition, survived the repeal, restated in the early chapters of the successor, with the general exceptions restated in Chapter III of the Sanhita. That survival matters more than the survival of any individual offence. Offences are the changing part of a penal code, added, revised and removed as judgment shifts. The exceptions answer permanent questions about mistake, incapacity, compulsion and defensive necessity. A successor that restated them acknowledged in structural terms that the old design remained serviceable, which is the strongest evidence for the claim running through this article that the design, not the numbering, was the Code.

## The verdict on the Indian Penal Code

A verdict on a code of such longevity should resist two temptations. The first is celebration by survival, as if age alone proved excellence, when institutions also survive through inertia and the cost of replacement. The second is dismissal by origin, rejected earlier in this article as a substitute for analysis. Judged by what it did, the Penal Code presents a mixed record, but not an evenly mixed one. Its central design was a substantial achievement that its worst provisions should not obscure, while its failures were real, prolonged and in several instances corrected by courts rather than by the legislature that owned the text. Both halves of that judgment belong in the verdict.

What worked best was the general part. The Code defined its terms and used them consistently, so that words such as dishonestly, fraudulently, voluntarily and good faith carried a settled meaning across hundreds of provisions. It treated abetment, conspiracy, attempt and joint liability as general problems, instead of leaving each offence to improvise its own rules of participation. It graded offences by harm and by mental state, so that punishment could in principle track culpability rather than outcome alone. Above all, it placed the general exceptions near the front of the scheme, announcing before any particular offence was defined that some acts within the words of a prohibition are not offences at all, because they were done under mistake, under necessity, in private defence or by a person incapable of the relevant fault. Liability was therefore the product of a definition, a mental element and the absence of an exception, and that discipline shaped the work of courts even when they disagreed about its application.

Clarity followed from design, and clarity in a penal code is a practical virtue. The Code was teachable to generations of police officers deciding whether a complaint disclosed an offence, to magistrates framing charges in the language of its sections, and to students learning criminal law by learning its structure. Officer and judge were in a meaningful sense reading the same book, and a charge citing a section communicated its essential allegation to everyone in the process. Because offences were defined in a published text with ingredients that courts enumerated and tested, prosecution had to fit facts to elements rather than invoke wrongdoing at large. That discipline was imperfect in practice, but the imperfection lay often in the distance between the Code's requirements and the institutions applying them. A code that states its rules clearly also gives defence counsel, appellate courts and the public a standard against which official action can be measured.

The Code could also absorb amendment. New offences could be inserted into the special part without disturbing the general part that governed them, because definitions, rules of participation and exceptions already stood ready to apply. Punishments could be revised offence by offence as legislative judgment changed, and provisions answering new social concerns, including the cruelty provision that became Section 498A, could be interpreted through the established apparatus. A text that receives scores of amendments over many decades, from legislatures of different complexions, and remains navigable as one code has shown a structural resilience that new drafting rarely achieves at the first attempt. The successor itself is evidence on this point, since it retained the plan of general explanations, general exceptions, punishments and graded offences while rearranging contents and revising selected provisions. Legislatures do not preserve the skeleton of a code they regard as a failure.

What did not work should be stated with equal plainness. The Code contained provisions that priced order above liberty, and some did so long after any transitional justification could excuse them. Sedition, even after narrowing by construction, remained an offence whose history and wording invited use against dissent, and it took an interim order in 2022 to hold it in abeyance for the final period. Public order provisions formed around the anxieties of a colonial administration continued to shape the response to assembly and protest in a republic whose Constitution protected both. Independent India had sovereign power to revise that ordering at any time, and its lateness distributes responsibility between the colonial draft and the republic's legislatures rather than laying the whole blame on the nineteenth century. The debate over punishment was likewise left unresolved. The courts removed the extreme case of a mandatory death sentence in Mithu because it excluded judicial consideration of circumstances, but that judgment answered a question of method rather than the underlying questions about capital punishment, sentencing purposes and the length of imprisonment, which Parliament adjusted only in particular contexts and never settled as a whole. The successor's addition of community service shows the range of sanctions could expand in a direction the old list had not taken, and that it did so only at repeal underlines how narrow the sentencing instruments had remained.

The long survival of provisions the Constitution had already condemned in principle is the third failure. The equality reasoning that destroyed Section 497 in 2018 depended on guarantees present from the founding of the republic, and the same is true of the reasoning that read down Section 377 for consenting adults and struck down Section 303 in 1983. For decades the Code carried provisions that failed standards adopted at the founding, and delay of that kind has costs an eventual correction does not cancel. People were prosecuted, threatened with prosecution or forced to arrange their lives around offences later declared unconstitutional or emptied of force. Repealing an offence costs a government political capital, while leaving it in place transfers the cost to the individuals it touches and the courts that must eventually confront it. The credit for ending that transfer belongs disproportionately to litigants and judges.

Those failures occupied, in the main, a different level of the Code from its successes. The failures were particular offences or punishments in the special part, the list any generation can revise as its judgment changes. The successes were the definitions, exceptions, rules of participation and grading that decided how any offence would operate. That distinction answers the question with which this article opened. The Code lasted because its architecture separated the permanent questions of criminal liability from the changing list of offences. It answered the permanent questions, what counts as a guilty mind, when an act is excused, how participants share liability, how attempts differ from completed harms and how offences are graded, in a general part that did not need rewriting whenever society revised its view of a particular wrong. Offences could be added, struck down, emptied or repealed while the framework that made the rest intelligible stood undisturbed, and any replacement is judged by whether it kept that separation. On the evidence of the successor's structure, with general explanations and general exceptions restated at its front and familiar offences reorganised behind them, the separation was kept. The Penal Code is gone as the governing text for new facts. Its design remains the standard by which its successor will be measured, and the measure is demanding, because a design is vindicated not by the ceremony of its enactment but by decades of cases in which police, prosecutors, magistrates, judges and citizens must all make it work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who drafted the Indian Penal Code and when did it come into force?

The Code was drafted by the First Law Commission of India, constituted in 1834 under the Charter Act of 1833 and chaired by Thomas Babington Macaulay, the first Law Member of the Governor-General's Council. The Commission submitted its draft in 1837. The draft then waited more than two decades, was revised under Barnes Peacock as Law Member, and was enacted as Act 45 of 1860 on 6 October 1860. It came into force on 1 January 1862, the interval allowing courts, police and the magistracy across British India to prepare for a single criminal code where several systems of criminal law had operated before it.

### Q: How was the Indian Penal Code structured?

The Code arranged 511 sections in twenty-three chapters. It opened with preliminary provisions and a chapter of general explanations that defined its working words once for the whole statute. A punishments chapter followed, then a chapter of general exceptions stating the defences that answer every offence, and chapters on abetment and criminal conspiracy. The offence chapters then moved outward: offences against the State and public tranquillity first, then offences by public servants, against public justice, and finally offences against the human body, property, documents, marriage and reputation, closing with a general provision on attempts.

### Q: How did the general exceptions in the Indian Penal Code protect an accused?

Chapter IV of the Code, Sections 76 to 106, stated the defences once and made them available against every offence in the statute. An accused could answer a charge by showing the act was done under a mistake of fact, by accident, from necessity, in infancy or insanity, under compulsion, with consent, or in private defence of body or property. The exceptions did not need the prosecution to disprove them in advance: the accused established the exception on a preponderance of probabilities, and the court then read the charge subject to it, so a killing in genuine private defence was no offence at all.

### Q: Is the Indian Penal Code still applicable to any case today?

Yes, to every case whose facts predate the changeover. The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita repealed the Penal Code from 1 July 2024, but Article 20(1) of the Constitution bars conviction for an act that was not an offence when committed and bars any heavier penalty than the one then in force, and the Sanhita's repeal and savings provision preserves proceedings and liabilities under the old Code. An offence committed before that date is therefore investigated, charged, tried and sentenced under the Indian Penal Code, and courts will apply it alongside its successor for years while the older cases work through.

### Q: Which IPC sections were struck down or read down by the courts?

Four provisions carry the main story. Section 303, which made death mandatory for murder by a life convict, was struck down in Mithu v State of Punjab in 1983. Section 377 was read down in Navtej Singh Johar in 2018 so that it no longer reached consensual adult conduct. Section 497, the adultery offence, was struck down in Joseph Shine in 2018. Section 309, attempt to suicide, was upheld in Gian Kaur in 1996 but emptied by the Mental Healthcare Act 2017. Section 124A on sedition was narrowed by construction in Kedar Nath Singh in 1962 and held in abeyance by interim order in 2022.

### Q: Why did the Indian Penal Code survive for more than 160 years?

Because its architecture separated what changes from what does not. The general part of the Code, its definitions, its general exceptions, its rules on abetment, conspiracy and joint liability, and its grading of offences, described the permanent questions of criminal liability, while the offence chapters could absorb new harms by amendment. A Parliament that needed a cruelty offence in 1983 or new sexual offences in 2013 could insert them into the structure without rebuilding it. Codes fail when every social change requires a new statute; the Penal Code's design let one statute carry the criminal law of a republic that changed beyond recognition around it.

### Q: Did the Indian Penal Code apply to every part of India?

Almost, but not quite, and the exception lasted most of its life. As enacted, the Code extended to British India, while the princely states stood outside it, many administering codes of their own. After independence it extended to the whole of India except the State of Jammu and Kashmir, which retained the Ranbir Penal Code, a code of 1932 modelled on Macaulay's work. Only with the reorganisation of 2019 did the Indian Penal Code come into force in Jammu and Kashmir, on 31 October 2019, giving the Code a genuinely all-India map for the last years of its operation.

### Q: What was the sedition provision in the Indian Penal Code?

Section 124A punished whoever, by words, signs or visible representation, brought or attempted to bring into hatred or contempt, or excited or attempted to excite disaffection towards, the Government established by law in India. The punishment ran to imprisonment for life, or imprisonment up to three years, with fine. Explanations to the section protected comments that expressed disapprobation of government measures without exciting hatred, contempt or disaffection. The provision was not in the Code as enacted in 1860; it was inserted in 1870, was used against Tilak and Gandhi, and was confined by the Supreme Court in 1962 to acts tending to public disorder or incitement to violence.

### Q: Why did Macaulay's draft of the Penal Code wait more than twenty years to become law?

The draft of 1837 arrived in a government that had other priorities and some doubts. The draft was referred for examination, its authors left India, and officials argued over provisions, including its treatment of offences the draft had drawn more liberally than English law. Administrative energy went to other codes and to revenue and judicial reorganisation, and the draft slept in official custody while commissions and law members succeeded one another. It was revived and revised under Barnes Peacock in the 1850s, and the rebellion of 1857 concentrated official minds on a uniform criminal law for a territory the state had nearly lost. Enactment followed in 1860.

### Q: Why does the Indian Penal Code carry illustrations after so many of its sections?

The illustrations were a working part of the drafting method, not decoration. Macaulay's Commission expected the Code to be applied by magistrates and judges many of whom had little legal training, across a vast jurisdiction with no shared body of case law to consult. An abstract definition of theft or dishonesty could be read in divergent ways; a short worked example fixed the intended reading on the page, showing which side of the line a described act fell. The illustrations therefore carried part of the Code's meaning, and Indian courts treated them as aids to construction that could not override the enacted words but could resolve doubt about them.

### Q: What was transportation as a punishment under the Indian Penal Code, and what replaced it?

Transportation was removal overseas for a term or for life, in practice to the penal settlement in the Andaman Islands, and it stood in the Code's original list of punishments in Section 53 alongside death, imprisonment, forfeiture and fine. A transported convict was exiled from mainland India and put to labour in the settlement, a punishment meant to combine severity with distance. The sentence disappeared in stages after independence, and in 1955 Parliament substituted imprisonment for life wherever the Code had spoken of transportation, which is why judgments of the earlier period record sentences of transportation for life where a later court would record imprisonment for life.

### Q: What is the difference between common intention under Section 34 and common object under Section 149 of the IPC?

The two provisions solve the same problem, group crime where the fatal act cannot be traced to one hand, by different routes. Section 34 needs a common intention shared by the participants, a prior meeting of minds, and it then treats the act done in furtherance of that intention as the act of each. It has no minimum group size. Section 149 needs an unlawful assembly of five or more persons sharing a common object listed in Section 141, and makes every member liable for an offence any member commits in prosecution of that object, or which the members knew was likely to be committed.

### Q: How did Sections 299 and 300 of the IPC divide culpable homicide from murder?

Section 299 defines the genus: causing death by an act done with the intention of causing death, with the intention of causing bodily injury likely to cause death, or with knowledge that the act is likely to cause death. Section 300 then marks off the graver species, murder, where the intention or the near certainty of death is at its highest, and five exceptions pull cases back down, for provocation, excess of private defence, excess of public duty, sudden fight and consent. Justice Melvill's analysis in Reg v Govinda explained the boundary as one of degree: the probability of death rises along a scale, and murder sits where that probability is highest.

### Q: Why did the Indian Penal Code leave special and local laws in force alongside it?

Section 5 of the Code provided that nothing in it affected the provisions of any special or local law. The drafters were building a general criminal code, not a monopoly. Military discipline, revenue and excise offences, municipal regulation and dozens of local conditions were already governed by laws fitted to them, and sweeping those away would have created gaps the general code could not fill. The saving also let provinces, and later states, legislate for local harms without amending the national code. The design choice explains a permanent feature of Indian criminal law: the Penal Code stood at the centre, surrounded by special statutes that it neither replaced nor governed.

### Q: Did the Indian Penal Code apply to offences committed by Indians outside India?

Yes. Section 4 of the Code extended it to offences committed by an Indian citizen in any place outside India, by any person on a ship or aircraft registered in India, and by a person who targeted a computer resource located in India. Section 3 complemented it by allowing a person liable under Indian law for an act committed beyond India to be tried in India as if the act had been done there. The reach mattered in practice for offences committed on the high seas, in foreign postings and, after the provision was widened, for electronic offences aimed at systems in India from abroad.

### Q: How did Indian states amend the Indian Penal Code for their own territories?

Criminal law sits in the Concurrent List of the Constitution, so a state legislature could amend the Penal Code as it applied within that state, subject to the constitutional rules on repugnancy and, where required, Presidential assent. States used the power across the Code's life to adjust punishments and procedures for local conditions, raising penalties for offences their governments treated as local emergencies and reshaping provisions on subjects such as offences against women. The result was a single national code with a state layer: the section numbers and definitions were common, while a lawyer moving between states still had to check the state amendments before advising on punishment.

### Q: Which countries outside India adopted codes modelled on the Indian Penal Code?

The Code was one of the British Empire's most successful exports. It was carried, with local changes, to the Straits Settlements, whose penal code became the ancestor of the codes of Malaysia and Singapore, and it shaped the penal code enacted for Ceylon, the predecessor of Sri Lanka's code. When the subcontinent was partitioned in 1947, Pakistan and the territory that became Bangladesh simply kept the Code of 1860 in force, renumbering nothing and amending it as their own statute, and it remained the penal code of both countries long after India replaced it. Burma, later Myanmar, likewise retained a code of the same lineage.

### Q: How did the enacted Penal Code of 1860 differ from Macaulay's draft of 1837?

The enacted Code was the draft after twenty years of official scrutiny, and the scrutiny cut as well as polished. Provisions of the draft were dropped, rearranged and rewritten during revision under Barnes Peacock and his colleagues, and the final text was in places more cautious than Macaulay's original. The most consequential absence ran the other way: a clause in the draft addressed to exciting disaffection was omitted from the enacted Code, leaving sedition to be inserted separately in 1870. What survived revision intact was the architecture, definitions stated once, exceptions stated once, offences graded by chapter, which is why the enacted Code still reads as Macaulay's design rather than a committee's compromise.

### Q: How did the Indian Penal Code punish an attempt that fell short of the completed offence?

Section 511, the last section of the Code, supplied the general answer: where no separate provision punished the attempt, a person who attempted an offence punishable with imprisonment, and did an act towards committing it, could be imprisoned for up to half of the longest term provided for the completed offence, or fined, or both. Many serious offences carried their own attempt provisions with heavier punishments, Section 307 for attempt to murder being the best known. Section 511 mattered because it completed the design: a code that defined offences without a general attempt rule would have left the failed offender unpunished wherever the drafters had forgotten to provide for him.

### Q: Why did sedition survive in the Indian Penal Code after independence?

The Constituent Assembly pointed one way and governments walked the other. The Assembly removed sedition from the list of permissible restrictions on free speech in Article 19(2), and Jawaharlal Nehru told Parliament in 1951 that Section 124A was obnoxious and ought to have no place in the law. Yet the governments of the new republic faced insurgency, communal violence and a fragile public order, and no ministry would surrender a weapon its predecessors had used. The First Amendment widened the speech restrictions on public order grounds, and after the Supreme Court saved the section by narrowing it in Kedar Nath Singh in 1962, repeal lost its urgency: the provision survived, read down on paper and available in the police station.
