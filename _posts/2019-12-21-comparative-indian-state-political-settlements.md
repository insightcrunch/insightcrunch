Comparative Indian state politics begins from a puzzle that the map alone cannot solve. One Constitution, one Election Commission, one set of national institutions, and yet the politics of Tamil Nadu, West Bengal, Uttar Pradesh and Kerala resemble one another so little that a voter moving between them crosses into a different political world: different parties, different idioms, different accounts of what a state government owes its people and what New Delhi may demand of it. The argument defended here is that the differences are neither accidents nor local colour. Each Indian state is a differentiated resolution of the same three tensions that run through the republic's politics, the tension between religion and the state, between the federal and the unitary pulls of the Constitution, and between rights and development; and the resolutions can be compared, rigorously, along four dimensions: class composition, language politics, leadership succession and the centre-state relationship.

## One Republic, Twenty-Eight State Political Worlds

The raw material of the puzzle is easily stated. Since 31 October 2019, when the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act of 2019 took effect, the Indian Union has comprised 28 states and 8 union territories. The Election Commission of India's statistical reports, published after every assembly election from 1951 and 1952 onward, record what those units do with the vote, and the record refuses to settle into a single national pattern. In the assembly elections of 2021, Tamil Nadu returned the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam to office after a decade out of power, West Bengal returned the Trinamool Congress with a larger majority against a full-scale national campaign by the Bharatiya Janata Party, Kerala returned the Left Democratic Front and broke a four-decade habit of alternating fronts, Assam returned the Bharatiya Janata Party, and Puducherry changed hands again. Five elections on one schedule, under one electoral law, produced five distinct kinds of verdict, each intelligible only inside the political history of the unit that delivered it. A framework that cannot explain why the same national moment lands so differently in Chennai, Kolkata, Thiruvananthapuram and Lucknow is not a framework for Indian politics at all, because the states are where most Indians meet the state: police, land records, schools, hospitals, water and agricultural prices are state subjects or shared subjects under the Seventh Schedule, and the government a citizen petitions, protests against or pleads before is almost always a state government.

The standard literature offers two ways of handling this plurality, and both fail in instructive ways. The first is the descriptive survey, the state-by-state tour familiar from textbooks and from the state chapters of general works on Indian politics: one chapter per state, each chapter accurate about its own territory, none of them answerable to a common set of questions. The survey tells the reader what Tamil Nadu contains, what Bihar contains, what Punjab contains, but it never says on what dimensions the contents are to be weighed against one another, so the chapters sit side by side like exhibits rather than evidence. The second is the single-case study, the deep monograph on one state's politics, a shelf that runs from Myron Weiner's studies of party organisation in the 1950s through the great state literatures on Bengal, Bihar, Tamil Nadu and Maharashtra. The monograph explains its own case with unmatched density: it can say why the Left Front endured in West Bengal or why the Dravidian parties displaced the Congress in Tamil Nadu. What it cannot do is discipline the explanation, because a cause that is never tested against a contrasting case may be nothing more than a local fact wearing theoretical dress. Between the survey that compares nothing and the monograph that compares only implicitly, the comparative question, on what dimensions do Indian states actually differ, and with what consequences, goes unasked.

### What makes Indian state politics hard to compare?

Three obstacles recur. The units differ wildly in scale, so Uttar Pradesh elects more legislators than the eight northeastern states combined. The evidence is uneven, with rich archives for Bengal or Tamil Nadu and thin records for smaller states. And each state speaks its own political idiom, so the same party label can name unlike organisations in different capitals.

The comparative-framework reading defended in this article is the third option, and it has a distinguished partial ancestry. Atul Kohli's *Democracy and Discontent*, published in 1990, compared regime types across Indian states and argued that the crisis of governability he diagnosed varied systematically with the coherence of the ruling organisation and the breadth of its coalition; Kohli's earlier state-level research on West Bengal, Karnataka and Uttar Pradesh had already shown that comparison, not narration, is what converts state histories into explanations. Lloyd I. Rudolph and Susanne Hoeber Rudolph, in *In Pursuit of Lakshmi* (1987), compared the political economy of Indian states through the paired concepts of a demand polity, in which organised social groups press claims upward, and a command polity, in which the state directs from above, and showed that the balance between the two differed by region and by class structure. Louise Tillin's *Indian Federalism* (2019) reconstructed the post-2000 federal architecture and its regional variety, and the state-election scholarship gathered in Suhas Palshikar, Sanjay Kumar and Sanjay Lodha's edited volume *Party Competition in Indian States* (2014) demonstrated that state party systems obey logics that national aggregates conceal. James Manor's essays on chief ministers and state politics made the same point from the side of leadership: the state arena has its own careers, its own resources and its own rules of survival. This article stands on that shelf and extends it in one direction. Where Kohli compared states to explain governability, the framework established here compares them to locate each state's settlement of the three tensions that organise this entire series, and it names the dimensions in advance so that the comparison can be checked, disputed and reused: class composition, language politics, leadership succession and the centre-state relationship.

The claim can be put in one sentence, and the rest of the article exists to earn it. Indian states are differentiated resolutions of the three House Thesis tensions, comparable along four named dimensions; the comparative framework is what makes case-pairings analytically productive. The pillar this article opens then applies the framework case by case: a study of the DMK-AIADMK system in Tamil Nadu, a study of West Bengal's passage from the Left Front to the Trinamool Congress, a study of Uttar Pradesh as bellwether and laboratory, a reanalysis of Bihar's Mandal politics, a study of the Shiv Sena's evolution in Maharashtra, a study of Akali politics in Punjab, a study of Kerala's alternation between two fronts, a study of the Andhra Pradesh and Telangana bifurcation of 2014, and a study of the Northeast's distinct settlement. Each of those studies answers the four dimensional questions for its own case; this hub article establishes the questions, populates them comparatively for the nine named cases, and adjudicates the rival framings that would dissolve the comparison into survey or monograph. The evidence base is the record the republic itself keeps: the Election Commission's statistical reports across state elections from 1947 to 2024, the text of the Constitution in Articles 1 to 4, 244, 245 to 263 and 366, the Sarkaria Commission Report of 1988 and the Punchhi Commission Report of 2010 on centre-state relations, and the scholarly literature named above.


## Comparative Indian State Politics and the Three Founding Tensions

The framework's first premise is that the states are not separate political universes. They are variations inside one constitutional order, and the order itself was built around [the three founding tensions](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/): the tension between religion and the state, settled at the founding through a secularism that regulated religious practice rather than walling it off; the tension between the federal and the unitary, settled through a Constitution that distributes legislative power yet arms the centre with emergency override; and the tension between rights and development, settled by splitting the difference between justiciable Fundamental Rights in Part III and non-justiciable Directive Principles in Part IV. The Constituent Assembly adopted the Constitution on 26 November 1949, and it came into force on 26 January 1950. B. R. Ambedkar, introducing the draft on 4 November 1948, warned the Assembly not to expect a rigid federalism: the Constitution, he said, could be both unitary and federal according to the requirements of time and circumstances. That remark is the seed of everything comparative in Indian state politics, because a constitution designed to flex between two poles will be flexed differently by different states, and the pattern of flexing is exactly what a state political settlement is.

A settlement, as the term is used here, is a state's durable configuration for answering the three tensions: durable enough to survive changes of government, specific enough to distinguish one state from its neighbour, and revisable only at moments of rupture that the record shows plainly. Tamil Nadu's Dravidian settlement survived alternation between the DMK and the AIADMK for five decades, because both parties governed inside it: social justice through reservation, Tamil linguistic primacy, suspicion of Hindi imposition and of Delhi's tutelage. West Bengal's Left Front settlement, built after 1977 on tenancy reform and panchayat power, survived seven successive elections before it broke in 2011, and its breaking is legible as a settlement change rather than a mere change of ministry. The Seventh Schedule explains why so much of the tension-settling happens at state level. Land, police, public order, public health and agriculture sit in the State List; education was moved to the Concurrent List by the Forty-second Amendment of 1976; religious endowments and charitable institutions sit in the Concurrent List as Entry 28. A state government therefore decides, in practice, how temple administration is organised, how tenancy is recorded, how the police treat a procession, and how a school teaches language, and each of those decisions is a small resolution of one of the three tensions.

The federal-unitary tension is the one whose state-level variation is written into the constitutional text itself, through [the asymmetric-federal design](/2019/01/03/federalism-indian-constitution-explained/). Article 3 empowers Parliament to form new states and alter boundaries, a power no coming-together federation grants its centre; Article 371 and its lettered variants then carve state-specific exceptions, protecting Naga customary law and land ownership under Article 371A, Hill Areas administration in Manipur under Article 371C, and analogous arrangements from Sikkim to Goa to the Hyderabad-Karnataka region; the Fifth and Sixth Schedules, read with Article 244, govern tribal areas through governors' powers and autonomous district councils. Alfred Stepan, in his 1999 essay on federalism and democracy, classified India as a holding-together federation, one in which a multinational polity devolves power to keep itself whole, and holding-together federations are constitutionally expected to be asymmetric. The asymmetry is not a defect of the design. It is the design's method for letting unlike states remain inside one union, and it means the federal-unitary tension has never had a single Indian answer: it has a Tamil answer, a Naga answer, a Kashmiri answer until 2019, and a Hindi-belt answer, each preserved in text or convention. The Article 3 power has been no dead letter: Parliament has redrawn the map repeatedly, splitting Bombay State in 1960, Punjab in 1966, Assam across the following decade and the Hindi belt in 2000, and creating Telangana in 2014, so that the fourteen states of 1956 have become twenty-eight, each redrawing a fresh negotiation of the tension rather than an administrative tidying.

The rights-development tension likewise resolves differently by state, and the sharpest evidence is land. Land reform was a state subject, and the states used the power at wildly different depths: Kerala's land reforms, legislated in 1963 and recast in 1969, took effect on 1 January 1970 and abolished the janmi landlord system outright; West Bengal's Operation Barga, run from 1978, recorded roughly 1.4 million sharecroppers and gave them heritable security without formal redistribution; Bihar's ceiling laws were evaded on a scale that left the agrarian hierarchy substantially intact into the Mandal era. Reservation policy shows the same gradient. Tamil Nadu legislated its way to a 69 percent reservation quantum and had the Tamil Nadu Backward Classes, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Reservation of Seats in Educational Institutions and of Appointments or Posts in the Services under the State) Act of 1993 placed in the Ninth Schedule by the Seventy-sixth Amendment of 1994, beyond the 50 percent ceiling the Supreme Court had set in the Indra Sawhney judgment of 1992. One tension, three states, three settlements, each defensible in its own political idiom and none of them interchangeable.

The religion-state tension, finally, is administered substantially by states even where its principles are national. Temple entry, endowment administration through bodies such as Kerala's Devaswom boards under the Travancore-Cochin Hindu Religious Institutions Act of 1950, the policing of processions and festivals, the regulation of religious trusts: these are the points where the abstract secular settlement touches citizens, and state governments of every ideology have handled them with local inflections, from Kerala's direct administrative inheritance of temple management to Tamil Nadu's Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments department, a state apparatus administering temples since the 1950s. The series' later reading of the national period showed [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) being renegotiated at the centre after 2014; the state-level point is older and quieter. The renegotiation at the centre lands on twenty-eight different settlements, and its effects are filtered, amplified or absorbed according to the configuration each state had already reached. That filtering is why a comparative framework for the states is not an ornament on the national story. It is where the national story's consequences are decided.


## The Founding Audit: Where the Settlements Came From

A settlement is a palimpsest, and the comparative audit begins by reading the oldest legible layer. For most states that layer was written between 1937 and 1956, in three founding episodes whose effects the four dimensions still carry. The first episode was rehearsal. Under the provincial autonomy of the Government of India Act of 1935, the elections of the winter of 1936 and 1937 produced Congress ministries in eight provinces, holding office from 1937 until their resignation in October and November 1939 over India's unconsulted entry into the war. Those twenty-eight months were the settlements in embryo: provincial ministries legislated tenancy protection, ran primary schools, chose the language of district administration and quarrelled with governors over the same ground their successor states would contest for a century. In Madras, C. Rajagopalachari's ministry introduced Hindi as a compulsory school subject in 1938 and provoked the first anti-Hindi agitation of 1938 to 1940, led by Periyar, a full generation before the agitations of 1965 settled Tamil Nadu's language column in its final form; the line from 1938 to the two-language policy of 1968 runs straight through the Dravidian movement's memory. The second episode was integration. More than five hundred and sixty princely states, covering two-fifths of the territory of undivided India, acceded in 1947 and 1948, Hyderabad holding out until the police action of 13 to 17 September 1948 and Junagadh until its plebiscite of February 1948. The Constitution of 1950 preserved the seam in its Part A, Part B and Part C state structure, the Part B states, Hyderabad, Mysore, Travancore-Cochin, Rajasthan, PEPSU and the rest, governed for six years under Rajpramukhs drawn from the old ruling houses, dynastic settlements administered inside a republic until the reorganisation of 1956 abolished the distinction. The seams matter comparatively because they endowed states unequally: Travancore and Cochin entered the Union with princely governments that had spent on schools, public health and a state bank for generations, the deep endowment beneath the Kerala settlement's celebrated indicators, and Mysore entered with the administrative tradition of M. Visvesvaraya's dewanship from 1912 to 1918 behind it.

The third founding episode was Partition, and it wrote the class column of two states in opposite scripts. In Punjab the exchange of populations was violent, near-total and fast: the evacuee property administration and the compensation arrangements of the early 1950s allotted land and urban property to refugee cultivators and traders, Jat Sikhs from Lyallpur and Montgomery settling the canal colonies' Indian mirror, Khatri and Arora refugees remaking the towns, so that by the later 1950s the refugee had been absorbed into the settlement as a proprietor. In Bengal the influx came in waves, 1947, 1950, 1964 and 1971, and was never absorbed on the same terms: Joya Chatterji's *The Spoils of Partition* of 2007 documents how the West Bengal state, pleading scarcity, left the refugees to seize their own footholds in the camp colonies and the squatter colonies of Calcutta's fringe, Bijoygarh founded in 1948 among the first, and created in the udbastu, the uprooted, a permanent political constituency whose grievances the Left converted into its urban machinery. Beneath both episodes lies the deepest layer of all: the Permanent Settlement of 1793, by which Cornwallis's Bengal government fixed the land revenue on the zamindars in perpetuity and created, in Bengal and Bihar alike, the rentier order whose educated sons became the bhadralok. One founding instrument, two destinies: Bengal's zamindari class faced Calcutta's port, professions and universities and mutated into a service middle class, while Bihar's faced only the countryside and hardened into the landlordism the twentieth century could not reform. The class dimension of every eastern settlement is, at bottom, an argument between those two readings of 1793.

The founding audit disciplines the framework's claims in one further way: it separates what a settlement inherited from what it chose. Tamil Nadu inherited a language quarrel from 1938 but chose, in 1967 and 1968, to constitutionalise it into a two-language state. Kerala inherited schools and a princely bureaucracy but chose, in 1957 and after, to put a Communist party in charge of the inheritance. Bihar inherited the Permanent Settlement's hardest form and chose, repeatedly, not to disturb it. Settlements are therefore neither destinies nor inventions; they are inheritances under continuous renegotiation, and the four dimensions are the instruments by which the renegotiation is conducted. With the founding layers read, the audit can proceed dimension by dimension.

## Dimension One: Class Composition, the Foundation Every Settlement Rests On

The first dimension asks who, in a given state, holds the land, staffs the parties and supplies the ministers: which classes, and which caste-class blocs within them, form the coalition that a settlement must satisfy to endure. The Rudolphs gave the comparative literature its most durable class actor in the bullock capitalists of *In Pursuit of Lakshmi* (1987), the self-employed peasant proprietors who owned their means of production, hired some labour and marketed a surplus, and whose political weight grew with the Green Revolution. M. N. Srinivas had earlier supplied the bridging concept in his 1959 essay "The Dominant Caste in Rampura" in the *American Anthropologist*: a caste is dominant in a locality when it combines numerical strength, ownership of a large share of the arable land and a high place in the ritual hierarchy, and dominance so defined converts directly into political command. Class composition in the Indian states is therefore never read off income alone. It is read off the fit between land control, caste rank and numbers, and the states differ precisely in how those three variables combine.

Tamil Nadu's settlement rests on the earliest organised answer to that fit. The South Indian Liberal Federation, founded in 1916 and known by its newspaper as the Justice Party, mobilised non-Brahmin landholders, merchants and professionals against a Brahmin preponderance in the Madras services that was grossly disproportionate to a Brahmin share of roughly three percent of the presidency's population. Periyar E. V. Ramasamy's Self-Respect Movement, launched in 1925, radicalised the claim into a civilisational critique, and the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, founded by C. N. Annadurai in 1949, converted it into an electoral machine. The class coalition the Dravidian settlement governs is a layered one: dominant agricultural castes of the regions, Vanniyars in the north, Thevars in the south, Gounders in the west, joined with urban traders, the Tamil film industry's new wealth and an expanding educated middle class produced by the state's own reservation regime. The point for comparison is that Tamil Nadu politicised the class question as a caste question early, thoroughly and under regional rather than national party control, and the paired study of the DMK-AIADMK system in this pillar traces how two parties born of one movement took turns administering the same coalition.

West Bengal assembled an unlike coalition. Its historic core was the bhadralok, the educated, largely upper-caste Hindu middle class of Calcutta and the district towns, swollen after 1947 by refugees from East Bengal whose dispossession radicalised a generation; beneath it, the Left Front's achievement after 1977 was to bind the middle peasantry and the sharecroppers to the party through organisation rather than rhetoric. The panchayat elections of 1978 put elected Communist-led bodies into the villages, and Operation Barga used them to record the bargadars whose names had never entered the revenue record. The settlement's class logic was tenancy security plus institutional presence: the middle peasant kept his land, the sharecropper gained heritable rights, and the party gained a rural machine that no rival matched for three decades. Whether caste was absent from Bengal's politics or merely submerged beneath class language is a live scholarly dispute, and the dispute itself is diagnostic: Bengal is the case where the class dimension presents itself in its purest form, with caste operating, if at all, through the composition of the bhadralok and the Scheduled Caste concentration in particular districts rather than as an open electoral idiom. The study of Bengal's passage from the Left Front to the Trinamool Congress examines what replaced that coalition after 2011.

Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, the two great Hindi-belt cases the pillar pairs against Bengal, ran the class dimension through caste with the filter removed. Zamindari abolition, legislated in Uttar Pradesh in 1950 and effective from 1952, destroyed the rentier layer but left land with the superior tenants, disproportionately upper-caste in the east and Jat in the west; Charan Singh built a career and a doctrine on the middle peasant proprietor who emerged from that settlement, first inside the Congress and then outside it. When the Mandal Commission's logic reached the ballot box after 1989, class composition in both states crystallised into competing caste blocs with party addresses: the Samajwadi Party, founded by Mulayam Singh Yadav in 1992, for the Yadav-led backward bloc; the Bahujan Samaj Party, founded by Kanshi Ram in 1984, for the Dalit bloc the bhadralok-style left had never organised in the state; and in Bihar, after Karpoori Thakur's implementation in 1978 of the Mungeri Lal Commission's reservation scheme for backward classes in state services, the long Yadav ascendancy that Lalu Prasad Yadav's victory of 1990 confirmed. Francine Frankel's studies of Bihar's agrarian order documented why the ceiling laws failed there: the forward castes, Bhumihar, Rajput, Brahmin and Kayastha, held the land, the Congress organisation and the district administration simultaneously, so every reform statute passed through the hands of the interests it was meant to curtail. The pillar's studies of Uttar Pradesh as bellwether and of Bihar's Mandal politics take the two halves of that story in turn.

### Why did land reform succeed in Kerala and West Bengal but stall in Bihar?

Party organisation made the difference. In Kerala, Communist cadres and peasant unions existed in the villages that reform had to reach, and in West Bengal the Left Front used elected panchayats from 1978 to record sharecroppers. In Bihar, the landed castes controlled the Congress organisation and the revenue administration alike, so ceiling laws were drafted to be evaded.

Maharashtra's class settlement is the Congress system's most successful provincial variant, and its machinery was economic before it was electoral. The Maratha-Kunbi cluster, roughly a third of the state's population, combined landholding across the Deccan districts with control of the cooperative sector: the first cooperative sugar factory at Pravaranagar, founded under Vithalrao Vikhe Patil in 1950, became the template for an archipelago of sugar cooperatives, district central cooperative banks and agricultural marketing societies through which rural credit, cane prices and political careers all flowed. Y. B. Chavan and his successors staffed the state Congress from that cooperative base, and the settlement's durability, Maratha predominance tempered by the inclusion of OBC, Dalit and Muslim junior partners, is the background against which the Shiv Sena's urban insurgency after 1966 must be read: the Sena mobilised a different class constituency, the Marathi-speaking urban working class and lower middle class of Bombay, against a Congress that spoke for the countryside's proprietors. Punjab presents the inverse profile. Jat Sikh landholders, roughly a fifth to a quarter of the population, rode the Green Revolution after 1966 to prosperity and political command through the Shiromani Akali Dal and the Congress in turn, while the state with the highest Scheduled Caste share in India, 31.9 percent in the Census of 2011, kept Dalit land ownership marginal; numbers without land, Punjab demonstrates, do not convert into settlement power. Kerala, finally, is the case where no single caste could dominate: Ezhavas, Syrian Christians, Nairs and Mappila Muslims each held regional pockets of land and commerce, so class organisation had to be built across community lines, and the Communist movement built it, through peasant unions, agricultural labour unions and the land reforms that took effect in 1970. The Andhra and Telangana country supplies the last pairing: Reddy and Kamma landed wealth, accumulated in the irrigated Krishna-Godavari deltas and in tobacco and chilli country around Guntur, financed rival party machines for a century, Congress and then the YSR Congress on the Reddy side, the Telugu Desam Party founded in 1982 on the Kamma side, while Telangana's dora landlordism and the armed struggle it provoked between 1946 and 1951 left a class grievance that the statehood movement later inherited. In the Northeast, the dimension bends out of shape entirely, since land in the hill states is held substantially under community and customary tenure rather than individual caste proprietorship; that bending is taken up in the complication section below.


## Dimension Two: Language Politics and the Map Language Drew

The second dimension asks what a state's language settlement is: which language commands the school, the secretariat and the street, and how the state stands toward the two rival link languages, Hindi and English. India is the rare federation whose internal map was drawn substantially by language, and the drawing was contested at every stage. The Congress had organised its own provincial committees on linguistic lines from the Nagpur session of 1920, so linguistic provinces were political facts a generation before the Constitution. The Constituent Assembly nevertheless hesitated: the JVP Committee of Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel and Pattabhi Sitaramayya counselled delay in 1949, fearing for national unity, and [the constitutional politics of linguistic reorganisation](/2019/01/19/linguistic-reorganisation-constitutional-politics/) turned on that hesitation. Potti Sriramulu's fast unto death, ending with his death on 15 December 1952, forced the issue; Andhra State was carved from Madras on 1 October 1953; and the States Reorganisation Commission, appointed on 29 December 1953 under Fazl Ali with K. M. Panikkar and H. N. Kunzru, reported in 1955. [The 1956 federal-architecture inheritance](/2019/02/10/states-reorganisation-act-1956-india/) followed: the States Reorganisation Act, effective 1 November 1956, redrew India into 14 states and 6 union territories with language as the primary, though never the sole, criterion. Every later state, from Maharashtra and Gujarat in 1960 to Telangana in 2014, was argued for in a vocabulary the 1956 settlement created.

### Why did language, rather than caste or religion, draw the internal map of India in 1956?

Language was the one identity the national movement had already organised. The Congress had run linguistic provincial committees since 1920, so linguistic provinces existed as political facts before the Constitution. Language also cut across caste and creed within each region, letting the States Reorganisation Commission present the new map as administrative reason rather than communal partition.

Within that shared inheritance, the settlements diverge along the dimension's main axis: the place of Hindi. Tamil Nadu stands at the pole of refusal. The Official Languages Act of 1963 was supposed to let Hindi succeed English as the Union's sole official language when the constitutional fifteen years ran out on 26 January 1965, and Madras State answered with the anti-Hindi agitations of January and February 1965, student protests in which scores died, several by self-immolation; the Congress never recovered the state, losing it in 1967 to the DMK. Nehru's assurance of 1959, that English would continue as an associate official language for as long as non-Hindi-speaking states wished, was written into law by the Official Languages (Amendment) Act of 1967, and Tamil Nadu under Annadurai adopted a two-language policy in 1968, Tamil and English, with Hindi excluded from the state school curriculum altogether. [The resistance to Hindi imposition in the 1950s](/2019/02/21/hindi-imposition-resistance-1950s-india/) was thus not an episode but a founding act of the Tamil settlement, renewed by every Dravidian ministry since, and asserted again in the state's refusal of the three-language formula carried in the National Education Policy of 2020. At the opposite pole, Uttar Pradesh and Bihar made Hindi the language of administration and of political ambition itself; the Hindi-belt states supply the national idiom's home ground, and their leaders address Delhi in the capital's own tongue, an advantage in the centre-state dimension that compounds quietly. Between the poles lie the negotiated positions. West Bengal cultivates Bengali pride, literary, institutional and fierce when provoked, without an anti-Hindi doctrine: Bengali identity there is a high-culture inheritance administered by the bhadralok tradition rather than a barricade. Kerala pairs Malayalam primacy, legislated for state administration through the Kerala Official Language Act of 1969, with pragmatic English for higher education and the Gulf-facing labour market. Maharashtra's Marathi settlement carries the memory of the Samyukta Maharashtra movement and its martyrs of the 1950s, and it has sharpened since 2000 in the Shiv Sena's later campaigns and in the Maharashtra Navnirman Sena's agitations after 2006 over signage, examinations and jobs for Marathi speakers. Punjab shows language fusing with religion: in the censuses of 1951 and 1961, language returns in Punjab became a proxy war, most Sikhs returning Punjabi and many Hindus returning Hindi, and the Punjabi Suba settlement of 1966 was simultaneously a linguistic state and a Sikh-majority state, a dual character the Akali study in this pillar examines. The Northeast closes the dimension by exceeding it: Arunachal Pradesh conducts its public life substantially in Hindi and English because its peoples speak dozens of mutually unintelligible tongues, Nagaland made English its official language, and the region's more than two hundred languages make the one-language-one-state formula unusable, which is one reason its settlements rest on tribe and territory instead.

The dimension also cuts across the class dimension in ways the comparison must register. A language settlement decides whose children can pass the state services examination and whose cannot: Tamil Nadu's two-language policy protected Tamil-medium students from a Hindi filter at the state level while leaving English as the mobility language, a combination that suited the non-Brahmin middle classes the Dravidian coalition had created. The Eighth Schedule's expansion tells the same story from the other end: Maithili, spoken in north Bihar and returned as Hindi in census after census by its own speakers' neighbours, won scheduled status only through the Ninety-second Amendment of 2003, alongside Bodo, Dogri and Santhali, a reminder that language recognition in India is a political achievement, not a linguistic fact. When the four dimensions are scored together in the matrix below, language is the dimension on which the settlements differ most visibly and concede least: class coalitions can be renegotiated in a single election and succession can be improvised in a night, but a state's language settlement is carried in every school textbook and railway signboard, and it changes, when it changes at all, by generational replacement.


## Dimension Three: Leadership Succession, How Power Changes Hands

The third dimension asks how a state replaces its leaders: by organisational election inside a party, by designation within a family, by charismatic acclamation that no procedure can gainsay, or by coalition arithmetic among partners; and how often replacement happens, since a settlement in which the chief ministership turns over every eighteen months distributes power differently from one in which a single figure governs for two decades. The states sort themselves into three succession technologies, and the sorting explains more about their politics than any manifesto. The first technology is organisational succession, in which a party apparatus outranks every individual and promotes by seniority and committee decision. The second is dynastic-charismatic succession, in which a founder's authority is inherited, contested or improvised within a household. The third is arbitrated succession, the old Congress method, in which New Delhi's leadership settled state leadership contests from above; the method's decay is itself one of the great facts of the period, tracked across [the Congress's dynastic-organisational tension](/2019/12/10/congress-dynastic-organisational-tension/) and its post-2014 decline.

West Bengal under the Left Front ran the organisational technology in its purest Indian form. Jyoti Basu governed from 21 June 1977 to 6 November 2000, the longest chief ministerial tenure in India at that date, and when age and health forced his resignation, the Communist Party of India (Marxist) did not hold a plebiscite or consult a family: its state committee and Politburo elevated Buddhadeb Bhattacharjee, the deputy and information minister, in an orderly internal transfer, and the Left Front won another term under him in 2001. The mechanism presumed what the settlement supplied, a cadre party whose authority was institutional. Kerala's Left Democratic Front runs a version of the same technology tempered by coalition: Pinarayi Vijayan's elevation in 2016 followed the Communist Party of India (Marxist)'s internal hierarchy, yet the front's portfolios had to be negotiated with the Communist Party of India and smaller partners, so succession there is organisational inside the lead party and contractual across the alliance. The United Democratic Front shows the Congress variant, leadership contested among K. Karunakaran's and Oommen Chandy's followings across decades and settled by a mixture of Delhi's arbitration and legislative-party arithmetic. Against these stands the Trinamool Congress, founded by Mamata Banerjee on 1 January 1998, where succession has no mechanism at all because the party and the leader are the same fact; Bengal therefore contains both poles of the dimension inside one state, separated by the rupture of 2011.

Tamil Nadu perfected dynastic-charismatic succession inside mass parties. Annadurai died in office on 3 February 1969, and the DMK's legislators and general council chose M. Karunanidhi, a founder-generation figure whose authority was organisational and literary at once; when M. G. Ramachandran broke away in October 1972 to found the AIADMK, he carried a screen charisma no committee could confer, and he governed from 1977 until his death on 24 December 1987. The AIADMK then fought over his inheritance, a party split between the Janaki Ramachandran and J. Jayalalithaa factions, reunified under Jayalalithaa by 1989, and after her death on 5 December 2016 the party passed through the Sasikala convulsion to Edappadi K. Palaniswami's ministry of February 2017, an improvisation that held the government but fragmented the inheritance further. The DMK, by contrast, routinised designation: M. K. Stalin served as working president from January 2017, succeeded his father as party president in August 2018, and took the chief ministership after the 2021 election, a transfer planned across a decade. Two parties of one movement thus exhibit the technology's two faces, designation rehearsed until it looks like procedure, and designation contested until it looks like war.

Uttar Pradesh historically exhibited the dimension's volatility extreme: between Charan Singh's first ministry of 1967 and the end of the century the state cycled through more than a dozen chief ministers, several ministries lasting months, with President's Rule repeatedly filling the gaps. The post-1989 settlement stabilised the persons while keeping the technology personalist: Mulayam Singh Yadav governed in 1989, 1993 and 2003, Mayawati in 1995, 1997, 2002 and for a full term from 2007 to 2012, each absolute within a party built around caste-bloc leadership, so succession in Uttar Pradesh happens between parties at elections and almost never inside them. Akhilesh Yadav's ministry of 2012 to 2017 added the dynastic variant, a son succeeding a father as party president in January 2017 while the father lived, and Yogi Adityanath's return in March 2022, the first Uttar Pradesh chief minister to retain office after a complete five-year term, introduced a further variant: a leader chosen by a national party's central leadership, whose succession is therefore decided in Delhi as much as in Lucknow. Bihar's record compresses the whole dimension into two careers. Lalu Prasad Yadav, chief minister from 10 March 1990, resigned on 25 July 1997 under the pressure of the fodder-scam charges and installed his wife Rabri Devi the same day, the subcontinent's most unembarrassed act of spousal designation; Nitish Kumar, in office from 24 November 2005 with one nine-month interruption, has kept the person constant while repricing the coalition beneath him, leaving the Janata Dal (United)'s alliance with the Bharatiya Janata Party in 2013, winning with the Rashtriya Janata Dal in 2015, returning to the Bharatiya Janata Party in 2017, reversing again in 2022 and again in January 2024. In Bihar, succession by alliance-switching replaced succession by election as the operative mechanism, and the settlement's voters have ratified the person through every recombination.

Maharashtra and the Andhra country supply the dimension's newest mechanism: the adjudicated inheritance. Sharad Pawar founded the Nationalist Congress Party in 1999 and led it without internal challenge for twenty-four years; when Ajit Pawar split the party in July 2023, the Election Commission's order of February 2024 awarded the party name and the clock symbol to the nephew's faction, applying the tests of legislative majority that the Commission had used a year earlier, in February 2023, to award the Shiv Sena's name and bow-and-arrow symbol to Eknath Shinde's faction after the split of June 2022 destroyed Uddhav Thackeray's ministry. Dynastic succession in the family parties has thus acquired a constitutional referee: the household still produces the claimants, but the Election Commission and, in disqualification matters, the Speaker and the courts decide which claimant owns the party. Andhra Pradesh wrote the pattern earlier and bloodier: N. T. Rama Rao, founder of the Telugu Desam Party in March 1982 and chief minister within nine months, was displaced in August 1995 by his son-in-law N. Chandrababu Naidu in a legislators' coup centred on Hyderabad's Viceroy Hotel; Y. S. Rajasekhara Reddy's death in a helicopter crash on 2 September 2009 opened a succession the Congress high command refused to settle on his son Jagan Mohan Reddy, who answered by founding the YSR Congress Party and winning the state in 2019; and Telangana's K. Chandrashekar Rao built the Telangana Rashtra Samithi around his family so completely that his defeat in December 2023 immediately reopened the question the designation of his son K. T. Rama Rao as working president in 2018 had tried to close. Punjab's Akali Dal adds the religious wrinkle: Parkash Singh Badal, chief minister five times across four decades, passed the party presidency to his son Sukhbir Singh Badal in 2008, converting a panthic movement-party, whose legitimacy once flowed from the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee and the Akal Takht, into a family firm, with consequences the pillar's Akali study weighs. Two further technologies complete the map. The arbitrated succession of the old Congress, in which Delhi's leadership granted and withdrew state ministries, reached its extreme under Indira Gandhi, when chief ministers held office at the centre's pleasure and were replaced by resolution from above; it decayed after 1989 as state leaders built bases no high command could withdraw, and its ruins are visible wherever a Congress state unit still waits for a decision its own legislators should be taking. Against it stands the hybrid technology of the Bharatiya Janata Party: an organisational party, staffed through the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh's cadre ladder, which nevertheless designates chief ministers from the centre, repeatedly choosing figures without an independent state machine of their own, Manohar Lal Khattar in Haryana in 2014, a first-time legislator, Yogi Adityanath in Uttar Pradesh in 2017, Biplab Kumar Deb in Tripura in 2018. The hybrid fuses the succession column to the centre-state column by design, since a chief minister who owes office to Delhi's parliamentary board begins the federal negotiation as a subordinate, and it is the institutional root beneath the double-engine claim examined later in this article. The Biju Janata Dal in Odisha, where Naveen Patnaik, in office from 2000, converted a designated inheritance into two decades of personal rule without a visible successor, and the Aam Aadmi Party, a movement party whose Delhi and Punjab governments both rest on a single founder's authority, mark the map's remaining corners: designation that outlived its designator's party, and charisma that has not yet faced its transfer test.

Scored across the nine cases, the dimension's lesson is that succession technology predicts settlement stability better than ideology does: organisational succession survives defeat, designation survives incumbency, and improvisation survives nothing for long.


## Dimension Four: The Centre-State Relationship, Sarkaria to Punchhi

The fourth dimension asks how a state stands toward the Union government: as a habitual opponent guarding its autonomy, as an ally trading support for resources, as a supplicant dependent on discretionary transfers, or, in the northeastern cases, as a special constitutional arrangement negotiated tribe by tribe and valley by valley. The instruments of the relationship are constitutional and fiscal at once: Article 356 and the Governor's office on the political side, Finance Commission devolution, centrally sponsored schemes and the Goods and Services Tax on the money side. No dimension is better documented, because the republic twice appointed commissions to write the relationship down. The Sarkaria Commission, appointed in June 1983 under Justice Ranjit Singh Sarkaria with B. Sivaraman and S. R. Sen, submitted its report in 1987, published in 1988: some sixteen hundred pages and 247 recommendations, preserved at the National Archives of India and still the fullest case-by-case record of centre-state friction in the federation's first four decades. The Punchhi Commission, appointed on 27 April 2007 under Justice Madan Mohan Punchhi, presented 273 recommendations in seven volumes on 30 March 2010. The two reports are the dimension's archive, and they circulate far beyond the archives: in university seminars the Sarkaria and Punchhi volumes are standard teaching material, commonly handled as presentation files that a reader can open in a [PPT viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) to work through the commission tables on governors, emergency powers and fiscal transfers. What the archive shows, read against the electoral record, is a relationship whose formal rules were repeatedly clarified and whose practice was repeatedly decided elsewhere, by which party held which capital.

### What did the Sarkaria Commission say about the use of Article 356?

Sarkaria held that Article 356 should be used sparingly, in extreme cases, and only as a last resort after every alternative had failed. The Commission wanted a warning to the state, a chance to correct the failure, and the Assembly kept in suspended animation rather than dissolved until Parliament had approved the proclamation.

The practice the Commission was answering had been brutal. Article 356, the power to impose President's Rule on a finding that a state's constitutional machinery had failed, was invoked on well over a hundred occasions in the republic's first seven decades. Its first dismissal of an elected government on frankly political grounds came on 31 July 1959, when the Centre dismissed E. M. S. Namboodiripad's Communist ministry in Kerala after the agitation known as the Vimochana Samaram, although the ministry's assembly majority was intact. In April and May 1977 the Janata government at the centre dismissed nine Congress-ruled state governments on the argument that the Lok Sabha verdict had withdrawn their mandate; in 1980 Indira Gandhi's returning Congress dismissed nine opposition-ruled governments on the mirrored argument. The nine-judge bench in S. R. Bommai versus Union of India, decided on 11 March 1994, finally fenced the power in: majority questions belong on the assembly floor, proclamations are open to judicial review, and a state government pursuing policies against the Constitution's secular basic structure alone invites intervention. Invocations fell away sharply after Bommai, and Sarkaria's institutional legacy, the Inter-State Council constituted under Article 263 in May 1990, met in full council only eleven times between 1996 and 2016, a frequency that itself measures how far the relationship runs on party channels rather than constitutional ones. Punchhi tried to go further, recommending a fixed five-year tenure for governors removable only by an impeachment-like process, consultation with states through the Inter-State Council before Concurrent List legislation, and a localised emergency that would let the Centre take a disturbed district, rather than a whole state, under its rule for a limited period. Most of it remains unimplemented, and the gap between the commissions' paper settlement and the practised one is the dimension's central datum.

Between the commissions' paper settlement and the states stands the Governor, the dimension's daily instrument. Articles 155 and 156 make the Governor a presidential appointee holding office during the President's pleasure, which in practice means on the Union cabinet's advice and at its mercy; Sarkaria's norms, that the appointee be eminent, non-partisan, from outside the state and chosen after consultation with its chief minister, were accepted in principle and honoured unevenly, and Punchhi's stronger medicine, a fixed tenure protected by an impeachment-like removal process, was never administered. The office's contested life continued into the period after 2014. In Maharashtra, Governor Bhagat Singh Koshyari swore in a ministry headed by Devendra Fadnavis in the early hours of 23 November 2019 on a claimed majority that dissolved within three days; the Supreme Court ordered an immediate floor test, and the ministry resigned on 26 November 2019 before the test could be held, Bommai's doctrine doing in days what commission prose could not. In Tamil Nadu, Governor R. N. Ravi's prolonged withholding of assent to bills passed by the Assembly carried the state government to the Supreme Court, which held in November 2023 that Article 200's direction to act as soon as possible leaves a Governor no power to stall a legislature indefinitely. The Governor remains the point where the centre-state relationship is transacted between the commissions' volumes, and every settlement prices the office into its Delhi posture.

Money tells the same story with the polarities reversed. The Fourteenth Finance Commission, chaired by Y. V. Reddy, raised the states' share of the divisible pool of central taxes from 32 percent to 42 percent for the award period beginning 1 April 2015, the largest single act of fiscal decentralisation in the republic's history, and the Fifteenth Finance Commission under N. K. Singh set the share at 41 percent from 2020, the one-point reduction accounting for Jammu and Kashmir's conversion into union territories. Against that devolution stands the centre's countervailing practice: cesses and surcharges, which are not shared with the states, grew as a proportion of central tax receipts across the same years, and centrally sponsored schemes attach central conditions and central branding to spending in State List fields. The Goods and Services Tax then pooled the fiscal sovereignty of both levels into [the cooperative-federalism architecture](/2019/07/25/gst-2017-cooperative-federalism-test/): the One Hundred and First Amendment of 2016 created the GST Council under Article 279A, where the centre holds one-third of the voting weight, the states together two-thirds, and decisions require three-fourths, an arrangement that has in practice run by consensus and that cost manufacturing states their independent indirect-tax handles while buying them a five-year compensation guarantee at 14 percent annual growth, running to June 2022.

Scored state by state, the dimension reproduces the pillar's cast in federal dress. Tamil Nadu and West Bengal are the habitual opponents: a Dravidian ministry in Chennai or a Left Front or Trinamool ministry in Kolkata has almost always faced a centre held by a rival, and both settlements encode the posture, Tamil Nadu's in its refusal of Hindi and of central education templates, Bengal's in confrontations that peaked, under Mamata Banerjee, in the February 2019 standoff between the Central Bureau of Investigation and the Kolkata Police and in the state's refusal to implement central citizenship and register programmes. Punjab's Akali Dal made the dimension doctrinal: the Anandpur Sahib Resolution, adopted in 1973 and reaffirmed at Ludhiana in 1978, demanded a genuine federal restructuring with the centre confined to defence, foreign affairs, currency, communications and railways, the maximal autonomy claim of the pre-1989 period, and its fate is bound up with the catastrophe the pillar's Punjab study recounts. Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, by contrast, play the alignment game: poor, populous and transfer-dependent, their governments have generally sought terms with whoever holds Delhi, and in the coalition decades their parties sold parliamentary support at the centre for ministerial berths and fiscal consideration. Maharashtra bargains from strength, its weight in national commerce giving its governments leverage whichever party rules Delhi; Kerala alternates its posture with its fronts, the Left Democratic Front confronting and the United Democratic Front negotiating, while its fiscal dependence on remittances and central transfers stays constant beneath the alternation. The Northeast stands outside the party-political pattern altogether: its states relate to Delhi through the special provisions of Article 371 in its lettered variants, the Sixth Schedule's autonomous councils, and the Inner Line Permit regime descended from the Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation of 1873, extended to Manipur in December 2019 by a decision taken in Delhi and received in Imphal as protection. There the centre-state relationship is not a posture but a negotiated constitutional architecture, tribe by tribe and district by district, which is why the framework treats the region as the dimension's limiting case rather than its exception.


## The Four-Dimensional Comparison Matrix

The sections above can be compressed into a single citable artifact. Table 1, the Four-Dimensional Comparison Matrix of Indian State Political Settlements, scores each of the pillar's nine cases on the four dimensions: class composition, language politics, leadership succession and the centre-state relationship. The rows are settlements, not governments of the day; a cell describes the durable configuration a state returns to across changes of ministry, and each cell summarises evidence laid out in the dimension sections and developed at length in the pillar's case studies. Read down a column and the matrix shows the range a dimension takes across the Union; read across a row and it shows whether a settlement coheres, whether its class base, language settlement, succession technology and Delhi posture reinforce one another or pull apart.

Two properties of the matrix deserve emphasis before it is put to work. The first is that its cells are dated configurations, not essences: West Bengal's succession cell would have read differently in 1995, when organisational succession was unchallenged, and it will read differently again if the Trinamool Congress ever routinises a transfer. The matrix therefore has to be redrawn at settlement changes, and the redrawing is itself a finding, since it forces the analyst to say which dimension moved and which held. The second property is what the matrix refuses to score. It does not grade governance, prosperity or administrative quality; a settlement is a political configuration, not a report card, and Kohli's governability question, whether a regime can implement its programme, is a further question the matrix prepares but does not answer. Kerala's settlement and Bihar's settlement are both perfectly legible in the table; the table is silent, deliberately, on which a citizen should prefer.


## Scoring a Settlement: Notes on Method

Because the matrix will be cited, its scoring rules belong in the open. Each cell rests on an evidence hierarchy. Electoral facts come from the Election Commission of India's statistical reports, the only series that covers every assembly election of the period 1947 to 2024 on uniform definitions: vote and seat shares, turnout, the number of parties winning seats, the survival of parties across elections. Demographic and language facts come from the decennial Census, handled with a caution the Punjab record imposes: a census language return is a political act, and the Punjabi and Hindi returns of 1951 and 1961 are evidence about the language settlement, not transparent measures of mother tongues. Centre-state facts come from the constitutional text, the two commission reports, Finance Commission awards and the reported judgments of the Supreme Court. Party documents, memoirs and leaders' speeches are admitted as evidence of what their authors claimed, and as evidence of fact only where the documentary record corroborates them. A cell is scored for the configuration that survives a change of government: the test of a settlement is what the incoming ministry, elected to repudiate its predecessor, nonetheless leaves untouched.

Scoring disputes are treated as findings rather than embarrassments. Maharashtra's class cell is disputed between scholars who read the cooperative economy as a Maratha preserve and those who stress the participation of OBC and Dalit growers, cane-cutters and directors within it; the matrix scores Maratha predominance because the chief ministership, the cooperative chairmanships and the district leaderships have run together for six decades, and the dissent is carried into the Maharashtra study rather than suppressed. Kerala's succession cell draws a parallel dispute, between the organisational reading of the Communist Party of India (Marxist)'s committee system and commentators who read the era of Pinarayi Vijayan, chief minister from 2016 and re-elected in 2021, as personal predominance inside an organisational shell; the matrix scores the technology by its transfer test, and the Kerala succession has not yet been transferred, so the cell stands as organisational with the dispute flagged. Punjab poses the live scoring problem of the decade: the Aam Aadmi Party's victory of March 2022, 92 of 117 seats under Bhagwant Mann, shattered a duopoly of Congress and Akali Dal that had alternated since 1966, yet a single verdict, however large, does not rewrite a cell under the survival rule. The Punjab row is therefore flagged provisional in the succession and class columns until the configuration the verdict created has passed a second electoral test, and the Akali study treats the question as open.

The method carries one further discipline, a revision protocol. The matrix is redrawn only at a settlement change, the moved dimension is named in the redrawing, and the previous score is retained in the case study's record with its dates, so that the pillar accumulates a dated series of matrices rather than a single timeless table. The redrawings section below applies the protocol retrospectively to 1956, 1967, the 1980s and the period after 2014. A framework that publishes its scoring rules, its disputes and its revision protocol exposes itself to correction in a way the survey and the monograph do not, and that exposure is deliberate: the comparative reading asks to be beaten on evidence, and it specifies the evidence on which it can be beaten.

## How the Dimensions Combine: Pairings That Test the Framework

A framework earns its keep when the dimensions interact, and the pillar's case-pairings are chosen to isolate single dimensions against held constants. Tamil Nadu against Kerala holds the south constant: two literate, politically saturated societies with strong regional idioms, which organised class in opposite ways, Tamil Nadu through caste blocs mobilised under a linguistic banner, Kerala through unions that organised workers across community lines because no caste could command the whole. The pairing shows in the succession column too: Kerala's organisational technology produces chief ministers few outside the state can name, while Tamil Nadu's designations produce leaders the whole country can name, and the difference is downstream of the class settlement, since a cross-community coalition needs a broker-party while a caste-bloc coalition can be inherited. West Bengal against Bihar holds the east constant and varies class organisation: both inherited zamindari's wreckage, one built a party that reached into the village and recorded the sharecropper, the other left the village to its landlords until caste assertion arrived as the substitute organisation. Uttar Pradesh against Maharashtra varies scale against stability: comparable demographic weight in the Union, opposite succession histories, one cycling ministers for three decades, the other running a single coalition's descendants from 1960 to 1995. Punjab against the Northeast tests the edge cases against each other: both stand at the federation's asymmetric rim, but Punjab's asymmetry was claimed through a mass movement inside the party system, a linguistic state with a religious majority, while the Northeast's was drafted into constitutional text around peoples the party system barely reaches.

### What happens when the four dimensions point in different directions?

The settlement strains at its weakest joint. Bihar pairs a polarised class structure with personalist succession, so every leadership quarrel becomes a caste-bloc quarrel. Kerala pairs cross-community class organisation with alternating fronts, so its strain appears as policy reversal rather than social conflict. The matrix predicts where the crack will open, not when.

Interactions also run between dimensions inside a single case, and three recur often enough to name. Language protects class: Tamil Nadu's two-language policy was never only about pride, since it kept Hindi out of the state services pipeline and reserved state employment for the Tamil-educated strata the Dravidian coalition had produced, so the language cell in the matrix underwrites the class cell. Succession imports the centre: when Bihar's Nitish Kumar changes alliance partners, a decision about Delhi's parliamentary arithmetic is simultaneously a decision about who governs Patna, and when the Bharatiya Janata Party's central leadership selected Uttar Pradesh's chief minister in 2017 and renewed him in 2022, the succession column and the centre-state column collapsed into one entry. Fiscal dependence disciplines federal posture: Kerala's Left governments confront Delhi on doctrine while depending on central transfers and on the remittance economy's stability, and the confrontation stops, reliably, at the borrowing ceiling the centre sets under Article 293; even the federation's most ideological state government negotiates its debt with the Union finance ministry. The coalition decades made these interactions national. Between 1989 and 2014, as [the coalition era's national governments](/2019/05/15/coalition-era-1989-2014-explained/) were assembled from state parties, the settlements exported themselves to Delhi: H. D. Deve Gowda moved from Karnataka's chief ministership to the prime ministership in June 1996 through [the interregnum governments of the United Front](/2019/06/06/deve-gowda-gujral-interregnums-india/), carrying a Karnataka farmer-politics idiom into the Union cabinet room; [the National Democratic Alliance assembled in 1998](/2019/06/11/1998-nda-formation-vajpayee-era/) rested on the Akali Dal in Punjab, the DMK and later the AIADMK in Tamil Nadu, the Telugu Desam's outside support and the Trinamool Congress in Bengal, so that the Union government's survival arithmetic was written in the succession and centre-state columns of the matrix; and the United Progressive Alliance after 2004 allocated portfolios to settlement parties, most consequentially the DMK, whose ministers held telecommunications and surface transport as coalition entitlements. A reader who holds the matrix can therefore read national politics of those decades as state politics by other means, which is the precise sense in which the comparative framework makes the pairings productive: the pair is never only two states, it is two resolutions of shared tensions, and the difference between them is the explanation.


## Adjudicating the Rival Framings: Survey, Case Study and Comparison

Three framings compete for the study of Indian state politics, and the disagreement between them is the one this article was commissioned to settle. The descriptive-encyclopaedic framing holds that a state is a container of facts and that knowledge advances by filling the containers completely: the gazetteer chapter, the textbook survey, the reference entry. The case-study framing holds that a state is a world and that knowledge advances by inhabiting one world completely: the monograph, the district study, the biography of a movement. The comparative-framework reading defended here holds that a state is a resolution, one among a family of resolutions of shared problems, and that knowledge advances by naming the dimensions on which the resolutions differ and testing explanations across the differences. The adjudication standard is explanatory reach. A framing wins where it can explain a pattern the others can only record, and the pattern that decides the contest is the one with which this article opened: identical national shocks land differently in different states, and the landing pattern is stable enough to predict.

Against the survey, the evidence is the behaviour of national waves. The Emergency of 1975 to 1977 was a single central act, yet its electoral aftermath in March 1977 annihilated the Congress across the north while the south, governed by settlements the Emergency had barely penetrated, returned the Congress and its allies; a container model can list both results and explain neither. The national wave of 2014 gave the Bharatiya Janata Party state governments in Maharashtra and Haryana within months, yet in November 2015 the Bihar electorate, eighteen months after voting heavily for the same party in the Lok Sabha election, installed the rival Mahagathbandhan in Patna, because Bihar's settlement routes national signals through its caste-bloc machinery and the machinery that year was assembled against Delhi's party. The survey records the Bihar result in the Bihar chapter. Only a framework that scores Bihar's class composition and succession technology in advance can say why the wave broke there and not in Jharkhand next door. Against the pure case study, the evidence is the fate of its favourite causes. Charisma explains M. G. Ramachandran's Tamil Nadu and Mamata Banerjee's Bengal with equal facility and therefore explains neither: the monograph that rests on a leader's gifts cannot say why charisma in Chennai built a two-party system that outlived the charismatic, while charisma in Kolkata built a party that remains its founder's instrument. Operation Barga becomes an explanation only at the moment Bengal is set against Bihar, where a near-identical statute book, administered by a landlord-held state, produced paper reform; the cause was never the statute. It was the organisation that carried it, and organisation is visible only in comparison.

The comparative reading is not an invention of this pillar, and its debts should be stated as precisely as its claims. Rajni Kothari's essay "The Congress 'System' in India", published in the *Asian Survey* in 1964 and developed in *Politics in India* in 1970, was the first settlement concept in the field: one dominant party containing its opposition as internal factions, a system that operated, as the pillar's earlier study of [the Kothari Congress system](/2019/11/18/kothari-congress-system-post-independence/) showed, with state-level variants from Gujarat to West Bengal. The four-dimensional framework generalises Kothari's move beyond the one-party-dominant era he described. From Kohli's *Democracy and Discontent* of 1990 it takes the discipline of comparing regime types across states and the insistence that organisation, not sentiment, carries causal weight; it declines Kohli's dependent variable, governability, as the sole question, since a settlement may govern effectively in defence of a configuration the analyst still needs to describe. From the Rudolphs it takes the class actors, the bullock capitalists and the demand polity, while declining to let a single binary stand in for a map. From Louise Tillin's *Indian Federalism* of 2019 it takes the post-2000 architecture, the fiscal and institutional setting in which the centre-state dimension is played. From James Manor's work it takes the chief minister as a political species with its own ecology. From the state party-system scholarship of Palshikar, Kumar and Lodha it takes the autonomy of the state arena, and from Yogendra Yadav's account of the second democratic upsurge and the third electoral system it takes the periodisation: after 1989, the state arena became the primary site where Indian democracy is decided, which is why a comparative framework for the states is a framework for India and not for a subfield.

The choice of four dimensions, no more and no fewer, requires its own defence, because the obvious objection is that religion deserves a column of its own in a country where the religion-state tension heads the House Thesis. The answer is that religion is not absent from the matrix; it is distributed through it, exactly as it is distributed in the states themselves. In Kerala, religion appears inside the class column, because the community blocs are the units class organisation had to cross. In Punjab, religion appears inside the language column, because the Suba settlement made Punjabi in Gurmukhi the carrier of Sikh political identity, and inside the succession column, because the Akali Dal's legitimacy was panthic before it was familial. In Tamil Nadu, religion appears as the endowments department and the atheist inheritance of the Self-Respect Movement inside a class coalition. A separate religion column would count these entries twice and would still miss the fact that the tension resolves through the other dimensions' machinery. The parallel objection, that economic structure deserves a column, fails for a different reason: class composition already carries the agrarian and industrial structure of each state, while income levels and growth rates are outcomes a settlement produces, in part, rather than components of the settlement itself. Party-system format, the third candidate for a column, is likewise an effect before it is a cause: whether a state runs two parties or seven is what the four dimensions, working together, yield. A framework that scored effects as dimensions would describe everything and explain nothing.

The framework also issues predictions by which it can be beaten, and two will be recorded here so the pillar's case studies can be marked against them. The first is that settlement change is dimension-led and appears in the matrix before it appears in seat totals: West Bengal's succession cell changed in January 1998, when the Trinamool Congress was founded as a founder-party inside a state whose other cells still described the Left Front order, thirteen years before the government changed in 2011; Tamil Nadu's class cell registered the Vanniyar assertion in the agitation of September 1987 and the founding of the Pattali Makkal Katchi in 1989, a generation before Vanniyar bargaining power reshaped alliance arithmetic in the state's north. The second prediction is that a shock which moves no dimension will move no settlement: national waves, central dismissals short of Bommai-proof abuse, and leadership deaths inside routinised designations should all be absorbed, and the record from 1956 to 2024, read through the Election Commission's statistical reports, is the test-bed. On the adjudication standard set at the head of this section, the verdict follows. The encyclopaedia is retained as the data layer, the monograph as the depth layer, and the comparative framework as the explanatory layer that tells the reader what the data mean and which depths are typical. The disagreement the brief named is therefore settled in favour of the comparative reading, not by courtesy but by reach: it is the only one of the three that can say, in advance and in public, what would count as being wrong.


## The Hard Cases: Small States, the Capital and the Northeast

The strongest objection to the framework is that it was built from large states with deep party systems and may merely describe its own evidence. The nine matrix cases are populous, archive-rich and party-dense; India also contains small states where a single industry or a single family can constitute the whole political field, a capital territory that is half a state, and a northeastern region where tribe, church, customary land law and armed movements, rather than caste and party, supply the grammar of politics. If the four dimensions cannot travel there, the framework over-fits, and the objection deserves to be met case by case rather than waved away.

The small states bend the dimensions without breaking them. Goa, a state only since 30 May 1987, holds land substantially through the comunidades, the village community institutions of the Portuguese period that own and lease agricultural land collectively, a class structure with no mainland analogue; its language settlement was settled by vote, the Opinion Poll of 16 January 1967, in which Goa rejected merger with Maharashtra, the only referendum-style decision on a state question in the republic's history, and then fought for two more decades over whether official Konkani would mean the Devanagari script alone, as the Official Language Act of 1987 provided, or the Roman script its Catholic constituency writes. Goan succession, meanwhile, is the dimension at fever pitch: ministries assembled and reassembled through defections, most starkly in July 2019, when ten of the Congress's fifteen legislators merged into the Bharatiya Janata Party in a single move. Himachal Pradesh, a state since 25 January 1971, runs the same four dimensions at low intensity: a class structure of apple horticulture, begun at Kotgarh by Satyanand Stokes in the 1920s, layered over state employment; a settled Hindi-Pahari language peace; and a two-party alternation between Congress and Bharatiya Janata Party so regular since the 1980s that the state changes its ruling party at nearly every election while its settlement barely stirs. The three states created in November 2000 test the framework's creation story, since none was linguistic: Chhattisgarh on 1 November, Uttarakhand on 9 November and Jharkhand on 15 November 2000 were carved out on claims of tribal identity, hill distinctness and developmental neglect inside the Hindi belt. Jharkhand carried the oldest claim, from the Adivasi Mahasabha of 1938 and Jaipal Singh Munda's Jharkhand Party of 1950 through the Jharkhand Area Autonomous Council of 1995, and its settlement has paired a tribal question no ministry has answered with the dimension's most volatile succession, repeated spells of President's Rule included; Chhattisgarh, created by the same week's statutes, produced a stable two-party order instead. Identical birth certificates, unlike settlements: the framework scores the difference rather than being embarrassed by it. Delhi inverts the fourth dimension altogether: as the National Capital Territory under Article 239AA, inserted by the Sixty-ninth Amendment of 1991, its settlement is a permanent negotiation with the Union, fought through the Supreme Court's judgments of 2018 and May 2023 on control of the services and then through Parliament's Government of National Capital Territory of Delhi (Amendment) Act of 2023, which took the services back. In Delhi the centre-state relationship is not one dimension among four; it is the constitution of the unit itself.

The Northeast is the objection's real weight, and its constitutional text concedes the point before the analyst can. Nagaland, a state since 1 December 1963 under the Sixteen-Point Agreement of July 1960, holds Article 371A, the strongest asymmetry in the Constitution: no Act of Parliament concerning Naga religious or social practices, Naga customary law and procedure, the administration of civil and criminal justice according to customary law, or the ownership and transfer of land and its resources applies to Nagaland unless its Legislative Assembly so resolves. Land there is held by villages, clans and tribes under customary tenure, so the class dimension's first question, who owns the land, receives an answer the mainland matrix cannot phrase: the community owns it, in a sense the audit of tenancy records nowhere captures. The statehood ladder tells the same story of settlements negotiated rather than inherited: the North-Eastern Areas (Reorganisation) Act of 1971 made Manipur, Tripura and Meghalaya full states on 21 January 1972; Sikkim entered by the Thirty-sixth Amendment of 1975 after its referendum; Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh became states on 20 February 1987. The Sixth Schedule's autonomous district councils, from the Bodoland Territorial Council created by the accord of 2003 and renewed as the Bodoland Territorial Region by the accord of January 2020, through the Khasi, Jaintia and Garo Hills councils of Meghalaya to the Chakma, Lai and Mara councils of Mizoram, govern land, forests and customary law inside states, a third tier the four dimensions register only through the centre-state column. Mizoram shows both the misfit and the fit: its settlement was negotiated between Delhi and an insurgency, the Mizo National Front's uprising of 1 March 1966, itself born of the Assam government's failures during the Mautam bamboo famine of 1959, and closed by the Mizoram Accord of 30 June 1986, which made Laldenga, the insurgent leader, chief minister of the interim government; yet the Mizoram that resulted routinised into two-party alternation and then, in December 2023, elected the Zoram People's Movement of Lalduhoma, a settlement maturing along lines the succession column can describe. Assam, the region's plains giant, fits the framework almost whole: an Assamese language settlement defended by the Assam Movement of 1979 to 1985 and the Assam Accord of 15 August 1985, a class order of tea tribes, Bengali-origin Muslims and Assamese Hindus, and a centre-state politics culminating in the updated National Register of Citizens whose list of 31 August 2019 excluded roughly 1.9 million applicants. Tripura exposes the framework's genuine gap: its tribal peoples, a majority at the merger of 1949, fell to 31.8 percent of the population by the Census of 2011 after Bengali migration from East Pakistan and Bangladesh, and the Tripura Tribal Areas Autonomous District Council governs a tribal settlement inside a state whose demography was transformed beneath it. Demography is the slow variable the matrix handles least well, folded imperfectly into class composition, and the Manipur violence of May 2023, whose characterisations by the Meitei and Kuki communities and by the state and central governments remain disputed, stands as the region's warning that a settlement can be stressed along lines, hill against valley, Scheduled Tribe status against land rights, that the four dimensions describe after the fact better than they predict in advance.

The adjudication of the objection is therefore a qualified surrender that leaves the framework standing. For the mainland and the small states, the four dimensions score every case offered against them, at intensities that vary from Goa's feverish succession column to Himachal's quiet one. For the Northeast, the framework operates as the default structure it was designed to be, and the pillar's study of the Northeast's distinct settlement supplies the augment the objection demands: a constitutional-architecture layer, Articles 371 in their variants, the Sixth Schedule and the Inner Line Permit, that sits across the four dimensions rather than replacing them, since Nagaland too has class coalitions, language settlements of English and sixteen-odd tribal languages, succession contests and a Delhi relationship, all of them legible once the layer is admitted. A framework that names its own augmentation point is stronger than one that pretends completeness, and the matrix's empty northeastern cells in the class column are a research agenda, not a refutation.


## Settlements in Motion: The Redrawings of 1956, 1967, 1989 and 2014

Settlements are durable, not eternal, and the framework's final test is whether the moments when the matrix was redrawn can be identified and shown to be dimension-led. The first draft of the matrix was the settlement of 1956 itself: the States Reorganisation Act fixed the language column for a generation, closed the princely order by abolishing the Rajpramukh offices the same year, and left the class and succession columns to the Congress system's state machines, which is why the India of 1960 can be described, in Kothari's terms, as one system with provincial accents. The first great redrawing came a decade later. On 1 November 1966 Punjab was divided, Haryana created and the hill areas transferred toward Himachal Pradesh, a language settlement that was simultaneously a religious one. In the elections of 1967 the Congress lost its majority across a swathe of states, Samyukta Vidhayak Dal coalition ministries of mutually hostile parties took office in Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, the DMK ended Congress rule in Madras permanently, and the Left established itself in Kerala and West Bengal. The redrawing of 1967 was succession-led and class-led at once: the Congress organisation ceased to be the common carrier of the state settlements, and every later configuration in the north and the south descends from that break.

The second redrawing was federal. The Janata interlude of 1977 to 1979 dismissed and restored state governments from Delhi, teaching every regional leader the price of a hostile centre; the lesson flowered in the early 1980s as the opposition chief ministers' conclaves, at Vijayawada in May 1983 and at Srinagar in October 1983, where non-Congress state governments jointly demanded a restructuring of centre-state relations, and in the southern chief ministers' council convened under Karnataka's Ramakrishna Hegde. The Sarkaria Commission was Delhi's institutional answer to that pressure, and the Telugu Desam's victory in Andhra Pradesh in January 1983 and the Asom Gana Parishad's founding in October 1985 and victory that December showed the pressure's electoral form: statehood movements and regional parties redrawing the centre-state column state by state. The third redrawing, after 1989, was class-led in the north and fiscal everywhere. Mandal re-sorted the class column of Uttar Pradesh and Bihar into the bloc parties that still hold those states; the coalition era rewrote the centre-state column by making state parties arbiters of national governments; and the economic reforms after 1991 added a practice no commission had designed, state governments competing for private investment, emblematically when Chandrababu Naidu's Andhra Pradesh built Hyderabad's HITEC City, inaugurated in November 1998, and sold the state to the world's technology firms with its chief minister as the salesman. The fourth redrawing is the contested one of the decade after 2014. A national party with a cadre organisation reaching into every state advanced what its campaigns call the double-engine claim, that a state governed by the centre's party receives the centre's favours in full, which is best understood as an attempt to homogenise the succession and centre-state columns of the matrix from Delhi. The settlements answered unevenly: Uttar Pradesh in 2017, Assam in 2016 and Tripura in 2018 were carried; Tamil Nadu in 2021 and West Bengal in 2021 refused, their class and language cells proving load-bearing; Maharashtra flipped twice, in 2019 and again in 2022, along its succession column's new fault line of adjudicated party ownership. Jammu and Kashmir stands apart as the sole case of a settlement redrawn by constitutional force from the centre, in [the abrogation of Article 370 in August 2019](/2019/07/31/article-370-abrogation-august-2019/) and the conversion of a state into two union territories that October, an act whose consequences the framework records in the centre-state column while the other three columns of the Kashmiri case remain in suspension.

Read in sequence, the redrawings vindicate the framework's second prediction. Each cluster of change followed a dimension: language in 1956 and 1966, succession and class in 1967, the centre-state bargain in the 1980s, class again after 1989, succession and the centre after 2014. No redrawing moved all four columns at once, and none arrived everywhere at the same speed; Bihar's class column was still being redrawn in 1990, a generation after Tamil Nadu's had settled, and the Northeast's constitutional layer has been under continuous negotiation from 1960 to the accords of 2020. That staggered, dimension-led pattern is precisely what a survey cannot see and a monograph cannot generalise, and it is the pattern the pillar's nine case studies take up one settlement at a time.


## Two Worked Comparisons: The Framework Applied

The matrix and the redrawings can still look like taxonomy until the framework is run end-to-end on pairs where the outcome to be explained is concrete. Take first the welfare pair, Tamil Nadu and Kerala, the two states whose social indicators embarrass their income levels. Kerala's record, analysed by Jean Drèze and Amartya Sen in *Hunger and Public Action* (1989), rests on public action: literacy of 93.9 percent in the Census of 2011, the highest in India, and life expectancy running roughly five years above the national figure, achieved on an unremarkable per-capita income. Tamil Nadu's record rests on competitive universalism inside the Dravidian settlement: Kamaraj's school midday meals of the late 1950s, generalised by M. G. Ramachandran's Nutritious Meal Programme of 1 July 1982 to every primary school child in the state, a near-universal public distribution system, and a pattern in which each Dravidian party in office outbid the other's welfare promises rather than repealing them. The framework reads the resemblance as a class-column effect reached by two routes. Kerala organised the labouring classes directly, through unions and land reform, and the fronts thereafter competed on the quality of a public system both had built. Tamil Nadu organised the intermediate castes into a dominant coalition whose electoral arithmetic made universal welfare the cheapest promise to keep, since a benefit given to all could be claimed by the coalition's every layer. The fiscal literature disputes the sustainability of the Tamil model and the remittance-dependence of the Kerala model; the comparative point survives the dispute, because in both states the welfare regime survived alternation between rival governments, the DMK and AIADMK trading office for five decades and the two Kerala fronts for four, which is the operational signature of a settlement rather than a policy.

The second pair, West Bengal and Bihar, isolates the organisation variable with something close to experimental cruelty, since the two states shared a colonial agrarian inheritance, a Congress past and a statute book. The difference was built in a single decade. Bengal's Left Front, from 1978, held panchayat elections that year and at five-year intervals thereafter, putting elected party-led bodies in every village as the machinery for recording sharecroppers and distributing the state's presence. Bihar held panchayat elections in 1978 and then none for twenty-three years, until 2001, although the Seventy-third Amendment of 1992 had made regular local elections a constitutional obligation across India; the state's villages were governed in the interval by the landlord-official nexus the settlement had never displaced. When Bihar finally appointed a serious land inquiry, the Land Reforms Commission under Debabrata Bandyopadhyay, its report of 2008 recommended statutory protection for the bataidars, Bihar's sharecroppers, and the recommendations were shelved after organised protest by the landed interests they threatened. The same Debabrata Bandyopadhyay had, as a West Bengal official, helped design the machinery of Operation Barga a generation earlier. One administrator, two states, two outcomes: the variable was not knowledge, law or intention. It was the class composition of the state and the party organisation that either embodied or confronted it, which is the first dimension doing exactly the work the framework assigns it. A survey of the two states lists these facts in separate chapters. The comparison makes them an explanation, and it is the kind of explanation, portable to Jharkhand's failures and Odisha's quieter tenancy history, that the pillar's case studies exist to multiply.


A third pair, Punjab and Haryana, holds the birth certificate constant and varies almost everything else. The two states were created on the same day, 1 November 1966, out of one Punjab, with kindred Jat agrarian societies, kindred Green Revolution prosperities in the Karnal and Ludhiana belts and a shared inheritance of Congress and Akali politics. Haryana's settlement organised itself around Jat and non-Jat arithmetic inside national parties, acquired an early notoriety for floor-crossing when the independent legislator Gaya Lal changed parties repeatedly within a fortnight in 1967 and gave Indian politics the phrase Aaya Ram Gaya Ram, and after 2014 settled into a Bharatiya Janata Party order built on non-Jat consolidation under a chief minister, Manohar Lal Khattar, chosen by the party's central leadership in the Delhi-designation manner the succession column records for Uttar Pradesh. Punjab's settlement organised itself around the panth: the Akali Dal's claim to speak for a religious community, the Anandpur Sahib programme, the catastrophe of the 1980s and the long Badal restoration. Haryana's Jats, dominant in Srinivas's sense, supplied the republic's most striking footnote to the class dimension in February 2016, when a violent agitation demanded that the state's dominant caste be classified backward for reservation purposes, dominance and disadvantage claimed in one breath by a community whose land values and army recruitment had shifted beneath it. The pair's lesson generalises: faith did not need a fifth column in Punjab because it was already present in the other four, in the class coalition the panth mobilised, in the language the Suba was demanded in, in the succession the Akali household captured and in the autonomy doctrine the state carried to Delhi. Frameworks earn their dimensions by refusing the ones the evidence can redistribute, and Punjab against Haryana is the refusal demonstrated.

## What the Framework Settles, and What It Leaves Open

This pillar's predecessor read the Indian National Congress as an organisation across [the long arc from 1885 to 2020](/2019/10/27/indian-national-congress-long-arc-1885-2020/), and the two readings are halves of one history. The Congress system's state machines were the first carriers of the state settlements: a Bengal Congress of district bosses, a Madras Congress of lawyer-notables, a Bombay Congress of cooperative barons each ran its province inside a shared national frame, and the settlements that succeeded the Congress order inherited its buildings, its factions and its enemies. The comparative framework established here is that history told from the states' side, with the party demoted from protagonist to one actor among the four dimensions. What it settles can be stated plainly. Indian states are differentiated resolutions of the three founding tensions, religion and the state, the federal and the unitary, rights and development. The differentiation runs along four dimensions that can be scored for any state from the public record: the class composition of the coalition a settlement must satisfy, the language settlement that fixes the state's public idiom, the succession technology by which power changes hands, and the posture the state strikes toward the centre. The scores cohere into settlements, the settlements change rarely and dimension-first, and the nine cases in the matrix, from the Dravidian south to the negotiated asymmetries of the Northeast, display the full range the Union contains. The pillar's nine studies apply the framework in depth: the DMK-AIADMK study stress-tests the succession column, the Bengal study the class column, the Uttar Pradesh study the collision of class and succession, the Bihar study carries the caste question toward the pillar that follows, the Maharashtra study reads class through the cooperative economy, the Punjab study reads language fused with faith, the Kerala study reads class organised across community, the Andhra-Telangana study reads the centre-state column at its most creative, a state won from Delhi, and the Northeast study supplies the constitutional layer the complication demanded.

What the framework leaves open should be stated with equal plainness, since a hub article that hides its gaps misleads the studies that follow it. Settlements nest: Vidarbha's long statehood demand inside Maharashtra, Saurashtra's distinct political memory inside Gujarat, Bundelkhand's claim across the Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh border, and the Gorkhaland Territorial Administration created by the agreement of 18 July 2011 inside West Bengal all record sub-state configurations the matrix does not score, and the device of Article 371J, inserted in 2012 to reserve development benefits for the Hyderabad-Karnataka region, shows the Constitution itself learning to settle grievances below the state line. Several substantial states remain unscored in the matrix: Gujarat, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka, Odisha and Haryana await the four questions, and nothing in the method restricts them to the pillar's nine; the framework is published here in full so that its scoring can be disputed and extended by any reader with the Election Commission's reports open. The outcomes question stands open behind everything: the framework describes configurations and deliberately declines, as the matrix section recorded, to grade them, and Kohli's governability problem, which states convert their settlements into delivered schools, recorded tenancies, functioning hospitals and honest police stations, waits on the far side of the description this article has supplied. There is finally the citizen's vantage, which no dimension captures: the settlement is experienced not as a matrix row but as the language of a ration card, the caste arithmetic of a scholarship list, the distance to a district court, the price a Delhi decision fetches in a Patna bazaar. The case studies that follow keep that vantage in view, because a framework that forgets what its abstractions are for will be captured by them.

The claim with which this article began can therefore be closed in its full form. Indian states are differentiated resolutions of the three House Thesis tensions, comparable along four named dimensions; the comparative framework is what makes case-pairings analytically productive. The states are not twenty-eight footnotes to a national text, nor twenty-eight sealed worlds. They are the republic's working answers to its founding questions, given in Tamil and Bengali and Malayalam and Punjabi, by dominant castes and bargadars and tea tribes and sugar barons, under founders and heirs and committees and accords, and they can be read together, dimension by dimension, settlement by settlement, until the pattern of the whole stands clear.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do Indian states differ politically?

They differ on nearly everything that decides how power is won and used. Their party systems range from Kerala's two disciplined fronts to Uttar Pradesh's field of caste-bloc parties; their ruling coalitions are recruited from different classes and castes; their public languages, and their terms with Hindi and English, follow settlements made generations ago; their leaders succeed one another by committee decision in one state, by family designation in a second and by alliance-switching in a third; and they face New Delhi as habitual opponents, as clients, as bargainers or as special constitutional arrangements. All of this variety operates under a single Constitution, a single Election Commission and a single body of national law, which is why the variety is a problem to be explained rather than a miscellany to be catalogued. The comparative-state framework explained in this article treats each state as a differentiated resolution of three shared tensions, scored along four named dimensions.

### Q: What is the comparative-state framework?

It is the method this article establishes for comparing Indian states as political configurations rather than as territories or as isolated stories. Each state is read as a settlement, a durable resolution of the three tensions that run through the republic, between religion and the state, between the federal and the unitary, and between rights and development. The resolution is then scored on four dimensions: class composition, language politics, leadership succession and the centre-state relationship. Scores are entered in a comparison matrix, and explanations are tested through case-pairings, two states held against each other so that one dimension varies while others stay roughly constant. The framework descends from Atul Kohli's comparative regime analysis of 1990, from the Rudolphs' political economy of 1987 and from Louise Tillin's account of Indian federalism of 2019, and it differs from them in naming its dimensions in advance, so that any reader can rescore a state and dispute the result.

### Q: Why does state-level politics vary across India?

Because the states were assembled from different materials under a Constitution designed to flex. Their agrarian orders diverged at the source: the Permanent Settlement of 1793 froze a landlord order across Bengal and Bihar, while the ryotwari settlements of Madras and Bombay dealt with peasant proprietors directly, and the princely states, two-fifths of the territory, kept their own administrations until 1948. Language movements then drew the internal map between 1953 and 1966, Partition struck Punjab and Bengal in opposite ways, refugees in one case absorbed as proprietors, in the other left as a permanent political constituency, and each region's parties grew from local soil: Dravidian, Communist, Akali, caste-bloc and tribal movements that no national party created. A Constitution that B. R. Ambedkar described in 1948 as capable of being unitary or federal as circumstances required was always going to be worked differently by such different hands.

### Q: What are the four dimensions of state political comparison?

The first dimension is class composition: which classes and caste-class blocs hold the land, staff the parties and supply the ministers, from Maratha cooperative proprietors in Maharashtra to recorded sharecroppers in West Bengal. The second is language politics: the settlement among the regional language, Hindi and English that fixes the language of schools, examinations and administration, two-language refusal in Tamil Nadu, unmarked Hindi dominance in Uttar Pradesh. The third is leadership succession: the technology by which power changes hands, organisational promotion in the Left parties, family designation in the Dravidian and caste-bloc parties, arbitration or central designation in the Congress and Bharatiya Janata Party traditions. The fourth is the centre-state relationship: the state's posture toward New Delhi, opposition in Tamil Nadu and Bengal, alignment in Bihar, bargaining in Maharashtra, negotiated constitutional asymmetry in the Northeast. Together the four scores define a state's political settlement.

### Q: How does the centre-state relationship vary across states?

It varies along a spectrum from guarded autonomy to negotiated dependence. Tamil Nadu and West Bengal sit at the opposition end, their governments habitually of a different party from the centre's and their settlements encoding resistance, on language, on fiscal conditions, on central agencies. Uttar Pradesh and Bihar play the alignment game, trading parliamentary support and administrative cooperation for transfers their budgets cannot do without. Maharashtra bargains from commercial strength with every regime in Delhi. Kerala alternates its posture with its fronts while its dependence on transfers and remittances stays constant. The northeastern states stand outside the party-political spectrum under the special provisions of Article 371, the Sixth Schedule and the Inner Line Permit. The instruments are common to all: Article 356 and the Governor on the political side, Finance Commission devolution, centrally sponsored schemes and the GST Council on the fiscal side; the settlements differ in how those instruments are played.

### Q: What is the Sarkaria Commission?

The Sarkaria Commission was the Union government's Commission on Centre-State Relations, appointed in June 1983 under Justice Ranjit Singh Sarkaria, a retired judge of the Supreme Court, with B. Sivaraman and S. R. Sen as members. It was appointed after a decade of friction between the centre and opposition-ruled states, and after the opposition chief ministers' conclaves of 1983 had demanded a federal restructuring. The Commission examined legislative, administrative and financial relations between the Union and the states, the role of Governors, the use of Article 356 and the machinery for inter-state consultation. Its report, submitted in 1987 and published in 1988, ran to some sixteen hundred pages and made 247 recommendations, broadly conserving the constitutional structure while disciplining its use: Article 356 sparingly and as a last resort, Governors chosen from outside state politics, and a permanent Inter-State Council, which the centre constituted under Article 263 in May 1990.

### Q: How does linguistic politics vary across states?

The variation runs from refusal to fusion. Tamil Nadu refuses: its two-language policy of 1968 teaches Tamil and English, excludes Hindi from the state curriculum, and treats every central language template as an imposition to be resisted, a stance founded in the agitations of 1965. The Hindi-belt states stand at the opposite pole, where Hindi is simultaneously the mother tongue, the administrative language and the idiom of national ambition. West Bengal cultivates Bengali literary pride without an anti-Hindi doctrine; Kerala legislates Malayalam primacy while relying on English for higher education and migration; Maharashtra's Marathi assertion, inherited from the Samyukta Maharashtra movement, sharpened after 2000 in campaigns over signage, examinations and jobs. Punjab fuses language with faith, since the Punjabi Suba of 1966 was at once a linguistic state and a Sikh-majority state. The Northeast exceeds the scale entirely: hundreds of languages, English official in Nagaland, Hindi serving as a link language in Arunachal Pradesh.

### Q: What is a state political settlement?

A state political settlement is the durable configuration that answers, for one state, the standing questions of Indian politics: which coalition of classes and castes must be satisfied, in which language the state speaks, how leaders succeed one another, and on what terms the state deals with New Delhi. A settlement is identified by its survival across changes of government: Tamil Nadu's Dravidian settlement governed through alternating DMK and AIADMK ministries for five decades because both parties competed inside it, while West Bengal's Left Front settlement, built on tenancy reform and panchayat organisation after 1977, is dated as a settlement precisely because it broke, in 2011, rather than merely losing an election. Settlements are neither constitutions nor governments. They are the layer between the two, the pattern a new ministry discovers it must either work within or spend its strength overturning, and they are the unit the comparative-state framework scores in its matrix.

### Q: What does class composition mean in Indian state politics?

Class composition names the social material a state's politics is made of: the distribution of land and other productive assets, the caste ranking that overlays that distribution, and the organised blocs through which the two become electoral power. The concept joins M. N. Srinivas's dominant caste, a caste combining numbers, landownership and high ritual rank, to the Rudolphs' bullock capitalists, the market-oriented peasant proprietors whose weight grew with the Green Revolution. Read through it, apparently unlike states become comparable: Maharashtra's politics is the politics of Maratha cooperative proprietors holding a third of the population and the sugar economy; West Bengal's after 1977 was the politics of middle peasants and recorded sharecroppers organised by a cadre party; Bihar's is the politics of forward-caste landlords against Yadav-led backward blocs on the most unequal agrarian order in India. A settlement's class composition sets the limits within which its parties compete.

### Q: What was the Punchhi Commission?

The Punchhi Commission was the second Commission on Centre-State Relations, constituted on 27 April 2007 under Justice Madan Mohan Punchhi, a former Chief Justice of India, to revisit the ground the Sarkaria Commission had covered two decades earlier in a polity transformed by coalition governments and economic reform. It reported on 30 March 2010 with 273 recommendations in seven volumes. Where Sarkaria had conserved, Punchhi proposed repairs: a fixed five-year tenure for Governors, removable only through an impeachment-like process; consultation with the states through the Inter-State Council before legislation on Concurrent List subjects; amendment of Articles 355 and 356 to permit a localised emergency, central rule over a disturbed district rather than a whole state, for a limited period; and a larger fiscal and planning role for the states. Most of its programme remains unimplemented, and the distance between the report's paper settlement and the practised one is itself evidence about the centre-state dimension.

### Q: Why do some Indian states have two-party competition while others have many parties?

The number of parties a state sustains reflects the shape of its class composition and the structure of its blocs. Where society sorts into two encompassing camps, two parties or two fronts monopolise competition: Tamil Nadu's Dravidian movement absorbed the non-Brahmin majority into a single tradition that split into two parties but never beyond them, and Kerala's community blocs were gathered into two fronts by the logic of coalition across class lines. Where the social order segments into ranked caste blocs of comparable weight, each bloc can sustain a party of its own, and Uttar Pradesh and Bihar run four serious parties because Yadav, Dalit, upper-caste and Muslim electorates each found separate addresses after 1989. Aggregation also responds to institutions: scholars of party systems note that parties merge and split with the level at which power is decided, so the coalition decades, when state parties could win office in Delhi, rewarded state-level fragmentation, while a centralised national contest rewards consolidation.

### Q: Why did the Fourteenth Finance Commission raise tax devolution to 42 percent?

The Fourteenth Finance Commission, chaired by the former Reserve Bank governor Y. V. Reddy, raised the states' share of the divisible pool of central taxes from 32 percent to 42 percent for the award period beginning 1 April 2015, the largest single increase in the history of tax devolution. The reasoning ran through the structure of Indian public spending: the states carry the larger share of development expenditure, on health, education, water, agriculture and policing, while the centre collects the larger share of revenue, and the Commission held that untied transfers, which states may spend by their own priorities, respect both efficiency and the diversity of the settlements better than centrally designed schemes. The increase also accompanied the abolition of the Planning Commission and the restructuring of plan assistance after 2014, compensating the states in untied funds for the discretionary plan transfers that disappeared with the old machinery.

### Q: What causes a state political settlement to break down?

Settlements break when one of their four dimensions fails under load, and the historical breaks are dimension-led. Bengal's Left Front settlement broke on its class dimension at Singur and Nandigram in 2006 and 2007, when the machinery built for tenancy reform was turned to acquiring farmland for industry and the coalition's rural base withdrew its consent. Tamil Nadu's succession dimension failed open in December 2016, when Jayalalithaa's death without a designated heir threw the AIADMK into factional war, although the state's other dimensions held and the settlement survived its party. Jammu and Kashmir's settlement was broken from outside in August 2019, by constitutional force from the centre. Tripura's was transformed slowly, by demography, as migration reduced its tribal peoples to a minority in their own state. The common thread is that elections alone rarely break settlements; a dimension must crack, and the crack, not the vote count, is the event to watch.

### Q: How do the smaller states and the Northeast test the four-dimensional framework?

They test it at its edges and leave it amended rather than refuted. The small states bend the dimensions without breaking them: Goa runs the succession dimension at fever pitch through defections while its comunidades give the class dimension a form no mainland state shares, and Himachal Pradesh runs all four dimensions at low intensity under a two-party alternation so regular that the settlement persists through constant changes of government. The Northeast presses harder, because land held under customary tenure, politics organised by tribe and church, and settlements negotiated with armed movements strain categories built for caste and party. The framework's answer is augmentation: the four dimensions still score, but across the northeastern cases they carry a constitutional layer, Article 371 in its variants, the Sixth Schedule councils and the Inner Line Permit, that the pillar's study of the region's distinct settlement supplies. Assam, fitting the framework almost whole, marks where the augmentation fades.

### Q: What is the difference between a state political settlement and a state party system?

A party system is the visible format of competition in a state: how many parties matter, which they are, and how their vote shares move between elections. A political settlement is the deeper configuration the format expresses: the class coalition a government must satisfy, the language settlement it speaks, the succession technology inside its parties and the posture it strikes toward Delhi. The two change at different speeds, which is how they are told apart. A verdict can transform a party system in one night, as the Aam Aadmi Party's 92 seats transformed Punjab's in March 2022, while the settlement beneath, Jat landholding, the panthic inheritance, the state's fiscal dependence, moves not at all; and a settlement can shift while the format stands still, as Bengal's did when the Trinamool Congress was founded in 1998 inside an apparently frozen Left Front order. Analysts who track only the format chronicle surprises; the settlement is where the surprise was prepared.

### Q: How does a case-pairing differ from a case study?

A case study maximises knowledge of one state: its archives, its personalities, its sequence of events. A case-pairing puts two states under one set of questions so that their difference can carry an explanation. The pairing works by holding dimensions roughly constant and letting one vary. West Bengal against Bihar holds the eastern agrarian inheritance constant and lets party organisation vary, which converts Operation Barga from a Bengal anecdote into evidence about what organisation does, since Bihar, with the same statute book, reformed nothing. Tamil Nadu against Kerala holds southern literacy and political saturation constant and lets the route of class organisation vary, caste-bloc against union, which is what makes their similar welfare outcomes an argument rather than a coincidence. A case study can be deep and still leave every cause untested, because a cause that never meets a contrast may be only a local fact; the pairing is the discipline that tells the two apart.

### Q: What evidence is used to compare state political settlements?

The framework rests on the record the republic keeps about itself. Electoral behaviour is taken from the Election Commission of India's statistical reports, published for every assembly election from 1951 and 1952 to 2024 on uniform definitions of votes, seats and turnout. Demography and language come from the decennial Census, read with the caution that a census language return can itself be a political act, as Punjab's returns of 1951 and 1961 were. The centre-state dimension is documented by the constitutional text, the Sarkaria Commission Report of 1988 and the Punchhi Commission Report of 2010, Finance Commission awards and Supreme Court judgments such as S. R. Bommai of 1994. Party documents, speeches and memoirs are used as evidence of what their authors claimed, and as evidence of fact only where the documentary record corroborates them. Each cell of the comparison matrix must be scored from sources of these kinds, and each score must survive a change of government before it counts as a settlement.

### Q: Why do national parties perform so differently from one state to another?

Because a national party is, in practice, a federation of state organisations that inherit different settlements. The Congress that ruled India for decades was a different animal in each state, a cooperative-baron machine in Maharashtra, a factional court in Uttar Pradesh, a declining shell in Tamil Nadu after 1967, and its national totals were the sum of those unlike parts. The Bharatiya Janata Party's cadre organisation narrows the variation without abolishing it: the same party that swept Uttar Pradesh in 2017 and Assam in 2016 was refused by West Bengal in 2021 and by Tamil Nadu in election after election, because those settlements' class coalitions and language orders were already organised by parties of their own. National waves are real, but they break on state settlements of different densities, and a party's national strategists spend their careers learning which states can be stormed, which must be bargained with and which must simply be waited out.

### Q: Why did the Congress system survive in several states after it weakened at the centre?

Because the Congress system was built from the states upward, and its parts decayed on state clocks rather than a national one. Rajni Kothari's system named a pattern in which state Congress organisations absorbed opposition factions and governed through internal bargaining; where that absorptive machinery stayed intact, the system outlived its national weakening of 1989 by years. Maharashtra's Congress, rooted in the cooperative economy, held office, with one interruption, into the mid-1990s and returned in coalition in 1999; Madhya Pradesh's Congress retained the state until 2003; Assam's Congress under Tarun Gogoi governed from 2001 to 2016, three full terms won while the party's national position collapsed around it; Andhra Pradesh's Congress, written off in the 1990s, returned to power in 2004 and governed for a decade. In each case the state unit's settlement, its factional balance, its class machinery, its succession habits, survived independently of Delhi's fortunes, and failed, when it failed, for state reasons first. The system's national obituary was therefore written at different dates in different capitals, a fact only a state-level framework can register.

### Q: What is a dominant caste, and why does it anchor so many state settlements?

A dominant caste, in M. N. Srinivas's formulation of 1959, is a caste that combines numerical strength in a locality with ownership of a large share of its arable land and a high place in the ritual hierarchy, a combination Srinivas later supplemented with education and government employment. Dominance so defined converts directly into political command: the dominant caste supplies the legislators, staffs the district party committees, controls the cooperatives and the panchayats, and arbitrates the disputes of those beneath it. The anchor castes of the settlements are a roll of dominants: Marathas in Maharashtra, Jat Sikhs in Punjab and Jats in Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh, Reddys and Kammas in the Andhra country, Vokkaligas and Lingayats in Karnataka, Yadavs across parts of Bihar and Uttar Pradesh. A settlement must satisfy its dominant castes or organise against them, which is why the concept sits at the foundation of the class dimension, and why a dominant caste's decline, or its demand to be reclassified as backward, shakes the whole structure.
