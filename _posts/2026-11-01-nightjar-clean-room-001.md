## The Round at Room Nine

The fog came off the canal an hour before midnight and took the wharf lamps one at a time, until the transit corridor of the Carrowmere bonded warehouse was lit at intervals like a street in a town that had given up on itself. Sabel Orry walked it the way she had been taught to walk it, slowly, with the oilcloth book open against her forearm and the pencil tied to her wrist with string, and she read each seal aloud before she wrote anything down, because a seal read silently was a seal half read. Room one, intact. Room two, intact. Room three, intact, the lead disc cold under her thumb, the hairline from the worn press die running through both faces the way it ran through every seal the Carrowmere die had ever pressed. The corridor smelled of wet rope and lamp oil and the iron tang of canal water through the gratings, and her boots found the worn boards without her looking, the way they had learned to do in the four months since the bonded chain had taken her on as a junior clerk and set her to the night rounds because nobody senior wanted them.

She was nineteen years of age and she wanted, with a force that sometimes embarrassed her, to be good at this. The wharf ran on paper and on habit, and she had decided early that paper was the part she could master. Eleven columns ruled across each page of the oilcloth book, one for the room, one for the seal, one for the hand that had pressed it, and the rest for the small change of a working port, and she filled every column as if the book were watching her back. Room four, intact. Room five, intact. A barge knocked somewhere out in the fog, timber against stone, and the sound came down the corridor flattened and late. Room six, intact. She wrote as she went, in the hand the chief clerk had praised once and never mentioned again, small, level, unhurried.

![A bonded transit corridor in fog, seal book open under a lamp - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-64.webp)

Room seven gave her the first wrong thing of the night, and it was a small one, the kind she had been trained to notice and trained not to make a story of. The seal was intact. The number read true against the book. But one digit of that number had been written twice in the book, the second stroke laid over the first to correct it, and a corrected digit in a seal book was a thing Voss had once spent a whole evening explaining to her: a seal number is read, not remembered, and a man who corrects a digit is a man who wrote from memory, and memory is where mistakes go to be believed. She stood with her pencil over the page and told herself that clerks were human, that the evening round had been a long one, that a digit was a digit. She wrote the room down as intact and put a small private mark in the margin, a dot, the mark she used for things she meant to ask about in the morning and did not yet have the standing to question at night. Room eight, intact.

Room nine stood at the end of the corridor where the building turned toward the press bench and the seal cupboard, and she knew something was wrong before she reached it, because the smell reached her first. Under the rope and the oil and the canal there was a sweetness, thin and patient, the sweetness of the winter fuel store two floors below when a bung had been left eased, except that the fuel store was two floors below and locked, and sweetness does not climb stairs and wait at a particular door. She stopped three paces short of room nine and stood listening, and the corridor listened with her, and there was nothing to hear. The lamp slit above the hasp showed dark. She set her own lamp down on the boards, carefully, the way a person sets down a thing they may need to find again in a hurry, and she knocked, three raps, the keeper's knock that anyone inside a transit room was drilled to answer, because a transit room was locked and sealed and opened from the corridor only, and the whole design of the place rested on the promise that no cargo was ever alone with a person and no person ever alone with a cargo. The seal was the witness. The seal said the room had been closed properly, by the book, and that nobody had passed the door since.

Nobody answered the knock. She knocked again and put her ear near the slit, and what she heard was the room itself, which is to say she heard the particular quality of air in a small sealed space where nothing was moving, not even slowly. Her hand, when she reached for the seal to read it, was steady, and she was proud of that for exactly as long as it took to read the number and match it against the book and understand what the book was telling her. The line for room nine sat on the oilcloth a fraction darker than the lines above and below it, its ink less settled, its figures formed by a hand that was not the evening round clerk's hand and had taken some trouble to resemble it. Room nine had been sealed after the evening round by somebody who had written the pressing into the book as if the round had done it. She looked at the dot she had made in the margin two rooms back and felt the night rearrange itself around her, the fog, the lamps, the long corridor behind her with its eight correct rooms, and this ninth door with its correct seal and its wrong line and its sweetness breathing patiently through the joints of the frame.

The supervisor's office stood at the corridor's other end, past the press bench, and Edran Voss kept the emergency keys on a board there, each key sealed to its hook with wire and a bead of wax, each one accounted for every morning by his own hand. Voss was a careful man in the way of men who had been careless once, early, and had built the rest of their working lives into a fence against repeating it. He checked the seal board himself. He checked the key board himself. When Sabel had made her first error in the book, in her first week, he had not raised his voice; he had sat her down and shown her his own first book, from twenty years before, and the error in it that had cost a consignment and very nearly cost him the wharf, and he had told her that the book was the only memory the place could trust, because people forgot and people lied and people, sometimes, were found where they should not be. She thought of that conversation all the way down the corridor to his office, walking faster than a clerk on rounds was supposed to walk, and the office was empty, and his coat was on its peg, and his own lamp stood cold on the desk beside a cup gone cold beside it, and the key board hung behind the desk with every wax bead unbroken except that she already knew, before she counted, which hook she was going to have to empty.

The movement ledger lay open on the desk where he always left it. The rule of the key board was written on the inside of the office door in Voss's own hand, in the block capitals he used for rules he intended nobody to argue with: the cause for breaking the seal of an emergency key is written in the movement ledger before the key is used, never after. She wrote it with the pencil still tied to her wrist, in her small level hand, the date line of the round, the hour by the office clock, and the cause, plainly, the way he would have wanted it written. Room nine lamp dark and no answer to the keeper's knock. Seal correct against the book, entry line in a second hand. Sweet smell at the door. Supervisor not in his office and his coat on the peg. She signed it with her initials and only then broke the wax of the room nine key, and the small crack the bead made going seemed to her the loudest sound the wharf had produced all night.

The storm bolt was the last thing. She knew it the moment the key turned and the door came toward her a thumb's width and stopped, because a transit room door with its storm bolt thrown from inside did exactly that, came a thumb's width and stopped, and she stood in the corridor with the fog at her back and understood that the room had been closed from within, by a person, as the bolt existed to be used by a keeper caught inside by a surge of canal water or a failure of the lamps. There was a way past a thrown bolt from the corridor side, a keeper's way, drilled into every clerk against the day a man might be trapped and senseless beyond his own door, and it took both hands and the flat bar from the seal cupboard and no elegance at all, and she did it the way she had been drilled, counting under her breath, while the sweetness thickened in the gap of the door. When the bolt gave, it gave all at once, and the door swung inward on the dark, and her lamp went in ahead of her hand.

He was seated against the great cask that filled the room's inner end, with his head down and one hand open on his knee, in the posture of a man who had sat down to rest a moment in the middle of work and had been overtaken. His lamp stood beside him, unlit, its wick unburned and its chimney clean, which was a thing she noticed the way she had been trained to notice, before she noticed anything else, and filed without understanding it. The room was cold. The cask's vent bung lay on the boards beside a tally disc of brass, and the sweetness came off the open vent in a slow breath that made the back of her throat ache. She said his name twice, the second time to the room rather than to him, and then she did the things in their order, the order he himself had written into the standing instructions she had copied out in her first week. She did not touch him beyond the two fingers at the wrist that told her what she already knew. She did not move the bung or the disc or the lamp. She backed out of the room holding her lamp high, pulled the door to without sealing it, and went down the corridor at a run for the gate bell, past eight correct rooms and one open door, with the oilcloth book still open against her forearm and the pencil swinging from her wrist on its string, and the column for room nine still empty on the page.

## Three Reads in Nine Days

The Ninth Office kept its rooms above a lock keeper's house on the east canal, in a building that had been a bonded warehouse itself once and still smelled faintly of other people's cargo when the damp got into the walls. Mira Kovac reached it an hour before dawn with the fog still standing on the water and her coat wet to the shoulder, and she climbed the stair slowly, not from tiredness, or not only from tiredness, but because the stair had seventeen steps and she had learned to spend the climb deciding what state she was in before anyone else in the building decided it for her. Her hands were cold. They had been cold since the second of the three reads Leyden had sent her on in the last nine days, a warehouse fire out past the mill bridges where the fear had been old and layered and had taken its time leaving her, and the cold was the part of it that stayed in the body after the feeling itself had gone back to being somebody else's. She had not slept since that read. She had lain down twice, in the Office's rest room and once in her own lodgings with her boots off and the lamp out, and both times her body had done everything sleep requires except the sleep.

Arvo Leyden was already at his desk, which meant he had either come in earlier than her or had not gone home, and with Leyden those two things were often the same thing wearing different coats. He was a spare man who dressed like a clerk and thought like a harbourmaster, and he rationed information the way the Office rationed everything else, by need, by weight, by what a person could carry without bending. He looked at her over the top of the message slip the wharf had sent by runner, and he did not ask her how she was, because they had settled long ago that he would not ask questions whose answers he intended to use.

"A supervisor dead in a transit room at the bonded wharf," he said. "Edran Voss. Found on the night round by a junior clerk, a girl named Sabel Orry, who did everything in the standing order and then held the corridor alone for two hours until the watch relieved her. The room was sealed. The bolt was thrown from inside. The wharf wants it to be an accident of the cargo, the chain wants it to be an accident of the man, and the harbourmaster wants it to be somebody else's by the morning tide." He turned the slip around and pushed it across the desk, not to her, but to the place on the desk where she would have to come and stand to read it. "It is yours, if you take it in the state you are in. Hear the state from me before you decide you have not. Three reads in nine days. No sleep since the second. The last one is still in your hands, unless the cold has gone out of them in the stairwell, which I can see from here it has not. If I give you this, I am giving it to an officer at the edge of her reserve, and the case will ask for reads, plural, and it will ask at the worst hours. That is the cost, named before the work, as we do it here. Refuse it and it goes to Rask alone, and no word of it follows you out of this room."

She read the slip standing, because sitting down in Leyden's office before a tasking was agreed had always felt to her like a kind of surrender she was not willing to make to furniture. The slip was short. Wharf, room, name, hour, the seal correct, the bolt thrown, the cargo a single great cask under bond for the Melora stores. At the foot, in the runner's hand, a line that had not been in any message slip she had ever been handed: the Office officer attending is requested to note that the room has been left exactly as found, at the direction of the junior clerk, who refused two orders to clear it before the watch came. She read that line twice. A clerk of four months, alone in a fog bound corridor with a dead supervisor and a chain of command pressing her to tidy the evidence away, had held the room instead. Whatever else the case held, it had begun with somebody doing her job properly at cost to herself, and Mira had a professional tenderness for that which she did not waste on many things.

"I will take it," she said. "On paper, so the paper remembers what my mouth agreed to. Rask with me, because a Stiller in the building changes what a room can do to me and we both know it. My reads are mine to spend, not yours to ration from this desk, but I will tell you before I spend the last of them, and that is a promise I have not made you before, so price it accordingly." She took the chit he was already sliding toward her, because Leyden never offered a thing he had not already decided to give, and his conditions and her conditions met on the chit in his small exact hand: the case, the wharf, her name, Rask's name beneath it, and under that the words reserve state, reduced, entered as a fact of the tasking rather than a judgement on the officer. She signed. The question of whether she would be allowed to work the case at all, and on what terms, closed there, on paper, before dawn, and it was the first of the day's questions to close and far from the heaviest.

Tomas Rask was waiting in the outer room with his coat on and two cups of the Office's bitter tea going cold on the ledge, which was his way of saying he had known the outcome before she had. He was built the way the wharf built men, through the shoulders, with a face that showed everything a half second before he decided whether to let it, and he had been her field partner often enough that they had stopped explaining themselves to each other in complete sentences. His discipline was the opposite of hers in everything but cost. Where she took the past of a place into herself and carried it, he could still a room entirely, press every working of practice flat within a circle around him, hers included, for as long as his strength held; it was blunt work and it exhausted him and it suppressed his own side as readily as any other, which was why the Office used Stillers the way a careful man uses a heavy tool, seldom and on purpose. "Supervisor," he said. "Sealed room. Leyden's face when the runner came in was worth the early hour." He handed her a cup. "Tell me what the slip does not say."

"The slip does not say whether the room is still as the clerk left it," she said, "and everything depends on that, so it is our first errand. It does not say whether the emergency key board was used, and a room locked from inside with a body in it makes that board the second witness, so we read the wax before we read anything else. It does not say whether the harbourmaster will freeze the cargo past the morning tide, and if the tide takes that cask out of room nine we are left interviewing paper. And it does not say what the gate book shows for the room that night, whether any consignment at all was booked into nine, by anyone, at any hour." She drank the tea because it was hot and because Rask had made it, which in his economy were the same reason. "Four questions and a dead man, and the wharf has had two hours to think about all of them before us. Walk, and I will tell you about the clerk on the way, because she is the best of this yet and I want you to hear it before the wharf starts explaining her to us in its own words."

They went down the seventeen steps and out into a Carrowmere that was still more fog than city, the canals holding the night's cold in long black bands between the warehouses, the first carts moving somewhere behind the mist with their lamps swinging. Her glasses were in her pocket. She needed them for close paper and resented them with the steady, unexamined resentment of a woman who had read bonded manifests by lamplight since she was seventeen and intended to go on doing it on her own terms. The wharf was twenty minutes at a working pace. She used the first of them to give Rask the clerk, the round, the key board rule, the lamp found unlit, and he listened the way he listened to anything that mattered, without interrupting and without mercy for the parts that were only colour. "And the room itself," he said at last. "The slip says sealed and bolted. What does the slip not say about the room itself." She was quiet for three more lamp posts, because the answer was the whole case and she wanted it said once and said properly. "The wharf's own people will not put it in writing yet," she said, "but the runner told the desk sergeant, and the desk sergeant told Leyden, and Leyden left it off the slip to see whether I would ask. The clerk read the room the way clerks read rooms, with her eyes, and found a dead man. Nobody has read it my way. But the watch officer who relieved her is an old bonded hand who has stood in rooms after deaths before, and he came out of the corridor and told the sergeant that room nine felt like a room in a house nobody had ever lived in. Clean, he said. Swept. He could not stand in it." She turned her cold hands over once, looking at them, the hands that had been reading rooms and cargoes since she was fifteen years of age, eight of those years without knowing there was a word for what she did, eleven years of reading in all. "In eleven years I have never walked into a room where something had certainly happened and found nothing there. Faint, yes. Old, yes. Burned down to the stones of the place, yes. Nothing, never. A room keeps what was felt in it the way a coat keeps weather. If room nine is empty, then empty is a thing somebody did, and it is the first thing in this case I am afraid of, ahead of the dead man, ahead of whoever left him there."

## What the Wharf Could Prove

The bonded wharf woke the way it always woke, in layers, the gate first and the water last. By the time Mira and Rask came through the gate arch the fog had thinned to a grey working light, and the place had begun its morning argument with itself: barrows on stone, a hoist complaining over the basin, gulls over the fish dock beyond the bonded wall, and under it all the smell of the transit warehouse, tar and wet hemp and cold iron, rolling out of the corridor doors as the day staff opened them one by one. All but one. Room nine's door stood shut at the corridor's end with a watchman in front of it who had plainly been told that his whole employment hung on the shutting, and Sabel Orry stood beside him, still in her night coat, with the oilcloth book held against her chest in both arms like something she was prepared to defend in a court. She had held the corridor for two hours against two orders to clear the room, and then she had held it for three more hours after being relieved, because nobody had told her she could leave, and she had decided that an absence of orders was not the same thing as permission. She was nineteen, hollow eyed, and furious in the contained way of a person who has done everything right and expects to be blamed for the shape of the result.

Mira took the book from her first, before the introductions the wharf's day officers were arranging themselves to perform, because the book was the witness that could not be got at in the night. Eleven columns, the hand small and level, the round complete to room eight and the room nine column empty, exactly as a round interrupted by a death should look and so rarely did. The key board, when the three of them went to the supervisor's office, told its own clean story: nine hooks, nine keys, the wax beads unbroken on eight of them and the ninth bead's fracture recorded in the movement ledger in Sabel's hand, with the cause written before the breaking, never after, in the order the dead man himself had painted on the inside of his own door. Whatever had happened in room nine, nobody had walked into it afterwards with an emergency key in their hand. That question closed on the wax, and Mira marked it closed in her own mind with the small inward nod she gave to facts that behaved. The harbourmaster's deputy arrived while they stood there, a broad woman with the tide tables already in her head and a grievance already in her mouth, and Leyden's warrant, when Rask produced it, settled the second errand without grace and without appeal: the cargo of the transit rooms was frozen under Accord seal until the Office released it room by room, the morning tide would load from the outer sheds or not at all, and room nine's cask would still be in room nine when anyone came back to ask about it. The deputy read the warrant twice, the way people read documents they intend to comply with and resent in equal measure, and signed the freeze with a pencil she broke in the signing, which Mira chose to take as the wharf's honest review of the morning.

Only then did she go down the corridor to read the room.

She had prepared herself on the walk over for the ordinary kinds of horror, because eleven years of reading had taught her what a place holds after a death in it, the fear laid down thick as paint, the violence if there had been violence, the long grief of a man who had known for some minutes that he was dying and had spent those minutes in a small room with his own thoughts. She stood in the doorway with Sabel's lamp in her hand and Rask a pace behind her, close enough that his stillness could reach her if she asked for it, and she opened herself to room nine the way she had opened herself to a thousand rooms and cargoes and burnt out cottages since she was fifteen years of age, expecting weight, and the room gave her nothing. It was not faint. It was not old. It was not the thinning of a thing nearly decayed, the way a room reads when a death in it lies months behind the reading. It was a clean and total absence, as if the cask and the boards and the stone had been made that morning and had never had a feeling spent inside their hearing. The cask stood at the inner end with its vent open. The dead man sat against it with his lamp unlit beside him and his hand open on his knee. The air smelled sweet and stale. And under her hand, when she put it flat to the door frame the way she had done since girlhood, the wood was only wood. She stood there with her palm on a wall that felt like a wall and felt the bottom go out of her professional world, quietly, the way a stair gives under a foot in the dark. It cost her more than a heavy read would have. A heavy read took her strength; this took her footing, and she came out of the doorway with her face arranged and her voice level and a fine shaking in her hands that she hid by giving the lamp back to Sabel and asking, in the tone of a woman discussing the weather, who had pressed the seal on room nine, and when, and whether the seal log was the same book as the round book or a better one.

The watch officer who had relieved Sabel in the night was waiting in the corridor when she came out, an old bonded hand with thirty seasons on the wharf and the careful diction of a man who knows that what he is about to say will make him sound foolish in a written record. He said it anyway, because she asked him the only question that mattered, which was not what he had found but what he had felt. He had stood in rooms after deaths before, he said: a fall from the hoist in the outer shed, a keeper taken by his heart in the lamp room, a drowning brought in off the basin in his second year. Rooms after deaths had a quality. They were heavy in a way that had nothing to do with air, and a man learned to breathe shallow in them until the feeling thinned, over days, the way smoke thins out of a coat. Room nine had none of it. Room nine felt, he said, like standing in a house that had been built and never lived in, swept and shuttered and waiting for a first tenant, and he had come out of it faster than a man of his years and standing liked to admit, and had sent for the Office before he sent for the chain, which was not the order the wharf paid him to follow and which he would do again. Rask, standing at her shoulder, offered the thing a Stiller can offer at the threshold of a hard read: his stillness, a radius of pressed flat quiet around her in which nothing of any room could reach her at all. She refused it, and the refusal was not pride but method, and she explained it once, for the watch officer's benefit as much as for Rask's. A stilled room told a Weigher nothing; suppression pressed every working flat, hers included, and whatever the emptiness in room nine was, it had survived in a building where a Stiller had been working all week, which meant it was not a working of the kind stillness pressed, or it was nothing stillness could touch. If she went in behind his quiet she would learn only that quiet was quiet. She had to take the room as it stood, undefended, and let it be as empty at her as it wanted to be. Rask accepted this the way he accepted most of her methods, with a nod and a repositioning of his boots so that he stood where he could see both her face and the corridor, and the watch officer, who had understood perhaps half of the exchange and all of its tone, went back to his post by the door with the air of a man who has handed a dangerous object to the correct authorities and intends to be elsewhere in his mind for the rest of the day.

The seal log was a better one, or at least a bigger one, kept in the assessors' room off the corridor, in a cabinet that also held the press dies in their fitted box and the spare lead and the cutting shears. The log recorded every seal pressed on the wharf, room by room and day by day, and it is the document the whole case would turn on, because a bonded seal is a promise made in metal: a disc of lead the size of a coin, pressed over the crossed wires of a hasp, the Accord mark on one face and the room number on the other, and the Carrowmere press die so worn with use that it left a hairline through both faces of every disc it made, a flaw the wharf treated as a signature. Any seal with the hairline had been pressed by that die, in that cabinet's reach. Any seal without it had come from somewhere else. The table below is the log's page for the night of the round, the nine transit rooms in order, each with the state of its seal as the night round found it and the initials of the assessor whose hand the log gave for the pressing.

| Room | Seal state recorded at the night round | Assessor initials |
|---|---|---|
| One | Intact, hairline present | H.D. |
| Two | Intact, hairline present | E.V. |
| Three | Intact, hairline present | H.D. |
| Four | Intact, hairline present | M.A. |
| Five | Intact, hairline present | E.V. |
| Six | Intact, hairline present | H.D. |
| Seven | Intact, hairline present, one digit corrected in the book | M.A. |
| Eight | Intact, hairline present | H.D. |
| Nine | Intact, hairline present, line entered after the round in a second hand | H.D. |

Eight rooms whose paper and metal agreed, and a ninth whose seal was correct in every particular except the hour and the hand that had recorded it. Mira read the page three times, once standing, once seated, once with her glasses on and the lamp brought close, and the page did not improve. The line for room nine had been written after the evening round had closed the book, by a hand imitating the round clerk's hand with care and not quite with success, and the initials it carried were the initials of the wharf's senior assessor, Henrik Dall, who was, as the day officers were at some pains to establish before she could ask, already on the premises, already aware, and already asking when the Office would be finished disrupting the work of the wharf. She sent for him, and for the wharf's retained Binder, and for the gatekeeper and his gate book, and she set Sabel Orry in a chair with tea she did not drink, because the clerk's evidence would be taken properly and in order, and the first interview of the case, as it so often was, would be with the person who had found the body and had the most to lose by having found it.

Sabel's evidence took an hour and gave the case its spine of hours, because she had noticed everything and arranged nothing, which is the rarest combination in a witness and the one Mira trusted most. The round begun at the hour the book said. The seals read aloud, room by room, in number order. The corrected digit at room seven, which she had marked with a dot and could not explain. The sweetness at room nine's door, which she described three separate times in three different ways, as a smell like the fuel store, as a taste at the back of the throat, as a wrongness in the air of a sealed place, and each description fitted the others the way a key fits a lock it was cut for. The knock unanswered. The office empty, the coat on its peg, the cup cold. The cause written before the key was taken. The bolt thrown. The keeper's way past a thrown bolt, both hands and the flat bar from the seal cupboard beside the press bench, counted under her breath. The lamp unlit beside him, its wick unburned, which she had noticed before she noticed he was dead, and had been ashamed of noticing, until Mira told her, in the flattest tone she could manage, that the unlit lamp was the single most important object in the case to this hour and that noticing it before everything else was not a failure of feeling but the whole of her training working at the speed it was meant to work. Sabel cried then, briefly and without noise, the way tired people cry when they are told they did a hard thing correctly, and Rask, who had said nothing for an hour, got up and made more tea with the air of a man defusing something.

The gatekeeper came next, Bram Tull, thirty years on the gate and a memory organized like his own book, in columns. The gate book was the wharf's second memory: every person and every consignment that passed the gate arch, in and out, with the hour and the hand that had written it. Mira set it beside the seal log on the assessors' table and put her glasses on, and the two books disagreed with each other in the one place that mattered and agreed everywhere else, and she was careful, at that first pass, to call it a disagreement and nothing more. The seal log said a seal had been pressed for room nine after the round. The gate book, at first reading, showed no consignment booked through the gate for room nine on that day at all: no cask in, no cask out, no stores drawn against the room's number. Every other room's seals had their matching gate lines, some of them twice over, in and out, in the ordinary untidiness of a working port. Room nine hung in the seal log with a seal and no history, a cask that had arrived from nowhere the gate had seen. A first reading of a gate book is not an audit, and she said so aloud, for the record of the room: gate books were written by tired men at busy hours and corrected in arrears, and until Petra Dunmore at the Carrowmere desk had reconciled the gate book against the tide log and the fuel store chits, line by line, the absence of an entry was a question and not an answer. But it was the shape of the case, standing up out of the paper for the first time. A seal that should not exist, pressed on a room the gate had never fed, by a hand that had taken trouble to hide itself in somebody else's handwriting.

The wharf's retained Binder was a narrow man named Odric Fenn who kept his indignation the way he kept his tools, oiled and ready. A Binder's work is the fixing of a property in place: a ward laid into a hasp so that a door holds against more than weather, a seal given a memory for the hand that set it, a contract that binds physically as well as on paper, given contact and given time. Every transit room on the wharf carried a light ward of his laying in its hasp iron, renewed by him season by season, and the second of the case's live possibilities stood up from his trade as naturally as a weed from a crack: a Binder hired to make a room secure, and paid again, by somebody else, to leave it unsecure, could explain a door that had behaved as no door should. Fenn walked the corridor with her and put his hands to room nine's hasp iron for a long minute, and then to rooms eight and seven for a long minute each, and what he reported he reported with the precision of a man who knows his report will be written down. The ward in nine was his, was old, was whole, and had not been touched, lifted, cut or persuaded in any way his hands could find; it was, if anything, in better order than its neighbours, because room nine stood nearest the press bench and got the benefit of his habit of testing the nearest ward first and the farthest last. His fee book, which he produced from his own satchel with the air of a man producing a character witness, showed the wharf's retainer and nothing else for the season: no private commissions, no evening work, no payments in any hand but the chain's. She believed him, and closed that possibility in her mind with the same small nod she had given the key board, while noting the thing his evidence actually proved, which was narrower and more useful than innocence. The door of room nine had not been defeated. Whatever had passed that door had passed it as a door is meant to be passed, with the seal pressed over it afterwards by the wharf's own die, or it had not passed the door at all.

Henrik Dall came to the assessors' room at midday, when the light through the corridor gratings had gone the colour of weak tea and the day's cargo work had resumed around the frozen rooms in a loud pretence that nothing had happened. He was a man in his middle forties with the build of the cargo work he had come up through and the manners of the office he had risen into, and he wore his assessor's gloves hooked at his belt by their cuffs, the heavy gloves of a man who handles lead and wire and cask iron all day, their palms darkened and stiffened with the seasons of that handling. He had pressed seals that day, he said, rooms one and three and six and eight among them, in the ordinary course; he had left the wharf before the evening round, at the hour the gate book would show, and he had been at his lodgings when the gate bell went in the night, and had come in at dawn when the chain sent for him, because a death on the wharf was an assessor's business before it was anybody else's. He answered what was asked and nothing that was not, in the level voice of a man who has given evidence before and intends to go on giving it at this temperature for as long as required, and the only heat he showed in an hour was when Mira asked him about Voss. "Edran Voss was the most careful man on this wharf," he said, "and I will tell you what the chain will not. He was careful the way a man is careful when he is building something. He had been building something for weeks. Checking tallies against discs, discs against the gate, the gate against the store. If he found a thing wrong in room nine, then room nine is where he would go, alone, at night, with the bolt thrown, because that is what the careful men do here, officer. They take the room alone so that nobody else can be made to share what they find in it. Whatever killed him, it used his own care as the door it came through. You may write that down and sign my name to it, because it is the truest thing anyone will tell you." She wrote it down. She also wrote down the gloves at his belt, and the stiffness of the leather at the palms, and the fact that his initials stood in the seal log against four rooms of the nine and in no place he had not accounted for, because in a case where one line of a book had been written by the wrong hand, a man's accounted hours were a wall, and walls were what she built cases out of, one verified fact at a time. The third possibility she carried out of that interview room was the one the dead man's own reputation kept suggesting: that Voss had been running an arrangement of his own inside the bonded chain, some quiet skim of cargo or paper, and that whatever he had gone into room nine to do alone at night had been his own scheme coming home to him. It was a reading the chain would love, because it buried the blame with the man, and she distrusted it for exactly that reason, and kept it on the table anyway, because a possibility an investigator dislikes is still a possibility, and the dead could not be insulted by being investigated honestly.

The assessors' room emptied slowly after the Office people went back to the corridor, and Henrik Dall stayed behind on the pretext of the day's pressing list, which was not entirely a pretext, because the wharf's work did not stop for a death and the seals of the afternoon still wanted pressing by somebody's hand. He worked through the list at the press bench with the die and the lead and the wire, and his hands, which had shaken at no point in an hour of questions, went on not shaking, because they had work to do and work had always been the place he put the parts of himself that could not be looked at. What he thought about, standing at the bench with the fog coming back early off the canal, was the wharf itself, and the thing he knew about it that the young officer with the steady eyes did not seem to know yet, or had not said she knew. The rot in the seal discipline had not begun with him, and it had not begun that season, or the season before. It had begun years back, in the small mercies of busy evenings: a line entered from memory because the rain was in the corridor and the book lived at the far end of it, a digit corrected because a tired clerk had misread his own reading and been too proud to strike the line and begin it again, a seal pressed for a consignment that was coming on the next tide and would certainly be through the gate by morning, so that the paper ran ahead of the cargo by a few hours and nobody was harmed and everybody's work was easier. He had done those things. Every assessor on the wharf had done those things. The chief clerk knew, and the chain knew, and the discipline had held anyway, held the way a rope holds, by habit and by luck, until men with money had noticed that a system held together by small mercies could be bought by the yard, at prices no assessor's wage could refuse forever. Voss had noticed the mercies. Voss had been building his case out of them for weeks, patient as frost, and the case Voss was building did not stop at one cask or one seal or one assessor: it ran the length of the roster, through names of men who had done nothing worse than write a number from memory in the rain, and when it landed it would close the wharf for a season and ruin a score of households whose only crime was the ordinary carelessness of tired people. That was the thing the Office did not understand, or understood and had priced in: on the wharf, paper was not administration. Paper was the difference between a port that fed its streets and a port under Accord seal with its gates chained, and a man who threatened the paper threatened the feeding of every family on the roster. He set the last seal of the afternoon into its box and closed the lid, and told himself, not for the first time, that whatever judgment the Office brought down the corridor, it would land on a wharf that had been failing honestly for years before anyone thought to buy its failures, and that the dead man's famous care had been, among other things, a luxury paid for by men whose names would never appear in any book as anything but initials. He was wrong about much, standing there, and the season would prove him wrong about more, but about the rot he was simply right, and some part of him knew that being right about it would not save him, and he worked on.

By the middle of the afternoon the case had acquired its false star, and it rose out of the wharf roster as inevitably as weather. The wharf employed, that week, exactly one Stiller of its own, a Teshkar man named Ondrej Pahl, retained to stand suppression over the renewal of the corridor wards, because a Binder works better and faster in a stilled room and Fenn had asked for him by name. A Stiller in the building was a fact that arranged itself, in most minds, into the shape of the whole mystery without any help from evidence at all. A room stripped bare of everything a Weigher could read: what was that, the day officers asked each other, if not a room that had been stilled to the bone, pressed flat by a man whose whole trade was pressing practice flat? Pahl's name went up and down the corridor ahead of the facts like smoke ahead of a fire. He was foreign, he was quiet, he was paid by the week and owed the wharf nothing, and he had been within the walls on the days that mattered. Mira let the talk run, because talk in a workplace under investigation is a net that catches things, and because she had not yet read the gate book against him, and because the possibility was not a foolish one: it was, on the facts as the wharf knew them, the most reasonable reading available, and she noted, for her own discipline, that she disliked it for the same reason she had disliked the reading that blamed the dead man. The readings a place provides for itself, quickly and with relief, are the readings that cost the place least. The corridor was heavy where the room was empty. That much she had already promised herself she would test before the day was out, whatever Leyden's orders about sleep, because the corridor outside room nine was a place where things had certainly happened too, arguments and passings and a death's whole approach, and if the corridor had kept its weight while the room had lost its own, then the emptiness had edges, and a thing with edges was a thing that had been done in a place, to a place, by a method that respected boundaries as a seal respects a hasp. She turned the day's facts over on the walk back to the supervisor's office, where Petra Dunmore was expected within the hour with the tide log under her arm and the fuel store chits in her case, and where Voss's own desk waited with its drawers unopened, his coat on its peg and his cold cup beside his pen, and the beginnings, somewhere in that desk, of whatever it was he had been building for weeks, patient as frost, against somebody whose name he had not yet written down.

## The Stiller Who Was Not There

Petra Dunmore arrived at the wharf in the middle of the afternoon with the tide log under her arm, the fuel store chits in a case that had once held somebody's lunch, and the expression of a woman who has already found the thing she was sent to find and is deciding how much of the room it is going to ruin. She was the Office's Carrowmere desk, paper and precedent, the analyst who read ledgers the way Mira read rooms, and she had the same professional deformity in mirror image: where Mira distrusted anything that could not be felt, Petra distrusted anything that could not be counted twice from two different books. She set the tide log and the gate book and the seal log in a row on the assessors' table, weighted them with the press die she took out of its box without asking anyone's permission, and worked for an hour in a silence that nobody in the room was foolish enough to break, while the light went down the corridor gratings in long bars and the frozen wharf ticked and settled around the investigation like a ship deciding whether to take the ground. Mira used the hour to walk the building's geography into her body, because cases turn on geography as often as on paper: the corridor with its nine rooms, the press bench and the seal cupboard at the corridor's far end beside room nine's door, the gate stair beyond them going down to the gate arch and the street, the fuel store two floors below with its own hoist, the lamp room above it, the supervisor's office at the corridor's near end with its key board and its cold cup. A building is a machine for deciding who could have been where, and by the time Petra took her glasses off and rubbed the bridge of her nose, Mira knew the machine by heart.

"The gate book is clean," Petra said. "That is the first thing, and I want it understood before the second thing frightens anybody. I have reconciled it against the tide log and the store chits, entry by entry, for the whole day and the night, and there is no consignment for room nine. Not under a wrong number, not under a corrected digit, not in arrears and not in advance. Every other room's paper closes like a door. Room nine's seal describes a cask that the gate never admitted, the tide never landed, and the wharf's own stores, on their face, never issued." She turned one of the chits around with a fingertip, the way a player turns a card he does not enjoy holding. "On their face. Because there is this. A requisition chit from the fuel store, drawn in the afternoon of that day, for one cask of winter fuel, drawn against the lamp room, signed in the store and countersigned at the loft hatch. The lamp room's own tally board shows the cask was never chalked in. The loft keeper assumed the store had shorted the delivery and thought no more of it, because the store and the loft have been feuding since before I was born and a missing cask was, to him, a message in a long standing argument. The cask did not vanish, officer. It travelled. It went up from the store on the hoist and across the building on a hand cart, on paper to the lamp room, and in fact to a room at the corridor's end whose door stood open all day awaiting stores that were never booked to it." She stood up and went out with Sabel and the flat bar and a rag, and came back ten minutes later with the rag darkened and her cuffs smelling sweet, and she laid the rag on the table like a flag. "The cask in room nine carries the fuel store's stencil under its manifest placard, half scraped and still legible if you know the store's mark, which I do, because I have now read everything this wharf has written down in a week. Its placard says salt fish in brine for the Melora stores. Its iron says winter fuel. The disc beside it says salt fish too, and the back of the disc has been filed, lately, by a man in a hurry, and under the filing there is the ghost of an older stamp. That is paper, officer. You may now have the human part of it, because the human part is worse."

The human part had been waiting in Voss's desk, in the second drawer, under a blotter weighted with a stone from the canal bank that somebody, presumably Voss, had once picked up for no reason the record would ever recover. It was a draft memorandum, four pages, in the dead man's small exact hand, addressed to the bonded chain's directors and never sent, and it was the reason a careful man had gone alone into room nine at night with the storm bolt thrown. Voss had been building a case, exactly as Henrik Dall had said at midday, in the only speech of the day that had sounded like a man describing weather he could see coming. The draft listed discrepancies the way a man lays out tools before a job: tallies that did not match discs, discs that did not match the gate, a digit corrected here, a line entered from memory there, and at the end of the list, underlined once, the fuel store pattern, a cask drawn against the lamp room in an earlier month and never chalked in, its absence explained away as a feud between two keepers who hated each other professionally and had not spoken in a year. He had found the pattern before Petra had, with nothing but the books and his own suspicion, and he had written, in the margin beside the underlined line, the single sentence that rearranged the case for everyone who read it afterwards. The fuel does not walk. Somebody carries it. He had gone into room nine to smell the cask's vent and read its stencil for himself before he put a name in the final page, because the final page of the draft was blank except for its heading, and the heading was Names, and there were no names under it. The possibility that the dead man had been running an arrangement of his own died in that drawer, in his own handwriting, and Mira closed it with a feeling she did not examine until later, which was grief of a professional kind: the wharf had lost the one man who had been trying to save it from the inside, and it had lost him to the very discrepancy he had underlined.

What the paper could not do by itself was kill a man in a locked room, and for that the case needed the building and the cask and the lamp, in that order, each established by its own method before any conclusion was allowed near it. The method was the fuel store keeper's evidence, taken standing in his own store two floors down, among the casks, in air that carried the same thin sweetness as room nine's door, fainter, the ordinary breath of the place. Winter fuel, the keeper said, was cut light for the cold months so it would pour and light in frost, and a cask of it breathed through its vent as the day warmed and cooled, breathed vapour, patiently, the way a sleeping animal breathes, and in an open store among fifty casks it was nothing, a smell and no more. In a small sealed room, with the vent eased open and hours to work in, it was the air itself going slowly wrong, and the first thing it took was flame: a lamp in bad air sickened and died before a man did, every keeper knew it, it was the reason the store's own rule was a lamp test at the door of any closed space where fuel had stood. The lamp found beside Voss had an unburned wick and a clean chimney. It had not been lit and blown out, and it had not run dry. It had been lit, and it had gone out the way a lamp goes out when the air stops feeding it, and its owner, who knew the lamp test as well as any man on the wharf, had understood what the darkness meant and had sat down anyway, because by the time a man understands that the air in a small room has gone wrong, the understanding arrives on legs that no longer entirely obey him, and the bolt he needed was across a room that had become much larger than its measurements. Petra stated the sequence once, in her driest voice, for the record of the assessors' room, and nobody in the room spoke for a while after she had stated it. There had been no violence in room nine. There had been an arrangement: a cask that breathed, a vent a curious man would ease, a lamp that would die as a warning he would read too late, a bolt he would throw himself out of the habit of care, so that no one who found him could share the blame for what he found. Whoever set it had not needed to be in the room. He had needed to know the man, and the room, and the cask, and to be able to write one line in a book afterwards and press one seal with the wharf's own die over a hasp, from the corridor side, at his leisure, so that the room would present itself to the night round as sealed and settled and nobody's business. The seal had been pressed after the death, from outside, over a door already bolted from inside, and the line in the seal log had been written in the same visit, in the evening ink Petra had spent her hour dating against its neighbours: younger than the round's entries, older than the clerk's, a line laid down in the hours when the wharf believed its supervisor was working late in his office with his coat on its peg, which, in a manner of speaking, he was.

And then the day's false star fell, the way false stars do, not with an argument but with a timetable. The case against Ondrej Pahl, the Teshkar Stiller, had assembled itself out of a roster and a reputation, and it came apart the same way, out of the gate book and the tide log and the signature of Bram Tull, who had signed the man off the wharf in his own unhurried hand and was prepared to be offended at length about the suggestion that the hand was anybody else's. Pahl had surrendered his gate pass late in the morning of that day, eleven hours before Edran Voss had signed the corridor lamp out of the supervisor's office and opened room nine's door for the last time. His boat, a Teshkar coaster that had waited two days for the ward renewal work to finish, had taken the midday tide, and the tide log carried her out with two boatmen's marks beside Tull's, three witnesses in three different kinds of ink, none of whom had any reason to conspire in the matter of a Stiller's whereabouts. He had been on the water, miles down the canal road to the sea, at every hour in which the case needed a man inside the walls. Mira put the question to Rask in the corridor, in the hearing of nobody, because it was the question the whole wharf had been asking itself since dawn and she wanted the answer on the record of the case even if the record was only the two of them. If a Stiller could not have done it, could a Stiller have done it at all: was a room stripped to nothing, she asked him, a thing his trade could make, given time, given access, given a man willing to try? Rask looked at the door of room nine for a while, the door with its correct seal gone now and its hasp standing open, and his face did the thing it did when the honest answer cost him something. "No," he said. "Not my trade. Not any Stiller's trade, whatever the roster says." She waited, because they had worked together long enough for her to know when a silence was an ending and when it was a wall, and this one was a wall with a man standing behind it who had decided, for reasons she could feel the outline of and could not see, that the wall was where he stopped. He did not say what could do it. He did not say whether he had ever seen its like, or heard of its like, in any room in any port. He said the one thing again, more quietly, as if repeating it made it more true rather than less, and then he went down the corridor to stand with Sabel, who was still holding her post by the door nobody needed held any more, and left Mira alone with the shape of the case. The shape was this. The reasonable reading was dead: no Stiller had emptied the room, no partner had smuggled, no Binder had sold his ward, and the dead man had been the investigator, not the scheme. What remained was paper that pointed at a cask, a cask that pointed at a requisition, a seal that pointed at a die in a cabinet any assessor could reach, and a corridor outside room nine that had been walked and argued in and fled down on the one night in the wharf's history when its weight would have named the weather of every passing soul. The corridor was heavy where the room was empty. She had felt the edge of it that morning, standing in the doorway with her hand on the frame, the nothing at her front and the ordinary laden air of the corridor at her back. Leyden had ordered her to sleep before she read again. Her reserve was a fact entered on a chit in his own hand, and the corridor would keep until morning, and every professional instinct she owned said the case could wait for a rested officer. The case could wait. She stood in the corridor at the close of the day shift, with the lamps coming on and the fog returning, and felt the day's questions settle into their new order behind her eyes, and knew with complete clarity that she was not going to let it.

There were two more pieces of the building to be interrogated before the light went, and Petra interrogated them with the tenderness of a woman defusing the building's last excuses. The first was the disc. A cask leaving the fuel store left it with its tally disc wired through the bung stave, wired hard, so that the disc and the cask travelled as one body and any separation showed in the wire. The disc found beside the dead man was wired through the placard ring, with fresh wire, neatly twisted, the work of a man who knew how wire should look and had not known, or had not cared, that the store's way was different from the wharf's way. Somewhere, therefore, there was another disc: the cask's own first disc, with the fuel store's stamp entire and the store's number on it, cut free of its wire at some point in the cask's short journey across the building. A man does not file a stamp off one disc and wire a second disc to a cask unless the first disc can hang him. The second piece was the seal itself, or what the night round had left of it. Sabel, who had done everything in the standing order on the worst night of her working life, had picked the cut lead of room nine's seal out of the hasp wires with her fingertips and folded it into the oilcloth book's back pocket rather than let it fall into the corridor sweepings, and she produced it now with the air of a person returning a borrowed tool. Two halves of a disc, the Accord mark on one, the room number on the other, the hairline of the worn die running through both faces exactly as the wharf's die left it. Whether it was the wharf's die that had pressed it, or a copy die cut by a craftsman who had studied the flaw, was a question with a test attached to it: the die itself, pressed into fresh lead beside the fragments, would say what it had to say in its own metal. Mira gave that errand to Rask, because his hands were steady and his patience with objects was longer than hers, and because the errand would keep him inside the building and inside the case while she did the thing she had already decided to do. He took the fragments and the die box away to the assessors' room without comment, and his lack of comment told her he knew what her decision was before she had finished making it, which was, in its way, a relief: a decision witnessed is a decision that cannot later be remembered differently.

The decision itself she made standing in the corridor, and she made it the way the Office required decisions to be made, aloud and on the record of whoever was present, so that the pressure beat of the case, when it came, would be a thing the reader of the file could watch being chosen rather than a thing that merely happened to an officer too tired to know better. Leyden's order had been an order: sleep before she read again. The order was sound. Her reserve stood at reduced on the tasking chit and the day had spent it further; the corridor read she was contemplating was not a light read, the skim of a doorway, but a full opening to the one stretch of the building where the night of the death had certainly been lived at full intensity, an argument, a vigil, a flight, all of it laid down in the boards and the stone within the last day, at the peak of its freshness, and she would take it on with no sleep behind her and a dead man's fear waiting somewhere in it with her name on it. Rask said the sensible things, once each, without wasting either of their time pretending he expected them to work. The corridor would keep until morning; weight decayed over days, not hours; a rested officer would read it cleaner; Leyden's order existed precisely to stop officers in her state from making the choice she was making. She agreed with every word, which was the worst of it, and then she gave her reasons, and they were also sound, and there were three. The wharf reopened at the morning tide, by the harbourmaster's signature on the freeze, and a reopened corridor was a trampled corridor, a hundred boots and barrows laying a day's ordinary feeling over the one night that mattered. The suspect, whoever he was, had already come back to the building once in the night, to press a seal and write a line, and men who come back once come back twice, and the disc that could hang him was still somewhere in the geography she had spent the afternoon learning. And the case's own clock was not the only clock in the building: somewhere behind the paper there was a method that could empty a room, a thing that had never happened in eleven years of her reading, and every hour the Office spent being rested and methodical was an hour in which that method was a rumour in a corridor instead of a fact in a file. Rask heard her out, and then he did the thing that made him the partner she asked for by name: he stopped arguing the decision and started arranging it. Sabel was relieved at last and sent to eat, with the book. The corridor was cleared and its lamps trimmed. The seal cupboard and the press bench were left exactly as the day had left them, in case a returning man expected them so. And Mira went to the supervisor's office and sat for ten minutes in the dead man's chair, with her hands flat on his desk and her eyes open, resting the only way she knew how to rest before a heavy read, which was not sleep and was not peace, but a deliberate emptying of her own surfaces so that what the corridor had kept would find, when she opened the door of herself at the corridor's end, as little of her in the way as she could manage.

## The Corridor Read

She went into the corridor at the supervisor's office end and walked it slowly in the lamplight with her fingertips resting on the wall the way a blind woman walks a familiar street, and for the length of the first five rooms the corridor gave her the ordinary sediment of a working building, decades of hurry and boredom and small triumphs ground down into the boards until they were no more than the texture of the place itself, and then she came within reach of room nine's end and the night of the death rose up in the stone around her all at once, the way cold water closes over a diver's head. An argument stood at room nine's door, still standing, the shape of it burned into the last few yards of the corridor at an intensity she had rarely felt from a thing a day old: two men, and one of them afraid, and the fear was not the fear of a man being threatened but the worse and more familiar fear of a man who has found the thing he was looking for and wishes, with the whole of his honest heart, that he had not found it. She knew whose fear it was by its texture before she had any right to know it at all. It was careful fear. It kept checking itself against a standard. It had a man's whole working life inside it, twenty years of books kept clean, and a sentence walked around inside it like a prisoner in a cell: the fuel does not walk, somebody carries it. She took those ten minutes into herself because taking them was the work, the last ten minutes Edran Voss had spent on the safe side of his own door, and they came with the cold and the shaking and the taste of metal, and they settled in her chest and did not ask her permission, and she understood, standing there with her hand flat on the corridor wall and her eyes shut, that they were not going to leave when the case did. Beyond the argument, further out, the corridor held a second shape, and it was the shape that turned her head, slowly, the way a compass needle turns. A vigil. Somebody had stood in the shadow of the seal cupboard for a long time on the night of the death, stood the way a held breath stands, with an intent so sustained and so carefully flattened that it had laid itself into the stone in a long low band, and then, at the end of the vigil, there had been movement, quick and relieved and guilty with relief, and the movement ran one way. She could feel the direction of it in her shoulders the way she had felt the set of a current in harbour water as a girl on the docks. Toward the gate stair. Down and out, toward the gate and the street and the ordinary night, at the hour when a supervisor lay dying or dead behind a bolted door and his lamp had already gone out in the bad air and nobody in the building knew it yet. The argument had not ended at the door. Weight does not keep words, and it does not keep faces, and on the worst nights of her working life she had been grateful for both of those mercies, but it keeps the shape of a thing with a fidelity that paper never achieves, and the shape of the argument's ending was plain to her standing there in the dark of it. The fear had turned, at the end, into something else: resolve, the particular iron taste of a man who has decided that the frightening thing and the necessary thing are the same thing and that he will do it now, alone, before his courage can be argued with. The other shape had let him go. That was the part she stood in longest, with the shaking climbing her arms: the other intent had not pressed, had not threatened, had not even, in the end, needed to persuade. It had simply stepped back, courteous as a clerk holding a door, and let a careful man walk into a room with a cask in it, and the courtesy of it was colder than any violence the corridor could have shown her. Then a door had opened and closed. Then the small sounds a room makes when it is being occupied by one man who believes himself unwitnessed: patience, method, the unhurried movements of a worker among his materials. Then the bolt, a single decisive note laid into the wood, a man locking the world out so that the world could not share his blame. And then the vigil outside had settled into its long band, and the room's sounds had thinned, and thinned, and somewhere in the thinning the lamp inside had gone out, though the corridor held no record of the moment itself, only of the watcher's attention sharpening at about the place in the night where the moment must have been, the way a man listening at a door attends harder when the sounds beyond it stop. She had what she had come for. She had the shape of the whole act: an argument at a door, a man entering alone out of care, a watcher in the shadow who waited until the waiting was done, and a flight toward the gate stair with a seal and a line of false handwriting still to make before the night round came. She stood in the corridor with Voss's fear beating in her chest like a second heart and her own reserve guttering somewhere below the place where she could measure it, and she was reaching, with the last of her discipline, for the edge of the read, for the clean withdrawal she had been taught to make before the past of a place could pull her under its surface, when a sound came up the gate stair at the corridor's end, small and unhurried, the sound of a boot that knew the steps. The read did not release her cleanly. It never did, when it was interrupted, and she came back into her own body the way a swimmer comes back into air, in pieces and too fast, with the corridor tilting once and steadying and the lamps burning too bright and her hands a half second behind her intentions. The man had come in under the arch without the gate bell, which meant he had a key or the gate watch in his pocket, and he walked the corridor the way she had walked it, quietly, a man in assessor's dress with a lamp turned low, and he did not see her standing in the room eight doorway because men returning to the scene of an arrangement never look for a witness in the first minute; they look at the place they have come for. He went past room nine's door without glancing at it. He went to the press bench at the corridor's end and set his lamp down and crouched, and his hand went under the bench into the shadow where the floor boards met the wall, and it came out holding a small disc of brass that caught the lamplight once, briefly, like a coin turning in water. She knew what it was. She knew it the way she knew everything in that corridor by then, in her bones and at a cost, and she knew that if he reached the gate stair with it, the case she had built out of paper for two days would hang a man on a smell and a stencil and a filed disc, and perhaps not even that. So she spent the last of her reserve on the oldest and plainest of her tools and stepped out of the doorway and said his name, not loudly, the way a person speaks to a horse they do not want to startle. Henrik Dall looked up with the disc in his hand and Voss's fear, still standing in the corridor around them both, did something to her face that she could not control and he could not read, and for one whole second neither of them moved, and then he ran. He ran for the gate stair because the gate stair was his, it was the direction his own flight had run in the stone the night before, and she went after him with her body arriving in pieces, her stride a fraction late, her balance a fraction borrowed, and the corridor narrowed around her as she ran, down to three things and a sound: the lamp on the bench behind her, the disc in his hand ahead of her, the boards under her boots, and the sound of his breathing, harsh and frightened, a sound she had already heard once that night laid down in weight and was now hearing again in the living air. She did not try to outrun him. She had never been the fastest person in any corridor and she had stopped needing to be years ago. She had spent the afternoon walking the building's geography into her body for exactly this reason and she used it the way she used everything, without flourish: the seal cupboard at the corridor's end stood with its door ajar on its hinges as Rask had left it after the flat bar was taken out, and as Dall came level with it she put her weight, all of it, everything the docks had built into her shoulders, into the cupboard door and swung it across his line. He hit it the way a man hits a door that was not in the corridor a second ago. The lamp jumped. The disc jumped with it, out of his hand, and rang on the boards and rolled, and she was on him before the rolling stopped, because that was the other thing the docks had taught her, that the moment after a man is surprised is worth more than any strength that comes later. He was strong. Cargo strong, like her, built by the same work. He got an elbow into the space between them and the frame of room eight's door took her shoulder and the whole corridor went white at the edges, and her right hand, when she asked it for the grip she needed on his wrist, arrived a half second behind the asking, a small cold delay that on another night might have been the whole story. It was not the whole story. She gave the wrist to her left hand instead and turned her body the way the Office had drilled her to turn it, close, fast, nothing held at distance, and used his own momentum and the door frame and the boards, and put him down on the corridor floor with his arm folded into the small of his back and her knee in the fold of it and her weight, all her weight, the cargo years and the mill town and the eleven years of other people's fear, settled over the hold like a seal over a hasp. He fought for four seconds. She has counted them since, in the dark, more than once. Four seconds of a strong man discovering that the thing holding him down was not stronger than him and was simply, flatly, not going to be moved, and that discovery reached him the way it reached everyone it had ever reached, all at once, in the body rather than the mind. Then he stopped. His breathing slowed against the boards. Somewhere near her knee the brass disc lay still, face up, stamped with a store's mark and a store's number, entire and unfiled. She did not look at it yet. She kept the hold and counted his breaths and listened to the corridor, to the whole building, and heard at the far end of it, coming fast, the boots of Tomas Rask, who had heard, through two rooms and a wall, the particular sound a press bench lamp makes when it is knocked and does not fall. He came down the corridor with her name in his mouth and stopped three paces off, reading the scene the way she had taught herself to read scenes, all of it, the door, the disc, the hold, her face, and what he said, when he said it, was not a question and not a congratulation and exactly what she needed to hear in order to trust that the pressure beat of the case was over and the corridor was a corridor again. "I have him from here," he said. "Let go slowly, officer. You are shaking hard enough to do paperwork." She let go slowly. Her hands came back to her a half second late, both of them, like messengers from a country whose time ran behind the world's, and she sat down on the corridor boards with her back against room eight's door and her head tipped back and the cold of the place going through her coat and her clothes and into her spine, and she stayed there, in the unbroken lamplight, while Rask lifted a strong man off the floor as if he weighed nothing at all and Henrik Dall, assessor, standing at last in the custody of the Ninth Office, looked down at the small brass disc beside her knee and said, to nobody in particular, the first unscheduled sentence he had offered the case: "That was under the bench since yesterday. I want that known. It fell. Things fall on a wharf." And she, with Voss's last ten minutes still beating in her chest and her reserve somewhere below the floor of herself, believed him entirely, because it had the ring of the truest kind of lie a man tells, the kind that is only lying about what it means.

## The Quiet Before She Spoke

They put Henrik Dall in the supervisor's office, in the dead man's chair, with Rask in the doorway and the gate bell silent and the wharf standing still around the frozen rooms in the last of the night, and Mira went to the assessors' room alone and sat down with the case arranged in front of her in objects, the way she had been taught to arrange it by a trade that did not trust memory: the oilcloth book, open at the round; the seal log, open at the darker line; the two halves of a cut lead seal on a square of rag; a brass disc with a fuel store's stamp entire; a second disc, filed on its back like a coin a man had tried to make into a different coin; and her own hands, flat on the table between the objects, still arriving a half second late when she reached for anything, which she noted, and factored, and did not forgive herself for, because it had nearly mattered in the corridor and on another night it would. She did not speak for a long time. The case did not need her voice yet, and there are kinds of work that only get done in the quiet before a person commits herself aloud, because once a thing is said in a room with a suspect in it, it belongs to the room and cannot be taken back and polished. She held what the reader of the case holds, and she held it in the order it would have to be spoken. A seal log, in which one line had been written by the wrong hand in the hours after a death, carrying the initials of the man now sitting in the dead man's chair. A gate book and a tide log and a store's chits, reconciled line by line by a woman who counted everything twice, saying that the cask in room nine had travelled across the building on paper that lied about where it was going. A Stiller who had not been there, and a partner of her own, a Stiller of the Office, standing in a corridor saying the one sentence he was willing to say: not my trade, not any Stiller's trade, whatever the roster says. And beneath the paper, under it the way a foundation is under a house, the fact that no paper could hold: that the corridor outside room nine was heavy with an argument, a vigil and a flight, while the room itself, where a man had certainly died, was as empty as the day it was built. The seal fragments were the last of it. Rask had pressed the wharf's die into fresh lead and laid the test piece beside the cut halves without a word, some time in the night, and left her to find the agreement herself: the hairline, yes, and beneath the hairline a second flaw, a nick in the die's rim that printed as a small bright tooth on every disc the die had made in the last year, and the tooth was on the fragments, and it was on the test piece, and a copied die, cut by any craftsman alive, would have copied the famous flaw and missed the small one. The seal on room nine had been pressed by the wharf's own die, in the wharf's own cabinet's reach, by the wharf's own people. She sat with her hands flat and her eyes open and Voss's last ten minutes keeping their own slow time in her chest, and she assembled, in silence, the four questions the speaking would have to answer: whether Leyden would back an arrest built of paper, shape and a returned man; whether the gloves at a prisoner's belt would tell the same story the corridor had told; whether the prisoner would speak or wait for advocates; and whether a seal that should not exist was enough, by itself, to hold a senior assessor past the tide while the Office decided what it believed about an empty room. Then she stood up, and went down the corridor to say all of it aloud.

## What the Paper Proved

Leyden came down to the wharf himself for the close of it, which he did perhaps twice a year, and the whole building knew what his presence meant before he had taken his coat off: the Office was not borrowing the wharf's rooms for an interview, it was taking a position. He stood in the doorway of the supervisor's office with the tasking chit in his hand, the chit that named her reserve state in his own writing, reduced, and he looked at the man in the dead man's chair and at the objects arranged on the dead man's desk, and he said nothing at all while Mira laid the case out in the order she had assembled it in the quiet, because that was the arrangement between them and it had never been broken: she spoke when the case was ready to be spoken, and he decided, afterwards and on the record, whether the Office stood behind the speaking.

She began with the seal log, because the case had begun with it, in a junior clerk's small level hand and a line that did not belong to that hand. The seal on room nine had been pressed after the evening round, by the wharf's own die, the die's second flaw standing in the cut fragments like a signature under a signature. The line recording it had been written to resemble the round clerk's hand, and had been written by a hand that formed its figures the way Henrik Dall formed his, a correspondence Petra Dunmore set out without ornament, figure by figure, against the countersignatures on the fuel store chits that Dall had signed in the ordinary course of his work and had never thought of again. One of those chits had drawn a cask of winter fuel out of the store on the afternoon of the death, on paper to the lamp room, and the cask had travelled instead to room nine, where its stencil still showed under its placard, and where a careful man had gone alone at night to smell its vent and died of the air it breathed, with his lamp gone out beside him as the only witness the room would keep. The disc wired to that cask had been filed and re-stamped to call fuel fish. The disc the cask had been born with, the fuel store's own disc, entire and unfiled, had spent a day and a night under the press bench where it had fallen during the cask's short journey, until its owner came back for it in the dark and found the Ninth Office standing in the corridor. His gloves she read last, because they were the part of the case that would cost her and she had learned to schedule costs at the end of a working day. Rask set them on the desk, the heavy assessor's gloves, dark at the palms, and she put her hands flat inside the shape of them, and the gloves gave her what gloves hold: not a name, never a name, weight has never in its nature held a name, but the long shape of a man's fear held down hard under both hands while a heavy thing was moved a short distance with great care, and then, again and again, the small repeated motion of palms being wiped clean of a smell that would not leave them. It cost her what the schedule said it would cost. She came back out of the gloves with his fear seated in her hands alongside the dead man's, and she knew she would not sleep that night either, because his fear would keep its hours in her the way fears do, and she set the gloves down gently, the way a person handles a thing that has told the truth.

The corridor she laid out after the gloves, and she laid it out with its limits stated first, the way a witness states the limits of her own sight before she is allowed to describe what she saw. Weight records intensity, she said. It does not record identity, it does not replay a scene, and it cannot be cross examined on the subject of a face. What the corridor outside room nine held, held at an intensity she would put against any read of her eleven years, was the shape of an argument between two men at that door, in one of whom the fear had the texture of a discovery made and regretted in the same breath; the shape of a long vigil kept afterwards in the shadow of the seal cupboard, patient, flattened, professional; and the shape of a departure, when the vigil ended, that ran one way only, toward the gate stair, at a pace she described to Leyden as the pace of a man whose errand in a building is finished and whose conscience has begun. She claimed no name from any of it. The names in the case came from paper, from metal, from a filed disc and an unfiled one, from chits and tallies and a line of false handwriting, and the corridor's evidence did a narrower and in some ways harder thing: it fixed the shape of the night so that the paper could be read by it, and it excluded, by its heat and its patience and its single direction, every account of that night in which the death had been an accident of cargo that nobody had arranged and nobody had waited for. A cask does not keep a vigil. A vapour does not flee toward a gate stair. The night had an author, and the paper named him, and the two together were the case. She asked the prisoner one question only, at the end of the laying out, and she asked it without any expectation that the record would profit. She asked him whether there was anything in the account of the cask, the chit, the die or the disc that he wished to correct as a matter of fact, as distinct from the matter of judgment that the Office had already reached. He considered this with the seriousness of a man reviewing a manifest for errors, and shook his head once. "The cask was drawn against the lamp room," he said. "That much of your paper is wrong in one particular. It was drawn against the lamp room by a chit I countersigned for another man's cask, on another day, in the ordinary course, and the chit has been married to this cask by your analyst because she needed a signature and mine was the one she could read. There. That is a correction of fact. Do with it what the Office does with facts." Petra, who had come down the corridor with the chit case in her hand in time to hear herself married to a forgery, opened the case on the desk without haste and produced the fuel store's counterfoil, the half of the chit that never leaves the store, and laid the two halves side by side along their torn edge, where the fibres married exactly, as torn paper does when the two pieces were one piece on one day and not on another. The prisoner looked at the married edge for a long moment, and something in him that had been held at attention for two days stood down, visibly, in the shoulders first. He did not speak again.

"Henrik Dall killed Edran Voss," she said, in the room where Voss's coat still hung on its peg, and she said it as a finding and not as a performance, with the paper on the desk and Leyden standing in the doorway and the corridor lamps burning down toward morning. "Not with his hands in the room. With a cask, a chit, a die and a man's own care, which he knew as well as he knew the roster, because he had worked beside it for years. He killed him to keep a page blank, the last page of a draft in a desk drawer, the page headed Names, and the killing very nearly worked, because the death read as the wharf's favourite story, a careful man undone by his own care in a sealed room." Dall listened the way he had listened at midday, at the same temperature, and when he spoke it was once, and level, and to Leyden rather than to her. "Voss would have closed this wharf for a season," he said. "A score of households. Men whose whole crime was writing a number from memory in the rain. I want that in whatever record you are making, since you are making one. It is not a defence. It is a fact of the wharf, and facts of the wharf are supposed to be your trade." Leyden, from the doorway, received this the way he received weather, and then he did the two things the close of the case required of the Office, in the order the Office required them. He signed the detention, an Office detention and not a wharf one, so that the prisoner would be held in the Office's rooms and not in any cell the chain could reach with its kindness or its pressure, and he held the seal, the chits, the discs, the books and the cask itself under Accord impoundment, in the same signature, so that the evidence of the case would sleep that night under better locks than the evidence of most cases gets. Rask's sweep of the building, reported in three sentences while the ink dried, closed the last of the corridor's questions: the wharf had been walked end to end, the gate watch examined, and no second man had entered or left by any gate, stair or hoist that night; the prisoner had come in alone, on his own assessor's key, which the night watch had logged without remark, because assessors come and go at all hours and the wharf's whole discipline ran on not remarking the movements of its senior men. She stated the remainder plainly, because a close that hides its own edges is a close that will rot. The room had been emptied. On the evidence, it had been emptied so that the death would carry no story with it, emptied to hide not the death but a seal that should not exist, and who had done that emptying, and by what method a room could be made to forget what a corridor remembered, this case did not reach, and the record would say that it did not reach it, in words, rather than let the silence be mistaken for an answer. Dall's face, at that, did something it had not done at the charge, or the detention, or the impoundment of his whole working life: it changed, by about the amount that a man's face changes when a subject is raised that he had not prepared himself to hear raised in that room, and then it closed again, like a book. She marked it. She did not press it. The case was closed, and a closed case is a house with its doors shut, and she had learned to leave one door of it standing open on purpose, with a lamp in it, for the next night's work.

## Room Nine, Released

The wharf reopened the way a held breath is let out, in stages and with some soreness. The detention party took Henrik Dall out through the gate arch an hour after dawn, in the plain carriage the Office used for such errands, with Rask beside the driver and the prisoner's gloves, his die box access, his keys and his whole working life travelling in a sealed evidence chest in the carriage's boot, under Accord impoundment and Mira's own seal, pressed that morning with a die she had checked twice. The cargo freeze lifted room by room as Petra signed the releases, one through eight by the mid morning, the hoists starting up in a ragged chorus and the day's trade making up, loudly, for the night it had lost. In the supervisor's office, Leyden wrote the last entry in the movement ledger himself, in his small exact hand, because some entries an institution should have to make in its own handwriting or not at all: the discovery of the death credited, by name, to the junior clerk Sabel Orry, whose adherence to the standing order had preserved the room, the book and the case entire, and whose name the Office directed the chain to enter in its own record in the same terms. She was asleep by then, finally, in the clerks' room with her coat over her like a blanket and the oilcloth book still within reach of her hand, and she did not hear the hoists start, which the whole corridor agreed afterwards was the first sensible thing she had done in two days. The report the Office filed was a model of its kind: a death, an arrangement, an arrest, an evidence list, a detention, all of it true and all of it proved. About the room itself the report said, in Leyden's phrasing, that no reading had been obtained from the locus, a sentence shaped like a door, and when she raised it with him in the corridor, once, quietly, he looked at her with the patience of a man who has already had this conversation with himself and priced both sides of it. "No reading obtained is the truth, officer," he said. "It is the whole of the truth the institution can afford this week. What you make of the remainder is your affair, until it is the Office's, and you will know the day it becomes the Office's because I will tell you so myself." She made of the remainder, in the event, one line in her own notebook, in her own hand, unheaded and unexplained, the way a person marks a place in a book they intend to return to: a room where something certainly happened holds no weight at all. She did not sleep. The gloves had seen to that, and beneath the assessor's fear, arriving in her hands in the small hours in instalments like a tide, the dead man's last ten minutes kept their own counsel in her chest, patient as furniture. Room nine was released last of all, when the impoundment party had taken the cask out under seal and the room stood empty of everything but its own dimensions. She went in alone, at the end of it, without a lamp, in the grey light coming through the grating, and she stood in the middle of the room where a man had died arranging his own careful death in reverse, and she put both hands flat against the wall at the height of her shoulders, and she opened herself to the room one final time, the way she had opened herself on the first morning, expecting nothing and finding it, completely, a silence with the shape of a place where a feeling should have been. The wall was cold. The wall was only a wall. Her hands rested on it a while, taking its temperature, learning nothing, and the room kept its counsel around her, swept, shuttered and waiting, like a house that had never been lived in.

## The Case File

### What is bonded transit, and why does the Accord care about it?

Bonded transit is the movement of cargo that has entered a member port without yet being cleared for release into that port's own market. Such cargo waits in bonded rooms under seal, owing its duty to no one until its destination is declared, and during the waiting it belongs, in effect, to the paper that describes it. The Accord cares because a bonded room is a place where the goods of many states sit within arm's reach of one another under a single roof, and a failure of honesty in that room is never a private failure. It becomes a dispute between treasuries within a season. The seal on the door is therefore a treaty instrument in miniature, and the Office treats any interference with one as a matter of state, however small the cargo behind it may be.

### What standing does the Ninth Office hold in a member port?

The Ninth Office is the operational arm of the Meridian Accord, maintained for the work the Accord cannot be seen to do. Its officers hold no rank in any member port and command no port official by right. Their authority in a port derives from the Accord's inspection warrant, which a harbourmaster may resent but may not lawfully refuse, and from the standing agreement that bonded premises under Accord seal are open to the Office at any hour. In practice the Office's standing is exactly as strong as its record. A port that has watched the Office close a case fairly will admit it quickly for a decade. A port that has watched it blunder will find procedural reasons to delay it for just as long, and the Office keeps its record accordingly.

### How is a Weigher's field read recorded?

A Weigher's read is recorded twice, once in the officer's own notebook at the time and once in the case file on return, and the two records are expected to differ only in tidiness. The record states the place, the anchor object or surface used, the intensity found, the shape of the affect, and the cost carried away, in that order. It does not state conclusions. A read is evidence of feeling laid down in a place, and the file treats any attempt to enter a name, a motive or a narrative into the read itself as a defect of the record rather than a strength of the officer. Interpretation belongs in a separate section, signed separately, so that a later reader can see exactly where the feeling ended and the officer began.

### How does the Carrowmere wharf system work?

The Carrowmere bonded wharf receives cargo by canal and gate, holds it in nine transit rooms off a single corridor, and releases it on the presentation of papers that must agree with one another in three separate books. The gate book records what entered the walls. The seal log records every seal pressed on every room. The movement ledger records every exception, every emergency, every key taken from its board and the cause written down before the taking. The system is deliberately redundant, because its designers understood that any single book can be falsified by one dishonest person working alone, while three books, kept by three different hands in three different rooms, can only be falsified by a conspiracy, and conspiracies are slower, dearer and easier to hear coming.

### What does a Stiller's suppression leave behind?

A Stiller suppresses all practice within a radius around his own person for as long as his strength holds, and what he leaves behind, when the suppression lifts, is precisely what was there before it, no more and no less. Suppression is a hand held over a flame rather than a flame put out. Weight laid down in a place before a Stiller works there remains in the place afterwards, readable by any Weigher once the radius has passed, because the Stiller has not touched the record of the past at all. He has only prevented anything new from being written while he stood there. Officers are reminded that a room which has been stilled feels quiet to an untrained visitor, and that this quiet is routinely mistaken, by witnesses and by junior officers alike, for a room that holds nothing.

### What is a bonded seal?

A bonded seal is a disc of lead, roughly the size of a coin, pressed over the crossed wires of a door's hasp so that the door cannot be opened without the disc being cut or the wires drawn. One face of the disc carries the Accord mark. The other carries the number of the room it closes. The seal is not a lock, and it is never treated as one. A determined person can defeat any seal in a minute. Its purpose is testimony rather than resistance: it is a witness that cannot be bribed, hurried or confused, and that states, to anyone who reads it afterwards, whether the door it guarded was opened by the book or outside it. For that reason the cutting of a seal is itself recorded, and its fragments are preserved as evidence in any inquiry that follows.

### What is a storm bolt for?

Each transit room carries a heavy bolt on the inside of its door, operable only from within, and known on the wharf as the storm bolt. Its purpose is the protection of a keeper caught inside the room by an emergency: a surge of canal water against the outer doors, a failure of the lamps in a room stacked with cargo, a fire in the corridor outside. A keeper in such a position shoots the bolt and waits, and the room becomes a refuge with one occupant who can account for himself when the door is opened. A thrown storm bolt is therefore evidence of a specific and limited kind. It establishes that someone was inside the room when the door closed. It establishes nothing about how that person came to be there, or in what condition, or for how long.

### How are emergency keys kept?

The emergency keys of the transit rooms hang on a board in the supervisor's office, one key to a hook, each key fastened to its hook by wire closed with a bead of wax. The arrangement is deliberately primitive. Wire and wax cannot be opened silently, cannot be reclosed convincingly, and can be audited by any officer in a single glance at the board, without instruments and without trusting anyone's account of the matter. The board is checked each morning by the supervisor's own hand, and the check is itself entered in the movement ledger. In any inquiry involving a locked room, the state of the key board is among the first evidence taken, because it divides the world of possibilities cleanly in two: either the room was entered by its emergency key, in which case the wax says so, or it was not.

### How are seal rounds walked and written?

Seal rounds are walked at set hours, room by room, in number order, by a clerk carrying the oilcloth bound book in which the state of every seal is kept. The walker reads each seal aloud, states its number and condition, and writes the entry before moving to the next room, so that no entry is ever made from memory at a distance. The book is ruled to eleven columns, covering the room, the seal, the pressing hand, the cargo, the hour and the exceptions. Reading aloud is not ceremony. It forces the eye to complete its work, it gives any person in the corridor the opportunity to hear an error at the moment it is made, and it means that a falsified line must be falsified in the knowledge that it will be spoken, in public, within the day.

### Why was the Meridian Accord founded?

The Meridian Accord is a treaty body founded by its member states to keep the practice of weight, binding and carriage out of open warfare. Its founders had each of them seen, in the years before the founding, what a state could do when it treated a discipline as a weapon rather than a craft: rooms that could not be entered, messages that could not be intercepted, objects that arrived where no road ran. The Accord exists so that such capabilities are licensed, inspected and accounted for by a body answerable to all the states that might suffer from them. It maintains no army. It maintains inspectors, auditors, and the Ninth Office, and its whole authority rests on the agreement of its members that a shared watchdog, however tiresome, is cheaper than an unshared arsenal.

### What does weight record?

Weight is the residue an event leaves in the place where it happened and in the objects that were present for it. What it records is intensity: fear, violence, grief, long repetition, sustained intent. A Weigher entering the place afterwards feels the shape of what occurred there, in proportion to how strongly it was felt at the time. Metal and stone hold weight longest. Cloth and paper hold it worst, and a crowded place holds it badly, because a crowd lays down so much feeling at once that the individual shapes blur into noise. Weight fades with time. A read taken within days of an event is ordinarily clear. A read taken after months is faint, and after years there may be almost nothing left to take, which is why the Office moves quickly or not at all.

### What can weight never record?

The limits of weight are entered in this file because more cases are lost by officers forgetting them than by any other single cause. Weight does not record identity. It holds the shape of an act and the affect that accompanied it, and it has never, in any recorded instance, held a face or a name. Weight cannot compel truth in speech and cannot detect a lie. It cannot replay a scene, and an officer who describes one is describing her own reconstruction, not the record. It cannot reach a place without a physical anchor in that place, it cannot be read from a body, living or dead, and it cannot be read reliably in a crowd. Every one of these limits is a protection for somebody: for suspects, who cannot be named by a feeling, and for officers, who are never permitted to stop investigating merely because a room felt conclusive.

### What do Binders do?

Binders fix a property in place. The seals on a bonded door, the ward laid into a hasp so that a door holds against more than weather, the contract that binds physically as well as on paper: these are a Binder's work, and the common element is that a quality which would ordinarily fade, loosen or be argued with is held exactly as it was set. Binding requires contact and time. It cannot be done at a distance, in haste, or by a practitioner who has not handled the object in question. To a Weigher, a binding is visible as enormous weight concentrated on a single point, because fixing a property in place is itself an act of sustained intent, and sustained intent is the thing weight records best. Bindings are therefore inspected, licensed and renewed on a schedule, and an unlicensed binding in a bonded place is treated as a serious offence in itself.

### What do Carriers do?

Carriers move small objects between two prepared anchors. The anchors must both be prepared in advance, by contact, at each end of the journey, and the mass that can be moved between them is strictly limited: a letter, a key, a vial, never a person, never a cask, never anything a smuggler would find convenient. The Office licenses carriage chiefly for the movement of documents between Accord offices, and audits the anchors regularly, because an unregistered anchor pair is, in embryo, a private road through every wall the Accord maintains. Officers investigating a disappearance of cargo are reminded that carriage cannot explain it. The mass limit is not a regulation that can be exceeded by a skilled or desperate practitioner. It is a property of the discipline, and it has no recorded exception.

### Why does the Carrowmere press die leave a hairline?

The press die at the Carrowmere wharf, by which every bonded seal on the premises is struck, is worn. Long use has opened a flaw in the die's face, and the flaw prints as a fine line running through both faces of every disc the die produces. The wharf has declined, over several seasons, to replace the die, on the reasoning, which the Office accepts, that a known flaw is worth more than a perfect face. A perfect seal can be imitated by any competent craftsman. A flawed seal can be imitated only by a craftsman who has studied the flaw, and the study itself leaves evidence. The die's deterioration is therefore monitored, and further flaws as they develop are recorded in the seal log's front pages, because a flaw that the Office has not recorded is a flaw that a forger may use first.

### What does a wharf assessor do?

An assessor is the bonded chain's examining officer for cargo in transit. He inspects consignments against their papers, attends the pressing of seals on rooms he has examined, countersigns the store chits that authorise internal movement of cargo within the walls, and answers, with his initials in the seal log, for the agreement between what a room contains and what its papers claim it contains. The post is one of unusual trust concentrated in a single pair of hands. An assessor can slow a port to a halt by refusing his initials, and can, in principle, walk a fortune through a wall by giving them wrongly. Assessors are therefore rotated between wharves on a schedule, audited without notice, and forbidden by the terms of their engagement from holding any interest in the cargoes that pass under their hands.

### What is the gate book for?

The gate book is the wharf's record of passage: every person and every consignment that crosses the gate arch, inward or outward, entered with the hour and the name or mark of the person conducting it. Its purpose is external rather than internal. What moves inside the walls is the business of the movement ledger and the store chits, but nothing may lawfully enter or leave the wharf except through the gate, and the gate book is the proof that the rule has been kept. In an inquiry, the gate book's value is negative as often as positive. The absence of an entry, where the book is otherwise complete and has been reconciled against independent records such as the tide log, is treated by the Office as evidence that the thing in question did not pass the gate, and a case is then required to explain how it came to be inside the walls at all.

### Why is the cause written before an emergency key is used?

The rule of the key board, at Carrowmere and at every bonded wharf the Accord inspects, is that the cause for breaking the seal of an emergency key is entered in the movement ledger before the key is taken down, never afterwards. The rule exists because explanations composed after an emergency are unreliable in a specific and predictable direction: they are written by a person who already knows what the room contained, and who adjusts the cause, unconsciously or otherwise, to fit what was found there. A cause written beforehand can be mistaken, hasty or foolish, but it cannot be fitted to the evidence, because the writer has not yet seen the evidence. The Office regards this ordering of a single sentence as one of the cheapest safeguards in the whole bonded system, and its observance, or its breach, is treated as significant in any inquiry that follows.

### How does the Office ration a Weigher's reads?

Reading spends the reader. A heavy read leaves a Weigher cold for hours, sleepless, and carrying the affect she has taken on as if it were her own, and heavy reads in succession put an officer out of the field entirely, sometimes for days. The Office therefore treats a Weigher's reserve as it treats any other finite instrument under its control: it is recorded on the tasking chit, it is spent deliberately, and an officer who has made three reads in nine days will be told so, by name and number, before she is offered a fourth. The rationing is not a judgment on the officer. It is an acknowledgment that the instrument and the person are the same thing in this discipline, and that an Office which spends its Weighers carelessly will shortly have no Weighers to spend, and no one to blame for the shortage but itself.

### How does the Accord count its Sessions?

The Accord counts its age in Sessions. A Session runs two years, and the Sessions are numbered consecutively from the founding, the First Session being the founding itself, so that the age of the Accord in years is twice the number of the current Session, less one. The present Session is the Twenty Ninth, and the Accord is therefore in its fifty seventh year, a figure the Office uses in its own records in place of any other calendar. Sessions are the unit in which appointments are made, licences renewed and audits scheduled, and officers writing case files are expected to mark their background entries by Session, because a file that borrows the calendars of individual member states must eventually be reconciled against all of them, and the Session is the only reckoning every member has agreed to share.
