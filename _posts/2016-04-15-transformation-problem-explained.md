The transformation problem begins with a tension that sits inside the most celebrated work of political economy ever published, and it can be stated without any algebra. In the first volume of Capital, Marx analyses a world in which commodities exchange in proportion to the labour time embodied in them, and on that assumption he explains where profit comes from. In the third volume, published after his death, he shows that commodities cannot exchange in those proportions in a competitive economy where capitals of equal size earn equal profit. The embarrassment is plain: the two volumes appear to rest on two different rules for what things sell for, and the bridge Marx builds between them, his own procedure for converting values into prices of production, is defective in a way that can be pinpointed exactly.

That defect, and the century of attempts to repair it, are the business of the pages that follow. The reader who finishes it should be able to state precisely what the difficulty is, why Marx's own handling of it falls short, and why the honest verdict on the literature is neither of the two slogans that dominate popular accounts. The problem has not been shown to be fatal to the theory it troubles, and it has not been solved in the form in which Marx left it. What the literature offers instead is a set of reconstructions, each internally consistent, each purchased at a price, and the live dispute concerns which price is worth paying. That framing, the price of consistency, organises everything that follows, and the solutions ledger in the middle of the article tabulates the competing repairs so that their costs can be compared side by side.

## The transformation problem in one statement

Stated canonically, the transformation problem is the difficulty of deriving prices of production, the prices that equalise profit rates across industries whose capitals are composed differently, from labour values, given that Marx's own procedure transformed the values of outputs into prices while leaving the values of inputs in labour terms. Every element of that sentence carries weight. Values are the labour-time magnitudes of the first volume. Prices of production are the prices proper to the third volume, each built from a cost price plus profit at the general rate. The derivation is supposed to connect the two systems without contradiction, showing the second as a modified form of the first rather than a replacement for it. The difficulty is that the only derivation Marx actually supplied performs the conversion on one side of the account and not the other.

The problem matters because of what hangs on the connection. If values and prices belong to two unconnected schemes, then the first volume's explanation of profit, which runs entirely in value terms, floats free of the observable economy described in the third. Defenders of the theory need the bridge; critics have always aimed at it. Yet the dispute is narrower and more technical than its reputation suggests. Nobody in the serious literature, on any side, doubts that individual commodities sell above or below their values as a matter of routine. Nobody doubts that competition redistributes profit between sectors. The argument concerns two totals and one procedure: whether the sum of all prices can equal the sum of all values while aggregate profit matches the whole of surplus value, and whether Marx's route from the first system to the second is internally sound.

### Why is the dispute about aggregates rather than individual prices?

Because nobody on any side expects an individual commodity to sell at its value. Competition, chance, and monopoly move single prices freely. The theory's testable-looking claims concern totals: whether the sum of prices equals the sum of values, and whether total profit equals total surplus value.

Single prices, in other words, were never the theory's promise, and refutations aimed at single prices miss their target entirely. A coat selling for more than the labour embodied in it proves nothing against Marx, any more than a coat selling for less proves something for him. The claims that can succeed or fail are the aggregate ones, because those are the claims Marx himself advanced when he presented his procedure as a transformation rather than a fresh start. He asserted that the transformation redistributes a given total: the values are all there before the conversion, the prices are all there after it, and nothing is created or lost in between except the allocation of profit among industries. It is exactly that conservation claim which the defective procedure fails to underwrite, and exactly that claim which every later reconstruction tries either to restore, to replace, or to reinterpret.

A second clarification belongs at the outset. The transformation problem is not a complaint about Marx's arithmetic, and it would not be met by more careful arithmetic. The flaw is structural: a step is missing, and the missing step, once supplied, changes the results. Nor is the problem a disguised way of asking whether the labour theory of value is true. That theory makes claims about the source of profit and the regulation of exchange which stand or fall on broader evidence, treated in the [labour theory of value explained](/2010/07/01/labour-theory-of-value-explained/) article in this series. The transformation problem asks something prior and narrower: whether Marx's own two sets of books can be reconciled with each other. A theory could survive a negative answer, by being rebuilt; it could also, as its critics insist, be badly damaged by one. But the questions are distinct, and keeping them distinct is the first discipline this subject requires.

## Smith's two measures and Ricardo's standard

The transformation problem has a genealogy, and knowing it prevents the common error of treating the difficulty as a Marxist eccentricity. Classical political economy was haunted from its birth by the question of what regulates exchange, and Adam Smith bequeathed his successors two measures of value that do not agree: the labour embodied in a commodity's production and the labour the commodity can command in exchange, the quantity of other people's work its owner can purchase with it. In a society of independent producers the two measures roughly coincide. In a society of capitals and wage labour they pull apart, because the owner of a product commands, through its price, more labour than the product embodies whenever profit is positive. Smith noticed the divergence and slid between the measures without resolving them, an ambiguity his critics have mined ever since.

David Ricardo built his system on the embodied measure and spent his final years tormented by its known defect. Ricardo understood that the relative prices of commodities shift when the division of the product between wages and profit changes, even with techniques frozen, because industries use labour and durable instruments in different proportions: a rise in wages cheapens the machine-heavy product relative to the labour-heavy one. He hunted for an invariable measure of value, a commodity whose own production conditions never changed, against which all others could be priced without distortion, and he died with the hunt unfinished and the defect acknowledged in his correspondence with unusual candour. The hunt was not a quirk. It was the recognition that a labour theory of exchange needs a bridge between the world where labour alone counts and the world where distribution moves prices, and that the bridge does not build itself.

Marx inherited Ricardo's problem and transformed it, which is one source of the word that names this article's subject. Where Ricardo sought a measuring commodity, Marx sought a derivation: prices of production were to be shown as values in a redistributed form, the same labour magnitudes wearing the dress competition forces on them. The inheritance explains both the problem's depth and its dignity. Marx was not careless where Ricardo had been careful; he was attempting, with full knowledge of the Ricardian defect, the stronger operation, deriving what Ricardo had only hoped to measure. The attempt's failure at the input side is therefore an event in a two-century argument about whether labour magnitudes can govern a price system at all, and the economists who formalised the failure understood themselves to be closing, or reopening, a classical file rather than scoring a sectarian point. Readers who grasp the genealogy will also grasp why the problem refuses to die: it is the modern form of the founding question of value theory, and each generation's best tools get applied to it in turn.

Marx's own reckoning with the inheritance, in the notebooks published as his history of economic theory, shows him working the Ricardian defect over with a thoroughness that rules out any notion that he stumbled into the transformation unaware. He saw that Ricardo had conflated cost price with price of production, charging each capital with profit at a rate Ricardo could not ground, and he saw that the conflation was not carelessness but necessity: without a theory of how the total surplus is formed and pooled, no classical economist could say where the average rate came from. His own construction was designed to supply exactly that missing ground, the pooling of surplus value produced in production and shared in circulation. That the pooling mechanism then failed its consistency test at the inputs is the tragedy's precise shape: the part of the inheritance Marx most clearly diagnosed is the part his own repair could not carry, and the part his repair could carry, the pattern of deviations and the formation of the general rate, has worn the best of everything the classical tradition produced.

## What the first volume assumes

The first volume of Capital, published in 1867, examines the production of value and surplus value under a deliberate simplification: commodities are treated as exchanging in proportion to the socially necessary labour time their production requires. On that footing Marx builds the volume's central results. Labour power, the worker's capacity to work, is bought at its value, the labour embodied in the worker's means of subsistence. The worker then labours longer than the time required to reproduce that value, and the excess, surplus value, is the source of the capitalist's gain. Exploitation, in this technical sense, is a ratio between two stretches of the working day, and the whole analysis proceeds as if prices never deviate from values at all.

The simplification was a method, not a mistake, and Marx said plainly in the third volume that the first volume's assumption was provisional. His strategy resembled that of any science that isolates a mechanism before studying its modifications: first establish what labour contributes and what profit is, in a setting stripped of the redistribution that competition performs; then introduce competition and watch the magnitudes change their form. The wages of the strategy are real. The origin of profit is genuinely hard to see at the level of prices, where one capitalist's gain looks like another's markup, and the value apparatus makes the aggregate origin visible by construction. The cost of the strategy is equally real, and it is the subject of this article: the return journey from the stripped setting to the modified one must actually be made, with the same rigour as the outward journey, or the two volumes remain two books rather than one theory.

### What does the first volume actually claim about prices?

Less than its critics often suppose. The first volume analyses exchange as if commodities traded in proportion to the labour embodied in them, because its question is where profit comes from, not what any particular good costs. The abstraction is announced and deliberate, a scaffolding for one inquiry.

It follows that a favourite popular refutation, the observation that real prices diverge from labour values everywhere and always, attacks a claim the first volume never made about the real economy. Marx did not believe that a watch sells for the price of the labour in it plus nothing else; he believed that treating exchange as value-proportional was the way to isolate the production of surplus. Whether that isolation is legitimate is a fair question, and the wider objections to the theory are set out in [labour theory of value criticisms](/2010/09/01/labour-theory-of-value-criticisms/). The transformation problem arises only when the scaffolding is meant to come down: when the third volume undertakes to show how the value relations reappear, transformed, as the price relations of a competitive economy. If the scaffolding cannot be removed without the building shifting, the metaphor the defenders prefer, of levels of abstraction, comes under strain, and it is that strain, precisely located, that the next sections examine.

One more feature of the first volume's scheme matters for what follows. Within it, the rate of surplus value, the proportion between the unpaid and paid portions of the working day, is taken as given and broadly uniform, while the composition of capital varies freely from trade to trade. One industry advances most of its capital in machinery and materials and little in wages; another advances little in plant and much in wages. Since only living labour creates new value on the theory's own premises, the mass of surplus value produced in each industry stands in proportion to its wage bill, not to its total capital. That asymmetry, harmless while prices are assumed proportional to values, becomes explosive once competition enters, because competition rewards capitals in proportion to their size. The next section takes up the collision.

It is worth pausing on what the first volume's analysis actually centres on, because later misunderstandings of the transformation often trace back to a misreading of the earlier book's purpose. The volume's beating heart is not a price list; it is the working day. Marx's characteristic procedure there is to follow a single magnitude, the new value created in a day, as it divides between the worker's equivalent and the capitalist's surplus, and his characteristic evidence is not market data but factory inspectors' reports, the recorded struggle over the day's length and intensity. Prices enter the volume mainly as the form in which that division is concealed: the wage looks like payment for all the labour performed, and the concealment is itself one of the volume's findings. A reader who approaches the transformation expecting the first volume to be a treatise on price formation will judge the bridge by standards the earlier book never set itself, and will correspondingly overrate the damage when the bridge sags. The bridge matters, this article has insisted and will insist, but it matters as the connection between a theory of the working day and a theory of competition, and its failure leaves the working day's analysis standing in a way that a failed price theory would not. Keeping the two books' purposes distinct is the second discipline this subject requires, after the discipline of levels learned earlier.

## Two systems of accounting

Before the collision can be understood, the two systems that collide deserve a slower introduction than surveys usually give them, because the transformation problem is at bottom a problem about what kind of things values and prices are. A value, in Marx's usage, is a quantity of socially necessary labour time: the hours an economy requires, on average and with average skill and equipment, to produce a thing. Three features of that definition matter for everything that follows. The labour is social, an average across all producers, so no individual firm's inefficiency or brilliance sets the magnitude. The labour is necessary, counted only insofar as the product meets a social want, so hours spent making what nobody will use register as nothing. And the magnitude is invisible in any single transaction: no buyer has ever handed over a quantity of abstract labour at a counter. Values are the economy's hidden wiring, inferred from the pattern of its exchanges rather than observed in any one of them.

A price, by contrast, is a sum of money attached to a particular thing at a particular moment, visible to anyone who cares to look. Marx's theory does not treat the visibility as a refutation of the wiring. His recurring methodological claim, across all three volumes, is that the forms in which economic relations appear systematically disguise the relations themselves: wages appear as payment for labour rather than for labour power, profit appears as a fruit of capital rather than of unpaid hours, and price appears as a property of things rather than as the money shape of a labour relation. The third volume's prices of production are one more appearance form in that sequence, the shape value takes when competition has finished redistributing the surplus. The transformation was meant to be the theory's demonstration that appearance and essence belong to one system, the first derivable from the second by stated rules. That is why its failure is not a peripheral embarrassment. A theory whose method promises to connect its levels, and whose central worked example of such a connection turns out to be inconsistent, has a problem at the joint where its philosophy of science meets its economics.

The two accountings also differ in their units in a way that will matter when the monetary redefinition arrives. Values are reckoned in hours. Prices are reckoned in money, and money in Marx's world is a commodity, gold, with a value of its own. Any bridge between the systems must therefore fix the exchange rate between hours and gold, and that rate is not a fact printed anywhere in the economy; it is set by the value of the money commodity relative to all others, which is itself one of the magnitudes in motion. The simultaneous formalisation will handle this by treating the money industry as one department among others. The monetary redefinition will handle it by defining the rate directly from the national accounts. The temporal reading will let it float from period to period. Each handling is a decision, and each decision costs something, which is the recurring shape of this entire subject.

One further asymmetry between the systems should be flagged now, since it organises the defence examined later. The value accounts are constructed to answer a question about production: where does the new value created in a period come from, and how is the working day divided within it? The price accounts answer a different summons: at what terms do finished goods change hands so that every capital earns the going rate? A theory can in principle need both accountings without needing either to be derivable from the other term by term, in the way that a physiology and a pharmacology describe one body for different purposes. Whether Marx's theory is entitled to that pluralism, or whether its own text demands the derivation, is the deepest form of the dispute, deeper than any question about equalities. The sections on the defence and on reconstruction return to it; for now it is enough to hold the possibility in view, because much of the literature's heat is generated by parties who have silently answered that deeper question in opposite ways and are arguing, in consequence, straight past each other.

A word, finally, on the abstraction that the value system's unit performs, since misunderstandings of the transformation often begin there. The labour counted in values is abstract labour: not the concrete, skilled, particular work of the tailor or the miner, but labour considered as expenditure of human effort in general, rendered commensurable across trades by the same social process that renders their products commensurable in exchange. Skilled labour counts as multiplied simple labour, intense labour as condensed labour, and the reductions are accomplished, on the theory's own account, behind the producers' backs, by the market's daily practice rather than by any bureau's coefficients. The abstraction matters for the transformation because it means the value magnitudes were never engineering data, never the sum of stopwatch readings in identifiable factories. They are social averages constituted by exchange itself, which is one reason the demand that they be computed independently of the price system and then fed into it as inputs has always sat uneasily with the theory's own description of what they are. The simultaneous literature computes them from technical coefficients as if they were engineering data, and obtains consistent results by doing so; whether those results are the theory's values or a well-behaved namesake is a question the form-oriented critics press, and it survives every repair in the ledger untouched, waiting at the end of each.

## Competition and the equal rate of profit

Industries differ in what their capitals are made of. Marx called the value composition of a capital its organic composition: the proportion between constant capital, laid out on means of production, and variable capital, laid out on labour power. A steelworks embodies the labour of generations in its furnaces and spends comparatively little on wages each week; a catering firm owns ovens and rented rooms but spends heavily on the cooks and servers who alone produce its service. If commodities sold at their values, the catering firm, employing much labour per unit of capital, would generate far more surplus value per unit advanced, and so earn a far higher rate of profit than the steelworks. Capital would flood toward catering and flee from steel, which no observer of capitalist economies has ever reported as the normal state of affairs.

What observers report instead is a rough equality of profit rates on capitals of similar risk and duration, enforced by exactly that movement of funds. Capital enters lines where returns run high, expanding supply and pressing prices down; it quits lines where returns run low, contracting supply and letting prices recover. Through this migration the surplus value produced across the whole economy is pooled, in effect, and shared among capitals in proportion to their size, like a dividend paid on shares rather than a prize paid to the employer of the most workers. The general rate of profit is the ratio of the economy's total surplus value to its total advanced capital, and each industry receives profit at that rate on its own advance, whatever its internal composition happens to be. Prices must therefore settle at levels that deliver this uniform return, and those levels, prices of production, deviate from values systematically: industries heavy in constant capital sell above value, industries heavy in labour sell below it, and the deviations cancel in the aggregate, or so the third volume asserts.

The migration that enforces the tendency deserves a word of institutional flesh, since in Marx's century it ran through channels a modern reader may picture too abstractly. Capital moved between trades chiefly as credit and as new investment rather than as the physical relocation of plant: banks and bill brokers directed fresh lending toward the trades where returns were talked up, joint-stock flotations gathered subscriptions for ventures in favoured lines, and established firms retained or disgorged earnings according to the same gradient. A cotton spinner could not become a railway, but the next increment of society's savings could become either, and it is increments, compounded over years, that reshape an economy's structure. The equalisation the transformation presupposes is therefore a slow pressure exerted at the margin of new investment, always incomplete, always being overtaken by technical change that reopens the gaps it closes. Marx's construction idealises the pressure into a completed state, as his method habitually idealises tendencies into their pure form before studying their disturbance. The idealisation is legitimate for the transformation's purpose, which is to ask what the price system would look like if the pressure finished its work, and the answer to that question is the standard against which the unfinished reality is then read. Critics who object that no finished state exists are correct and beside the point in equal measure; the construction is a limit, and limits in economics are instruments for organising the measurement of distances, not predictions that the distance will close.

### Why must competition equalise the rate of profit?

Because capital moves. Investors shift funds toward whichever line of production yields more on each unit advanced, and away from lines yielding less, until the yields converge. A persistent gap would be an open invitation to enter the favoured trade and abandon the disfavoured one.

The equalisation is a tendency, not a photograph: risk, monopoly, patents, and the sheer immobility of sunk plant keep actual rates scattered around the average at any moment. Marx's argument needs only the tendency, because the tendency suffices to break value-proportional pricing. Even a rough gravitational pull toward a common rate means that the price of a capital-intensive industry's product must carry profit unrelated to the labour performed inside that industry, and must therefore depart from the product's value in a direction fixed by the industry's composition. The third volume's achievement was to state this mechanism clearly and to name its price form. Its vulnerability lies in the bookkeeping by which the mechanism is reconciled with the first volume's totals.

### Of what two parts is a price of production composed?

A price of production has two parts. The first reimburses what the capitalist laid out: the cost price, covering used-up materials, wear on equipment, and wages. The second adds profit at the general rate on the whole capital advanced, whether or not that capital employed much labour.

Cost price deserves a moment's attention, because it is where the trouble will enter. The cost price of a commodity is what its production cost the capitalist: the value of the materials and equipment used up in its making, plus the wages of the labour employed. In the first volume's world, those inputs are naturally entered at their values, since everything is valued in labour time. But the inputs were bought in the same economy whose outputs the third volume reprices. The steel in the catering firm's ovens, to bend an example, was sold by a capital-intensive industry whose price of production stands above its value. If outputs are repriced and inputs are not, the books mix two currencies. Marx's procedure, examined next, does exactly this, and the entire subsequent literature is an argument about how to stop doing it and what the repair costs.

Between value and price of production, Marx's system also lodges an intermediate magnitude that surveys often skip and that matters for understanding what the transformation is not. Within a single industry, the many producers work at different levels of efficiency, and the market does not reward each with a price matching individual labour time. It forms a market value, governed by the conditions under which the bulk of the industry's output is produced, and individual producers gain or lose according to their distance from that social standard. Market prices then oscillate around market values with supply and demand, sometimes for long stretches. The transformation operates one level up from all of this: it concerns the centres around which an industry's prices gravitate once profit equalisation between industries is taken into account, not the daily oscillations within the industry. Critics who exhibit a commodity whose price has departed from every plausible centre for a decade are exhibiting the oscillation, which the theory predicts, and not touching the transformation, which concerns the centre's relation to value. Much confusion in the popular literature is a confusion of these two levels, and examiners reward candidates who separate them before discussing either.

### Why do compositions of capital differ across industries?

Because production techniques differ. Smelting, refining, and transport tie up large sums in plant and materials for every worker employed, while clothing, catering, and personal services spend comparatively more on wages. The ratio between dead and living labour in a sector is set by its technology, not chosen freely.

Composition differences are therefore not a surface irregularity that better accounting could iron out. They express the physical character of production itself, the fact that making steel is mostly a matter of tending expensive equipment while making garments is mostly a matter of guiding inexpensive equipment with hands. Mechanisation deepens the differences over time, as industries automate at uneven speeds, so the deviation between values and prices of production is not a fixed blemish but a moving one, widening wherever machinery displaces labour fastest. The wider argument about the ratios involved in these comparisons is developed in [surplus value vs profit](/2011/02/15/surplus-value-vs-profit/), which separates the proportion of unpaid to paid labour from the proportion of profit to total capital advanced; the two ratios are easily confused, and the confusion has generated a literature of its own. For the transformation problem, what matters is the bare structural fact: equal capitals, unequal labour employed, hence unequal surplus produced, hence prices that cannot be proportional to values if profit rates are to be equal.

## The uniform rate of surplus value

One assumption in Marx's construction passes so quietly that readers often miss its presence, yet the transformation's shape depends on it. Marx works throughout with a single rate of surplus value for the whole economy: the proportion in which the working day divides into paid and unpaid labour is taken as roughly the same in steel as in catering, in transport as in tailoring. His justification is that competition among workers and the mobility of labour across trades press toward a common intensity of exploitation, just as competition among capitals presses toward a common rate of profit. The assumption is the labour-market twin of the profit assumption, and the construction needs both: only if the unpaid proportion is uniform does the deviation of price from value track an industry's composition cleanly, rising with its constant capital and falling against its wage bill.

Relax the assumption and a second redistribution appears on top of the first. Industries exploiting at above the common rate would generate surplus out of proportion even to their workforces, and their prices of production would reflect both their composition and their intensity of exploitation in a blend no observer could disentangle. The literature has generally kept the assumption in place, not because anyone believes workplaces are uniform, but because the problem it isolates is already hard enough: the difficulty of the transformation survives the assumption's removal unchanged, merely acquiring company. There is a methodological moral here that recurs in this field. Critics sometimes present Marx's simplifying assumptions as evasions, as if removing them would expose the theory. In this instance the opposite holds. The uniform rate of surplus value is the transformation's best case, the setting in which the derivation has its easiest run, and it fails there. Assumptions that flatter a derivation and still cannot save it are evidence about the derivation, not about the assumptions, which is why the formal literature has been content to grant Marx his uniform rate and his other simplifications and to press the objection on the ground he chose himself.

## Marx's own procedure in the third volume

The third volume of Capital appeared in 1894, eleven years after Marx's death, assembled by Friedrich Engels from manuscripts written mainly in the eighteen sixties. Its ninth chapter performs the transformation. Marx forms the general rate of profit from the aggregates of the whole economy, total surplus value divided by total capital, and then constructs each industry's price of production by adding to its cost price a profit calculated at that general rate on the capital it advanced. He then states the two conservation claims on which everything else depends. The first aggregate equality holds that the sum of the prices of production of all commodities equals the sum of their values: the transformation redistributes, it does not create or destroy. The second aggregate equality holds that the sum of all profits equals the sum of all surplus values: competition reallocates the surplus among capitals but neither augments nor diminishes it. In the illustrative calculation Marx gives, both equalities come out satisfied, and the chapter presents the matter as settled in principle, with the deviations of price from value cancelling across the economy as a whole.

Marx was not unaware that loose ends remained. In the same chapter he remarked, in passing, that the cost price of a commodity could stand above or below the value of the means of production consumed in making it, since those means of production are themselves bought at prices of production rather than at values. Defenders of the text have leaned heavily on that remark ever since, reading it as an acknowledgement that the procedure is a first approximation awaiting a second pass in which inputs would be repriced too. Critics reply that the remark sits in the chapter like an unexploded shell: once the second pass is made, the aggregates move, the general rate of profit itself changes, because it was computed from value magnitudes that the repricing alters, and the two equalities can no longer both be simply asserted. Which reading is fair is a question of scholarship this article will return to; the mathematical fact beneath the dispute is not in question, and the next sections state it as plainly as prose allows.

The procedure's defect can be seen without symbols by following one commodity through the books. Consider the means of production that every industry consumes: iron, fuel, machinery services. In Marx's calculation these enter each industry's cost price at their values, the labour embodied in them. But iron is the output of an industry, and on the third volume's own showing the output of an industry sells at its price of production, which for a capital-intensive industry like ironmaking stands above its value. The iron that the machine builder buys should therefore be entered at the iron industry's price of production, not at its value. Marx entered it at value. The same applies, in the opposite direction, to wage goods: the bread and clothing that labour power's value represents are outputs of labour-intensive industries whose prices of production stand below their values, so the variable capital advanced as wages is also misstated when entered at value. Outputs were transformed; inputs were not. The two equalities hold in Marx's table because the table was constructed so that they would, with the input side frozen at the first volume's magnitudes.

## Inside Marx's own example

Marx's chapter nine does not argue in the abstract alone; it works an example, and the example repays inspection because its construction explains both why Marx believed the matter settled and why the belief could not survive scrutiny. He lays out five branches of industry, each with a capital of the same size, the capitals divided between constant and variable portions in five different proportions, running from a branch where machinery and materials dominate almost entirely to one where wages dominate almost as completely. On each capital he sets the same rate of surplus value, computes the value of each branch's product, forms the average rate of profit from the five taken together, and then rebuilds each price as cost price plus that average profit. The rebuilt prices scatter around the values exactly as the theory predicts, high for the machine-heavy branch, low for the labour-heavy one, and their total comes out equal to the total of the values, with total profit matching total surplus value to the unit.

Looked at slowly, the equality of the totals is doing less work than it appears to. The average rate was manufactured from the value totals, so the sum of the profits it distributes is the sum of the surplus values by the manner of its construction; the equality of profit and surplus is built into the procedure's first step rather than extracted by its last. The equality of price and value then follows from the first, since each price is a cost price reckoned in values plus a slice of that pre-counted profit. The example's real information lies elsewhere, in the pattern of the individual deviations, which is genuine and important. Its totals are a hall of mirrors: what was placed in the aggregates at the start is found in the aggregates at the end, and the finding is announced as a conservation law. This is not a charge of bad faith. It is the normal shape of a first pass through a hard problem, in which the investigator fixes the quantities he cares about and checks that the mechanism can display them consistently. The failure enters only when the same courtesy is extended to the inputs, for the five branches buy from one another: the machine-heavy branch's product is the others' equipment, the labour-heavy branch's product is every branch's wage goods, and the example has silently valued those purchases at the old figures while repricing the sales.

Marx himself seems to have sensed that the example's quiet input side would not stay quiet, and the textual evidence for his unease has been combed by generations of scholars. Beyond the remark about cost price already noted, there is his handling of the difference between the capital a branch advances and the capital it consumes: a machine worth a fortune enters production's costs only by the fraction of its value worn away each year, while the profit it must help earn is reckoned on the whole fortune advanced. Marx handles that distinction correctly and explicitly, which shows a mind attending precisely to the difference between stocks advanced and flows consumed. The input transformation is a problem of exactly that family, a place where the figure that enters the account is not the figure the theory's own rules assign, and its author's demonstrated alertness to the family makes the omission harder, not easier, to explain away. The likeliest scholarly verdict is the least dramatic: the manuscripts were drafts, the pass was postponed, and death and an editor's timetable converted a postponement into a doctrine.

## The wage basket on the input side

The input difficulty has a second front, quieter than the machinery front and in some ways deeper, which runs through the definition of variable capital itself. Variable capital is the wage bill, and the wage bill's value, in Marx's construction, is the labour embodied in the basket of goods that workers consume: food, clothing, shelter, the historically shaped necessities of a working life. Those goods are outputs, produced by industries with their own compositions of capital, and in a transformed world they fetch prices of production, not values. The basket's price therefore departs from its value, which means the money wage that buys the basket represents a quantity of labour different from the quantity the basket embodies. The value of labour power, the magnitude on which the rate of surplus value and hence the entire aggregate construction rests, must itself be transformed along with everything else, and Marx's procedure leaves it standing at its first-volume figure beside the other untransformed inputs.

Bortkiewicz's three-department scheme is best understood as machinery built to face this second front squarely. His second department produces nothing but workers' consumption goods, so the wage basket has an address in the system, a price formed by the same simultaneous discipline as every other price, and the value of labour power emerges from the solution rather than being inserted into it. His third department, producing luxuries and the money commodity, gives the system's scale an address as well. The scheme's elegance is the elegance of completeness: every magnitude that Marx's procedure took as given from the value world is, in the rebuilding, required to earn its place inside the price world. That the rebuilding then fails to conserve both of Marx's totals is therefore all the more telling. The failure is not a product of hostile simplification. It appears at the exact point where the scheme becomes fully honest about the wage basket and the machine, the two inputs whose transformation Marx postponed.

The monetary redefinition, examined in its own section below, can be read as the most radical response to the wage basket knot. If the basket's labour content cannot be carried through the transformation without disturbance, redefine the value of labour power so that no basket needs costing: the money wage itself, divided by the money that an hour of social labour expresses in that period, is labour power's value. The knot is not untied but cut, and whether the cutting is liberation or amputation is, once again, a judgement about what the theory's terms were for. Hold the knot in mind as the ledger's families pass in review; several of them are, at root, different decisions about what to do with the contents of a worker's shopping basket.

## The gap: inputs left at their values

The previous section ended with the defect in view; this section isolates it, because everything in the later literature is a response to this one point. A transformation that converts outputs and not inputs is not a conversion between two systems but a mixture of them: each commodity leaves its industry priced by the third volume's rule and enters the next industry valued by the first volume's rule. The mixture is not a harmless shortcut. The magnitudes on the two sides of the account are supposed to be the same goods, bought and sold in one economy at one set of prices, and an account that prices a good twice, differently, according to which side of the ledger it sits on, has not yet described any actual economy at all.

### What is wrong with Marx's own solution?

He transformed the values of outputs into prices of production while continuing to value inputs at their labour values. Since inputs are the outputs of other industries, they should have been transformed too. The procedure is therefore incomplete, which is the core of the objection.

The objection is sometimes phrased as if Marx had made an oversight, a slip of the pen that a careful editor might have caught. That phrasing undersells the difficulty. Transforming the inputs is not a matter of applying the same addition a second time, because the inputs feed back into every magnitude the procedure uses. The general rate of profit was computed from value totals: total surplus value over total capital advanced. Reprice the inputs and the capital advanced changes, sector by sector; change the capital advanced and the general rate changes; change the rate and every price of production changes; change those prices and the inputs must be repriced again. The calculation chases its own tail, which is precisely why the rigorous treatments that followed Marx abandoned his step-by-step order and posed the whole system at once, as a set of conditions to be satisfied simultaneously. Whether that simultaneous pose is faithful to Marx's intentions is a separate quarrel, taken up below. The point here is the narrow one: the gap is real, it is located exactly at the input side of the account, and no defence of Marx has ever succeeded by denying its existence. Defences succeed, when they do, by arguing that the gap matters less than it appears, or that Marx's framework contains the resources to close it in a different way.

### Why are the inputs the crux of the whole objection?

Because every industry's inputs are some other industry's outputs, bought in the same market where outputs are sold. A procedure that reprices what a sector sells while leaving what it buys at old values mixes two systems in one calculation, and the mixture is where the inconsistency lives.

There is a temptation, resisting which is part of understanding this subject, to treat the input problem as a small correction that could not overturn anything important. The numbers in any particular case might indeed move only modestly when inputs are repriced; in the worked illustration tabulated in the next section, the shift is visible but not dramatic. The objection's force is not numerical but logical. The two aggregate equalities were advanced as conservation laws for the transformation, proofs that the price system is the value system in a redistributed form. A conservation law that holds only because half the entries were never converted is not a law but an artefact of the bookkeeping. Once inputs are honestly converted, either one of the equalities fails, or some further stipulation must be added to save it, or the framework itself must be reinterpreted so that the equalities mean something different. Those three exits, stipulation, addition, reinterpretation, generate the families of solutions that fill the ledger, and each exit has its toll booth.

It is worth dwelling on why Marx could not simply close the loop by hand, repeating his own procedure until the figures settled, since the question occurs to every reader who meets the iterative repair and wonders why the founder did not perform it. The answer is partly practical: each pass through five industries is tedious, through fifty prohibitive, and the mathematics that certifies the loop's convergence did not exist in his lifetime. But the deeper answer is conceptual. Marx's procedure is not an algorithm awaiting iterations; it is an exposition, arranged to display a mechanism in a single pass, the pooling of the surplus and its redistribution by capital size. A second pass would have required him to treat the first pass's prices as data and recompute the pool itself, and at that moment the exposition's claim, that the pool is formed in value terms prior to prices, would have had to be restated in the weaker form the simultaneous literature eventually gave it. The founder can be forgiven for not writing the sentence that demotes his own construction. His successors had no such excuse and no such inhibition, and the history of the repairs is the history of that sentence being written in five different hands.

## A note on the word transformation

The vocabulary of this dispute carries a history worth a short section, because the word at its centre is a translation with consequences. Marx's German term, Verwandlung, is the ordinary word for a change of form, a metamorphosis, the same word used for a caterpillar's becoming and for a costume's transformation on a stage. Its connotations in his usage are precise and often lost in English: the thing transformed persists through the change of form, as the butterfly is the caterpillar continued, and the form is not a disguise laid over the thing but the mode in which the thing exists socially. When Marx writes of the transformation of values into prices of production, the German ear hears a claim about one substance in two shapes, not about two substances requiring a courier between them. Much of the English debate's flavour, its picture of values in one room and prices in another with a defective corridor between, is an artefact of translation flattening a metamorphosis into a conversion.

The variants listed in this series' reference apparatus, transformation of values into prices, the Marx transformation, the value price transformation, record the English debate's successive attempts to name the corridor it thought it saw. The schools' own names carry similar freight. A single-system interpretation announces in its title that the two rooms were one. A temporal interpretation announces that the corridor runs through time. The New Interpretation's very name is a claim about reading rather than about economics, a reminder that its founders presented it as a discovery about the text before it was a construction in its own right. Even the eraser is a piece of vocabulary warfare, an attempt to fix the debate's image before its arguments are heard. A reader alert to the names is halfway to the map, since in this literature the schools christen themselves by their solutions, and the christening is usually the most honest abstract of the position that its author ever wrote.

## Reading the ledger's hardest column

Of the ledger's columns, the one readers find hardest is the column of surrenders, and it is worth a section of its own because the difficulty is conceptual rather than technical. A surrender, in the ledger's sense, is not a confession extracted from a defeated school; it is the identification of the exact point at which a consistent theory parts company with the text it descends from. Every descendant parts somewhere. The simultaneous correction parts at the second conservation law. The monetary redefinition parts at the independence of values. The temporal reading parts at the timeless frame. To name the parting point is not to convict the descendant of infidelity; it is to make comparison possible, since two theories can be compared only when the respects in which each differs from their common ancestor are on the table. The column exists so that the comparison this article exists to enable can actually be performed.

A worked tour of one row shows the column in operation. Take the simultaneous correction, the family with the best claim to be the theory's orthodox continuation. Its preservation column is genuinely impressive: a complete, rigorous derivation of prices and the profit rate from the economy's technical data, with one of Marx's two conservation laws standing as a theorem inside it. Its surrender column lists the other law, and a reader might reasonably ask whether one law out of two is a good bargain. The ledger's answer is that the question is incomplete until the surrender column is read together with the criticism column, where the eraser waits: the law that was surrendered turns out to matter less, in the long argument, than the fact that the surviving law's system computes its prices without consulting its values at all. The deepest surrender of the simultaneous family is not in its surrender column as a separate item; it is the quiet consequence of its method, and it took a critic of Samuelson's calibre to drag it into the open. The lesson generalises. Read every row's surrender column twice, once for what it lists and once for what the listed item drags with it, because in this literature the listed price is rarely the whole price, and the difference between the two is where the interesting disputes live.

## Engels, the promised solution, and the first critics

The transformation problem was not discovered by enemies of the theory ransacking it for flaws; Marx set the problem up himself, and its first public framing came from his own collaborator. In the preface to the second volume of Capital in 1885, Engels taunted the economists of the day with a question he said Marx's manuscripts answered: how can an equal average rate of profit arise on capitals that employ very unequal quantities of living labour, when surplus value springs from living labour alone? He invited attempts at a solution in advance of the third volume's appearance, and something like a prize competition followed in the journals, with several German writers offering constructions that Engels later reviewed, courteously and destructively, in the preface to the third volume itself. When that volume appeared in 1894, its ninth chapter was received by the faithful as the promised derivation and by sceptics as a promissory note.

The sceptics spoke first in print with full force. Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk, the Austrian economist, published his critique of the Marxian system's close in 1896, arguing that the first and third volumes contradicted each other outright: the first made value the regulator of price, the third made prices obey a law that values could not deliver. Rudolf Hilferding, then a young Marxist theorist, answered in 1904 with a defence that remains instructive, since it rested the theory's significance on its account of the social relations behind the magnitudes rather than on the exact arithmetic of the conversion. That division of labour between the camps, one side pressing the formal point, the other relocating the theory's centre of gravity, prefigures the entire subsequent century. What the formal side still lacked, in the eighteen nineties, was a rigorous statement of exactly where the procedure broke. Supplying it required the method of simultaneous determination, which economics was then developing for other purposes, and a writer willing to apply that method to Marx's text line by line.

### Why did decades pass before anyone formalised the gap?

Partly because the third volume appeared posthumously, in an editor's arrangement, and readers treated its ninth chapter as a finished solution rather than a draft procedure. Partly because the simultaneous method needed to expose the gap only became standard equipment in economics during the following generation.

There was a further, quieter cause. The Marxist movement's own economists had strong motives to treat chapter nine as canonical, and its academic critics, after Böhm-Bawerk, largely treated the matter as closed in the other direction and moved on. A problem that one camp regards as solved and the other as fatal attracts little patient work, and the transformation question spent decades in that neglected condition, examined in specialist venues while the public argument recycled the slogans of the eighteen nineties. The pattern is worth naming because it recurs in this field: the middle position, that the problem is genuine, located, and reparable at a price, had to wait for writers with no institutional need for either slogan.

## Hilferding's answer and the strategy of relocation

Hilferding's 1904 reply to Böhm-Bawerk deserves separate treatment, because it founded a strategy that defenders of the theory have used ever since, in forms its inventor would not always recognise. Confronted with the charge that the first and third volumes contradict each other, Hilferding did not primarily defend the derivation. He relocated the theory's significance. Value, on his account, was never chiefly a device for computing prices; it was the expression of a social relation, the form taken by the connection between private producers in a society that organises its labour through exchange. The third volume's prices were the same relation at a further stage of development, and to demand that the magnitudes match term by term was to mistake a theory of social forms for an adding machine. Böhm-Bawerk's contradiction, on this reading, arose from reading a dialectical exposition with an accountant's expectations.

The relocation strategy has obvious strengths, and its descendants include some of the most sophisticated work in the tradition, the value-form scholarship that reads the first volume's opening chapters as an analysis of why labour takes the form of value at all. It also has a standing weakness that its critics, including later Marxists of a more formal temper, have never let it forget: a theory relocated beyond the reach of its own arithmetic can become impossible to embarrass, and a theory that cannot be embarrassed cannot be confirmed either. If every failed derivation is reclassified as a misunderstanding of the theory's genre, the theory's defenders have purchased safety at the price of content, the one price the ledger in this article exists to make visible. The mature literature's health can be measured by its refusal of that bargain in its pure form: the schools in the ledger accept genre arguments only as supplements to constructions that also do the arithmetic, and Hilferding's own later career, which produced a major work of empirical political economy, suggests he felt the pull of both obligations himself. The strategy and its limit frame the modern defence examined in later sections, where relocation reappears in the disciplined form of the origins argument, with its concession itemised rather than concealed.

The relocation strategy also explains a rhythm in the debate's reception that purely formal histories miss. Whenever the formal objection has been pressed with fresh rigour, in 1907, in the nineteen fifties, in 1971, the theory's defenders have answered on two levels at once: the technicians among them have gone back to the constructions and repaired them, while the philosophers among them have questioned whether the repair was the point. The two answers are not coordinated and sometimes embarrass each other, since a successful repair concedes that the formal question was real, and a successful relocation implies that it was not. Outside observers, meeting the double answer, have often concluded that the school cannot make up its mind. The fairer conclusion is that the theory itself is double, a formal economics and a critique of social forms bound in one set of covers, and that its defenders divide the labour of defence according to which binding they take to be load-bearing. The transformation debate is unintelligible as a single argument because it is the seam where the two bindings meet, and every participant is tugging, knowingly or not, at the stitching.

## The early answers: a German rehearsal

The attempts Engels's challenge provoked deserve more than the footnote they usually receive, because two of them contained, in embryo, positions the twentieth century would develop at length. Conrad Schmidt, a German economist in Marx's own circle, proposed that the equal rate of profit be understood as a kind of averaging enforced through the price mechanism over time, and groped toward a construction in which each industry's price gravitates around a centre set by its costs plus the average return. Engels, reviewing the attempt in the third volume's preface, treated it with respect and found it wanting on grounds that anticipate the whole later debate: the construction described the gravitation without deriving the centre from the value magnitudes, so the promised bridge was asserted at the point where it needed to be built. Schmidt himself, to his credit, seems to have felt the gap; his later work moved away from the problem toward other questions, and his attempt survives as evidence that the difficulty was visible, to an honest insider, before any formalisation existed.

Wilhelm Lexis, a statistician of standing outside the movement, offered the answer Engels treated most warmly, and it is not hard to see why. Lexis proposed that the contradiction dissolves once the level of the claim is fixed correctly: individual prices obey the ordinary play of supply, demand, and competition, while the law of value governs the aggregate, the totality of exchanges, where the deviations cancel. On that reading the prices of production of the third volume are the mechanism by which the aggregate law enforces itself through a screen of individual departures, and demanding that the law hold commodity by commodity is a category error. Engels's preface praised the answer's essential insight while noting that it left the derivation of the average rate itself under-argued. The aggregate reading of the law of value, as it came to be called, has had a long underground life: it resurfaces in the monetary interpretations, in the defence that values explain totals rather than items, and in the common scholarly verdict that Marx's conservation claims were always meant at the level of the whole. It also carries, from birth, the weakness its critics have always pressed: a law that holds only in the aggregate, where its failures cancel by construction, risks explaining everything in general and nothing in particular, and the line between a macro constraint and a tautology is exactly what the aggregate reading's friends and enemies have argued about ever since. The early rehearsal thus contained the mature dispute entire, lacking only the mathematics that would let its parties state their positions without ambiguity. That mathematics arrived in 1907.

## The first formalisation

Ladislaus von Bortkiewicz, a statistician and economist working in Berlin, published his correction of Marx's construction in 1907, and the problem has been defined by it ever since. His method was to rebuild the whole scheme as a simultaneous system. He divided the economy into three departments: one producing means of production, one producing the goods workers consume, and one producing the goods, including the money commodity, that capitalists consume. Within each department, the outputs must be priced so that every department's accounts balance at once, with inputs bought at the very prices the system assigns to outputs, which is the requirement Marx's procedure violated. Bortkiewicz then asked whether Marx's two equalities could both survive under that discipline, and his answer, delivered with full technical courtesy, was that in general they could not.

What the formalisation established, and what gives it permanent standing, is the shape of the impossibility. The simultaneous system fixes the relative prices of the departments and the rate of profit, but it contains one more magnitude than its conditions can determine: an overall scale, the absolute level at which the price system sits relative to the value system. To pin that scale down, one further condition must be imposed from outside. Impose the condition that total price equals total value, and total profit will in general depart from total surplus value. Impose instead the condition that aggregate profit shall equal the whole of surplus value, and the first equality gives way. Both can hold together only when the department producing the money commodity happens to have the average composition of capital for the economy as a whole, a coincidence there is no reason to expect. The two equalities, which Marx presented as twin conservation laws flowing from his procedure, are in the rigorous version rivals for a single available slot.

## Why three departments?

The architecture of Bortkiewicz's scheme is sometimes mistaken for a simplification adopted for convenience, a toy economy of three industries. It is better understood as the smallest stage on which the whole drama can be performed. Department one, producing means of production, exists because the machinery front of the input problem needs a home: the constant capital consumed everywhere must be shown being produced somewhere, at a price the system itself assigns. Department two, producing workers' consumption goods, exists because the wage basket needs the same treatment: the value of labour power must be formed inside the system rather than imported into it. Department three, producing luxuries and the money commodity, exists because the system's scale needs an anchor that is itself produced, and under a gold standard the anchor is a produced thing, mined and minted like any other, whose own price of production participates in the general equalisation. Remove any department and some magnitude that Marx's procedure took for granted becomes a free gift from outside the model; retain all three and nothing is a gift.

The scheme also imposes simple reproduction, the condition that each department's output exactly replaces what the economy consumes of its product, so that the system can repeat itself without growing or shrinking. That condition is a strong one, and later writers relaxed it in various directions, but its purpose in the original construction is diagnostic rather than descriptive: a stationary economy is the easiest setting in which to ask whether the accounts can be made consistent at all. If the transformation fails in the stationary case, no appeal to growth, accumulation, or technical change can rescue it, since those complications add magnitudes without removing the structural gap. If it succeeds there, the successes and failures in richer settings can be sorted into those inherited from the stationary core and those added by the complications. The subsequent literature's habit of beginning with reproduction schemes descends from this diagnostic choice, and the schemes themselves, Marx's own constructions from the second volume, are the subject of their own article in this cluster, since the reproduction framework has an independent life in the theory of accumulation and crisis.

What the three departments make visible, finally, is that the transformation problem is a problem about an economy, not about a firm or a market. A single industry can always be described in either accounting without strain; the strain appears when every industry's output is simultaneously another's input and the descriptions must agree everywhere at once. That is why the problem resisted the nineteenth century's partial methods and yielded its shape only to a simultaneous treatment, and why popular versions that exhibit the transformation for one commodity and declare the matter closed are invariably, if sometimes innocently, trading on the absence of the other departments from the stage.

### What did the first formalisation actually establish?

Working with three departments of production and requiring the accounts to balance simultaneously, it showed that Marx's two aggregate equalities cannot in general hold together. One can be preserved by a deliberate choice about measurement; the other then fails except under special conditions on industrial structure.

Later writers generalised the result beyond the three departments. Francis Seton, writing in 1957, extended the construction to an economy of any number of industries and confirmed the same structure: the system determines relative prices and the profit rate, and exactly one aggregate invariance condition may be added freely. Others, notably Jiro Winternitz in 1948 and Ronald Meek in successive editions of his studies, explored which condition a Marxist should prefer, Meek arguing at one stage for one anchor and later reconsidering, an oscillation that itself illustrates the problem: if the theory cannot say which of its two conservation laws is the real one, the choice between them is exposed as the repairer's judgement rather than the text's deliverance. None of this makes Bortkiewicz an enemy of the theory in any simple sense. His own posture was that of a technician correcting a construction he treated as historically important, and his correspondence shows respect for the edifice whose ninth chapter he had dismantled. The popular framing of the dispute as ideology at war survives contact with the documents badly, a point this article will insist on again: the participants were, and are, competent economists making technical arguments, and their disagreements are about what consistency costs.

Bortkiewicz himself is the standing refutation of the ideological caricature, and his profile is worth a paragraph. A scholar of Polish background formed in the Russian universities and settled in Berlin, he worked across mathematical statistics, insurance theory, and the history of economics, and his engagement with Marx belonged to a broader project of testing the classical economists with modern tools; he wrote on Ricardo's value theory in the same years, with the same combination of respect and severity. His Marx papers were corrections offered to a construction he regarded as the most important attempt of its century to solve the classical problem, and his private judgements of Marx's powers were considerably warmer than his public conclusions about chapter nine. The pattern, a critic whose esteem for the author rises as his verdict on the argument hardens, recurs throughout the serious literature and is one reason the dispute has been so durable: it is carried on by people who think the theory worth the trouble of refuting or repairing exactly, and exactness of that order is a form of regard that slogans cannot counterfeit.

### What does an invariance condition do in the simultaneous repair?

It supplies the missing equation by decree. The simultaneous system has one more unknown than its conditions can fix, so the repairer must stipulate which aggregate stays anchored: total price to total value, or total profit to total surplus value. The stipulation, not the data, decides which equality survives.

The stipulation has an intuitive face that the technical language hides. Imagine the price system as a photograph of the value system taken from an unknown distance. The simultaneous conditions fix the photograph's internal proportions completely: every price relative to every other price, and the profit rate, are settled by the technology and the wage. What they cannot fix is the distance, the scale at which the photograph is printed. Declaring that total price shall equal total value is choosing a print size so that the areas match in aggregate; declaring that total profit shall equal total surplus value chooses the size so that one designated region matches. Either choice is available, neither is dictated, and whichever region was not designated will mismatch. Marx's text behaves as if both regions match at once, which is possible only for one special print, the photograph that did not need scaling at all. That special case is the economy in which prices equal values throughout, the first volume's world, where the transformation was never required. The rigorous literature's enduring point is that the transformation is needed exactly when it cannot deliver both of its advertised conservations.

## The sibling problem in the second volume

The transformation is not the only place where Marx's two accountings must be made to agree, and its sibling problem in the second volume illuminates it by resemblance. The reproduction schemes of that volume divide the economy into two great departments, one making means of production and one making articles of consumption, and trace the exchanges by which each department's output replaces the other's inputs and feeds its workers and owners. The schemes are kept entirely in values. Every magnitude in them is a labour magnitude, and the balance conditions they derive, the proportions between the departments that let the economy repeat or expand itself, are conditions on labour allocations. The moment the third volume's prices are admitted, a question of exactly the transformation's family arises: do the balance conditions still hold when the exchanges occur at prices of production, which systematically revalue each department's output relative to its labour content? The departments have different compositions, so repricing swells one department's money receipts relative to the other's, and the physical proportions that balanced in values need not balance in money without a compensating movement somewhere.

Marx never posed that question in the second volume's terms, and the literature has generally treated the schemes and the transformation in separate compartments, the first belonging to accumulation theory and the second to price theory. The separation is a convenience with a cost. The temporal school's constructions, which follow the economy period by period, are in effect attempts to run reproduction and transformation as one process, each period's prices emerging from the last period's advances; the single-system readings likewise lean on the second volume's money circuits. A full treatment of the schemes belongs to this cluster's article on the reproduction schemas themselves, and the present article notes the kinship for one reason: it corrects the impression that the transformation is an isolated blemish on an otherwise seamless text. The value accounting and the price accounting meet at several joints in the mature work, and at every joint the same question waits, whether the meeting has been engineered or merely assumed. Chapter nine is simply the joint where the assumption was first caught.

## The luxury department and the special case

The special case in which both equalities survive deserves a slower look than it usually gets, because it teaches the general case's logic by inversion. Recall the structure: the simultaneous system leaves one scale free, and Bortkiewicz showed that both conservation laws can hold together when the department producing the money commodity has the same composition of capital as the economy's average. In that configuration, the money industry's own output undergoes no net deviation between its value and its price of production: it sells, in aggregate, at its value, so measuring the whole price system in its product introduces no tilt, and the two accountings share a scale without any stipulation. The coincidence required is exact and fragile. The money industry's composition is a fact of mining technology and monetary institutions, not a magnitude that adjusts to oblige theorists, and in any actual economy it will sit somewhere off the average, by a margin that fixes the size of the discrepancy between the two equalities whichever one is chosen as anchor.

Defenders of Marx have occasionally tried to promote the special case into the general truth by arguing that the money commodity's industry might be expected, under competition and the free movement of capital, to gravitate toward the average composition. The argument does not survive inspection: competition equalises profit rates, not compositions, and compositions are set by the physical techniques of each trade, over which the profit rate's equality exercises no levelling power. Gold mining is as capital-intensive as its geology dictates whether or not its returns match the average. The special case is therefore best read as Bortkiewicz's courtesy and his precision combined: he identified the exact conditions under which his predecessor's claims would have been right, conditions the world has no tendency to supply. There is a methodological lesson in that courtesy which the whole literature would later need. Stating the conditions under which a claim holds is a more devastating form of criticism than denying the claim, because it converts a slogan into a measurement: every subsequent defender must argue that the conditions obtain, and every subsequent critic need only point to the gold industry's actual balance sheet. The transformation debate after 1907 has that measured character at its best, and the special case is where the character was set.

## The money commodity and the question of scale

One strand of the formalisation deserves isolation, because it explains why the missing condition shows up as a choice about measurement rather than as a missing fact about the world. In Marx's economy, money is gold, and gold is produced by an industry like any other, with its own composition of capital and its own price of production. The price system is denominated in gold, which means the scale of prices relative to values is partly a fact about the gold industry: if gold is produced by a capital-intensive industry, the price of production of its output exceeds its value, and every price quoted in gold inherits a tilt from that single industry's composition. Bortkiewicz saw that the freedom to impose one aggregate equality is precisely the freedom to decide how the gold industry's anomaly is distributed across the accounts: absorbed into the total of prices, or into the total of profits, or shared. The special case in which both equalities hold unforced is the case where the gold industry's composition matches the social average, so its anomaly vanishes and no distribution decision arises.

This is more than a curiosity about commodity money. Strip away gold and substitute a modern unit of account, and the same freedom reappears as the choice of a measuring convention: any system that must express two accountings in one unit has to fix the unit's relation to both, and one relation more than the data determine must always be stipulated. The monetary redefinition's measuring rod, the money that an hour of labour expresses, is exactly such a stipulation, offered openly as a definition where the simultaneous school offers its anchor as an added postulate. The temporal school lets the rod lengthen and shorten as prices move between purchase and sale. Seen from the money side, the three families are three policies toward a single unavoidable act of measurement, and their disagreement is about whether that act should be performed once and fixed, once and by definition, or continuously and historically. That reframing does not dissolve their differences, but it locates them, and it explains a fact that puzzles many newcomers: why a dispute that began as an accusation of arithmetical error matured into a dispute about what measuring an economy in labour time could even mean.

## The simultaneous correction and its price

The family of repairs that descends from Bortkiewicz accepts his frame entire: inputs and outputs valued simultaneously, one system of prices, technology and the real wage as the given data. It is the most conservative of the families in the ledger because it changes the fewest of Marx's commitments beyond the defective step itself. Its representatives, from Seton through the systematic treatises of the nineteen seventies, converge on a common posture: the transformation can be carried through rigorously, the rate of profit and all prices of production can be derived, and exactly one of Marx's aggregate equalities can be retained as a theorem, with the other demoted to a special case. On this reading Marx was substantially right in architecture and wrong in one detail, the detail being his belief that both conservations came free.

The price, however, is not only the surrendered equality, considerable though that is. A subtler cost appears when one asks what work the value magnitudes do inside the simultaneous construction. The prices and the profit rate are determined by the technical conditions of production and the real wage; the labour values can be computed from the same data, in parallel, and the two computations never touch. The value system becomes a second set of books kept alongside the first, accurate, consistent, and causally inert, a shadow cast by the physical data rather than a mechanism driving the prices. It was this feature, visible already in the formalisation's first generation, that a later intervention would weaponise into the eraser objection, the strongest single argument in the entire literature. The simultaneous family survives that objection, if it does, not by denying the parallel bookkeeping but by contesting what the value books are for, a defence postponed to a later section where it can be given at full length.

For the field as a whole, the field mapped in [marxist economics complete guide](/2016/04/01/marxist-economics-complete-guide/), the simultaneous correction holds a particular position: it is the version of the transformation that a mainstream-trained economist is most likely to meet and to grant as intelligible, and its concessions have therefore done much to define what educated opinion takes the problem to have established. That standing makes precision about its price doubly important. It does not show that Marx's procedure was sound. It shows that a neighbouring procedure is sound, that the neighbouring procedure preserves one of his two conservation laws as a theorem, and that the labour values within it function as an accounting of embodied labour rather than as the hidden springs of price. Readers who understand that trio of claims understand the simultaneous family entire.

Within the family, one internal argument is worth a reader's attention because it reveals how much judgement the supposedly mechanical repair still requires: which equality should be the survivor? The case for anchoring total price to total value rests on the thought that the price system is the value system's form, so the form should preserve the substance's total measure. The case for anchoring total profit to total surplus value rests on the thought that the theory's explanatory heart is the surplus, so the identity that must not break is the one equating what capital receives with what labour surrendered. Meek's migration from one preference toward the other across his career tracks the argument's genuine difficulty: he began by favouring the price anchor and came to treat the profit anchor as the more defensible, on the ground that the theory can survive a rescaling of its price level more easily than a divorce between its two accounts of the surplus. Nothing in the mathematics prefers either; the preference encodes a decision about what the theory is for, taken inside the family that prides itself on having the fewest decisions to take. The ledger's lesson could not ask for a cleaner exhibit.

## What the deviation pattern predicts

A feature of the transformation that both camps sometimes underplay is that its deviations are not noise. On the theory's own construction, the direction of each industry's departure from value is fixed by its composition: the heavier the constant capital relative to the wage bill, the further its production price rises above its value; the lighter, the lower it falls below. The pattern is a prediction, and a surprisingly exposed one. It says that across an economy, the ratio of price to value should rise systematically with capital intensity, crossing equality near the industry of average composition, where price and value coincide. Whatever one thinks of the derivation's consistency, the pattern gives the theory a handle on the world that pure bookkeeping would not: a random scatter of deviations would embarrass the construction in a way that the aggregate argument alone never could, and the empirical literature's sectoral studies, whatever their other disagreements, have generally found the predicted tilt rather than its absence, with argument concentrated on the tilt's size and explanation.

The crossing point repays a moment's attention, because it corrects a lazy assumption about what the average composition means. The industry whose composition matches the social average is not a typical industry in the statistical sense; it is a construction, the point at which the redistribution happens to leave a sector's receipts unchanged. In a real economy few industries sit exactly there, and the identity of the crossing industries shifts as techniques change, with one era's average industry becoming a later era's capital-heavy one as its rivals mechanise around it. The transformation is thus not a fixed table of markups but a moving relation between each sector and the whole, re-derived every time the whole's average composition moves. That mobility is one reason the temporal school finds the timeless version of the construction uncongenial: a relation re-derived by history at every step sits awkwardly in a frame that allows only one derivation, performed once, outside time. The point does not decide between the schools, but it disciplines the imagery both should use, away from architecture and toward weather.

## Which capital earns the profit?

A quieter technicality runs beneath the main dispute and periodically surfaces to complicate it: the rate of profit is a ratio, and a ratio has a denominator, and the denominator's identity is less obvious than it looks. Profit accrues, in Marx's construction, on the total capital advanced: the whole value of plant, materials, and wages laid out, including the portion of the machinery that survives the year intact. But the costs charged against the year's revenue include only the portion of the machinery consumed, its wear and tear transferred piecemeal into the product. A capital-intensive industry therefore advances a mountain and consumes a molehill, earning its average profit on the mountain while replacing only the molehill, and its price of production must be built to respect both figures at once. Marx handled the distinction carefully in his example, and the formal literature preserves it, but the distinction multiplies the places where a valuation decision hides: the surviving stock must be valued at each accounting date, and valued at what, its historical cost, its replacement cost, or its price of production are three different answers that three different schools have defended.

Turnover adds a second layer to the denominator question. Capitals in different trades cycle at different speeds: a baker's advance returns in days, a shipbuilder's in years. The general rate of profit that competition equalises is an annual rate, profit measured against capital over a common period, so slow-turning capitals must earn more on each cycle to match the annual yield of fast ones. Marx's third volume knows this and treats turnover in its own chapters, but the transformation as stated abstracts from it, and every rigorous rebuilding must decide whether to restore it. The decisions interact: a scheme that values the surviving stock at replacement cost and adjusts for turnover is a different instrument from one that does neither, and comparisons between schools that ignore the interaction mistake differences of instrumentation for differences of doctrine. The ledger's columns cannot display every such decision, which is why the empirical literature's apparently contradictory results so often dissolve, on inspection, into bookkeeping. The lesson for readers is the one this article keeps teaching in new forms: in transformation country, always ask what the denominator is doing before asking whether the numerator is right.

## Rent, interest, and the later divisions of the surplus

The transformation, as this article has presented it, ends with industrial capitals sharing the pooled surplus at a uniform rate, and a reader of the third volume knows that the book does not end there. The surplus undergoes further divisions that the transformation abstracts from: a portion is surrendered as interest to money capital, a portion as rent to the owners of land and other scarce conditions of production, a portion retained by merchant capital for the labour of circulation. Marx treats those divisions after the formation of the general rate, and in his order of exposition they presuppose it: rent and interest are shares carved from a profit whose average magnitude must first exist before it can be carved. The abstraction is defensible, and every repair in the ledger shares it, but it is worth stating plainly because it locates the transformation in the theory's larger architecture. The problem this article examines is the first joint between production and distribution, the passage from surplus value to profit of enterprise in its averaged form. The later joints, where rent's peculiar logic (its dependence on fertility, location, and monopoly rather than on capital advanced) creates difficulties of its own, belong to other controversies and mostly to other articles.

The abstraction has one consequence for the transformation debate itself that is rarely noticed. Because rent is paid out of the surplus and arises from conditions that no amount of capital movement can replicate, the land-using industries never fully join the equalisation the transformation describes: their prices carry a rent component that answers to scarcity, not to composition. A complete price theory in Marx's manner therefore contains, alongside the transformed values, an element that was never a value magnitude at all in the same sense, and the tidy picture of one system converting into another is complicated from within the theory's own later chapters. The point matters for grading the repairs: a reconstruction that handles the industrial core elegantly has not thereby handled the whole of the third volume, and the ledger's silence about rent is a scoping decision, inherited from the entire literature, rather than a judgement that rent is simple. Readers proceeding from here to the cluster's other articles should carry the caution with them: in this theory, every solved joint reveals the next.

## An exposition carries the problem into English

For its first three and a half decades the rigorous debate ran in German and in the journals of the Marxist movement itself, which limited its audience severely. The crossing into English is conventionally dated to 1942, when Paul Sweezy's Theory of Capitalist Development, a systematic exposition of Marxian economics written for anglophone readers, presented Bortkiewicz's construction and its conclusion in an appendix that generations of students then worked through. Sweezy's own verdict was measured in a way that set a tone the later English literature did not always keep: he treated the formal objection as sound against Marx's letter, denied that it touched the theory's essential claims about exploitation, and predicted that the problem would shrink in importance as the theory's other parts were developed. The appendix mattered more than the verdict. Once the construction existed in English, embedded in the most widely read Marxian textbook of its generation, the transformation problem became a standard topic wherever the theory was taught or attacked, and the terms Bortkiewicz had fixed, the two equalities, the simultaneity requirement, the anchoring choice, became the terms of the English debate.

English-language engagement had not waited for Sweezy in complete silence. Maurice Dobb, the Cambridge economist whose Political Economy and Capitalism appeared in 1937, had already treated the transformation as a live question for English readers, defending a reading on which the third volume's construction was substantially sound and the critics had mistaken a change of analytical level for a contradiction. Dobb's treatment mattered less for its verdict than for its location: it placed the problem inside the Cambridge conversation about capital theory that would later produce Sraffa's own work, and so connected the Marxian debate, early and almost accidentally, to the milieu from which its most formidable external challenge would eventually come. The postwar decades in English added refinements rather than revolutions. Seton's generalisation has been mentioned. Michio Morishima's work in the early nineteen seventies rebuilt the simultaneous framework with modern methods and proved, within it, a result that both camps have used since: in a viable economy, positive profit exists if and only if the rate of surplus value is positive, a proposition known as the fundamental Marxian theorem. Whatever else the theorem does, it shows that the simultaneous frame is not a hospitable environment for the claim that exploitation is an illusion generated by bad accounting; inside the critics' own preferred construction, the existence of profit and the existence of unpaid labour time stand or fall together. That result will matter when this article reaches the question whether the transformation problem destroys the labour theory of value, since it separates the fate of the price derivation from the fate of the exploitation claim in a way neither slogan allows.

## The systematisation era

Between the exposition of 1942 and the eraser of 1971, and with gathering speed afterward, the transformation passed from a controversy into a curriculum. The postwar treatises on Marxian economics gave it a standard chapter; the new journals of heterodox economics, founded as the universities expanded, gave it a standing forum; and a generation of mathematically trained writers rebuilt the simultaneous construction with the full apparatus of linear theory, proving the existence and uniqueness results described elsewhere in this article and exploring the construction's behaviour under technical change, joint production's simpler cousins, and alternative closures for the wage. Morishima's treatise of the early nineteen seventies stands as the era's monument: a complete restatement of the theory's formal core in which the transformation occupies its settled place as a solved problem in one sense, the simultaneous sense, and the fundamental theorem stands guard over the exploitation claim. To read the era's best work is to watch a research programme doing what research programmes do at their healthiest, converting a founding embarrassment into a set of theorems and a teaching order.

The systematisation had a subtler effect on the dispute's sociology, visible only in retrospect. Once the simultaneous construction became the version taught, it became the version the theory's critics outside the field believed themselves to be criticising, and the version its defenders inside the field felt obliged to defend, so that the monetary and temporal alternatives, when they arrived in the nineteen eighties and after, had to fight not only for their merits but against the curriculum's inertia. A construction's pedagogical victory can outlast its argumentative one, and several participants in the later phases remark, with varying bitterness, that they are debating a textbook as much as a theory. The pattern is general in economics and worth a student's notice: the transformation problem's history is, among other things, a history of which statement of it got into the textbooks at each era, and the statements' succession explains the debate's rhythm of settlement and reopening better than any single argument does. Settlements in this field are real but institutional, and institutions have revision cycles.

## The iterative route

One repair refuses the simultaneous frame while accepting that inputs must be transformed. Anwar Shaikh, in work published in the late nineteen seventies, proposed taking Marx's procedure literally as the first step of a repetition: transform the outputs, then feed the resulting prices back as the values at which inputs are bought, transform again, and continue until the figures stop moving. Each pass corrects the previous pass's input error by a smaller amount, and the sequence settles, in the cases studied, close to the prices the simultaneous system delivers in one stroke. The attraction of the route is its fidelity to the text's own order of operations: Marx, on this reading, was not wrong but unfinished, and the iteration is the second pass his own chapter nine remark about input prices had gestured toward. A draftsman who stops after the first ruling of a line has not drawn a false line, only an incomplete one.

The price of the iterative route is written in its destination. If the repeated transformation converges to the simultaneous solution, then its final position inherits every question that solution faces, including the question of what the value magnitudes explain once prices are fully determined by technical data and the wage. The iteration buys a story about process, about how an economy or an analyst might grope toward consistent prices, and process stories have genuine value in a theory that prides itself on dynamics. What it cannot buy is a different resting place. The ledger therefore records it as preserving both equalities only in the limit, which is a courteous way of saying that at every finite stage, the stage at which any actual economy or textbook finds itself, some residue of the original inconsistency remains on the page. Whether that residue is a fatal flaw or an acceptable approximation is a judgement about standards, and the dispute between the iterative school's defenders and its critics is best understood as a dispute about how exact a derivation must be before its author may claim to have derived anything at all.

The iterative idea also carries a suggestion about the real economy that its author has pressed and its critics have resisted, and the suggestion is worth recording because it shows how a bookkeeping proposal can grow theoretical ambitions. If prices in an actual capitalist economy are formed by successive adjustment, each round of buying and selling repricing inputs in light of the last round's outputs, then the economy itself performs something like the iteration, never resting at the simultaneous solution but perpetually approaching it through the noise of trade. On that picture, the simultaneous system is not the economy's state but its unattainable limit, and the deviations between observed prices and both values and prices of production become the expected texture of a process photographed mid-adjustment. Critics reply that a convergence story needs a demonstration that the real process converges rather than wanders, and that the mathematics of the iteration, performed on paper with the whole economy's data in hand, is a poor portrait of decentralised haggling conducted in ignorance. The exchange remains unresolved, and it marks the iterative family's distinctive contribution: alone among the repairs, it tries to make the transformation happen in time, and so alone faces the question of what a derivation means for a world that never sits still long enough to be derived.

## The monetary redefinition

The second great family of repairs changes not the order of the calculation but the meaning of one of its terms. Marx defined the value of labour power as the labour embodied in the goods a worker's wage commands, the subsistence basket. The interpretation associated with Gérard Duménil's work of 1980 and Duncan Foley's of 1982, and known since the early nineteen eighties as the New Interpretation, redefines that value in money: the value of labour power is the share of the economy's living labour that the money wage represents. A measuring rod connects the two worlds, the monetary expression of labour time, which states how many units of money correspond to one hour of socially performed labour across the economy. Divide the money value added by the hours worked and the rod is set; express the wage through it and the wage becomes a quantity of labour, not because the basket was costed in labour terms but because money itself is being read as labour's representative.

### What exactly does the monetary redefinition redefine?

It redefines the value of labour power. Rather than treating that value as the labour embodied in a basket of wage goods, it treats the money wage itself as labour power's value expression, a claim on a definite share of the living labour performed across the economy.

Within this frame the two aggregate equalities change their address. They are asserted for the net product, the value added during the period, rather than for the gross output including replaced materials, and at that address they hold exactly and always. Total money value added, read through the measuring rod, is total living labour; total profit, read the same way, is total surplus labour. The conservation Marx asserted is recovered, not as a theorem derived from a transformation procedure but as a consequence of how the terms are defined. Defenders regard this as the interpretation's elegance: Marx's deepest claims, they argue, were always about the net product and the division of the working day, and the gross-output framing that generated the problem was a legacy of the text's unfinished state. The rate of exploitation becomes, on this reading, the ratio of profit to wages in money, a magnitude national accountants already publish, and the theory gains an empirical handle that the embodied-labour bookkeeping never offered.

The price is the one the ledger records. Equalities that hold by definition cannot be confirmed by anything, and cannot fail; a critic is entitled to ask whether the theory has been saved or merely restated in a vocabulary where its central claims are true by construction. The older question, whether value magnitudes determined independently of prices explain those prices, is not answered by the monetary redefinition but set aside, since on this reading there is no independent system behind the money accounts for the explanation to run from. Proponents reply that the demand for such a system was the mistake, a relic of treating values as physical quantities rather than as social accounting. The exchange is genuine and unresolved, and it illustrates the ledger's purpose better than any other row: the New Interpretation is internally consistent, empirically fertile, and bought by surrendering the two-system architecture that made the transformation problem statable in its original form. The full comparison of this family with the temporal one occupies [new interpretation and tssi](/2016/05/15/new-interpretation-and-tssi/) in this series; what matters here is its cell in the ledger, consistency purchased by redefinition.

The restriction of the equalities to the net product, which the monetary family's critics treat as a quiet retreat, deserves a defence of its own logic before the family is left. Gross output counts the replaced materials twice over in a certain sense: the steel in the machine and the machine in the factory both enter the year's product figures, and the value of the materials is a transfer, not a creation, since their labour was performed in earlier periods. What the economy's living labour adds in the period is the net product, and it is arguable that Marx's conservation intuitions, formed around the working day's division, were always really about that addition and its split between wages and profit. On this view the gross framing was the accident, inherited from the example's five industries and their full capitals, and the monetary reading's restriction to the net product is a recovery of the claim's true scope rather than a narrowing of it. The argument does not end the dispute, since the text of chapter nine speaks of totals without the restriction, and the restriction's defenders must still explain the text away. But it converts the family's price from a bare definitional toll into a substantive thesis about which totals the theory was ever about, and substantive theses can be argued on evidence in a way that tolls cannot, which the family counts, reasonably, as progress.

## The temporal route

The third family attacks the frame from the opposite side, denying that the simultaneity requirement is legitimate at all. The temporal single-system interpretation, developed from the late nineteen eighties by Andrew Kliman, Alan Freeman, and their collaborators, begins from a simple observation about time. Inputs are bought before outputs are sold. The prices at which a capitalist acquires machinery and materials are fixed at the moment of purchase, in the past; the prices at which the finished output sells are fixed later, in a market that may have moved. To demand that one set of prices value both sides of the account is, on this reading, to impose a timelessness on the theory that its subject matter forbids, and the famous inconsistency is then an artefact of the imposition: Marx's procedure looks defective only when judged by a rule, simultaneity, that he never accepted and that the formalisation smuggled in.

Enter the inputs at what was actually paid for them at the moment of purchase, as historical fact, and enter the outputs at the prices their market eventually gives them, and the two aggregate equalities can be maintained across the sequence of periods, each period inheriting the last period's results as its starting data. The price of production of a commodity in this scheme is anchored to what was actually advanced for it, not to what its inputs would cost if repurchased at the moment of sale, and the general rate of profit is a magnitude with a history rather than a solution to a timeless puzzle. Advocates present this as a recovery of Marx's own monetary, sequential way of thinking, obscured by a century of equilibrium habits imported from the economics the theory was meant to criticise. They point, with some justice, to the textual evidence that Marx thought in circuits extended over time, outlays preceding receipts, rather than in the frozen cross-sections of the simultaneous construction.

### How does the timing of valuation change the outcome?

The temporal reading values inputs at the prices actually paid when they were bought, and outputs at the prices ruling when they are sold. Because the two valuations belong to different moments, no single price vector must satisfy both sides of the account at once, and the contradiction dissolves.

Critics of the temporal reading, who are numerous and include economists sympathetic to much else in Marx, press two charges, and fairness requires stating them as strongly as their authors do. The first is that the equalities are preserved only because the bookkeeping conventions for carrying unsold stocks and partly used equipment from one period to the next have been arranged to preserve them, so that consistency is achieved by the accountant's choices rather than exhibited by the economy. The second is that a valuation anchored to historical purchase prices loses contact with the reproduction of the system: what a machine cost a decade ago matters less, for the economy's continuation, than what replacing it costs now, and a theory of reproduction built on sunk figures describes the past at the price of the present. Defenders answer that replacement valuation is itself a convention, that the charge of arbitrariness cuts in every direction, and that the critics' frame simply reimposes the simultaneity under dispute. The argument remains open, conducted in the journals at a high technical level, and the ledger's entry reflects that openness: both equalities preserved, the toll recorded as the abandonment of the timeless frame, the criticism recorded as dependence on convention. A reader who wants the two monetary and temporal families set against each other directly, in their own terms and at length, will find that treatment in the series article already linked above.

The sharpest form of the critics' first charge deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is the point on which the temporal school's fate may ultimately turn. If inputs are carried at their purchase prices while outputs are valued at their sale prices, then a period of generally rising prices will manufacture accounting profit from the revaluation of stocks alone, and a period of falling prices will destroy it, whether or not any surplus labour was performed. Critics have constructed examples in which the temporal books show healthy profit alongside negative surplus value for a period, and they present the examples as a reductio: a theory of exploitation whose central magnitude can change sign because the price level moved has confused a monetary phenomenon with a real one. The school's defenders reply that the examples trade on an equivocation, since the surplus relevant to the theory is measured over the circuit as a whole and in money terms that already register the price movement, and that the critics' own frame hides the same phenomenon by freezing it out of view. The exchange is technical, unresolved, and conducted at a level of mutual comprehension that the dispute's popular reputation would not lead one to expect, which is itself a datum worth recording in an article pledged to describe the literature as it is.

## Single-system readings

A fourth family dissolves the problem by dissolving its stage set. On single-system and macro-monetary readings, associated in their developed form with Fred Moseley's work, there never were two systems awaiting connection. The economy's monetary aggregates, the total price of output, the total money advanced, the total profit, are the primary magnitudes, given in the actual accounts of actual firms, and labour values enter as an interpretation of those aggregates: the total price expresses the total labour performed, and profit expresses surplus labour, without any prior realm of embodied-labour coefficients from which prices must be derived. The transformation then is not a journey between realms but a redistribution within one, the sharing of a given money surplus among sectors according to the size of their advances, and it poses no consistency problem because nothing had to be converted.

The elegance is real, and so is the toll, which the ledger states as the surrender of the two-system architecture. In the architecture's place stands a reading on which Marx's value theory was always a monetary theory of the aggregate, and the embodied-labour magnitudes of the first volume were a provisional device for displaying the source of surplus before the monetary form could be developed. Critics from within the Marxian camp reply that this ascribes to the text a coherence purchased by demoting some of its most emphatic claims, and that a theory in which values are read off prices cannot then explain prices without travelling in a circle. Proponents answer that the circle is the critic's invention, since the explanation was never of prices by values but of profit by surplus labour, with prices as the form the explanation's object necessarily takes. The dispute replicates, at the level of architecture, the same choice the whole ledger displays: which of Marx's apparent claims to treat as load-bearing and which as scaffolding. No family escapes the choice; the single-system readings are simply the most candid about making it.

A note on evidence is appropriate before leaving this family. Its proponents lean, more than the other schools do, on Marx's monetary writings: the analysis of money in the first volume's opening chapters, the insistence that value without its money form is an abstraction awaiting its necessary expression, the treatment of the total social capital in the second volume's reproduction schemes, where the accounts are kept in money throughout. Read with those texts in the foreground, the two-system picture can indeed look like a scaffolding Marx himself dismantled as he worked, and the embodied-labour coefficients of the transformation debate like a scholastic hardening of what was always a monetary argument. Read with the third volume's ninth chapter in the foreground, the same texts look like stages toward a derivation that the single-system reading then declines to perform. The textual evidence underdetermines the choice, which is why the family's critics and defenders can each assemble a plausible Marx, and why the ledger, rather than the archive, must carry the comparison: whatever the texts permit, the readings differ in what they preserve, and preservation has a price list even when intentions are honourable on every side.

## Why the problem is also about money

Step back from the constructions and a pattern in them becomes visible that the participants do not always state: every family is, among other things, a doctrine about money. Marx's theory insists that value must express itself in money, that the labour embodied in commodities counts socially only when it has been validated in exchange against the universal equivalent. The value accounts are therefore never, even in the first volume, a self-sufficient physical ledger; they are a claim about what money prices are secretly measuring. The transformation problem is the point where that claim must be cashed, where the secret measure must be shown generating the public prices. Its failure can then be redescribed in monetary terms: the theory possesses a doctrine of what money measures and lacks, in its founder's draft, a consistent account of how the measuring proceeds when competition rearranges the amounts. Each repair is a way of completing the monetary doctrine. The simultaneous school completes it by making the measurement timeless, a snapshot in which the measuring rod and the measured are fixed together. The monetary redefinition completes it by enthroning the rod: money first, labour as money's interpretation. The temporal school completes it by letting the measurement happen in historical sequence, purchase by purchase and sale by sale, as it does in any actual firm's books. The single-system readings complete it by denying that there was ever a pre-monetary realm to measure from.

This reframing is not a decorative alternative; it explains the dispute's otherwise puzzling emotional register. Arguments about money's nature are among the oldest and least settleable in economics, because money is at once a tool, a social relation, and a unit of account, and theorists who privilege different faces of it talk past one another with complete sincerity. A reader who has watched the transformation literature's participants accuse one another of elementary confusion, while each side's mathematics checks out on its own premises, has usually been watching a money argument conducted in the vocabulary of a price argument. Naming the submerged dispute does not end it, but it converts it from a scandal into a subject, and it prepares the reader for the objection taken up next, which is powerful precisely because it refuses the monetary reframing and insists on judging the theory as a piece of price machinery alone.

## The circuit that must close

There is a way of stating the transformation's stakes that bypasses the technicalities and explains why non-specialists have always sensed that something large is at issue. Marx's capitalism runs on a circuit: money advanced, commodities produced, commodities sold, more money returned. The increment between the money returned and the money advanced is the system's fuel and its puzzle. The first volume locates the increment's birth inside production, in the gap between the labour performed and the labour repaid. The third volume watches the increment being divided among capitals that mostly did not attend its birth in proportion to their shares. For the theory to be one theory, the division must be a division of that increment and not of some other fund, and the prices at which the division occurs must be the prices at which the produced commodities actually clear. The transformation is the audit of that requirement: it asks the circuit's books, kept in labour at the point of production and in money at the point of sale, to reconcile at the year's end.

Audits can fail in two ways, and the distinction maps the literature's camps precisely. The books may fail to reconcile because the bookkeeping is wrong, in which case better bookkeeping, the repairs, is the answer. Or they may fail to reconcile because they were never books of the same firm, in which case the demand for reconciliation is the error, and the single-system and form-oriented readings are the answer. Every participant in the dispute is, knowingly or not, voting on which kind of failure this is. The eraser objection is the limiting case of the second vote: the value books are not even the firm's books, merely a consultant's parallel report, impressive and disposable. The defence's origins argument is the limiting case of the first vote disciplined by concession: the books are the firm's, the audit found a genuine discrepancy, and the discrepancy is confined to the distribution ledger while the production ledger stands. Framing the dispute as an audit's failure does not settle it, but it gives the general reader the correct instinct about its scale: what failed is not a sum but a reconciliation between the two languages in which one economy describes itself, and economies, like firms, can survive unreconciled languages for a long time while misleading everyone who reads only one of them.

## The road to the eraser: two decades of preparation

The eraser objection did not arrive from a clear sky in 1971, and its author had been circling the target for twenty years. Samuelson's engagement with Marxian economics began in the nineteen forties, in papers that treated the theory's constructions with a technician's detached respect: the models were formalised, their assumptions exposed, their valid results separated from their rhetoric, in a manner that treated Marx as a minor post-Ricardian whose system could be translated into the profession's standard notation and assessed there. A celebrated dissection at the end of the nineteen fifties examined the theory's account of wages and interest in that spirit. The posture throughout was neither worshipful nor contemptuous; it was annexationist. Whatever in Marx could be stated as a determinate model would be, and whatever survived translation would be credited, and whatever did not survive would be classified as metaphysics wearing the costume of economics.

The eraser paper is what that programme looks like when its patience runs out. By 1971 the simultaneous constructions had been fully absorbed into the profession's toolkit, in part through Samuelson's own earlier formalisations, and the transformation could be presented entire within a few pages of standard notation. At that point the question of redundancy, which requires the price system to be completely derivable before the value detour can be pronounced erasable, could be posed in its strongest form, with the derivation exhibited rather than alleged. The paper's tone, playful, quotable, lethal, was calibrated to its venue in a journal of surveys read by the whole profession, most of whom would never open Bortkiewicz or Seton. Its effect on the profession's settled opinion was out of all proportion to its length, and it fixed the terms in which two generations of economists who never studied the problem nonetheless knew its verdict: an interesting detour, erased. The Marxian literature's response, several books and a journal subfield, has been working to complicate that verdict ever since, with the partial successes this article has recorded. Understanding the eraser's preparation matters for judging it: an objection built by the man who helped build the frame it judges deserves the full-strength hearing the next section gives it, and also deserves to have its own premises, the annexationist programme itself, held up to the light in the section after.

## The eraser objection at full strength

Every repair surveyed so far accepts that something in Marx needs repair. The most damaging intervention in the literature accepts nothing of the kind, because it denies that the repaired theory retains any explanatory function at all. Paul Samuelson's 1971 paper on the transformation, written with the simultaneous construction fully in view, gave the objection its enduring image. The analyst contemplating Marx's two systems, Samuelson suggested, writes down the value schema, contemplates it, takes an eraser and rubs it out, and then writes down the price schema in the space cleared. The values are a detour: everything in the final account, every price and the profit rate itself, can be derived directly from the physical data of production, the techniques in use and the goods workers consume, without passing through labour values at all. A detour that can be erased without the destination changing is not a road; it is a ritual performed on the way to a destination reached by other means.

The objection deserves its reputation, and this article's neutrality discipline requires giving it without softening. Its force is not the mockery in the image, though the image did real work in the profession's memory. Its force is a claim about determination. If the technical conditions and the real wage fix the price system completely, then any magnitude computed from those same conditions, as labour values are, stands downstream of the explanation, an alternative bookkeeping of results obtained independently. Bookkeeping can be elegant, can be illuminating, can even be the bookkeeping a theorist prefers for moral clarity, but it cannot claim to explain what it merely redescribes. On the eraser view, the labour theory of value after the transformation is in exactly that position, and the century of repairs surveyed above are so many ways of polishing the detour. Ian Steedman's later development of the objection, built on Piero Sraffa's reconstruction of classical price theory, sharpens the charge further by exhibiting economies in which the value bookkeeping behaves pathologically; that development belongs to the Sraffian challenge and is treated in [sraffa and marxist economics](/2016/05/01/sraffa-and-marxist-economics/), to which readers are directed rather than given a summary here that would do it less than justice.

### What precisely does the eraser image claim?

It claims that the value calculation is a redundant detour. On this view, the economy's technical conditions and the real wage already determine prices and the profit rate completely, so the analyst may write down the value schema, rub it out, and write the price schema in its place.

Two features of the objection are often missed by those who cite it most gleefully. The first is that it grants the simultaneous repair everything it asks. Samuelson did not deny that prices of production can be consistently derived, or that one aggregate equality can be preserved, or that the fundamental theorem connects profit to surplus labour. His claim was that none of this rescues the value apparatus, because the apparatus is not doing the deriving. The objection is therefore untouched by further improvements in the repairs; a more elegant detour is still a detour. The second feature is that the objection's standard of explanation is itself a philosophical commitment, not a neutral measuring rod. To say that values explain nothing because prices can be computed without them is to assume that explanation means computational priority, that the explainer must be an input to the calculation of the explained. Whether that assumption is the right one for a theory of social forms is a question the objection must argue, not presuppose, and it is at exactly that joint that the defence attacks.

## The defence: what values are meant to explain

The defence begins by conceding the detour's geography and disputing its interpretation. Prices of production, the defenders grant, can be computed from technical data and the real wage; they were computable so, in principle, all along. But the computation answers a question about distribution: given a surplus to be shared, how is it shared among sectors, and at what prices does the sharing proceed? It does not answer, and does not pretend to answer, the prior question of origin: why is there a surplus at all, a mass of profit confronting the economy's capitals as a whole, available for sharing in the first place? The value account exists to answer that prior question. Aggregate profit exists, on this account, because the labour performed in the economy exceeds the labour embodied in the goods the performers receive back as wages; the surplus is produced in the working day before it is distributed in the market. Values are not a rival algorithm for prices but the theory's answer to a question the price algorithm never poses.

### What do values explain that prices leave unexplained?

Prices distribute a surplus among competing capitals; they do not say where the surplus comes from. The value account answers that prior question: profit exists in aggregate because workers perform more labour than the labour embodied in what their wages buy. Distribution presupposes something to distribute.

The fundamental Marxian theorem, mentioned earlier, gives the defence a formal foothold inside the opposition's own construction: within the simultaneous frame itself, profit and surplus labour are positive together or not at all, so the connection between exploitation and profit is not a verbal gloss but a theorem of the shared mathematics. Defenders differ on how much weight that foothold bears. The stronger claim is that it shows the value theory's core proposition survives translation into the critics' notation, which is what a robust explanation ought to do. The weaker claim is merely that the eraser image, whatever its force against price determination, does not reach the origin question, and that Samuelson's rhetoric invited readers to mistake a blow against one function of the theory for a blow against all of them. Either way, the defence relocates the theory's centre from the computation of prices to the existence and magnitude of the surplus, and on that ground it stands up reasonably well. The eraser, on the defence's telling, rubs out a calculation the theory never needed for the purpose the objector assumes, while leaving untouched the calculation it does need.

### Why does the defence narrow what the theory claims?

Because the defence works by retreating to ground that holds. It concedes that value magnitudes do not determine individual prices, nor even the general profit rate, and claims instead that they explain the existence and size of the aggregate surplus. A theory of origins replaces a theory of prices.

Honesty requires the concession inside that H3 answer to be faced squarely, because it is the hinge of the whole modern dispute. The first volume's presentation invites a stronger reading than origins alone. Its language of value as the substance regulating exchange, its magnitudes computed before prices are mentioned, its author's evident belief in the third volume that a derivation had been supplied, all support the impression of a theory in which values do determine prices, mediately and through the transformation. The defence asks readers to surrender that impression and to hold instead that the determination language was the scaffolding of an inquiry into origins. Many readers, friendly and hostile, have judged the surrender too large to be called an interpretation; they call it a replacement. The ledger cannot settle that judgement, but it can display its cost in the same row as the others: the defence preserves the theory's account of exploitation and its two equalities in spirit, and pays by narrowing the claim from price determination to surplus origin. Whether the narrowed theory is still Marx's theory is the question the next sections approach from two directions, first through the distinction between reconstruction and correction, then through the question of what the problem does and does not destroy.

## The answer from the side of form

A further family of responses should be recorded even though it declines the ledger's terms, because its influence on how the texts are read has been large. The value-form tradition, descending from Isaak Rubin's essays of the nineteen twenties and revived by the German re-reading of Marx in the nineteen sixties and seventies, holds that the transformation problem in its standard form rests on a misidentification of what value is. If value is not a substance embodied in products, a quantity of congealed labour awaiting conversion into another quantity, but the social form that private labour takes when its products confront one another as commodities, then the demand for a magnitude-by-magnitude derivation mistakes the theory's object. On this reading, the first volume's analysis is about the necessity of the money form, about why labour in this society must appear as value and value as price, and the third volume's prices of production continue that analysis of forms at a more concrete level. The gap the formalisers found is real but belongs to a quantitative sub-problem that the tradition's founder left genuinely unfinished, not to the theory's core, which was never a machine for deriving prices from labour jelly.

The ledger cannot grade this response in its own columns, and the reason is instructive. The form reading does not preserve the equalities, or surrender them, or redefine the value of labour power; it disputes the jurisdiction of the court, arguing that the conservation claims themselves were a concession Marx's exposition made to the Ricardian idiom of his century. Critics from the formal schools reply that the jurisdiction objection arrives suspiciously late, after the quantitative claims failed, and that Marx's text asserts the equalities in so many words, so that a reading which dissolves them into idiom is a correction more radical than any in the ledger, disguised as a hermeneutic. Defenders answer that the quantitative passages must be read within the exposition's whole movement, and that the tradition mistook a ladder for the building. The exchange reproduces, at the level of reading, the dispute's deepest division: whether the theory's centre of gravity is its magnitudes or its forms. This article's discipline is to record the response, to note that it changes the subject in a principled rather than evasive way when practised at its best, and to observe that its price, in ledger language, is the highest in the room: it surrenders the quantitative theory of price determination entire, in exchange for an account of why the question tempted Marx's century and traps ours.

## The aggregate reading: an older defence in new dress

The defence of the previous sections, relocating the theory's centre to the aggregate surplus, has a pedigree older than the objection it answers, and recognising the pedigree clarifies both. When Lexis proposed in the eighteen nineties that the law of value rules the totality of exchanges while individual prices play free, he was offering the first version of a strategy that recurs whenever the transformation is pressed: ascend a level. At the level of the whole economy, the claims are about conservation, about the identity between the labour performed and the value realised, between the surplus produced and the profit distributed, and those identities have a robustness that item-level claims lack. At that level the eraser loses much of its edge, because the price algorithm, which distributes, genuinely does not generate the totals it distributes among; the totals come from production, from the working day's length and division, and the value language is the language in which the theory states where totals come from. Ascending a level is therefore not mere evasion. It is a claim about which of the theory's propositions were ever load-bearing.

The strategy's cost, flagged when Lexis first offered it and never since removed, is the risk of vacuity at the summit. A conservation identity that holds because its terms are defined to make it hold explains nothing; a conservation identity that holds as a theorem of a construction explains the construction. The modern forms of the aggregate defence differ chiefly in how they manage this risk. The monetary redefinition embraces definition and argues that the identity, definition though it is, organises empirical inquiry fruitfully, which is a pragmatic defence. The temporal reading claims theorem status within its own sequential construction, and its critics dispute the construction's conventions. The simultaneous school, holding one equality as theorem, occupies the middle ground its founder staked. What no form of the defence can do is restore the strong, item-level, derivationist reading that the first volume's rhetoric suggested and the third volume's example seemed to perform. The aggregate reading is the theory after its most ambitious claim has been honourably retired, and the argument between its proponents and its critics is, at the last, an argument about whether what remains is the theory's essence finally clarified or its ambition finally surrendered. That argument is philosophical, and the article's discipline requires leaving it open, with both descriptions on the record.

The aggregate reading received its most distinguished mainstream endorsement in the nineteen seventies, when William Baumol, an economist of impeccable professional standing and no Marxist allegiance, argued in a leading survey journal that the transformation had been persistently misread by its critics: what Marx meant, on Baumol's reading, was precisely an aggregate doctrine, a claim about the totals and their interpretation, and judged as that doctrine the construction was coherent and its conservation claims its substantive core. The endorsement did not end the argument, since the textual question of what Marx meant cannot be settled by authority, but it changed the argument's weather. After Baumol, a critic who treated the aggregate reading as a desperate loyalist invention had to reckon with its presence in the profession's own flagship venues, defended on grounds of textual scholarship by a reader with nothing to rescue. The episode is a useful corrective to the sociology assumed by both camps' folklore, in which charity is supposed to flow along lines of allegiance. In the transformation literature, some of the most careful readings have come from readers who wanted the theory to be wrong in the right way, and some of the least careful from readers who needed it to be right.

## The conservation metaphor and its limits

Marx's generation thought in the shadow of physics' great conservation laws, and the two aggregate equalities bear the mark of that shadow. Energy, the physicists had shown, is neither created nor destroyed in any transformation, only converted between forms, and the analogy to a social magnitude that persists through its conversions, value passing into price as motion passes into heat, has an evident attraction for a theory seeking scientific dignity. The attraction should be resisted past a certain point, and the point is worth marking because the metaphor silently governs much of the dispute's rhetoric. Energy is a substance-like quantity defined independently of the systems that carry it, measurable by procedures that do not presuppose the conservation they test. Value is not obviously anything of the kind. Its magnitudes are constituted by the social process they are meant to measure, and the procedures for computing them, from coefficients or from accounts, presuppose framings that the conservation claim itself needs. To demand of value the behaviour of energy is therefore to demand more than an analogy can deliver, and several of the literature's sterner verdicts amount to punishing the theory for failing a standard its central category never promised to meet.

Yet the metaphor cannot simply be dismissed as the critics' imposition, because the text invites it. Marx's language of totals that are preserved, of a sum of prices equal to a sum of values, of profit as surplus value in distributed form, is the language of a conservation claim, and his defenders' insistence on the equalities across a century shows the claim's centrality to the tradition's self-understanding. The balanced verdict is that the equalities are conservation claims of a peculiar, social kind: assertions that the redistribution performed by competition is a redistribution of a magnitude produced elsewhere, in production, and not a creation ex nihilo by exchange itself. Read so, their content is the denial that markets manufacture the surplus they divide, which is a claim about capitalism's anatomy that could be true even if the strict arithmetic of chapter nine fails. Read as physics, they fail with the arithmetic. The schools in the ledger can be redescribed, one final time, by how literally they take the metaphor: the simultaneous school takes it literally and pays an equality, the monetary school takes it definitionally and pays independence, the temporal school takes it historically and pays simultaneity, and the form school declines the metaphor and pays the quantitative theory. The price list survives every redescription, which is the article's evidence that the price list, and not any of its redescriptions, is the subject's real structure.

There is a corollary about the metaphor's direction of travel that the literature illustrates beautifully. Conservation in physics is discovered, not stipulated: the physicist finds the books balancing and infers a substance. In the transformation debate the order is reversed as often as not: the balance is desired, for reasons of theoretical architecture, and the constructions are then arranged, by anchor, by definition, by frame, to deliver it. Nothing disreputable attaches to the reversal; much of theoretical science works by positing a conservation and hunting for the quantity that obeys it. But the reversal changes the epistemic situation entirely, because a stipulated balance cannot afterwards be cited as independent evidence for the architecture that stipulated it. A surprising share of the debate's circularity, charges of which fly in every direction, reduces to parties citing as discoveries what their constructions installed as premises. The grading questions in the next section are designed to catch exactly this: the second question, whether an equality survives as result or as construction, is the circularity detector, and a reader armed with it can save a great deal of time in a literature where the installations are not always labelled.

## The price of consistency as a grading rule

The claim that gives this article its organising idea can now be stated in its full form. Every internally consistent reconstruction of the transformation abandons at least one thing Marx appears to assert. The simultaneous correction abandons the second aggregate equality. The iterative route abandons exactness at every finite stage. The monetary redefinition abandons the independence of the value system. The temporal reading abandons simultaneity itself. The single-system readings abandon the two-system architecture. The origins defence abandons price determination. Since the abandonments are unavoidable and differ in kind, the rational question is not which reconstruction solves the problem, a form of words that smuggles in the idea of a costless solution, but which abandonment is cheapest relative to what the theory is for. That is the price of consistency, and the dispute's real subject, once the slogans are stripped away, is the price list.

### How should a reader grade the rival reconstructions?

By asking four questions of each. What did it change in Marx's procedure or definitions? Which equalities survive inside it, and by result or by construction? What did it surrender that the original appeared to claim? And what is the strongest criticism its opponents press against it?

Those four questions are the ledger's columns restated as an interrogation, and they protect a reader against the two characteristic frauds of this literature. The first fraud is the claim of costless victory, in which a reconstruction's preservation of the equalities is celebrated and its redefinitions pass unmentioned; the second and third questions expose it. The second fraud is the claim of total refutation, in which a repair's genuine surrender is treated as the theory's death certificate; the first and fourth questions expose that, since a surrender itemised is a research programme with a known cost, not a corpse. Grading by the four questions also explains why intelligent economists, shown the same texts and the same mathematics, reach different verdicts: they weight the rows differently. A theorist who prizes continuity with standard price theory grades the simultaneous correction highest and the temporal reading lowest. A theorist who prizes the conservation claims grades the monetary and temporal families highest. A theorist who prizes the exploitation account grades the origins defence highest and treats the rest as variations. The mathematics is shared; the weights are philosophical, and the literature becomes much less mysterious once the weights are made explicit.

It is worth adding that the grading rule cuts against this article's own temptations. A survey that presents five repairs and a defence must resist arranging them as a staircase toward its favourite, and the four questions, applied evenly, are the resistance. Where the sections above have characterised a family's price, the characterisation comes from the family's own critics and defenders in the published record, not from a house preference, and the strongest version of each position has been the version presented. The rule's final virtue is portability: it grades any future reconstruction on arrival, including ones not yet published, which is the property that lets an article on a living dispute remain useful after the dispute moves.

One refinement of the grading rule is worth adding, because experienced readers of this literature apply it instinctively. Grade the surrender against the theory's own hierarchy of claims, not against a flat list. Marx's writings assert many things with many degrees of emphasis, and a repair that surrenders a peripheral flourish is not paying the same coin as a repair that surrenders a load-bearing proposition. The conservation of the surplus, the claim that profit is unpaid labour in aggregate, sits near the theory's centre; the independence of the value system sits further out; the timeless frame sits further out still, arguably outside the text's own commitments altogether. This is why the temporal school's partisans regard their toll as trivial and the simultaneity they abandon as an import, while their critics, who locate the frame nearer the centre of any science of prices, regard the same toll as ruinous. The grading rule cannot perform the weighing for the reader, since the hierarchy is itself part of what is disputed, but it can force the weighing into the open, where it belongs: every verdict on a reconstruction is secretly a verdict on the hierarchy, and the honest writers in this literature are the ones who publish theirs.

## Reconstruction or correction: drawing the line

### How can a reconstruction be consistent and still be a correction?

Consistency is a property of the repaired system, not proof that the repair was already present in the original. A reconstruction earns its name by showing where the author's procedure failed and what had to change; calling every successful repair a mere clarification hides the change it made.

The line matters because the literature's courtesies blur it. Every repairer calls the work a reconstruction, a recovery of what Marx meant, and some of the claims to recovery are textually strong. The temporal readers can point to Marx's circuits and his money form; the monetary readers can point to his insistence that value must appear as money; the iterative readers can point to the chapter nine remark about cost prices erring from the values of the inputs they embody. That remark deserves its prominence. It shows Marx registering, inside the very chapter at issue, that cost price contains elements bought at prices of production, so the inputs in his table are known by him to be provisional. A writer who notices a difficulty and proceeds anyway, because the provisional calculation displays the mechanism, may fairly be reconstructed by successors who complete the pass he postponed.

But a postponed pass that changes the destination is a correction wearing reconstruction's clothes, and the distinction is not pedantry. When the second pass alters the general rate of profit itself, as the simultaneous literature shows it does, the first pass's results are not approximations to a truth the author possessed; they are results of a different, inconsistent procedure, and the completed theory differs from the text in its quantitative claims and in which of its conservation laws survive. Scholars of the text, notably those who have worked through the manuscripts of the eighteen sixties in their critical edition, have complicated the picture further by showing how provisional the third volume's arrangement was, how much of its finish is Engels's editorial hand, and how Marx's own last notes on the rate of profit range over questions the published chapter treats as closed. None of that diminishes the problem's interest. It relocates some of the blame from the author to the edition, and it strengthens the case for the article's central framing: what exists is a family of consistent theories descended from an inconsistent draft, and descent with modification is the honest description of every one of them.

## The editor's hand: what the manuscripts show

A question of evidence shadows every claim made about Marx's procedure, and the last generation's scholarship has made it impossible to ignore. The third volume is not a book Marx finished. It is an editorial construction, assembled by Engels from notebooks of very uneven finish, the principal manuscript dating from the middle eighteen sixties, before the first volume was even published, with later fragments and second thoughts scattered across two further decades. Engels worked with legendary devotion and considerable intervention: ordering, dividing, titling, smoothing transitions, and constructing from exploratory drafts the appearance of a treatise advancing in steps. The ninth chapter's procedure, with its air of an exercise completed, is partly that appearance's product. Marx's own manuscripts on the rate of profit, worked and reworked across the eighteen seventies, show him worrying at adjacent problems, the effect of rising productivity on the profit rate, the behaviour of the rate under changing compositions, with a tentativeness the published chapter's confidence does not suggest.

The critical edition of the complete writings, publishing the manuscripts in their original state, has allowed scholars to test the editorial construction against the drafts, and the results cut in two directions at once. For the defence, they establish that the transformation as a finished procedure was never Marx's settled doctrine, so that convicting him of its inconsistency convicts a draft; the appropriate response to a draft's gap is exactly what the literature has supplied, completions in several directions, none of which need claim to be the unique recovery of a finished intent. For the critics, the same evidence establishes that the theory's canonical form, the form in which it influenced a century, is the edited form, and a theory is answerable for its canonical form regardless of its draftsman's intentions; readers who built on chapter nine were building on what the tradition handed them. Both points are sound, and their conjunction explains why the textual scholarship, however fascinating, has not closed the dispute: it redistributed responsibility between author, editor, and tradition without dissolving the inconsistency itself, which sits in every version of the argument that tries to transform outputs alone. The transformation problem survives its own archaeology, which is more than can be said for most nineteenth-century controversies in economics.

## The historical transformation: Engels's supplement

One offshoot of the main dispute deserves recording because it shows the problem reproducing itself in the dimension of history. In a supplement to the third volume, written to answer critics who asked when commodities had ever actually exchanged at their values, Engels proposed a historical reading: for long stretches of pre-capitalist production, he argued, producers who owned their means of production and exchanged their own products did trade at prices approximating labour values, since no pooled profit rate existed to bend prices away from them; prices of production then arrived with capital itself, as the specifically modern form. The transformation, on this reading, is not only a logical passage between two accountings but a historical passage between two epochs of exchange, and the first volume describes, among other things, the older world's law.

The supplement has had a contested career. Economic historians have doubted whether pre-capitalist exchange was ever as value-governed as the sketch requires, pointing to the roles of custom, coercion, guild restriction, and seigneurial extraction in setting historical prices. Logicians within the Marxian camp have doubted the supplement's coherence with the text it supplements, since the first volume's analysis is conducted throughout in terms of capital and wage labour, not of independent artisans. Defenders reply that the supplement needs only the modest claim that labour time regulated exchange more directly where producers faced one another without capitals between them, a claim about tendencies and not about a golden age. The debate matters here for a structural reason: it is the transformation problem wearing historical dress, the same question about how one regime of prices succeeds another, asked of centuries instead of chapters. Its inconclusiveness is also the same, and instructive in the same way. Whether the passage is logical or historical, the theory owes an account of the bridge, and at both bridges the toll collectors are waiting with the same price list.

## The mathematics that arrived too late

There is a historical irony at the centre of this subject that deserves a section of its own. The transformation problem is, in modern dress, a problem about a system of simultaneous linear relations, and the mathematics for handling such systems with full rigour, the theory of non-negative matrices and their dominant roots and vectors, was developed in the first decades of the twentieth century, a generation after the third volume appeared. The economist who first saw the problem whole, Bortkiewicz, worked with the tools of his moment and reached the structural answer anyway. His successors, Seton above all, restated the construction in the matrix language as it matured, and Wassily Leontief's input-output economics, which grew from the same matrix soil and acknowledged its classical ancestry, turned the simultaneous description of an economy into an empirical industry. A problem that had been an accusation of muddle became, in the process, a founding exhibit of a respectable method, and the transformation literature's later, highly formal phase is unintelligible without that background: the Marxians and their critics were, by the nineteen seventies, arguing inside a shared mathematical house that neither Marx nor his first critics could have entered.

The irony has a second storey. The simultaneous frame that the mature mathematics made natural is the very frame the temporal school accuses of smuggling in timelessness, and the accusation has force precisely because the frame's prestige discourages examining it. A method's success in a hundred other applications does not guarantee its innocence in the next one, and the history here is a clean case: the tool that finally let economists state Marx's problem exactly also fixed the terms, simultaneity above all, in which exactness would thereafter be judged. None of this rehabilitates Marx's draft, whose gap is visible without any mathematics at all. It does mean that the dispute's mature phase is partly a dispute about the jurisdiction of a mathematical method, and that is a dispute economics has with itself in several fields at once, far beyond the boundaries of Marxian theory. The transformation problem, pursued honestly, keeps leading its students out of the sectarian enclosure and into the general questions of their discipline, which may be the strongest reason for a student to pursue it at all.

## Existence, uniqueness, and the shape of a proof

Readers new to the formal literature are sometimes surprised that so much of it is given over to proving that solutions exist at all, and the surprise is worth dispelling, because it marks the distance between the transformation as a debating topic and the transformation as mathematics. A system of price relations for a whole economy is not guaranteed a solution merely because it can be written down. The prices must be positive, since a price of zero or less is not a price; the profit rate must be single, since the construction's point is a common rate; and the solution must be the only one, since an economy with several consistent price systems has not been given a theory of which one it inhabits. The mature simultaneous literature therefore leans on the mathematics of non-negative systems, whose central results guarantee, under conditions on the economy's structure that can be stated and checked, exactly one positive solution for prices and the rate together. Bortkiewicz worked before those results were available in their clean form and reached his conclusions by construction in three departments; Seton's generation could invoke them; the modern literature treats them as the floor beneath the argument.

The bearing on the dispute is double-edged, in the now familiar pattern. On one edge, the existence and uniqueness results are a genuine achievement of the repair tradition: the simultaneous economy is not a wish but a theorem, and critics who speak as if the whole construction were hand-waving are a generation out of date. On the other edge, the results clarify exactly what has been proved, and it is the consistency of the price system, not the derivability of that system from an independent value system. The values enter the modern proofs as a parallel computation, elegant and dispensable, which is the eraser objection wearing the robes of rigour. A proof's shape thus becomes an object of interpretation in its own right: the same theorem that certifies the repair also certifies the redundancy, and the schools divide, once again, over which certificate matters. There may be no better illustration in economics of the principle that mathematics settles the questions it is asked, and that the choice of questions is where theories live and die.

## Does the problem destroy the labour theory of value?

### Would settling the problem settle the theory's truth?

No, in either direction. A completed derivation would show the two volumes consistent, not the theory's account of capitalism correct, which depends on evidence about profit, wages, and accumulation. A failed derivation shows the account needs rebuilding, not that exploitation is absent from the economy it describes.

The destruction claim and the vindication claim are mirror images, and both commit the same error of treating an internal consistency question as a verdict on a worldly theory. Suppose the strongest critical result in full: the transformation, as Marx stated it, cannot be completed without surrendering one equality or redefining one term. What follows is that Marx's derivation fails, a fact about a text and its descendants. What does not follow is that profit is not unpaid labour, since that proposition can be, and by the monetary and temporal schools has been, stated and defended inside frameworks that never rely on the failed derivation. The exploitation claim and the derivation claim travel separately, and the fundamental theorem discussed earlier is the standing proof of their separability: inside the very simultaneous construction used to exhibit the derivation's failure, positive profit and positive surplus value are equivalent. A frame that refutes the transformation sustains the exploitation equivalence in the same breath, which is why careful critics, Samuelson among them, aimed their objection at redundancy rather than at the existence of the surplus.

The converse discipline binds the theory's defenders. Completing the transformation, in any of the ledger's senses, would not vindicate the labour theory of value as an account of actual economies. It would show that the theory's books can be kept consistently, and consistency is an entrance requirement for a scientific claim, not its confirmation. Confirmation runs through the empirical literature: the statistical relation between labour contents and market prices, the behaviour of profit rates, the measurement of exploitation in national accounts. That literature is substantial, contested, and treated on its own terms in [labour theory of value evidence](/2010/10/01/labour-theory-of-value-evidence/), where the studies and their critics are examined directly. The transformation problem's proper dignity is narrower than either slogan allows. It is the theory's most instructive internal difficulty, the place where its two levels of abstraction were supposed to meet and did not, cleanly, and the century spent at that junction has produced some of the most rigorous thinking in the whole Marxian tradition. A problem can earn that description without being either a tombstone or a trophy.

One more separation protects readers against over-reading the result in either direction. The labour theory of value, as a research programme rather than a single proposition, carries other load-bearing parts that the transformation never touches: the analysis of the commodity form and of money, the theory of the working day and of relative surplus value through machinery, the reproduction schemes, the accounts of accumulation, reserve armies, and crisis tendencies that fill the later volumes and the manuscripts. A verdict on the transformation, however severe, is a verdict on one derivation, and the programme's other parts stand where their own evidence puts them. Conversely, the programme's defenders gain nothing by treating the transformation's repairs as a general acquittal: a consistent price derivation does not validate the crisis theory, and a proven exploitation equivalence does not establish the falling rate of profit. The cluster of articles to which this one belongs exists because the parts must be judged severally, and the habit of several judgement, applied to friend and foe alike, is the closest thing this literature has to a professional ethic.

## The problem in the textbooks of its critics

Outside the specialist literature, the transformation problem leads a double life in the wider profession's memory, and a survey owes readers a glimpse of both lives. In one life, the problem is a cautionary tale told to students: a great system-builder erected a two-storey theory, the storeys failed to connect, and a century of ingenious repair work by loyalists demonstrates only the human capacity for rescuing a beloved doctrine from its own contradictions. Historians of economic thought of a sceptical temper have told the tale in roughly that form, sometimes adding that the episode's real lesson concerns the sociology of schools rather than the logic of prices. In the other life, the same episode figures as an early, imperfect chapter in the prehistory of general equilibrium and linear economics: Bortkiewicz's construction is a genuine technical achievement, Seton's generalisation a respectable theorem, and the whole sequence a case where a heterodox research programme inadvertently advanced the mainstream's own methods. Both lives are lived in reputable books, sometimes by the same author in different chapters.

The double life matters because most economists, and most readers of journalism about economics, meet the problem in one of these two condensed forms and carry the condensed verdict into any encounter with the living dispute. Meeting the living dispute is the corrective. The specialists who populate the ledger's rows do not behave as the cautionary tale predicts: they publish their concessions, itemise their surrenders, and attack one another's repairs with a ferocity that loyalist rescue operations rarely display. Nor do they behave as pure technicians in the prehistory tale, since their choices among anchors, definitions, and frames are visibly guided by judgements about what the theory is for. A reader who has worked through the families in this article is equipped to see the textbook condensations as what they are, summaries with a thesis, and to ask of any summary the question the ledger teaches: which rows has it silently deleted, and what do the deletions cost its verdict?

## How the choices propagate into the profit rate debates

The transformation is not the cluster's only famous controversy, and its solutions leak into the neighbouring one. The long argument over the tendency of the rate of profit to fall, treated in this series under its own heading, turns on measurements: whether the profit rate, computed over long stretches of capitalist history, displays the downward drift Marx's law predicts. Every such computation must value the capital stock and the surplus in some chosen accounting, and the choice among the ledger's rows is not innocent for the result. A simultaneous, replacement-cost accounting tends to produce one trajectory; a temporal, historical-cost accounting can produce another from the same underlying history, because a stock valued at what was paid for it shrinks relative to a stock valued at what replacing it would cost in an age of cheapening equipment. Participants in the profit rate debate have accused one another, with some justice on all sides, of building their conclusions into their valuation conventions.

The propagation runs in the other direction too. A theorist convinced on independent grounds that the profit rate falls will find the temporal family's bookkeeping congenial, since it was partly designed to keep Marx's law statable; a theorist convinced the law fails will prefer the simultaneous frame in which the famous counter-theorem, that viable technical change raises the general rate when the real wage is fixed, was proved. The transformation literature and the profit rate literature are thus not two disputes but one dispute with two fronts, sharing personnel, journals, and a stock of constructions, and a reader who masters the ledger's grading questions on the price front is equipped to grade the same schools on the profit front, where the identical pattern recurs: consistency available in several frames, each frame exacting its price, the argument proceeding by price list rather than by knockout. The cluster's architecture, which gives each controversy its own article, should not disguise the plumbing that connects them.

## What the empirical literature does with the problem

Empirical work cannot wait for the theorists to agree, because measuring anything in this tradition requires computing labour values for real industries, and every computation silently adopts one of the ledger's rows. The large literature that compares labour contents with market prices across national input-output tables works, in its dominant practice, with simultaneous computation: the technical coefficients of an economy are taken as given, labour values are extracted from them by solving the system, and the closeness of values to prices is then assessed statistically. Studies in the monetary tradition work instead from the national accounts directly, reading exploitation off money magnitudes through the measuring rod, and so adopt the New Interpretation's row whether or not they announce the choice. The temporal school's empirical work follows profit rates through time on its own bookkeeping. A reader of any such study is therefore entitled to ask the ledger's question of it: which row did you occupy, and does your conclusion survive translation into a neighbouring row? The better studies ask it of themselves.

The findings of that literature, and the long argument about what closeness between values and prices would even show, belong to the evidence article linked in the previous section and are not summarised here, in keeping with this series' practice of keeping each question in its own article. What belongs here is the narrower observation that the transformation problem is not, for working researchers, an antiquarian puzzle. It is the choice of instrument panel for every measurement the tradition publishes, and the price of consistency is paid in the methods sections of empirical papers by authors who may never cite the theoretical debate at all.

One consequence deserves emphasis, since it reverses the usual direction of condescension between theory and measurement. The theoretical schools sometimes write as if the empirical literature were an application awaiting their verdict, a downstream activity that will proceed once the foundations are settled. The record shows the opposite traffic as well. The measurers, forced to compute, have repeatedly exposed ambiguities the theorists had left asleep: how to treat fixed capital's surviving stock, how to handle the government's and the financial sector's claims on the surplus, how to draw the boundary of productive labour in an economy of services. Each measurement decision, taken under deadline by researchers who needed a number, amounts to a small ruling on questions the transformation literature debates in the abstract, and the rulings accumulate into a de facto standard practice that the theorists then inherit. Foundations in this field are partly built by their supposed tenants, and a reader tracing any particular controversy to its roots should expect to find, somewhere near the bottom, an empirical paper that had to decide something by Friday.

The correlations themselves deserve one careful paragraph, because they are the empirical literature's most quoted and least understood product. Market prices, values, and prices of production all sit close to one another in study after study, with measured deviations commonly small beside the variation between sectors. Partisans of each magnitude claim the closeness as vindication, and sceptics of the whole exercise reply that closeness is guaranteed in advance, since any two price-like measures built from the same wage and materials data will move together. The defenders' better answer is that the contest was never the correlation but the tilt: the systematic relation between deviation and capital intensity, which a spurious correlation would not produce in the predicted direction. On the tilt, the studies broadly favour the theory's construction, while continuing to argue about its size and its explanation, and the temporal school has added that the same closeness is what its own sequential bookkeeping predicts for a monetary economy, so the empirics cannot be conscripted to certify the simultaneous repairs. Evidence has genuinely accumulated here; verdicts have not accumulated with it, and the difference between those two statements is the empirical chapter's whole lesson.

## The problem in the age of the computer

One transformation in the transformation debate has nothing to do with values and prices: the cost of computation collapsed. Bortkiewicz solved three departments by hand and ingenuity. His successors solved dozens with matrices and patience. A modern researcher, working from a national input-output table with several hundred industries, extracts labour values and prices of production from the same coefficients in seconds, tests the sensitivity of every result to every convention, and repeats the exercise for forty countries and fifty years before lunch. The simultaneous system, whose solution was once a research achievement, is now a keystroke, and that change in the price of arithmetic has quietly altered what the dispute can be about. No participant any longer needs to wonder whether a consistent simultaneous accounting exists for a real economy's data; it does, trivially, whenever the data are coherent. The argument has been forced upward, from existence to meaning: not whether the two accountings can both be computed, but what their relation signifies and which conventions deserve allegiance.

Computation has also changed the empirical wing's character. Where an earlier generation computed values for a handful of industries from published tables and argued from the closeness of the fit, the current practice assembles panels across countries and decades, corrects for taxes, finance, and the state's take, and argues about the structure of the residuals: whether the deviations between values and prices carry the composition signature the theory predicts, whether they shrink or grow as economies mechanise, whether they behave as the temporal school expects across price revolutions. The transformation problem, which began as a question about a manuscript's consistency, now functions among other things as a specification choice inside an empirical research programme with real data and real falsification risks. That migration, from the study to the dataset, is the surest sign that the problem is not a relic. Relics are not argued about by people with regressions to run.

## Misreadings to correct

Five misreadings recur so reliably in accounts of this subject that correcting them is part of explaining it. **The problem was discovered by Marx's critics.** It was not. Marx constructed the setting, asserted the equalities, and noticed the input difficulty in his own chapter; Engels framed the problem publicly as a challenge and reviewed the early answers. Bortkiewicz formalised what the text had set up, and the formalisation is continuous with questions the theory's own custodians had been asking since 1885. Treating the problem as an external ambush flatters neither side: it understates the critics' debt to the text and the defenders' two decades of warning.

**The first volume claims that real prices equal values.** It does not, and the claim survives only in summaries written by the theory's opponents and, regrettably, by some of its popularisers. The first volume's exchange at values is an analytical device whose provisional character its author stated; the economies it describes are populated by capitals that would behave very differently if the device were a description. A refutation of the device as description leaves the device as analysis untouched, and must be re-aimed at the transformation, which is the sole place where the device is cashed out. Much popular controversy consists of the two sides firing at different targets in the belief that there is one.

**The problem concerns individual prices.** It concerns the relation between two aggregate systems, as the earlier sections established, and specifically whether two totals survive a conversion that every party agrees redistributes individual prices massively. An argument that some commodity's price deviates from its value is not evidence about the problem in either direction, and a defence that some aggregate roughly holds in some dataset is not a solution to it. Keeping the level of the claim straight, totals rather than items, dissolves a surprising fraction of the published disagreement, which turns out to be cross-talk between levels.

**Solving the problem would vindicate the labour theory of value.** It would vindicate the consistency of one derivation inside the theory, no small thing, and nothing more, since the theory's truth about the world depends on the evidence marshalled in the empirical literature and on the fate of its other parts, crisis, accumulation, the falling tendency of the profit rate, none of which the transformation touches. Symmetrically, **the problem is a matter of arithmetic.** It is a matter of what the theory's magnitudes mean: whether values are a prior system that prices express, a translation of monetary accounts into labour terms, or a historical record of advances actually made. Arithmetic is the courtroom; the dispute is about the law. Readers who keep that distinction in hand will find the literature's temperature easier to understand, since questions of meaning are exactly the questions on which competent specialists can disagree for a century without any of them making an error in calculation.

**Marx concealed the difficulty from his readers.** The record runs the other way. The difficulty's elements were published by the theory's own custodians with something approaching advertisement: Engels announced the problem as a public challenge years before the third volume appeared, reviewed the attempted solutions in its preface, and printed the procedure whose gap is the whole subject. Whatever the tradition's later dogmatisms, concealment is the one charge its founding documents refute on their face. The misreading persists because it flatters a picture of doctrine defending itself, but the transformation debate began as an open invitation, and some of its continuing vitality is the invitation's long echo.

## A dispute with addresses: where the argument lives

It may help a reader who wishes to follow the argument beyond this survey to know its institutional geography, described at the level of generality that an evergreen account allows. The dispute has never belonged to a single journal or school. Its nineteenth-century phase ran in the theoretical organs of German social democracy and in the academic journals of the German-speaking universities. Its formal phase after 1907 ran in statistical and economic periodicals across Europe. Its English phase after 1942 ran first in textbooks and the journals of the Marxist movement, then, as the universities absorbed the subject, in the heterodox journals founded in the nineteen sixties and seventies to house political economy outside the mainstream's gates, and in occasional raids upon the mainstream's own flagship reviews, of which the eraser paper remains the most successful. The temporal and monetary schools of the last four decades have maintained their own conferences, collections, and journal special issues, and the manuscript scholarship has its own philological venues attached to the critical edition.

Two features of that geography explain the dispute's unusual longevity. The first is that no institution owns it: there is no academy whose ruling could end it, no examination board whose syllabus could retire it, and each generation of entrants meets the problem in the form its teachers' generation left unresolved. The second is that the dispute is genuinely exportable: its core construction, a small simultaneous economy with a conservation question, can be taught in a week to any competent economics graduate, who then possesses all the machinery needed to form a position and, frequently, does. Few controversies in economics reproduce themselves so efficiently in new minds, because few present so clean a ratio of depth to prerequisites. The transformation problem is, among its other identities, a nearly perfect teaching instrument for the philosophy of economic theory, and institutions keep it alive for that reason even where its original stakes have faded from view.

The geography has one more feature that a reader should carry into any encounter with the literature's polemics: the participants read one another. Unlike many long disputes, in which camps cease to cite across the divide and the argument degenerates into parallel monologues, the transformation debate's schools have remained in genuine, if often exasperated, conversation, reviewing one another's books, answering one another's examples, and borrowing one another's constructions. The temporal school's sharpest critics include economists formed in the same heterodox milieu; the monetary school's founders answered the simultaneists in detail before departing from them. That mutual literacy is the literature's saving grace and the reason a survey can present its positions in their own terms without caricature: the terms exist, well formed, in the record, and the strongest version of each case has usually been stated by its own side and then improved by the other side's objections. A dispute so conducted is an asset to its discipline whatever its verdicts, and it deserves readers willing to be as literate as its participants.

## The honest position

### What would count as solving the problem outright?

A solution outright would derive prices of production from value magnitudes while preserving both aggregate equalities as results, not as assumptions or definitions, and without redefining any term of the original theory. Nothing in the literature meets that standard, which is why the honest verdict remains open.

The position this article commends to its readers is the one the ledger enforces. Marx's procedure is inconsistent, at a located point, for a statable reason. The inconsistency is not fatal to the theory built around it, because the theory's central claim about the origin of profit can be and has been restated within several consistent frameworks, one of which proves the profit-exploitation equivalence inside the critics' own preferred construction. The inconsistency is not dissolved either, because every restatement pays, and the payments are neither hidden nor trivial: an equality, a definition, a frame, an architecture, a claim about determination. The dispute that remains is a dispute among competent economists about which payment purchases the most truth, conducted with shared mathematics and divergent philosophical weights, and it shows every sign of continuing in that form. Positions have been described throughout rather than a league table, deliberately, since the literature's own state is one of structured disagreement, and an article that awarded a winner would be reporting a state of play that does not exist.

For the general reader, the transformation problem repays study out of proportion to its technical surface, because it is the cleanest available specimen of how a great theory meets its own standards. The standards here are Marx's: conservation, derivation, the demand that one level of analysis be recoverable from another. The theory fails one examination it set itself, passes others its critics set, and survives as a family of descendants each marked by the repair that defines it. There are worse fates for a nineteenth-century system of political economy, and there are few better introductions to the discipline of reading one.

A last word about why this article has refused, at several points where a verdict was available cheaply, to deliver one. The refusal is not indecision; it is the finding. A dispute in which every consistent position costs something identifiable, in which the costs are of different kinds and are weighted differently by reasonable judges, and in which the founder's text is a draft mediated by an editor, is a dispute whose truthful summary is a price list and not a podium. Readers who came wanting to know whether Marx was refuted can now give the accurate answer: his derivation was, and his theory was thereby set a problem it has spent a century answering at itemised prices. Readers who came wanting to know whether the problem was solved can give the matching answer: the derivation was not, and the theory was thereby given a research programme whose entries are still being written. Both answers are more interesting than the slogans they replace, and both fit on the ledger's single page, which is where this article began and where, in the end, it is content to leave the matter.

## What the problem teaches about reading this theory

The transformation problem has one final use, as a training ground in how to read the theory it troubles, and a survey that omitted the lesson would waste the reader's journey. The mature Marx works by levels of abstraction: a mechanism is first isolated in its pure form, then followed through successive layers of complication, each layer modifying the form the mechanism's results take. Value in the first volume, prices of production in the third, market prices in the fluctuations of trade: the sequence is a descent from the abstract toward the concrete, and the theory's defenders have always insisted that judging any level by the standards of another is the beginner's characteristic error. The transformation is the hinge between the first two levels, the place where the descent must be shown to be continuous. Its failure is therefore not one local error among others; it is a stress fracture at the method's central joint, which is why the argument about it has always been, at bottom, an argument about whether the method itself is sound. A reader who understands that understands why competent people have spent careers on a question of bookkeeping: the bookkeeping is where the philosophy of the whole enterprise becomes testable.

The fracture also teaches the complementary lesson, about the method's resilience. A descent through levels can survive a defective hinge if the levels retain independent support: the first volume's account of the working day rests on evidence and argument that owe nothing to the transformation, and the third volume's account of competition rests on observations any economist can verify. The building metaphor the tradition favours, of foundations and storeys, misleads here; the structure is more like a bridge held by several cables, of which the transformation is one, conspicuous and frayed. Cutting it, as the eraser proposes, changes the bridge's engineering and the loads its other cables must bear, which the remaining sections of any honest survey must then redistribute. This article has performed that redistribution as it went, assigning the exploitation claim to the production ledger, the price pattern to competition, and the conservation claims to the several schools that keep them at their several prices. A theory so redistributed is not the theory as its founder drafted it. It is the theory as a century of exact criticism has rebuilt it, and the rebuilding, cable by cable and price by price, is the transformation problem's final meaning.

## A reader's itinerary through the primary texts

A reader who wants to verify this article's account against the sources can do so in a defined order, and the order matters because each text presupposes the previous one's terms. Begin with the first volume's opening chapter, where the commodity's two factors and the value form are set out, and its chapter on the buying and selling of labour power, where the wage's secret is stated; those two stations supply the whole vocabulary of the value side. Then take the third volume in its own sequence: the chapter on the differing compositions of capitals and the turnover's effects, which assembles the asymmetry; the ninth chapter, which performs the transformation and asserts the equalities, reading it slowly and marking the passage where the cost price's possible deviation is acknowledged; and the tenth chapter, on the equalisation of the profit rate and the formation of market values, which shows the mechanism the transformation presupposes. Engels's two prefaces, to the second volume for the challenge and to the third for the early answers and the editorial apologia, frame the reading with the problem's intended publicity.

From the primary texts the itinerary forks by appetite. For the formalisation, Bortkiewicz is most approachable through the exposition that carried him into English, the appendix in Sweezy's 1942 treatise, after which Seton's generalisation and the relevant chapters of Morishima's restatement give the construction its modern form. For the objection, Samuelson's 1971 paper in the survey journal of the American profession is short, self-contained, and best read whole rather than in quotation, since its rhetoric is part of its action on the reader. For the repairs, the founding statements of the monetary interpretation in the early nineteen eighties and the temporal school's programmatic collections of the nineteen nineties state the positions in their authors' own terms, and the series article devoted to those two families provides a guided comparison. A reader who completes even the first half of this itinerary will know the problem more securely than most of the people who pronounce on it in print, which is perhaps the most useful sentence in this article.

The critics repay reading in pairs. Böhm-Bawerk's assault of 1896 and Hilferding's response of 1904, read together, teach the debate's two native languages at their sources: the language of formal consistency, in which a contradiction between volumes is the gravest charge economics knows, and the language of social form, in which the same pages describe a relation unfolding rather than a sum failing. A reader who can hear both languages, and can say at any moment which one a given writer is speaking, possesses the master key to the whole literature, since nearly every subsequent contribution is a development of one or a negotiation between the two. The itinerary therefore ends where the public debate began, with the suggestion that the controversy be read, at least once, in the order its participants lived it: the promise, the assault, the relocation, the formalisation, the textbook, the eraser, and the repairs. Chronology is not the ledger's order, and the ledger's order is the better analytical instrument, but chronology is the order in which the arguments acquired their emotional charges, and no reader fully understands a price until its history has been felt as well as tabulated.

## For students and exam candidates

Fix three things in memory and most examination questions on this topic become manageable. First, the defect in one sentence: Marx converted outputs into prices of production and left the inputs at their values, although the inputs are other industries' outputs and should have been converted as well. Second, the two aggregate equalities as separate claims, since questions reward candidates who can say which equality a given solution saves and which it drops. Third, the grading habit: for any named solution, state what it changed, what it preserved, and what it cost, rather than declaring it right or wrong. Candidates who want model treatments of these questions under examination conditions will find them in [marxist economics exam answers](/2016/08/15/marxist-economics-exam-answers/). ## For teachers and seminar leaders

The two-sector illustration inside the solutions ledger was built for the classroom. Set it before any theory: two industries, equal capitals, unequal workforces, and let the group compute values, watch both aggregate equalities hold in the totals, and then ask the question that detonates the construction, namely who sold the sectors their inputs and at what prices those inputs should have been entered. Students who find the discrepancy themselves retain the problem permanently, and the subsequent literature arranges itself as a series of answers to their own discovery. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the transformation problem?

It is the difficulty of passing from the labour values of the first volume of Capital to the prices of production of the third without contradiction. Competition drives profit rates toward equality between industries, yet industries employ very unequal amounts of labour for each unit of capital advanced, so prices must depart from values according to each industry's makeup. Marx sketched a conversion and claimed that two totals crossed it intact, aggregate price matching aggregate value and aggregate profit matching aggregate surplus value. His sketch converted what industries sell and not what they buy, and the century of repair and objection that grew from that omission is the problem's literature.

### Q: Why do values and prices diverge?

They diverge because profit is shared by capital's size rather than by labour's quantity. Only living labour produces surplus value within the theory, so a trade employing many workers per unit of capital generates more surplus than a trade employing few. Were goods sold at their values, the labour-heavy trade would earn a far higher return, and money would rush in until its prices fell. The settling point is a set of prices that hands every capital the same rate of return on its advance, which means capital-heavy industries sell above value and labour-heavy industries below, the departures offsetting across the whole economy.

### Q: What was Marx's own solution to the transformation?

His solution, in the ninth chapter of the third volume, works from the economy's totals. Add up all surplus value and all capital advanced, and their ratio gives a general rate of profit common to every industry. Then build each commodity's new price from its cost price plus profit at that common rate on the capital behind it. Marx presented the outcome as a redistribution: the prices so formed were said to sum to the same total as the values they replaced, and the profits to sum to the same total as the surplus values, so nothing was gained or lost overall, only reapportioned among industries.

### Q: What is wrong with Marx's own solution?

The conversion is applied to one side of the accounts only. What each industry sells is repriced, but the materials, equipment services, and wage goods it buys are still entered at their labour values, even though those purchases are the products of other industries whose selling prices the conversion has just altered. An input bought from a capital-heavy industry is understated or overstated accordingly, and once the inputs are repriced honestly the whole calculation moves, including the general rate of profit on which every price depends. The procedure therefore assumes in its starting figures part of what it claims to derive.

### Q: Who first formalised the transformation problem?

Ladislaus von Bortkiewicz, a Berlin statistician and economist, in a study published in 1907. He rebuilt Marx's scheme as a set of conditions that had to hold simultaneously, dividing production into three departments: means of production, workers' consumption goods, and capitalists' consumption goods including the money commodity. Requiring every department's inputs to be valued at the prices the scheme assigns to outputs, he showed that the two aggregate equalities Marx asserted cannot both hold in general. One could be imposed as a fixing condition; the other would then fail, apart from special cases he identified.

### Q: What is the eraser argument?

The eraser argument is Paul Samuelson's 1971 charge that the value scheme performs no work in reaching the price scheme. Given an economy's techniques and the real wage, prices of production and the rate of profit can be calculated directly, completely, and consistently. The labour values are computable from the same data but enter the calculation of prices nowhere. Samuelson pictured the theorist drafting the value accounts, rubbing them out with an eraser, and drafting the price accounts in the cleared space. A route that can be deleted without changing the destination, the argument concludes, explains nothing about the destination.

### Q: Are both aggregate equalities preserved?

In Marx's own worked illustration they are, because the illustration was arranged so that they would be, with inputs held at values throughout. In the rigorous simultaneous rebuilding they are not, in general: the system settles relative prices and the profit rate but leaves one overall scale unfixed, so a single equality can be imposed as the anchor and the other then holds only by coincidence of industrial structure. Two later families restore both, one by defining the terms monetarily so the equalities become identities for the net product, another by valuing each period's inputs at the prices of their purchase. Each restoration changes the equalities' meaning.

### Q: Has the transformation problem been solved?

Not in the form Marx left it, and repeatedly in forms he did not. No published derivation obtains prices of production from value magnitudes while keeping both aggregate equalities as theorems and leaving every definition untouched; that is the strict standard, and the literature's own specialists largely concede it is unmet. Weaker standards are met several times over: consistent systems exist in which one equality is a theorem, or both are identities, or both survive a sequential bookkeeping. Whether those count as solutions depends on what one thinks the problem was asking, which is why the dispute persists as an argument about costs rather than closing as a verdict.

### Q: Does the problem destroy the labour theory of value?

No, because the problem and the theory answer different questions. The problem asks whether one derivation inside Capital is consistent, and it is not. The theory's core proposition, that profit rests on labour performed beyond what wages repay, can be formulated without that derivation and has been, several times. Strikingly, within the simultaneous framework used to expose the derivation's failure, a formal result connects the two magnitudes directly: an economy shows positive profit exactly when its workers perform surplus labour. The derivation's collapse damages a chapter and a claim about determination; it leaves the exploitation proposition standing on other legs, to be judged by evidence.

### Q: What are prices of production?

A price of production is the price a commodity must fetch if its producer is to recover costs and earn profit at the economy-wide average rate on the capital advanced. It has two components: the cost price, which replaces the means of production consumed and the wages paid, and an addition of average profit proportional to the whole capital, large or small in labour terms. Prices of production are the third volume's successor to values as the regulators of market exchange under developed competition, and they depart from values systematically, standing higher where plant and materials dominate an industry's capital and lower where wages dominate.

### Q: How does the price of consistency grade a proposed solution?

It grades by exchange rather than by applause. For each proposal it asks four things: what the proposal altered in the procedure or the definitions it inherited; which of the two aggregate equalities remain standing inside it, and whether they stand as derived results or as definitional fixtures; what the proposal gave up that the original text appeared to claim; and which criticism its opponents press hardest. A proposal scores well when its preservations are results and its surrenders are peripheral, badly when its preservations are fixtures and its surrenders reach the theory's core claims. The grading cannot name a winner, since readers weight surrenders differently, but it prevents any proposal from posing as free.

### Q: What should a student separate between the two aggregate equalities?

The student should separate their jobs. The equality between total price and total value concerns the scale of the whole system, whether the price accounts and the value accounts measure the same economy at the same overall size. The equality between total profit and total surplus value concerns the surplus specifically, whether competition merely moves a fixed fund of unpaid labour between industries or also changes its size. The two can succeed or fail independently, different solutions rescue different ones, and most confused writing on the subject comes from treating them as a single claim that stands or falls as one.

### Q: Why did the problem stay invisible for so long after publication?

Several veils overlapped. The third volume reached print in 1894 as a posthumous construction, edited from drafts, and its ninth chapter was received as a settled derivation rather than read as a provisional procedure with a known gap. The mathematical habit needed to expose the gap, handling all prices as determined together rather than in sequence, was still becoming standard in economics. Loyalty discouraged the theory's friends from probing a canonical text, while its declared opponents considered the matter already settled against it and looked elsewhere. English readers, finally, lacked any full presentation until an exposition of 1942 carried the rigorous statement across the language barrier.

### Q: How does the solutions ledger order a century of replies?

The ledger orders replies by their costs instead of their dates. Each proposed solution occupies a row, and the columns record what the proposal changes, which aggregate equalities survive within it, what it surrenders, the strongest criticism aimed at it, and whether empirical researchers actually use it. Reading along a row gives one repair whole; reading down a column gives the dispute's anatomy, since the surrenders column shows that no repair is free and the preservation column shows that no repair is empty. Chronology would flatter the newest entry; the ledger's arrangement flatters none, which is its use as a reference instrument.

### Q: What separates a reconstruction of Marx from a correction of him?

A reconstruction completes the author's procedure using resources the text itself supplies, and reaches the destination the text promised. A correction reaches consistency by abandoning or redefining something the text asserted. The boundary case is a completion whose finished calculation alters the text's own results: once repricing the inputs shifts the general rate of profit, the finished theory no longer merely spells out the draft but supersedes it, however faithful the intention. Honest scholarship labels the two operations differently, since calling every successful correction a reconstruction hides the price that the price of consistency exists to display.
