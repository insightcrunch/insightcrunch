Advertising is the most visible persuasive machinery of a commercial society, and for that reason it attracts the loosest thinking in the whole Marxist repertoire. A poster promises that a perfume will confer desirability, a soft drink will deliver friendship, and a car will certify that its driver has escaped the ordinary. The critic looks at the poster, feels the dishonesty in his fingertips, and reaches for the heaviest words available: manipulation, brainwashing, false consciousness, ideology. The words arrive as a bundle, and inside the bundle three quite different arguments are travelling together without being distinguished. One argument concerns the structure of commodity production itself and would hold in a world where no copywriter had ever lived. A second concerns a deliberate industry, its crafts and its costs, and can be examined in the texts that industry produces. A third concerns the wants of consumers and asserts that some of those wants are false, an assertion that turns out to need a criterion nobody has supplied. This article separates the three, because the separation is where the strength of the critique actually lies. Bundled together, the arguments inherit the weakness of the feeblest member and can be dismissed wholesale by anyone who notices that shoppers are not hypnotised. Separated, two of the three are formidably hard to answer, and the third can be marked honestly as an open wound rather than paraded as a triumph.

![Advertising, consumption and ideology, manufactured needs, branding and the sale of a way of life - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-41.webp)

The question a Marxist analysis of advertising is supposed to answer is often misstated, so it deserves a plain statement at the outset. The question is not whether advertisements lie, since many do and many do not, and lying is a matter for trading standards rather than for social theory. Nor is the question whether people enjoy goods, since they plainly do and the enjoyment is frequently genuine. The serious question is what kind of work the selling apparatus performs inside a capitalist economy: what it does to the meanings attached to objects, what share of social resources it absorbs, what it presupposes about the wants it addresses, and whether the picture of human satisfaction it circulates amounts to an ideology in the precise sense this series has given that term, a representation that misdescribes a real relation in a way that serves the relation's continuation. A reader who wants the general concept before the application will find it in the [Marxist theory of ideology guide](/2012/02/01/marxist-theory-of-ideology-guide/), which sets out the senses of ideology the tradition has used and the tests each sense must pass. Advertising is the application where those tests meet the shopping basket, the broadcast break and the sponsored post, and it is a harder application than the polemical literature admits, precisely because the audience keeps answering back.

## The phenomenon on its own terms: what advertising is

Begin with description, since the application template of this series insists that the phenomenon be rendered accurately before any category is brought to bear on it. Advertising is paid, mediated communication whose declared purpose is to favour one seller, product, brand or cause over its rivals in the minds of an audience. It is an industry with its own firms, crafts and internal divisions: research into audiences, strategy, copywriting, art direction, media buying, and the management of client accounts. Its products are messages, but its commodity is attention organised for sale, and its clients purchase neither paper nor airtime in the last analysis but access to minds at moments when those minds might be turned toward a purchase. The industry is old in recognisable form. Nineteenth century newspapers carried patent medicine notices of extravagant promise; the department store and the branded packaged good made the maker's name itself an object of promotion; the twentieth century added the agency system, the broadcast spot, the sponsorship and the jingle; the twenty first has added targeting, influencer endorsement and the auctioning of impressions in fractions of a second. Through every technical change, the economic function has remained stable enough to define: advertising exists because sellers compete for buyers who cannot inspect most of what distinguishes one offering from another before purchase, and because the scale of production requires demand to be gathered, steadied and expanded at a matching scale.

Two features of the phenomenon matter for everything that follows, and both are regularly missed by its critics. The first is that most advertising is competitive rather than expansionary in its immediate aim. The ordinary advertisement does not attempt to persuade the audience that soap, beer or insurance is desirable in general; it attempts to move an existing intention from a rival brand to the client's brand. The battlefield is market share within a category, and the category itself is usually presupposed rather than created by the message. This matters because a critique that pictures advertising as a machine for manufacturing desire from nothing has aimed at the exceptional case and generalised it. New categories do get created, and wants do get cultivated where none existed, but the daily bread of the industry is the redirection of wants already in motion. The second feature is that advertising is expensive, and its expense is itself a datum. Firms do not sustain large selling budgets out of vanity across generations. The expenditure persists because, at the margin and against rivals doing the same, it works well enough to defend, which is a weaker and more interesting claim than the claim that it works absolutely. An arms race in which each side's spending largely cancels the other's is still a race each side must run, and the social question of what the race costs and what it produces survives every doubt about the power of any single advertisement.

Consumption, the other pole of the article's title, also needs description before judgement. To consume is to use up goods and services in the course of living, and under developed capitalism the overwhelming share of consumption is mediated by purchase: food, shelter, clothing, transport, entertainment and care arrive as commodities. That mediation is the deep fact. It means that the satisfaction of need passes through the market, that the objects satisfying need carry prices and brand identities alongside their uses, and that a person's material life is legible to others as a pattern of purchases. From these premises a great deal follows without any conspiracy. Objects will accumulate meanings beyond their functions, because they are displayed, compared, gifted and envied. Producers will compete over those meanings as well as over function and price. A specialised industry will arise to manage the competition of meanings. None of this requires a single advertiser to intend an ideology, and none of it is undone by showing that a particular campaign failed. The structural argument, when it comes, will rest on exactly this impersonality, and it is stronger for resting there.

## Three critiques wearing one coat

Public argument about advertising rarely separates its strands, and the failure is not innocent, because each strand has a different burden of proof and a different exposure to evidence. Consider the sentences a hostile critic might utter in one breath. Capitalism makes things appear magical, so that a trainer carries the aura of athletic triumph although it was stitched by strangers whose labour is invisible in the shop. Advertisers deliberately exploit that magic, writing the spells. And the wants the spells awaken are not even real wants, since nobody needs a third pair of trainers. The first sentence belongs to the theory of commodity fetishism and is about the form of the product itself. The second belongs to the study of a profession and its texts. The third belongs to moral psychology and requires a tribunal for needs. A defender of advertising who refutes the third sentence, by pointing out that the critic also owns shoes he enjoys, has refuted nothing in the first two, yet the bundled form lets the refutation feel total. The same bundling damages the critic's own side, because the first sentence, which is the most defensible, gets tarred by association with the third, which is the least.

The method of this article is therefore separation followed by separate judgement. The structural critique will be stated first, in the form Marx gave it, and its independence from advertising will be insisted on: fetishism is a property of commodity production, not an effect of copywriting. The semiotic critique comes next, through Raymond Williams and Judith Williamson, and it is about how advertisements mean, a question that can be pursued with the advertisement on the desk and no speculation about souls. The macroeconomic critique follows, from Paul Baran and Paul Sweezy, and it is about where advertising sits in the accounts of a whole economy, a question about surplus and absorption that would remain standing even if every individual advertisement were ineffectual. The psychological critique, Herbert Marcuse's doctrine of false needs, comes last and is handled as the tradition's exposed flank: powerful as rhetoric, incomplete as argument, because of what this article names the needs criterion gap. Only after the four have been separated will the defence of advertising be allowed its full hearing, and the verdict at the end will be a set of distinct verdicts rather than a cheer or a boo. Readers who want the general procedure for taking an ideological formation apart in this way can consult the account of the [ideology critique method](/2012/03/15/ideology-critique-method/), of which the present separation is a worked instance.

## Commodity fetishism: the structural critique

Marx's doctrine of commodity fetishism, set out in the opening chapter of the first volume of Capital, holds that in a society of generalised commodity production the social relations between producers assume the appearance of relations between things. A coat and a quantity of linen exchange in a definite proportion, and that proportion presents itself as a property of the objects, as natural as their weight or colour, although it is in fact a crystallisation of the labour and social organisation behind them. The labour of the tailor and the weaver, the division of work between them, the dependence of each on the other's product, all of this disappears into the price tag, and what remains visible is a world of objects with values that seem to inhere in them. Marx reached for the vocabulary of religion deliberately: the commodity is a mysterious thing, he argued, because the social character of the labour that made it appears stamped upon it as an objective character of the thing itself. The mystery is not a trick played by sellers. It arises from the form of production, in which private labours are socially validated only through exchange, so that the relation between producers can only ever show itself in the relation between their products. The full exposition belongs to the series article on [commodity fetishism explained](/2010/08/01/commodity-fetishism-explained/), and the present article needs only the consequence: fetishism has no author, no office and no budget, and it predates the advertising industry by the whole history of the market.

### Is advertising the same as commodity fetishism?

No. Commodity fetishism is a structural appearance produced by commodity production itself, with no author and no intention. Advertising is a deliberate industry that attaches meanings to goods. Advertising can exploit fetishism, but it does not cause it and would not abolish it if it ceased.

The distinction carries a test that any reader can apply. Imagine a society of generalised commodity production in which advertising had been prohibited entirely, every hoarding blank and every broadcast free of spots. Would the commodity have ceased to be fetishised? On Marx's account, plainly not. Prices would still present social relations as properties of things; the worker would still confront the product of a world of strangers as an alien power with a number attached; money would still appear to breed money in interest bearing capital, the fetish form Marx considered the most complete. The mystery of the commodity arises in exchange, not in promotion. Now run the test in the other direction. Imagine production organised without generalised exchange, in a household or a planned commune, but with persuasive posters urging members to admire the communal loaf. Whatever those posters were doing, flattery, morale management, incitement, they would not be advertising in the capitalist sense, because there is no competition between sellers for buyers, and the loaf's standing would not be a market value wearing a human meaning as disguise. The two phenomena cross without coinciding. Advertising operates on ground that fetishism has already prepared, ground where objects habitually carry social meanings in condensed and mystified form, and the copywriter's art consists in aiming that habit at a brand.

This is why the structural critique is the strongest of the four and also the most modest in what it claims about advertising itself. It claims that the world advertising works in is one where meaning attaches to things in ways that systematically hide the labour and relations that produced them. When an advertisement associates a watch with inheritance and paternal love, it is drawing on a reservoir it did not fill: the standing readiness of commodities to stand for relations between people. The advertisement's specific association is invented, tested and bought; the readiness is structural and free of charge. Critics who collapse the distinction end up blaming copywriters for the metaphysics of the market, which flatters the industry with a power it does not possess and lets the structure escape while its decorators are scolded. Critics who keep the distinction gain a harder target. They can say that abolishing advertising, however desirable on other grounds, would leave the fetishism of the commodity untouched, because that fetishism is reborn in every act of exchange; and they can ask, separately and empirically, what the decorating industry adds to a mystery it inherited. The structural critique also disciplines the critic's rhetoric. If the appearance is generated by the form itself, then consumers who perceive it are not fools or dupes; they are perceiving something real about the surface of their economy, a surface that genuinely presents relations as things. The error, if error it is, is built into the object before any mind errs about it, and that is a far more interesting charge than the charge of mass gullibility.

## Raymond Williams and the magic system

Raymond Williams, the Welsh critic who did more than anyone to found the serious study of advertising in Britain, published his essay on the subject in 1960 and gave the tradition its most quoted characterisation. Advertising, he argued, is a magic system. The comparison was precise rather than decorative. In societies described by anthropologists, magic is the practice of attempting to secure real goods, safety, fertility, success, by ritual means that cannot causally deliver them, and the practice persists because the goods are genuinely needed and the practical means of securing them are blocked or insufficient. Williams proposed that the modern advertisement has the same shape. It takes a material object whose use is limited and specifiable, a soap that washes, a drink that refreshes, a saloon car that carries four people at legal speeds, and it surrounds that object with promises pitched at needs the object cannot meet: to be loved, to be envied, to be youthful, to belong, to be at home in one's own life. The gap between the promise and the product is not a defect of particular campaigns. It is the system's working principle, because the needs invoked are real and chronic, while the goods on offer are particular and exhaustible, so the magic can be sold again with every purchase and is never finally dispelled by disappointment.

### What did Raymond Williams mean by calling advertising a magic system?

Williams meant that advertising performs a kind of organised magic: it promises that a soap, a drink or a car will deliver love, status, youth or belonging, satisfactions the object itself cannot supply. The promise works only because real human needs for those goods remain unmet by other means.

The essay's second movement is the one that lifts it above denunciation, and it is the movement most often dropped when the argument is summarised. Williams turned the customary sneer at consumer society on its head. The usual complaint, he observed, is that modern people are too materialistic, too devoted to objects, too eager for the next acquisition. His answer was that the opposite diagnosis fits better: a society organised by advertising is not materialist enough. A genuinely materialist culture would attend to objects in their material reality, valuing a chair for how it bears weight and wears, a meal for its nourishment and flavour, workmanship for its fitness to purpose. The magic system does the reverse. It teaches its audience to slight the object as an object, to treat it as a token in a drama of meanings, valuable for the feeling it signals rather than the use it performs. The trainer is not bought as footwear but as a condensed biography of striving; the whisky is not bought as a drink but as a certificate of arrival. On this reading the offence of advertising is not that it makes people love things too much but that it makes them attend to things too little, replacing the honest satisfactions of use with a shimmering economy of signs in which nothing is ever quite possessed because the meaning, unlike the object, evaporates on acquisition and must be bought again.

Williams also refused the easiest version of the manipulation thesis, and his refusal matters for the neutrality this article owes its subject. He did not describe a passive mass injected with desires by cunning persuaders. The magic works, in his account, because it speaks to genuine frustrations generated elsewhere in the social order: work that offers no recognition, communities thinned by mobility and privatised leisure, a public world in which worth is continually assessed and seldom confirmed. Advertising exploits those frustrations; it did not create them, and a criticism that stopped at the advertisement would be aiming at the symptom's salesman rather than at the arrangement that keeps the symptom supplied. His proposed remedy followed from the diagnosis. Since the magic feeds on the gap between human needs and the socially permitted means of meeting them, the answer lies in closing the gap by other means, in forms of provision and association that meet needs for recognition, security and fellowship directly, and not in sermons against shoppers. The essay thus models what the separation of critiques achieves. Williams's subject is the deliberate industry and its texts, yet his explanation keeps reaching back to structure, and at no point does he need to declare the audience's wants false in order to explain why the spells are cast or why they find hearers.

The Frankfurt School had reached a neighbouring position by another road, and the comparison sharpens both. Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer, writing in exile in the 1940s, folded advertising into their account of the culture industry, in which film, radio, magazines and promotion formed a single apparatus standardising consciousness along with entertainment. Where Williams saw magic answering real frustration, the Frankfurt analysis stressed pseudo individuality, the careful differentiation of products that are essentially alike, so that the consumer's sense of choosing freely is preserved while the range of choice is quietly narrowed to variations on the standard. The culture industry argument is treated in its own right in the series article on the [culture industry explained](/2015/07/15/culture-industry-explained/), and its heirs and critics are weighed there. For the present separation, the point is taxonomic. The Frankfurt strand is a semiotic and institutional critique: it examines what the messages say and how the apparatus is organised. It is not, in its careful versions, a claim that audiences believe what they are told, and Adorno's own writings are full of the suspicion that the audience sees through the performance and participates anyway, which is a subtler and more troubling thought than hypnosis.

## Reading the advertisement: the semiotic critique

If the magic system names what advertising promises, semiotics names how the promise is made, and the method's classic statement is Judith Williamson's Decoding Advertisements, published in 1978. Williamson's procedure begins with a refusal to ask whether an advertisement is true or false, since its characteristic operations happen below the level of assertion. An advertisement seldom states that its perfume is the scent of liberation or its cigarette the companion of independence. Instead it places the product beside something that already carries the desired meaning, a landscape, a face, a fragment of film still, a style of dress, and invites the viewer to perform the transfer. The viewer's own knowledge completes the circuit: she knows what the landscape connotes, she supplies the connection the layout merely juxtaposes, and the product emerges wearing meanings it never had to claim in words. Williamson called the borrowed domain the referent system, and her central finding was that the advertisement creates the appearance of a natural bond between product and meaning by concealing the labour of connection, much of which the audience performs itself, unpaid and unthanked, inside its own head.

### How does a semiotic reading of an advertisement actually proceed?

A semiotic reading slows the advertisement down and asks how its parts transfer meaning. An image of one thing, a landscape, a face, a style of life, is placed beside the product so that qualities associated with the image migrate to the product, which then appears to contain them naturally.

Worked slowly on a single example, the method shows its power, and an example from the documented history of the industry serves better than any invented case. Consider the celebrated mid century campaigns for Marlboro, among the most studied in the literature of the trade. The cigarette had been sold earlier in the century with appeals to mildness and elegance, at points addressed to women. The agency's celebrated repositioning of the 1950s attached the brand instead to the figure of the cowboy, photographed against open range country, weathered, silent, self sufficient. No sentence in those advertisements asserted that smoking the brand made a man autonomous or hardy. The transfer did the work: everything the audience already felt about the frontier figure, independence from bosses and offices, competence in a hard landscape, masculinity without display, migrated along the juxtaposition until the packet on the newsagent's counter seemed to hold a ration of the range. Williamson's method reads such a page in stages. It inventories the elements and their connotations, identifies the referent system being borrowed, traces the exact visual and verbal devices, cropping, gaze, typography, the positioning of the product as the resolution of the scene, that conduct the transfer, and then asks what had to be excluded for the transfer to succeed: the labour of tobacco farming and factory work, the chemistry of addiction, the medical evidence accumulating in those same decades, the office worker who was the actual buyer, seated nothing like a horseman. The exclusions are not incidental. An advertisement is a machine for showing one face of a commodity and is structurally unable to show the others, since its space is bought to favour, and the semiotic critique at its best is an audit of that one sidedness performed with the artefact open on the desk.

Two consequences follow that keep the semiotic critique honest. The first is that it convicts the text, not the reader. A decoding demonstrates what an advertisement does, the meanings it mobilises and the absences it requires; it does not demonstrate what any audience made of it, and Williamson's tradition, at its most careful, resists sliding from the analysis of messages to verdicts about minds. The second consequence is that the method is available to anyone, including the industry's own practitioners, who practise a version of it professionally when they test whether an intended association has landed. Semiotics is therefore the least mysterious of the four critiques and the easiest to teach, which is why the teacher persona layer later in this article builds its classroom exercise on it. Its limitation is the mirror of its strength. Confined to the text, it can show that meanings are manufactured; it cannot by itself show that the manufacturing matters, how much, or to whom, and for those questions the analysis must leave the page and enter the economy and the evidence, which is where the remaining critiques operate.

## The sales effort: advertising in the accounts of the whole economy

The third critique changes the scale of the question entirely. Paul Baran and Paul Sweezy, in Monopoly Capital, published in 1966, were not primarily interested in what advertisements say or in what audiences believe. Their problem was macroeconomic. A mature capitalist economy dominated by large corporations, they argued, generates a rising economic surplus, the difference between what society produces and the costs of producing it, while the outlets for absorbing that surplus through profitable investment and capitalist consumption fail to grow at the same pace. Unabsorbed surplus means stagnation: unsold goods, idle capacity, unemployed labour. Into this analysis steps what they named the sales effort, the vast apparatus of advertising, marketing, packaging variation, model changes, credit promotion and salesmanship whose function is to move goods that would otherwise accumulate unsold and to generate the demand that production on a giant scale presupposes. The sales effort, on their account, is not a parasitic decoration on the system. It is one of the system's essential absorption mechanisms, as load bearing as military expenditure in their wider argument, and the advertising industry is the sales effort's most visible department.

### What is the sales effort argument in Baran and Sweezy?

Baran and Sweezy argued that under monopoly capitalism the economic surplus grows faster than profitable investment outlets, so part of the surplus must be absorbed by activities that sell rather than produce. Advertising, packaging changes and model churn belong to that sales effort: they are costs of absorption, not persuasion alone.

The separation this article is performing shows its value at exactly this point, because the sales effort argument is constantly misread as a claim about persuasion. It is not. Baran and Sweezy's reasoning would stand almost untouched if it were demonstrated tomorrow that advertisements barely influence individual choices at all. Their claim concerns the disposition of resources and the conditions of realisation: value produced must be sold to be realised, output on a monopolistic scale must find buyers whose incomes were not generated by that output alone, and an economy that devotes a large and growing share of its labour to selling, styling, differentiating and financing consumption is telling the analyst something structural about itself, whatever the copywriters achieve. The argument also explains a phenomenon the persuasion model cannot: the persistence and growth of advertising expenditure through every public wave of scepticism about its effectiveness. If the sales effort is a cost of doing business at monopoly scale, a competitive necessity for each firm and an absorption channel for the system, then its budget is set by rivalry and by the surplus problem, not by fine measurements of marginal persuasive return, and executives who privately doubt the potency of their own campaigns will still sign the cheques, because unilateral disarmament in a share war is a gift to competitors. The wider stagnation framework within which the sales effort sits, its debts to Keynes and its quarrels with orthodox growth theory, is set out in the series article on [monopoly capital and stagnation theory](/2014/02/01/monopoly-capital-stagnation-theory/); the present article needs only the department of that framework that faces the consumer.

Qualifications belong inside the statement rather than as afterthoughts, since the sales effort argument has real exposures. Its category of waste is normatively loaded: to call the sales effort unproductive is to measure it against a standard of what production is for, and liberal critics have always replied that persuasion, information and variety are themselves services consumers value. The argument also fits some eras and sectors better than others; it was formed in the age of the giant American corporation and the national brand, and the digital reorganisation of selling, in which the audience's data rather than the advertiser's message becomes the prized asset, requires the framework to be extended rather than merely repeated, an extension taken up later in this article. But notice what kind of objections these are. They are disputes about macroeconomic classification and historical fit. They do not appeal to the inner lives of shoppers at all, and no evidence about whether Mrs. Miggins believes the washing powder advertisement touches them. That insulation from psychology is precisely the argument's strength, and it is purchased by giving up the thrill of exposing the consumer as a dupe. A critique that wants to keep that thrill must look elsewhere, to the fourth member of the family, where it will find both the thrill and the trouble.

## False needs: Marcuse and the psychological critique

Herbert Marcuse gave the psychological critique its canonical form in One Dimensional Man, published in 1964, and the book's argument about needs remains the passage of Marxist writing on consumption that general readers are most likely to have absorbed at second hand. Advanced industrial society, Marcuse argued, integrates its opposition by delivering goods: the system produces not only commodities but the wants those commodities satisfy, so that the individual who might have resisted the order instead experiences its reproduction as personal satisfaction. He drew the distinction on which everything else in the argument depends. Some needs are true, rooted in the requirements of life and flourishing that any society must answer. Others are false, superimposed on the individual by particular social interests in repression, perpetuating toil, aggressiveness and injustice while presenting themselves as the individual's own spontaneous desires. The goods of rest, fun and consumption according to the slogans of advertising belonged, in his treatment, largely to the second class, and the proof of their falsity lay in the character of the satisfaction: it is euphoria in unhappiness, a contentment that reconciles the satisfied person to the very arrangements that stunt him. The argument's rhetorical power is undeniable. It names something real in the experience of affluent societies, the suspicion that satisfaction itself has been conscripted, that the newest desire arrived suspiciously pre formatted, that relaxation has become another shift worked for the economy.

Yet the argument contains, at its centre, the question it cannot answer, and Marcuse, to his credit, saw the circle and wrote it down. Who is to distinguish true needs from false ones? If the individuals themselves are the products of the system that manufactures their wants, their own testimony is disqualified in advance, since the manipulated witness will sincerely report the implanted desire as his own. But any tribunal standing outside the individuals, composed of theorists, a party, a ministry of culture, reintroduces exactly the authority the emancipatory project was supposed to dissolve. Marcuse's own answer was frank about its difficulty: the distinction must be made by the individuals themselves, but only when they have become free to make it, which is to say after the liberation the distinction was meant to guide. The circle is acknowledged, even embraced as a feature of a total critique, but a circle acknowledged is still a circle, and seventy years of commentary have not broken it. Later defenders have tried substitutes for the missing criterion. Some appeal to needs that can be grounded in a theory of human nature, drawing on the young Marx's account of species being and rich human need, where the needs for creative work, unalienated association and developed senses supply a standard against which the manufactured appetite for the next gadget can be measured and found thin. Others appeal to the conditions of formation: a need is suspect when its satisfaction requires the expansion of the very apparatus that implanted it, or when it cannot survive reflection under conditions of full information and real alternatives. Still others abandon the adjective false and speak instead of heteronomous wants, wants whose origin lies outside the wanting subject's deliberation. Each repair purchases part of the argument at a price. The human nature repair must defend a contestable anthropology. The formation repair condemns much ordinary socialisation along with advertising, since tastes in music, dress and food are also formed outside deliberation. The relabelling repair keeps the sociology and quietly drops the verdict.

The honest summary is that the false needs argument functions best as a provocation and a research question, and worst as a settled doctrine. As a provocation, it forces every defender of consumer sovereignty to confront the endogeneity of wants: preferences are not formed in a vacuum and then merely served by the market; the market is one of the institutions that forms them, and a theory of freedom that refuses to look at preference formation has looked away from half its subject. As a doctrine, it licenses its holder to disregard what people say they want whenever their wants are inconvenient to the theory, and that licence has been used, historically, in ways the Marxist tradition has reason to regret. The separation performed in this article therefore assigns the false needs claim a precise status. It is the only one of the four critiques that requires a criterion distinguishing authentic from manufactured desire. No such criterion has been supplied with the generality the argument needs. And the other three critiques, structural, semiotic and macroeconomic, require no such criterion at all, which is why their demolition, often triumphantly performed by pointing at the fourth, leaves them standing. The gap has a name, and giving it a name is this article's namable claim, so the next section states it as a thesis in its own right.

## The needs criterion gap

The needs criterion gap is the following defect, stated as exactly as it can be stated. The false needs argument cannot be completed without a criterion that distinguishes true needs from false ones independently of the preferences people actually express. Marxist theory, in seventy years of trying, has not supplied that criterion. Therefore every version of the advertising critique that rests on the falsity of consumer wants rests on an unfinished argument, and every version that rests on structure, on texts or on the accounts of the economy is independent of the unfinished one and survives its failure. The gap is a claim about the state of an argument, not about the innocence of advertising, and it cuts in both directions with equal edge. Against the lazy critic, it forbids the word manipulation as a substitute for analysis: show the mechanism, read the text, follow the money, or lower the claim. Against the lazy defender, it forbids treating the gap as a refutation of the whole tradition: the defender who has answered Marcuse has answered the weakest of four arguments and has not yet met Williams on the magic that answers real frustration, or Baran and Sweezy on the surplus that must be absorbed, or Marx on the fetishism that no copywriter invented and no ban on billboards would cure.

Why has the criterion proved so hard to supply? Because the candidates fail in instructive ways, and the failures map the difficulty of the underlying problem rather than mere carelessness by theorists. A biological criterion, under which true needs are those whose frustration damages the organism, handles food, shelter and rest, and then falls silent exactly where the argument needs it, on the contested terrain of culture, status and novelty, where nobody's organism is damaged by lacking the advertised good and nobody's flourishing is straightforwardly damaged by having it. A historical criterion, under which needs are true when they arise from the free development of human capacities, smuggles the conclusion into the word free, since which developments count as free is the whole dispute. A procedural criterion, under which a need is true if it would be endorsed after undistorted deliberation, faces the regress that the conditions of undistorted deliberation must themselves be specified by someone, in a society none of whose members deliberates undistortedly, and the someone inherits the tribunal problem in a new costume. A majoritarian criterion, polling the population on its own needs, dissolves the argument entirely, since the population will ratify most of the wants the critic calls false, and the critic will then have to overrule the poll by the very authority in question. The pattern across the failures is constant: at the decisive step, each criterion either goes silent, argues in a circle, or appoints a judge. Recognising the pattern does not make the underlying suspicion disappear. Wants are formed; some formations are engineered for profit; engineering can be documented case by case. What the gap forbids is the promotion of that suspicion into a general theory of which wants count. Case by case demonstration, where a campaign's internal documents show an intention to cultivate insecurity and sell its relief, is possible and has been done. A universal criterion has not been produced, and the difference between those two achievements is the difference between the critique that survives scrutiny and the critique that survives only among believers.

The three critiques separated, the table that fixes the taxonomy for citation, therefore includes the psychological critique as a fourth row under its proper description, and the table's vulnerability column is where the needs criterion gap does its work. Teachers set the table as an exercise because it converts an attitude into an anatomy; researchers cite it because the rows have different evidence bases and cannot be pooled; students borrow its distinctions because an essay that keeps them apart reads as a different order of competence from an essay that merges them. The table follows, and the sections after it test the separated critiques against the two courts they must still face: the evidence about what advertising actually does to behaviour, and the defence of advertising mounted at full strength.

Read as a map, the table explains the odd resilience of the Marxist critique of advertising across decades in which its popular version has been refuted weekly. Refutations land on the row they aim at. A demonstration that consumers are sceptical ironists damages the popular hypnosis picture, which belongs to none of the four rows in its crude form, and grazes the fourth row's rhetoric while leaving its underlying question about preference formation intact. An econometric finding that advertising elasticities are small comforts the defender against a persuasion theory that the second and third rows never held. Meanwhile the first row sits outside the reach of audience research entirely, since it is a claim about the form of the commodity, and the third row sits outside the reach of laboratory persuasion studies, since it is a claim about the disposition of the social surplus. The table also exposes the tradition's genuine weak point without flinching: the fourth row's vulnerability column names a missing criterion, not a hostile review, and the missing criterion is missing after seventy years of motivated search by able theorists. An honest critique carries that admission in its own table rather than waiting for an opponent to supply it, because the admission is what licenses the confidence expressed about the other rows. Precision about where an argument ends is not a concession extracted under pressure; it is the method by which the surviving claims earn the right to be believed.

## Does advertising work? The evidence problem

Every critique of advertising eventually collides with an empirical question it would prefer to postpone: how much does advertising actually change behaviour? The honest answer, from a research literature that is large, technical and genuinely contested, is that effects at the level of the individual campaign are real but modest, unevenly distributed, and much easier to demonstrate for brand choice within a category than for the creation of new wants or the expansion of whole categories. Advertising tends to work, where measurements can isolate it, by reminding, by keeping a brand available in memory at the moment of choice, by defending share against rivals doing the same, and by supporting the price premium a recognised sign can command. It works least demonstrably at the task the popular critique assigns it, the implantation of desires in passive minds for objects nobody would otherwise want. Campaigns fail constantly and expensively. Products launched with enormous promotional weight die in the market at rates that fill the trade press with post mortems. Audiences skip, block, mute and mock. None of this is compatible with the hypnosis model, and the Marxist critique, in its separated form, has no need of the hypnosis model, which is precisely why the separation matters.

### Why are advertising effects so hard to measure?

Advertising effects are hard to measure because purchases have many causes, exposure is uneven, and the counterfactual, what the same person would have bought unexposed, is unobservable. Experiments and econometric studies therefore disagree, and most find brand switching easier to produce than wholly new desire or new categories of consumption.

The measurement difficulty deserves a section of its own because it disciplines both camps. A purchase is an overdetermined event: income, price, habit, availability, weather, imitation and need converge on it, and the advertisement is one voice in a crowd of causes, usually not the loudest. Exposure itself is elusive, since the industry historically bought opportunities to see rather than verified attention, and a spot broadcast into a room is not a message received by a mind. Econometric attempts to isolate advertising's contribution fight endogeneity at every step, because firms advertise most heavily exactly when demand is already moving, so correlation between spending and sales testifies to both directions of causation at once. The findings that survive these difficulties converge on a pattern rather than a slogan. Effects are strongest for new products that carry genuine information about existence and function, which is why launch advertising outperforms the reminder advertising of mature brands. Effects decay quickly, which is why continuity of spending matters more than brilliance of any single execution. Effects on total category demand are small and hard to find compared with effects on the division of that demand among brands. And the aggregate share of national income devoted to advertising has remained, across long stretches of the twentieth century in the economies where the series of figures is kept, within a surprisingly narrow band, a stability that sits awkwardly with stories of an ever tightening grip and fits better with the sales effort picture of a structural cost set by the scale of production and the intensity of rivalry. The researcher persona layer returns to the design implications of this literature, since what can responsibly be claimed from it is a live professional question for anyone reporting on the industry.

Where does this leave the four critiques? The structural row is untouched, since it never predicted persuasion. The semiotic row is untouched in its proper domain, since decoding a text was never a prediction about behaviour, though popularisers who slide from text to mind are duly rebuked by the evidence. The macroeconomic row is, if anything, strengthened in its central observation, since expenditure that persists and grows through demonstrated modesty of marginal effect is exactly what an absorption and rivalry account predicts and what a pure persuasion account cannot explain. The psychological row takes the real damage. If audiences are selective, sceptical and forgetful, if campaigns fail and wants refuse to be implanted on schedule, then the picture of manufactured desire must be revised downward from manufacture to something weaker: cultivation at the margins, amplification of tendencies already present, the patient normalisation of a world in which satisfaction is expected to arrive by purchase. That weaker claim may still be true and important, but it is a different claim from Marcuse's, and it needs the criterion question answered even more urgently, since marginal cultivation of a want is harder, not easier, to distinguish from the ordinary social formation of taste. Evidence, honestly weighed, thus performs the same service as the taxonomy: it grades the critiques, sparing the strong and taxing the weak, which is all that this article has ever asked a reader to do.

## Branding: the sign as an economic asset

Between the text of the advertisement and the accounts of the economy stands the brand, the durable form that advertising builds and maintains, and a Marxist analysis has to say carefully what a brand is. A brand is a sign with a legal fence around it: a name, a mark, a design, protected by trademark law, attached to a stream of associations accumulated through product experience, promotion, distribution and imitation. Its economic reality is not in doubt. Brands are bought and sold, valued on balance sheets, licensed, franchised and fought over in courts, and the premium a strong brand commands over an unbranded equivalent of similar physical specification is among the most reliable facts in marketing. The analytical question is what kind of value that premium represents, and here the tradition's categories give a discriminating answer rather than a jeer. The brand does not, in the labour theory's strict terms, create value in production; the sign is not a machine and weaves no cloth. What the brand does is organise the realisation and distribution of value. It steers demand toward one capital and away from others, it stabilises that steering across time, it converts fleeting attention into a durable asset that can be capitalised, and it allows the owner to appropriate a larger share of the social surplus than the underlying productive operation alone would command. Branding is thus best understood as a technology of appropriation built on top of production, which is why it flourishes most luxuriantly exactly where products converge in function and the sign must carry the whole weight of difference.

### What does branding add to a product that production did not?

Branding adds a durable sign: a name, a mark and a stock of associations that let one soap, shoe or phone be recognised, remembered and preferred over near identical rivals. The addition is real as an asset on a balance sheet, yet it produces recognition rather than the underlying use the object provides.

The social meaning of the brand runs deeper than the balance sheet, and the semiotic critique returns here with its strongest material. To consume branded goods is to speak with objects, since the sign is legible to others and the purchase therefore communicates: allegiance, aspiration, tribe, irony, arrival, refusal. Sociologists outside the Marxist tradition, from Thorstein Veblen's account of conspicuous consumption at the end of the nineteenth century to Pierre Bourdieu's analysis of distinction in the twentieth, mapped this communicative economy of goods in detail, and their findings are not refuted by the Marxist framework but located by it. Status competition through goods is real, ancient and not invented by advertisers. What capitalist branding adds is the industrialisation of the sign supply: meanings that earlier societies attached to goods slowly, through custom and sumptuary rule, are now designed, tested, refreshed and retired on commercial schedules, and the lexicon in which people announce themselves is substantially written by firms selling the words. The critic who wants to object to that arrangement should object to it accurately. The objection is not that consumers are lying when they communicate through goods, since the communication is genuine and the meanings are socially real. The objection is that a sphere of human expression has been enclosed as private property and is rented back to its speakers one purchase at a time, a point about ownership of the means of signification that parallels, without duplicating, the older argument about ownership of the means of production. Even here the needs criterion is not required. Nothing in the enclosure objection depends on declaring the wish to signal false; the wish is granted as human and perennial, and the critique falls on who owns the signalling system and what its owners charge for admission.

## Consumption beyond the advertisement: needs, uses and pleasures

A critique of advertising that never examines consumption itself will mistake the salesman for the society, so the phenomenon must be widened before the defence is heard. Consumption under capitalism is contradictory in ways no campaign creates. Goods are genuinely useful and genuinely enjoyed: the washing machine abolished a day of crushing labour in the week of the households that acquired one, the refrigerator rewrote the possibilities of diet and health, recorded music carried performances across oceans to listeners who would never have entered the concert halls where the music was made. Any Marxism that cannot say these things plainly has traded analysis for posture, since the tradition's own founder treated the expansion of needs and capacities through production as part of the historical achievement of the bourgeoisie, the restless development of the productive forces that his most famous pamphlet praised in the act of indicting their owners. At the same time, consumption is the terrain on which class is displayed and felt daily, where the wage's adequacy is tested against the shop window, where credit mediates the gap between the life that is advertised as normal and the income that is actually paid, and where the private household absorbs, unpaid, the work of turning commodities into meals, comfort and care. The ideology question about consumption is therefore not exhausted by advertising at all. It extends to the presentation of a historically specific standard of living as the natural shape of a decent life, a presentation carried as much by the built environment, the credit system and the neighbour's driveway as by any spot or poster.

This widening protects the analysis against a vulgar error that has dogged the tradition's popular reception, the belief that Marxism condemns consumption as such and dreams of an austere world of unbranded grey goods. The textual record points the other way. Marx's scattered remarks on need are expansionary: the realm of freedom begins beyond necessary labour, the rich individual of the Grundrisse is the one whose needs and capacities have been developed universally, and the critique of capitalism is that it develops needs lopsidedly, lavishly for commodities that can be sold at a profit and stingily for the needs, leisure, security, unpolluted surroundings, unhurried care, whose satisfaction has no buyer. Advertising belongs inside that lopsidedness as its herald, announcing with trumpets the needs the market can meet while the unmet needs without purchasing power behind them go unannounced and, in the public imagery of satisfaction, largely unimagined. Williams's inversion returns with full force here. A culture saturated with promotion for the purchasable and silent about the unpurchasable is not a materialist culture revelling in things; it is a culture that has narrowed the idea of the good life to the catalogue, and the narrowing, not the shopping, is the ideological event. Notice, once more, what the argument does not need. It does not need any shopper's desire to be false. The desire for the advertised good can be authentic, informed and satisfied, and the narrowing stands, because the narrowing is a property of the agenda of public imagination, not of any individual's soul.

## Rebellion for sale: the borrowing of dissent

No phenomenon tests the separated critiques like the advertising industry's long habit of borrowing the language and imagery of rebellion to sell commodities, since the habit seems, at first glance, to prove the strongest version of every critique at once. The documented record is extensive. Soft drink campaigns of the late 1960s dressed themselves in the iconography of a generation's unrest. A celebrated computer launch of the 1980s cast the purchaser as a heroine smashing the screen of a grey totalitarian order, in an advertisement directed by a major film maker and remembered long after the machine it launched. Sportswear brands have built decades of identity on slogans of defiance and on the sponsorship of athletes whose public stands made them controversial. Denim, fragrance, motorcycles and mobile phones have all been sold as instruments of nonconformity, frequently in the same seasons and sometimes by the same agencies that sold conformity's comforts to other clients. The trade has a name for the pattern in its candid moments, and critics outside the industry, notably the Canadian writer Naomi Klein in her study of branding published at the end of the 1990s, documented the machinery by which dissent is scouted, signed and merchandised like any other cultural raw material.

What does the pattern actually prove? The semiotic critique reads it cleanly: the referent system being borrowed is the audience's own stock of meanings about freedom and defiance, and the transfer works because those meanings are honoured by the audience before the brand arrives. Advertising follows the culture's values at least as much as it leads them, and the rebellion campaigns are evidence of the audience's values, not of the industry's invention of them. The structural critique adds the colder observation that a system which can sell its own negation as a product line has demonstrated something about the commodity form's hospitality: no content is unassimilable once it can be attached to a saleable object, and the assimilation is not hypocrisy on any individual's part but the ordinary operation of a market in signs. The psychological critique, by contrast, gains the least here, despite appearances. That rebellion can be merchandised does not show the rebel wants were false; the young purchasers of the defiant trainer mostly wanted the trainer and the defiance together, received both in the forms on offer, and were not obviously deceived about the transaction. The genuinely sharp question the phenomenon poses is political rather than psychological: whether dissent that is routinely converted into a marketing resource loses force in the conversion, whether the availability of rebellion as a style drains rebellion as a practice. That question is real and unsettled, it concerns movements rather than shoppers, and honest analysis leaves it open rather than decorating a conclusion with it. What can be said without overreach is that the borrowing is diagnostic. An industry obliged to dress commodities in the language of liberation is confessing, in its own expenditures, that the values its audience already holds are not the values of contented consumption, and the confession is worth more to the critic than any theory of implanted desire.

## The audience answers back

The empirical and ethnographic record of audiences is the graveyard of the hypnosis model, and a Marxist analysis that respects evidence must walk through it rather than around it. Audiences for advertising display, in study after study and in the industry's own anxious research, a repertoire of defences so ordinary that calling them resistance flatters them: inattention, scepticism as a default setting, genre literacy that decodes the sales intention in the first seconds, parody, zapping and skipping technologies adopted with enthusiasm, and the folk wisdom, transmitted to children early, that advertisements exaggerate because exaggeration is their job. Persuasion knowledge, as consumer researchers name the lay theory of how selling works, is widespread and develops young. The same publics that critics picture as sponges produce advertising satire as a folk art, sustain a flourishing genre of complaint about manipulative campaigns, and punish brands whose claims insult their intelligence with a swiftness that agency executives describe in memoirs with undiminished awe. Failed campaigns are not exceptions in the industry's history but a standing condition of it, and the archives of celebrated disasters, launches that consumed fortunes and moved nothing, are part of the documented record any honest account must weigh.

Two subtleties keep this section from becoming a simple acquittal. The first is that seeing through a message and being unaffected by the world the message belongs to are different achievements. A viewer may decode every advertisement ironically and still live inside a built environment, a credit system and a calendar of festivals organised around purchase, so that scepticism operates at the level of the text while the level of the structure goes unexamined, rather as a citizen may mock every speech from a throne and never question the throne. Irony, in other words, is compatible with accommodation, and several of the tradition's subtler writers, including the Frankfurt analysts at their best, argued exactly that the knowing smirk is one of the culture industry's products rather than a refuge from it. That argument must be stated, but it must also be disciplined: a theory under which both belief and disbelief confirm the diagnosis has made itself unfalsifiable at that point, and the honest critic will specify in advance what audience behaviour would count against the accommodation thesis, such as organised refusal, sustained alternative provision, or measurable indifference to the consumption calendar, all of which also exist in the record in partial forms. The second subtlety is distributional. Persuasion knowledge and advertising literacy are unevenly spread, and the audiences with the least armour, children above all, and adults at moments of grief, insecurity or illness, are precisely those the industry's least scrupulous historical campaigns addressed, from tobacco promotion aimed at the young to the marketing of remedies to the frightened. A separated critique can say this exactly: the semiotic machinery is general, the vulnerability is specific, and regulation debates belong to the specific terrain where vulnerability and targeting coincide, not to fantasies about a hypnotised species.

## Advertising after the audience became data

The digital reorganisation of advertising altered the industry's economics enough to require the separated critiques to be re-run on new material, and the exercise is the best available test of their durability. The older broadcast model sold audiences in bulk to advertisers on the basis of estimated exposure. The platform model that succeeded it identifies individuals, accumulates behavioural records across services, predicts responses, prices each impression in automated auctions and reports measured outcomes back to the buyer, so that the commodity traded is no longer attention in the abstract but attention specified, targeted and audited. Within the Marxist tradition this transformation has generated a genuine theoretical dispute that honest exposition must name rather than smooth over. Shoshana Zuboff's account of surveillance capitalism, published in 2019 and adopted far beyond academic readers, describes a new economic order built on the extraction of behavioural surplus, data taken beyond what service improvement requires and refined into prediction products sold to those who would modify behaviour at scale. A number of Marxist critics have accepted the phenomena while disputing the theory, on grounds that matter. Surveillance, they argue, is not a new logic of accumulation but an intensification of the old one: the platforms are capitals competing for advertising revenue and monopoly position, the data extraction is better understood through rent, monopoly and the enclosure of social activity than through a novel rogue capitalism, and Zuboff's framing, by presenting the development as a deviation from a supposedly benign market order, mislocates the mechanism and flatters the order it condemns. The series treatment of [platform capitalism in Marxist analysis](/2020/11/15/platform-capitalism-marxist-analysis/) follows that dispute through the wider political economy; the present article needs the narrower consequence for advertising.

### How did advertising change when audiences became data?

Broadcast advertising addressed audiences as statistical masses and guessed at their response. Digital advertising identifies individuals, follows behaviour across services, tests messages continuously and charges for measured responses, so the audience itself becomes a product sold to advertisers, and attention, prediction and personal data join the older arts of persuasion.

Run the four critiques across the new terrain and they separate as cleanly as before, which is evidence for the taxonomy. The structural critique deepens without changing its nature: the commodity form now extends to the audience's own activity, since attention and behavioural traces are packaged and sold while appearing, in the mirror of the interface, as the free expression of the user, a fetishism of the profile in which the social relations of surveillance and extraction present themselves as personalisation and convenience. The semiotic critique survives but is joined by an algorithmic sibling, since the message is now assembled dynamically, tested in thousands of variants and retired by performance data, so that the unit of analysis shifts from the crafted advertisement to the optimisation process that breeds advertisements, a shift Williamson's method can absorb by treating the system of variations as its text. The macroeconomic critique gains a sharp new fact: the largest advertising businesses in the digital order are not agencies at all but the owners of the spaces where attention gathers, and the sales effort's revenue has been substantially captured as rent by platform monopolies, a development Baran and Sweezy's categories of surplus absorption and monopoly reach without strain. The psychological critique, finally, faces the terrain where its claims sound most plausible, since targeting by inferred vulnerability and the continuous testing of appeals against behaviour look like manufacture of desire with better instruments, and yet the needs criterion gap stands exactly where it stood, untouched by the sophistication of the machinery: to call the influenced wants false still requires the criterion nobody has supplied, and the documented cases that convict, internal records of campaigns designed to exploit identified insecurities, convict as cases, within the separated method, rather than as a general theory of the audience's soul.

## A short history that the critiques must fit

The separated critiques make different historical claims, and a compressed history of the industry is the fairest way to test them against something other than each other's rhetoric. Organised persuasion on behalf of sellers is older than capitalism, since market criers, inn signs and printers' handbills served trade for centuries, but advertising as a distinct industry belongs to the nineteenth century's conjunction of cheap print, national markets and branded manufacture. The branded packaged good was the pivot. When soap, tea, cocoa and patent remedies were sold loose from the grocer's barrel, reputation attached to the retailer; when manufacturers sealed and named the packet, reputation migrated to the maker, and the maker acquired both the motive and the means to address the final buyer over the retailer's head. The great agencies grew up to service that address, and the illustrated press, financed substantially by their spending, acquired the economic constitution that later media theory would anatomise: a publication sells its readers to its advertisers as much as it sells its pages to its readers, an arrangement the audience commodity analysis of later Marxist media scholarship would formalise. Radio and then television repeated the constitution in stronger form, delivering audiences to sponsors as the explicit product of the broadcasting business, while the creative revolution of the 1960s professionalised the semiotic craft Williamson would decode, replacing the shout of the salesman with wit, irony and the borrowed glamour of art direction.

Against this record the critiques score differently, and the scoring is instructive. Fetishism precedes the industry by centuries and survives every change in its techniques, exactly as the structural row predicts. The semiotic craft visibly intensifies with the professionalisation of the agencies and the arrival of media whose messages must work in seconds and in images, exactly as the second row implies. The sales effort grows with the scale of manufacture and the nationalisation of markets, surging when mass production outruns the purchasing routines of local trade, exactly as Baran and Sweezy's timing implies. The psychological row, by contrast, has no natural periodisation: the wants of the handbill reader of 1850 and the feed scroller of the platform age are declared equally manufactured, and a claim that fits every period equally well explains the peculiarities of none, which is one more symptom of the missing criterion, since a criterion would discriminate among cases and eras rather than blessing the whole record with one adjective. History also supplies the control case the tradition rarely mentions: the socialist states of the twentieth century, which abolished commercial advertising almost entirely, did not thereby abolish the fetishism of the commodity where commodity production survived, nor the appetite for status goods, nor queues for the very Western branded objects their own propaganda denounced. The record must be reported plainly in an article governed by this series' neutrality rules, and its lesson cuts for the separation: abolish the industry and the meanings of goods persist in altered forms, because the meanings were never the industry's creation alone.

## Is consumerism an ideology?

The word consumerism does heavy and sloppy work in public argument, and precision about it is a precondition for answering whether it names an ideology in any of the senses this series recognises. The term belongs only partly to the Marxist vocabulary. In ordinary usage it oscillates between three meanings that should never be traded for one another mid argument: a description of societies in which consumption occupies a large place in life and identity, a moral complaint about the character of people who enjoy shopping, and a doctrine, held by someone, that consumption is the proper road to the good life. Only the third meaning is a candidate for ideology, since the first is a sociological observation and the second is a sneer. Tested as a doctrine, consumerism in the strict sense, the proposition that the meaning of life is adequately expressed in the acquisition of goods, is seldom asserted by anyone in so many words. It has no canonical text, no party, no catechism. What exists instead is a default: an environment of messages, built forms, credit arrangements and social comparisons in which the purchasable satisfaction is continually presented and the unpurchasable satisfactions are seldom pictured at all. Ideology in the structural sense developed across this cluster of the series does not require a catechism. It requires a representation of a real relation that misdescribes it in a direction favourable to its continuation, and the default presentation of the good life as a catalogue meets that description more exactly than most doctrines do. It misdescribes the relation between income and satisfaction, since the catalogue's implied promise, that the next acquisition closes the gap between the life one has and the life one sees, is contradicted by the purchaser's own repeated experience and renewed with every campaign. It misdescribes the relation between production and consumption, presenting the moment of purchase as the economy's human face while the labour behind the goods stays offstage. And it misdescribes the citizen as a consumer in domains, health, education, political choice itself, where the consumer's stance of exit and preference is a poor substitute for the citizen's stance of voice and deliberation.

Stating the case that strongly obliges the exposition to register the disciplined counterpoints, and there are three. A representation can only misdescribe against a standard of accurate description, and the critic who calls the catalogue a misdescription of the good life is closer than he may notice to the tribunal problem that haunts the false needs argument, although the distance is real: judging a public representation against evidence about satisfaction is a different and more defensible operation than judging an individual's wants against a theory of his nature. Populations also vary the default with their own materials, bending consumption to kinship, faith, festivity and mutual aid in ways no catalogue scripted, and the ethnographic record of how goods are actually used is a standing correction to any theory of total ideological saturation. Finally, the default has internal critics supplied by the market itself, since simplicity, durability and abstention are also sold, which shows either the system's infinite hospitality, as the structural critique suspects, or its genuine pluralism, as its defenders reply, and the separated method leaves that fork visible instead of pretending it away. The verdict available at this point is deliberately narrow. Consumerism as a moral sneer is not an analysis. Consumerism as a named doctrine is mostly a phantom. The consumption default, the unargued framing of purchasable satisfaction as the visible form of the good life, qualifies as ideology in the structural sense, and advertising is that default's most concentrated, most professional and most examinable expression, which is why this article studies the industry rather than the abstraction.

## Influencers, attention and the labour of being watched

The influencer form deserves separate treatment because it rearranges the elements the earlier sections separated, and the rearrangement fools commentators into declaring a new era when the taxonomy still holds. In the influencer arrangement, a person with an accumulated audience promotes goods inside content that presents itself as personal life, advice or entertainment, and the promotion's effectiveness depends on the audience's sense that the endorsement arises from the person's taste rather than from a media plan. Agencies and platforms intermediate, audiences are measured with the full apparatus of the data economy, and the promoted object arrives wrapped in the referent system of a biography. Semiotically, nothing fundamental has changed since Williamson: meanings are transferred by juxtaposition, and the labour of connection is still performed by the audience, except that the borrowed domain is now a person's presented self, and the concealment of the transfer is deeper because the advertisement and the editorial matter share one voice, one face and one feed. The disclosure rules that regulators in several jurisdictions have imposed, requiring endorsements to be labelled as advertising, are an official acknowledgement of exactly the concealment the semiotic critique described, and the running quarrels over their enforcement are best read as a contest between the critique and the craft, conducted in regulatory language.

What is genuinely novel sits in the macroeconomic and structural rows rather than the semiotic one. The influencer performs labour, the continuous production of a saleable self, under conditions that reproduce the classic relations in miniature: the creator owns a means of production in the form of a channel and a persona, yet depends absolutely on platforms that own distribution, set the terms of visibility and take their share of the revenue, so that the platform relation analysed elsewhere in this series reappears wearing the costume of independence. The audience, meanwhile, supplies attention and data that are sold onward, and the boundary between consuming and producing grows porous in ways the older audience commodity argument anticipated from the broadcast era. For the psychological row, influencer marketing is the hardest case and should be conceded as such. Appeals routed through trusted persons, calibrated against measured insecurities, delivered inside intimate media habits, are the closest approach in the record to the manufacture of wants as the popular critique imagines it. The concession changes nothing about the needs criterion gap, since proximity is not a criterion, but honesty requires registering where the fourth critique's plausibility peaks, just as the evidence section registered where it collapses. An analysis that concedes the hard cases grades itself; an analysis that notices only the easy ones is advertising of another kind.

## The defence of advertising at full strength

The neutrality discipline of this series requires the competing account to be built as strongly as its own advocates build it before any verdict is returned, and the defence of advertising, properly assembled, is formidable. Its first pillar is information. Markets work badly when buyers cannot learn what exists, what it costs and what it does. Advertising, in the economist's classic statement associated with George Stigler's work on the economics of information at the start of the 1960s, is a technology for reducing search costs: it publicises prices, announces entrants, spreads word of innovations and disciplines sellers, who cannot repeatedly advertise what repeated experience refutes without burning the asset of reputation they are paying to build. On this account the advertisement for a new product with real advantages is among the most socially useful texts in the economy, and the empirical pattern noted earlier, that advertising's measurable effects are strongest for new products and launches, supports the information reading at least as readily as any darker one. The second pillar is the signal. Expenditure on advertising can itself certify quality, since only a firm expecting repeat purchases can rationally burn money on promotion for an experience good whose faults buyers would discover and punish; the willingness to be known is a bond posted against the product's performance, and heavy promotion of worthless goods is, on this theory, a strategy the market weeds out.

The third pillar is consumer sovereignty, and it is the one that carries the moral weight of the defence. In a free society, the liberal argument runs, the individual's wants are the individual's business, and the presumption that satisfaction honestly reported is genuine satisfaction may be displaced only by arrogance. Consumers are not the passive material of the critic's imagination. They are experienced, defended, ironical and busy; they develop brand loyalties for reasons that include real differences in taste, fit and reliability invisible to the seminar table; they enjoy goods, including frivolous goods, with a gusto that requires no excuse; and they punish deception, boredom and condescension by the simple expedient of not buying, which is why the industry's history is littered with expensive failures adjudicated by the very audience the critique calls duped. Every failed launch is therefore evidence in the sovereignty case, and there are thousands of them. The fourth pillar is the pleasure of the texts themselves. Advertising at its best is a popular art, compressed, witty, visually inventive, a form the public frequently enjoys, quotes, collects and remembers with affection long after the products have vanished, and a critique that cannot account for the genuine aesthetic pleasure of the artefact, or that treats that pleasure as further proof of bewitchment, has arranged to be right whatever happens, which is the signature of a closed system rather than an analysis. The fifth pillar reaches beyond commerce. Advertising revenue has historically financed a press and a broadcasting culture that readers and viewers received below cost or free, an imperfect subsidy with real democratic consequences, and its decline in the platform age has been accompanied by newsroom contractions that democrats of every persuasion have reason to mourn. The defence rests there: information, signal, sovereignty, pleasure and subsidy, five pillars, none of them dependent on denying that advertisements also flatter, omit and insinuate.

Against the separated critiques, the defence scores honestly different results, and saying so is the verdict's groundwork. Against the psychological critique it scores heavily, and the needs criterion gap explains why: every attempt to override reported satisfaction needs the missing criterion, and until the criterion arrives the sovereignty pillar stands. Against the semiotic critique it scores partially, since decoding is not refuted by enjoyment, indeed the pleasure pillar concedes the texts reward attention, but the defence successfully insists that a reading of a message is not a measurement of an effect, and that the move from artefact to audience requires evidence the semiotic tradition does not itself supply. Against the macroeconomic critique it scores least, because the sales effort argument never depended on any shopper being fooled, and the information defence, applied at the level of the whole economy, must explain why an information technology's budget should be set by rivalry and absorption rather than by the value of the information conveyed, and why the same sums recur in sectors where buyers are few, expert and unpersuadable. Against the structural critique it does not score at all, since fetishism was never the industry's doing, and the defence of advertising, however complete, leaves the metaphysics of the commodity exactly where Marx put it. That pattern of scores, heavy against one row, partial against another, silent against the rest, is what a separated analysis was built to produce, and it is more damaging to lazy versions of both camps than any general verdict could be.

## Where the critique overreaches

A tradition earns the right to be believed about its strong claims by policing its weak performances, and the Marxist writing on advertising contains performances that deserve policing in the open. The first overreach is the inflation of vocabulary, in which every appeal becomes manipulation, every preference becomes internalised oppression and every purchase becomes false consciousness, until the words have been spent so freely that nothing remains with which to describe the genuine cases of engineered insecurity and targeted exploitation that the record does contain. The second is the condescension documented in the paternalism objection, which this article was required to state at full strength and now states as a verdict on part of the tradition's own history: too much criticism of advertising has been written from the security of educated taste about the pleasures of people with less money and less security, mistaking the difference between the critic's basket and the shopper's basket for the difference between authenticity and illusion. The middle class horror at the working class enjoyment of the conspicuous, the branded and the sweet is an old class feeling wearing theoretical dress, and the tradition's own class analysis is the instrument best suited to exposing it. The third overreach is the unfalsifiable turn, in which audience agreement proves manipulation and audience disagreement proves its depth, a structure that no evidence could embarrass and that therefore forfeits the name of analysis. The fourth is nostalgia, the quiet assumption that wants were authentic in some earlier dispensation, before the agencies, as though the village fair, the sumptuary code and the pulpit had not formed desire with instruments blunter and crueller than the poster.

### Where does the Marxist critique of advertising overreach?

The critique overreaches when it treats every advertised desire as implanted, every consumer as deceived, and every pleasure in goods as evidence of domination. Those moves require a standard of authentic wanting that the critics cannot supply, and they dissolve the difference between manipulation, ordinary persuasion and the social character of all taste.

Each overreach has the same root, and naming the root is more useful than deploring the symptoms. All four are ways of avoiding the criterion problem: inflate the vocabulary and no criterion is needed, since everything is already condemned; condescend, and the critic's own taste becomes the criterion by default; turn unfalsifiable, and the criterion is excused from ever being tested; grow nostalgic, and the criterion is located in a vanished past that cannot be cross examined. The needs criterion gap is therefore not a pedantic footnote to the tradition but the discipline that keeps its critique of advertising an analysis rather than a mood. A critic who has absorbed the discipline writes differently. He specifies the mechanism, whether structural appearance, textual transfer, surplus absorption or documented targeting, and he grades his claim to his evidence, saying of one campaign that its internal strategy papers show deliberate cultivation of insecurity, of a sector that its selling costs belong in the national accounts of waste or absorption depending on a stated theory, of a text that its exclusions are demonstrable on its face, and of the audience, most often, that the honest finding is mixed, defended and unfinished. Such criticism is harder to write and harder to dismiss, which is the exchange the separation offers.

## The dependence effect and the engineers of consent

Two bodies of writing stand between the classic critiques and the documented record, and an honest exposition must give them their place because they supply the strongest case by case evidence the psychological critique possesses. The first is the work of the industry's own engineers and their historians. Edward Bernays, a nephew of Freud who became the founding publicist of public relations, published Propaganda in 1928 and wrote without embarrassment about the conscious and intelligent manipulation of the organised habits and opinions of the masses as a legitimate instrument of governance in democratic society. His documented campaigns included the celebrated intervention of 1929 in which debutantes marched in a New York parade smoking cigarettes presented as torches of freedom, an operation designed to break the taboo on women smoking by borrowing the meanings of emancipation for a tobacco brand. Whatever a reader concludes about Bernays's boasts, and self dramatisation was among his products, the operation is a documented instance of the semiotic mechanism aimed deliberately at a population's expanding sense of its own rights, with the commercial beneficiary concealed. Two decades later the motivational researchers, Ernest Dichter prominent among them, sold manufacturers depth interviews and psychoanalytic readings of consumer reluctance, and Vance Packard's bestseller The Hidden Persuaders, published in 1957, carried an alarmed account of these practices to a mass readership. Packard's book matters to this article less as analysis than as evidence of a public that was never the sponge of legend: the depth manipulators became a scandal precisely because audiences recognised the intention to work on them below awareness and resented it. Stuart Ewen's Captains of Consciousness, published in 1976, remains the standard history of the American industry's founding generation, documenting how the new mass advertisers of the 1920s understood themselves as engineers of a consuming public, addressing a population they regarded as driven by instinct and in need of tutelage. The historians' archive thus convicts and acquits in the same breath: the intention to manufacture desire existed, in writing, at the industry's foundation; the success of the manufacture is a separate question, and the archive, full of failed brands launched by the same captains, does not settle it.

The second body of writing is John Kenneth Galbraith's doctrine of the dependence effect, set out in The Affluent Society in 1958, and it deserves notice because it is the closest approach to a criterion that the wider critical tradition has produced, and because its failure mode is so instructive. Galbraith argued that in an affluent economy production does not merely satisfy pre existing wants; through advertising and salesmanship it creates the wants it then satisfies, so that the urgency of production cannot be inferred from the urgency of the wants, since the process that satisfies a want also manufactures it. A hungry man wants food before any bakery advertises; the want for the advertised refinement is downstream of the apparatus. The elegance of the formulation is that it seems to need no tribunal: the origin of the want, inside the producing system itself, marks it as suspect. Pressed, however, the criterion dissolves for the reason this article has tracked throughout. Origin is not a reliable test of legitimacy, since wants of unquestioned standing are also socially produced, by families, schools, friendships and public health campaigns, and wants of dubious standing arise spontaneously without any advertiser's help. Galbraith himself aimed the argument at the social balance between private affluence and public provision rather than at the authenticity of individual desires, and it is strongest in that register, as an argument about what a rich society neglects while it perfects the arts of selling. Read in that register it joins the macroeconomic row. Read as a criterion of false needs it joins Marcuse in the gap. The whole episode is the needs criterion gap in miniature, demonstrated by its ablest attempted escape.

## Advertising and the media it pays for

The political economy of media adds a dimension that none of the four rows quite captures on its own, and the Marxist tradition's sharpest contribution here is Dallas Smythe's argument, first pressed in the 1970s, that the commodity produced by commercial media is the audience itself. A newspaper or broadcaster operates, on this account, in two markets at once: it sells content to readers at a price covering only part of its costs, and it sells those readers' attention to advertisers, whose payments cover the rest and supply the profit. The audience commodity reframes everything it touches. Editorial matter becomes, in the cold language Smythe borrowed from the industry itself, the free lunch that draws the crowd whose attention is the real merchandise, and the ideological consequences follow from the business model rather than from the politics of proprietors: content must not shrink the audience commodity, must deliver it in a purchasing frame of mind, and must sort it into the demographic packages advertisers wish to buy. The argument's own history illustrates the separation ethic, since Smythe's intervention began as a complaint that Marxist media theory had a blindspot, neglecting political economy for ideology, and his critics replied by defending the autonomy of the ideological, so that the dispute inside the tradition replays, at the level of media, the same tension between structure and meaning that this article has separated at the level of the advertisement.

For the critique of advertising the audience commodity argument contributes something specific and testable. It predicts that the content surrounding advertisements will be selected for compatibility with selling, that audiences valued by advertisers will be courted with provision while audiences without purchasing power will be underserved by commercial media regardless of their numbers or needs, and that the advertiser's influence on content will operate structurally, through the anticipation of offence, more often than through the telephone call of legend, although the documented record contains enough telephone calls, withdrawn sponsorships, killed stories and punished programmes, to show that the direct route also exists and is used when the stakes justify it. Evidence for the structural route is the quiet kind: the geography of media plenty and media deserts tracks advertiser demand more faithfully than it tracks citizenship, and the historical dependence of the press on advertising revenue shaped what a newspaper was, a cheap, copious, advertiser safe object, long before any theorist named the arrangement. None of this requires a single reader to believe a single advertisement. It requires only that attention became a commodity and that institutions grew around its sale, which is why the media dimension belongs with the structural and macroeconomic rows in strength, and why journalists, addressed in the persona layer that follows the remaining sections, are professionally obliged to understand the business that pays for their own megaphone.

## The industry's other products: credit, fashion and planned obsolescence

Advertising does not work alone, and a materialist account must place it among the companion mechanisms that raise the tempo of consumption, since several of the effects popularly credited to the poster belong to the partnership. Credit is the first companion. The expansion of consumer credit across the twentieth century, hire purchase, the revolving account, the general purpose card, detached purchase from the rhythm of the wage and allowed the advertised standard of living to be inhabited before it was earned, at a price collected in interest. The ideological work of credit is quiet and profound: it presents the future income of the borrower as a present asset, converts prudence from a virtue into a missed opportunity, and makes the maintenance of appearances across a bad season a private financial problem rather than a public fact about wages. Fashion is the second companion, a social mechanism older than the industry, in which the sign value of goods is made perishable by convention, so that objects still serviceable as objects expire as statements. Advertising accelerates and administers fashion but did not invent its cruelty or its charm, and the distinction matters for the taxonomy, since a critique aimed only at the poster will leave the fashion system, which runs happily in societies and subcultures with minimal promotion, entirely untouched. Planned obsolescence is the third companion and the most concrete: the deliberate design of products for shortened life, through fragile construction, withheld parts, incompatible fittings or software abandoned on a schedule. The practice has a documentary history reaching back to the interwar years, when a Geneva cartel of lamp manufacturers standardised the short life of the light bulb and fined members whose bulbs lasted too long, and when an American real estate broker could publish a pamphlet proposing compulsory obsolescence as a cure for the Depression. Here the critique needs no psychology at all. A product engineered to fail is a fact of the workshop, verifiable by engineers, and the waste it organises belongs with the sales effort in the macroeconomic row, as another mechanism by which a surplus producing economy manufactures the demand it requires.

Placing the companions beside the advertisement also corrects the scale of the advertisement's own responsibility, in both directions. Credit, fashion and obsolescence would operate in a world of modest promotion, and promotion in a world of durable goods, patient fashion and cash purchase would be a tamer art; the system of consumption is a system, and its ideological effect, the continual presentation of renewed purchase as the form of a good life, is an emergent property of the whole apparatus rather than the achievement of any single department. That admission strengthens rather than weakens the analysis, because it matches how the audience actually encounters the phenomenon: not as a poster in a void but as a total environment of shops, screens, statements of account and social comparisons, in which the advertisement is merely the only element polite enough to announce that it is trying to persuade. There is a sense, rarely granted by its critics, in which the advertisement is the most honest text in the environment, since its intention is declared by its placement and format, while the credit agreement's small print, the fashion cycle's silent verdicts and the engineered failure timed for the month after the warranty speak without ever confessing that they are speaking. Ideology critique worthy of the name attends to the silent texts at least as carefully as to the confessed one, and the sections of this article devoted to structure are its attempt to do so.

## Experiments in absence: bans, removals and what they show

The rarest and most valuable evidence in this whole field comes from occasions when advertising was subtracted from an environment and observers could watch what changed, and the separated critiques predict different results for such subtractions, which makes the experiments precious. Restrictions on tobacco promotion, adopted in country after country from the 1970s onward in the face of the industry's resistance, supply the largest case. The public health literature that evaluated the bans generally found that comprehensive prohibitions, covering all media and sponsorship, were followed by measurable declines in consumption attributable in part to the removal of promotion, while partial bans mostly displaced spending into unregulated channels, a pattern the separated method reads without strain: the semiotic machinery was dismantled where it had demonstrably served recruitment into a lethal habit, audiences for the remaining channels carried their scepticism with them, and the commodity's fetishism, the romance of the object itself, persisted in the unbranded spaces where smokers still congregated. Quebec's prohibition, in force since 1980, on commercial advertising directed at children under thirteen supplies a second case, aimed at the audience the evidence section identified as least armoured, and studies comparing exposed and unexposed child populations across the province's linguistic media markets found differences in product recognition and requests consistent with the ban doing real, bounded work. The removal of billboards from São Paulo under its clean city law of 2006 supplies the third case and the strangest, since the subtraction was aesthetic and civic rather than protective: residents reported, in the surveys that followed, an unexpected revelation of their own city, its architecture and its poverty alike made visible by the silence of the hoardings, and the episode entered the international debate as evidence that the volume of commercial address in public space is itself a political fact, independent of any message's content.

Each experiment disciplines a different row of the table. The bans show that advertising's effects, modest at the margin for mature brands, are not nothing, and are largest exactly where the separated critique would expect, at the recruitment of new consumers and the normalisation of a category, rather than in the hypnotic command of the unwilling. The billboard removal shows that the structural presence of promotion, its occupation of the visual commons, is a separable grievance from persuasion, one a citizen can hold while granting every claim of the sovereignty defence about individual messages. The children's case shows that the audience's defences are a variable, not a constant, and that a critique graded by vulnerability is empirically respectable while a critique of the universal dupe is not. What no experiment shows is the abolition of wanting, of status competition, of the meanings of goods or of the fetishism that attaches to them, since the subtracted environments went on consuming, comparing and displaying with undiminished energy by every account their observers filed. Subtraction confirms the separation from the negative side: remove the industry and you change the recruitment into habits, the texture of public space and the protection of the unarmoured, while the deeper structures the first row describes continue on schedule, which is either a counsel of despair or a precise map of where reform can bite, depending on the reader's politics, and the article's discipline is to hand over the map without confiscating the politics.

## Selling costs and competition: the economists' quarrel the Marxists inherited

Long before advertising became a Marxist theme it was a scandal inside economics itself, and the Marxist critiques inherited positions from a quarrel the economists had been conducting in their own vocabulary. Edward Chamberlin's theory of monopolistic competition, published in 1933, gave the scandal its technical name by distinguishing production costs, which make a product, from selling costs, which make a market for it, and by treating the second category as a systematic feature of economies where products are differentiated and sellers are many. Nicholas Kaldor, writing in 1950, pressed the critical side of the case: advertising, he argued, tends to advantage the large firm, raises the height of the fence a new entrant must climb, and carries the scale economies of attention into industries whose production has no such economies, so that promotion becomes an engine of concentration independent of manufacturing efficiency. The Chicago tradition replied in kind. Stigler's information economics recast advertising as the buyer's servant, and later work in the same vein treated heavy promotion as the challenger brand's weapon against entrenched distribution and sleepy incumbents, pointing to industries where advertised entrants broke comfortable oligopolies that quiet competition had preserved for decades. The empirical literature that accumulated around the quarrel proved as graded as the advertising effects literature surveyed earlier: advertising intensity correlates with concentration in some sectors and with turbulence in others, and the same expenditure reads as information, signal, barrier or waste depending on the market's structure and the observer's theory of what competition is for.

The Marxist contribution to this quarrel is not a fifth position on the same axis but a change of axis, and the change is worth stating exactly because it shows the separated critiques doing work no economist's category does. Where the economists ask whether advertising serves or subverts competition, the macroeconomic row asks what the aggregate of selling costs reveals about an economy that must spend them, and the structural row asks why the objects of competition arrive in the market already dressed in meanings that hide their origins. Both questions survive the economists' quarrel whatever its outcome. If Kaldor is right and promotion concentrates industry, the sales effort grows with monopoly as Baran and Sweezy require. If the Chicago school is right and promotion is the entrant's ladder, the sales effort is a standing cost of the competitive process itself, and the question of what share of society's labour should be devoted to mutual persuasion among sellers remains exactly as pressing, since a ladder every entrant must climb is a toll every consumer finally pays. The separation thus protects the Marxist argument from a dependence it is often accused of: it does not need advertising to be a monopoly plot, a waste in the accountant's sense or a failure of competition policy. It needs only the observations that selling costs are enormous, that they are governed by rivalry and absorption rather than by the value of information conveyed, and that the meanings they trade in are drawn from a reservoir the commodity form supplies. Those observations stand on both sides of the economists' quarrel, which is the mark of a structural argument as opposed to a brief for one faction of economists.

## Needs once more: Heller, adaptive preferences and the limits of repair

Because the needs criterion gap is this article's named claim, fairness requires a fuller hearing for the most serious attempts to close it, including two that come from outside the advertising literature and one from inside Marxism itself. The Marxist attempt of greatest sophistication is Agnes Heller's theory of needs, developed from the manuscripts of the young Marx in her work of the 1970s. Marx's own scattered remarks distinguish, without ever systematising, between the needs of survival, the historically produced needs that each epoch's development makes ordinary, and the rich needs of the developed individual, whose satisfaction is the expansion of the senses and capacities themselves. Heller built the distinction into an ethic: needs are open to criticism when their satisfaction requires the treatment of other persons as mere means, when they are organised so that some can satisfy theirs only by frustrating the equivalent needs of others, or when they are quantitative addictions, wants whose structure is mere accumulation without qualitative development. The criterion has real discriminating power. It condemns the want whose satisfaction is positional domination while acquitting the want for music, tools, travel and adornment that the condescending critic had swept into the same bin. Yet even sympathetic readers notice the criterion's centre of gravity shifting as it is applied: judgements about which satisfactions develop the senses and which merely repeat them must still be made case by case by someone, and the someone, once again, is the theorist with a theory of the human essence in his briefcase. Heller's repair is the best the tradition has produced, and its residue of judgement is the gap in its narrowest and most honest form, reduced from a missing tribunal to an unavoidable, arguable discretion.

Philosophy outside Marxism reached a parallel position through the study of adaptive preferences, and the convergence is evidence that the difficulty is real rather than a local failure of Marxist ingenuity. Jon Elster's analysis of sour grapes showed that preferences adapt to what is available, so that the contentment of the deprived cannot certify the adequacy of their condition, while Amartya Sen's capability approach rebuilt welfare economics around what people are able to do and be rather than around the satisfaction of whatever wants they happen to report, partly to escape exactly the trap in which the well manipulated subject reports bliss. The capability criterion is the tribunal problem answered with a list, of functionings a society should guarantee the real opportunity to achieve, and its defenders present the list as the object of public reasoning rather than as a theorist's decree. A Marxist can borrow the move, and some have: the critique of advertising then claims not that wants are false but that a fraction of the social surplus spent manufacturing urgency for the purchasable might, under other arrangements, underwrite capabilities the market leaves unbuilt, and the claim is comparative and institutional rather than psychological. Notice, however, what the borrowing costs. The argument that survives is no longer a critique of consumers' desires at all; it is an argument about public priorities that happens to pass through the advertising budget, and Marcuse's thunder about the falsity of the wants themselves has been honourably discharged rather than vindicated. That is the pattern this article has reported throughout: every successful repair of the psychological critique converts it into one of the other three, and the conversion, repeated across seventy years by Galbraith, by the capabilities literature and by the tradition's own best theorist of needs, is the strongest confirmation available that the gap is a property of the argument and not of its critics' imagination.

## One good, followed whole: what a biography of a trainer can and cannot show

Method in this field improves when abstractions are forced to walk through a single case, so consider, as a composite drawn from the documented features of the trade rather than from any single brand's archive, the biography of an athletic trainer. The object begins as materials and labour distributed across continents: synthetic uppers and rubber compounds from petrochemical supply chains, assembly in factories whose wages and conditions have been the subject of sustained journalistic and campaign scrutiny since the 1990s, logistics that move the finished pair through container ports to regional warehouses. At the point of production the pair is worth what its materials, machinery time and labour make it worth, and the workers who assembled it could not, on their wages, be its intended customers. The object then crosses the threshold where the second economy of the good begins. Design teams refine its silhouette as a sign; marketers attach it to athletes whose bodies supply the referent system of excellence and striving; limited editions ration its availability so that scarcity itself becomes part of the message; the retail environment stages it under light like sculpture. The price the final buyer pays divides, in proportions that vary by brand and season, among production, freight, rent, retail margin, tax and the substantial share that recovers the cost of the sign's manufacture and pays its owners for its use. Every stage of the biography is a fact of a different discipline, and the separated critiques each claim their stage. Fetishism names the threshold at which the factory disappears and the aura condenses. Semiotics reads the staging. The sales effort counts the share of the price that selling absorbs. The sovereignty defence speaks for the buyer, who may know all of this, value the object as footwear and emblem together, and buy with open eyes.

The biography also shows what no stage can claim. It cannot show the buyer deceived, since the buyer's reasons, durability, fit, beauty, allegiance, the pleasure of the emblem among friends, are his own and are frequently articulated with more precision than the critic's account of them. It cannot show the price as mere illusion, since the sign is a real social fact whose possession really does communicate what it promises to communicate; the aura is false only about the factory, not about the street. And it cannot show the arrangement as anyone's design, since the factory's location, the athlete's charisma, the rationing of editions and the buyer's longing were decided by different actors answering different incentives, and the biography's coherence is the coherence of a system, not of a plot. A critique that has followed one good whole has thereby rehearsed the entire argument of this article in miniature, which is why the biography exercise is recommended in the teacher layer that follows, and why its lesson is the separation itself: each critique is true of its stage, false when imperial over the others, and silent, as every honest instrument is silent, outside the range it was built to measure.

## The global circuit: cultural imperialism and its critics

The critique of advertising acquired a geopolitical wing in the second half of the twentieth century, and its fate is a final test of the separation because the wing's strongest and weakest claims travelled together for decades. Herbert Schiller, the American scholar who led the school, argued from the 1970s that the export of commercial media and advertising by the dominant economies constituted a cultural imperialism: the advertising financed media system was itself the trojan structure, since a country that adopted advertiser supported broadcasting imported not merely programmes but the institutional priority of the consumer over the citizen, and the flood of branded goods and their promotion carried the consumption default into societies whose own patterns of provision and prestige it then corroded. The school documented real asymmetries, in the flow of programming, in the ownership of agencies, in the dependence of poorer media systems on richer ones for content and technique, and its analyses of the New World Information and Communication Order debates of the period remain the record of a genuine grievance conducted in the language of political economy. Its weakness appeared where the psychological row always weakens the tradition, at the audience. Later reception research, conducted in the societies receiving the exports, repeatedly found viewers who read imported programmes against the grain of their makers, who preferred local production whenever it existed at comparable craft, who hybridised the imported forms into national genres, the telenovela being the standing example of a form that conquered its exporter, and the school's picture of cultural syringes emptying into passive populations required the same revision downward that the domestic evidence had imposed on Marcuse.

The global circuit also returned the macroeconomic row with fresh evidence, since the clearest durable effect of the advertising system's export was institutional rather than psychological: media systems reorganised around the audience commodity, public service broadcasters pressed to imitate commercial rivals, and local producers of goods facing the entrant's toll Kaldor described, in the form of global brands whose promotion budgets no domestic manufacturer could match. Whether that toll is best described as imperialism or as the ordinary operation of scale in an integrated market remains contested, and the contest is partly terminological, since the facts of asymmetry are agreed by most parties while their moral name is not. The separated method files the dispute where it belongs, in the macroeconomic row's vulnerability column, as a question about the historical fit of categories, and it files the school's audience claims in the psychological row, where the record has not been kind. What survives, once again, is the discipline of the separation: a school that had kept its rows apart would have lost less credit when its weakest row was audited, and the general reader of any era's critique, including the reader of this article, does well to perform the separation on every bundled indictment that arrives claiming the whole truth about a poster, a platform or a pair of trainers.

## Advertising enters politics: selling candidates and the public sphere

No account of advertising and ideology is complete without the migration of the craft into political communication, since the migration tests whether the separated critiques describe a commercial peculiarity or a general logic of persuasion under mass democracy. The migration is old and documented. Parties employed the agencies' techniques between the wars; the American presidential campaign of 1952 brought the spot advertisement to television politics, with a Madison Avenue executive devising jingles and short appeals for a general turned candidate; the celebrated Daisy advertisement of 1964, aired once as a paid spot and endlessly as news, demonstrated that a political message could work entirely by association and implication, never naming the policy it existed to frighten voters about. Jürgen Habermas, in his account of the structural transformation of the public sphere published in 1962, gave the migration its theoretical frame: a sphere once constituted, in ideal, by private persons reasoning about public matters had been refeudalised, his deliberately archaic word, by the staging of publicity, as public relations and commercial media converted citizens into audiences and debate into managed display. The frame belongs in this article because it shares the separated method's instincts. Habermas did not claim voters were dupes; he claimed the institutional setting of their judgement had been rebuilt around the techniques of selling, which is a structural argument about venues, not a psychological argument about skulls.

The political case also disciplines the critique in a way the commercial case sometimes escapes, because politics supplies something close to a measurement: elections are counted. Decades of research on campaign effects converge on a pattern familiar from the commercial evidence, in which advertising and campaign communication mostly reinforce prior dispositions, mobilise the sympathetic and decide outcomes at the margins among the undecided, with the largest effects where information is genuinely new or the contest is obscure and low in prior commitment. Margins, of course, are where elections are decided, so modest average effects are compatible with enormous political consequence, and the same is true commercially, where the margin is market share and the share is profit. The parallel is the deepest available version of the article's central lesson. Persuasion industries do not need to be omnipotent to be important; they need only to be systematic, well financed and permanently at work on the margins of millions of decisions, while their opponents, where opposition exists, are intermittent and poor. A critique that begins by exaggerating the industry's power ends by being unable to explain its importance, since omnipotence contradicted by every failure leaves nothing to analyse. A critique that locates the importance correctly, in the asymmetry of presence rather than in hypnosis, can explain both the failures and the fear the industry inspires. The democratic worry, soberly stated, is not that citizens believe advertisements. It is that the means of addressing citizens at scale are for hire, that the hire is priced beyond the reach of most of what citizens might organise to say, and that the resulting imbalance is a property of the market in attention rather than of any campaign's dishonesty. That worry belongs to the structural row, requires no criterion of true interests to state, and is the point at which the critique of advertising joins the wider Marxist analysis of formal equality and material inequality that runs through the whole series.

## The attention economy: a rival formulation weighed

A vocabulary younger than the critiques has come to compete with them, and the separated method should be applied to the newcomer before the verdict is returned. The attention economy formulation, anticipated by Herbert Simon's observation in 1971 that a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention, and developed by writers such as Michael Goldhaber in the 1990s and by theorists of cognitive capitalism thereafter, proposes that attention is the scarce currency of the digital order, the thing platforms harvest, advertisers buy and users spend. The formulation has genuine descriptive charm. It names the experience of the feed, the notification and the metric dashboard. From a Marxist standpoint, however, its charm conceals a category question that decides whether it is an analysis or a metaphor. Attention is not produced as a commodity is produced, by labour applied to materials under relations of production that can be located and owned. What the platforms sell to advertisers is not attention in the raw but access, prediction and the opportunity to intervene, assembled from the audience's activity and the platform's infrastructure. To call attention a currency is to repeat, at the level of theory, the fetishism the first row describes: a social relation, between audiences, platforms and advertisers, is made to appear as a substance flowing between heads. The older audience commodity argument covers the same ground with the relations left visible, which is why this article has preferred Smythe's vocabulary to the newer one while granting the newer one's phenomenological truth. People do experience their attention as spent, taxed and stolen, and an analysis that dismisses the experience because its theory is imprecise repeats the condescension error in a scholarly register.

The attention formulation also reopens, in fresh language, the oldest dispute in this article, and watching it do so is the final confirmation of the taxonomy's durability. Claims that platforms engineer compulsion, that design teams optimise for the capture of minds against their owners' interests, are the psychological critique rebuilt with better documentation, since the engineers' own writings, metrics and internal presentations are sometimes available to auditors, and the documented case method this article has defended throughout applies to them with unusual force. Claims that the harvest of behavioural data constitutes a new mode of production are the macroeconomic row attempting an upgrade, and they stand or fall on the contestable classifications that row always carries. Claims that the interface's presentation of surveillance as personalisation is a mystification are the structural row in new dress and are exactly as strong as the old dress was. Nothing in the digital transformation has produced a fifth critique, and nothing in it has closed the needs criterion gap, since the question whether the captured user's wants are his own awaits the same criterion in the age of the algorithm that it awaited in the age of the poster. The taxonomy thus ends the article's survey where it began: the value of the separation is not that it flatters a tradition but that new phenomena keep arriving pre sorted by it, and the analyst equipped with the distinctions can begin work on a platform, a campaign or a format immediately, asking of each claim the only question that has ever mattered in this field, which row it belongs to and what evidence that row requires.

## The special case of children: where the defences run out

Children are the exception that tests every generalisation in this article, and the separated method's willingness to treat them as a special case is a mark in its favour, since a theory of advertising that cannot distinguish a seven year old from a forty year old has failed descriptively before it begins. The developmental evidence is not controversial in outline. Persuasion knowledge, the lay understanding that a message has been designed to sell and that its claims should be discounted accordingly, assembles itself gradually through childhood, and the younger child characteristically reads the advertisement as information or entertainment rather than as an interested argument, unable to hold the seller's intention in mind while processing the seller's content. The industry has always known its side of this asymmetry. Its own literature discusses the influence children exert over household purchases far beyond the goods bought for them directly, and the trade's candid term for the child's pressure on the parent, and for the parent's eventual surrender, entered the research vocabulary because agencies studied and priced the phenomenon. The regulatory history follows the asymmetry with unusual clarity. The American attempt of the late 1970s to restrict television advertising addressed to young children, the Federal Trade Commission's children's advertising proceeding opened in 1978, was defeated after intense industry lobbying and a congressional intervention that also curtailed the agency's powers, a sequence historians of regulation treat as a landmark in the politics of consumer protection. Other jurisdictions drew the line and kept it, as the Quebec prohibition described earlier attests, and broadcasters' rules in several countries fence the child's viewing hours to this day.

The case matters theoretically because it isolates the one audience for which the sovereignty defence is structurally unavailable. The child cannot be presumed to discount, to compare, to defer or to detect the transfer mechanism, and the liberal presumption that expressed wants settle the question fails for the same reason it fails in contract law, where the child's signature binds no one. Even here, however, the separated critique keeps its discipline. What the children's case establishes is a claim about a defined population, a defined incapacity and a defined set of practices, which regulators can address with defined remedies, age fences, separation of programme and promotion, prohibitions on direct exhortation. It does not establish that the adult population stands in the child's position, and critics who borrow the child's case to describe the citizen commit the inflation this article has policed throughout. The gradient is the finding: defences assemble with development, vulnerability concentrates where they are absent, and a critique graded by audience is both more humane and more accurate than one that must choose between the universal dupe and the universal sovereign. Marcuse's error, seen from this vantage, was not malice or elitism but overgeneralisation, the extension to all consumers of a relation that genuinely holds at the nursery door, and the needs criterion gap is what the overgeneralisation needed and never found to justify the extension.

## Gender, bodies and the imagery of lack

The representation of women and of the body is the terrain on which the semiotic critique won its widest public, and an exposition that omitted it would fail the query record this article serves. The feminist analysis of advertising, developed from the 1970s in work that includes Williamson's own and the documentary studies of Jean Kilbourne in the United States, catalogued the recurring grammar with which women's bodies were used to sell to everyone: the fragmented body presented in parts, the equation of worth with a narrow and retouched physical ideal, the depiction of women as decorative adjuncts to products from cars to power tools, and the address to female readers as managers of a permanent bodily emergency, in which every feature is a problem awaiting a purchased solution. The strongest form of this critique is semiotic and documentary at once. It does not require the reader to believe that any individual woman has been hypnotised by a lipstick advertisement. It requires only that the accumulated imagery be read as a public environment, a standing lesson in what a body is for and what it lacks, repeated at a scale no countervailing institution matches, and it asks who pays for the lesson's delivery and whose goods the lesson sells. Kilbourne's method, accumulating instances across decades until the pattern convicts itself, is the case method this article has defended, applied to representation rather than to wants, and its findings about the narrowness of the ideal and the rhetoric of lack have been absorbed, at least as cautions, even by much of the industry's own training literature.

The defence enters its strongest counter here, and the verdict must register it. Imagery follows and flatters its audience as well as instructing it; the women's magazines that carried the emergency rhetoric were bought willingly by readers who used them as resources for their own projects of presentation, skill and pleasure; and the same commercial system that narrowed the ideal also, across the decades, widened it under pressure from the audiences and models it had excluded, since a market in signs can sell inclusion as readily as exclusion once the excluded have purchasing power and organisation. The pattern of concession and absorption is the familiar one from the rebellion section: critique changes the imagery, the imagery's economics change more slowly, and the argument between the readings is an argument about which level, text or structure, deserves the emphasis. What the gender case adds to the article's taxonomy is the clearest demonstration that the semiotic row can move the world without touching the criterion problem. Regulators in several countries now police the retouching of bodies in advertisements and the use of underweight models, not because a tribunal declared the audience's desires false, but because a defined representational practice was shown, case by case, to carry costs its defenders could not answer. The critique that won was the critique that stayed in its row.

## Hayek's rebuttal: the sharpest answer the tradition has received

Every argument in this field should be made to face its ablest opponent, and the ablest opponent of the manufactured wants thesis remains Friedrich Hayek, whose short essay of 1961 answered Galbraith's dependence effect directly and whose reasoning reaches Marcuse as well. Hayek's move was to widen the frame until the dependence effect lost its power to discriminate. Almost every want beyond the barest requirements of subsistence, he observed, is acquired rather than innate, and the instruments of its acquisition are the whole of civilisation: literature creates the want for literature in those who would never miss it unexposed, music creates the taste by which music is enjoyed, painting, cuisine, manners and philosophy all form the desires they satisfy, and nobody supposes that the novelist has manipulated his reader into a false need for novels merely because the need arrived through the work itself. If dependence on the producing system invalidated a want, then every cultivated want stands invalidated together, and the critic who wields the argument against the advertisement for soap must, in consistency, wield it against the teacher who creates the desire for history and the friend who creates the desire for conversation. The argument that proves everything proves nothing, and the dependence effect, wielded as a criterion, is exactly such an argument. Galbraith's own escape, that he aimed at the social balance rather than at individual wants, concedes Hayek's point in practice, since the social balance argument is the macroeconomic one and needs no doctrine of manufactured desire.

The rebuttal deserves its place as the defence's intellectual summit because it does not deny a single fact the critics assembled. Bernays still boasted, the motivational researchers still probed, the campaigns still borrowed emancipation to sell cigarettes. Hayek's point is about inference: from the social formation of wants, nothing follows about their falsity, because social formation is the universal condition of wanting in a civilised species, and a criterion that would separate the advertisement's formations from the library's has not been produced by the critics who need it. A Marxist reply exists, and the separated method can state it at its true, reduced strength. The reply is that the formations differ in their governor: the library's formation of taste is not systematically subordinated to the requirement that value be realised at a profit, and the advertisement's is, so that the asymmetry worth criticising is not between formed and unformed wants but between formation in the service of the formed person's development and formation in the service of a balance sheet. That reply is real, and versions of it animate the best of the tradition, from Williams's closing hopes to Heller's ethics of need. But notice its destination. It is an argument about the purposes institutions serve and the surplus they command, which is to say it has migrated, once again, into the structural and macroeconomic rows, leaving the psychological row in possession of the field it can actually hold: documented cases, vulnerable audiences, and a permanent, honourable question mark over the wants of everyone else. Hayek, the tradition's opponent, thus performs the same service as the taxonomy, forcing the critique back onto the ground where it is strong, and an exposition that grants him the victory he earned, on the point he attacked, has not weakened its case. It has located it.

## A note on method: the advertisement read against its archive

One methodological upgrade separates the criticism of the last decades from the polemics of the first, and it deserves recording before the verdict because it shows the separated critiques acquiring a shared evidentiary instrument. The upgrade is the archive. Advertising, alone among ideological formations, documents itself compulsively: agencies keep strategy papers, research reports, storyboards, testing results and post campaign evaluations, and litigation, regulation and corporate succession periodically force those papers into the open. The tobacco industry's internal documents, released through litigation in the 1990s and now housed in public repositories, supplied the model instance, since they allowed historians to compare, for the same campaigns, the public text, the intended audience, the internal description of that audience's vulnerabilities and the private assessment of what the promotion was expected to do. Where the three records coincide, the critic's case is documentary and does not depend on decoding alone; where they diverge, the divergence is itself the finding, since an industry that privately describes its persuasion in modest terms while publicly selling omnipotence to clients is practising its craft on its own customers. The archive method generalises beyond tobacco. Freedom of information releases, competition inquiries, platform transparency reports and the memoirs of practitioners all feed it, and each feed disciplines a different row: strategy papers grade the psychological claims, expenditure ledgers grade the macroeconomic ones, and the drafts and rejected executions of campaigns, in which alternative meanings were weighed and discarded, give the semiotic reading a control group it never had when the finished text was all that survived. A criticism equipped with the archive no longer needs to choose between denouncing the advertisement and believing it. It can show its working, and an argument that shows its working can survive the discovery that part of it was wrong, which is the final difference between the analysis this article has attempted and the mood it set out to replace.

## Verdict: what survives the separation

The closing assessment can now be rendered as a set of graded judgements rather than a slogan, and the grading is the article's answer to the question its title poses. Advertising is an ideological practice in the structural sense, but the ideology does not reside where the popular critique puts it. It resides first in the commodity form itself, which no advertiser created and no advertising ban would abolish, and which guarantees that goods will present human relations as their own magical properties. It resides second in the texts, where determinate meanings are transferred to products by a craft that can be read, taught and audited, and whose characteristic exclusion, the labour and conditions behind the goods, aligns the advertisement's silence with the fetishism it exploits. It resides third in the economy's disposition of resources, where the sales effort absorbs a substantial share of the social surplus in the work of selling, a fact about the system's metabolism that survives every sceptical finding about persuasion. It resides least securely, and often not at all, in the souls of consumers, whose wants are formed socially as all wants are, sometimes engineered in documented cases, frequently defended with skill, and nowhere shown to be false by a criterion the tradition has been seeking for seventy years without success. The reader who wants a single sentence can have this one: the strongest Marxist critique of advertising is the one that says least about the gullibility of shoppers and most about the structure that makes meaning a department of selling, and the weakest is the one that appoints the critic the judge of other people's needs.

Three practical corollaries follow for the audiences this series serves, before the persona layers develop them. For anyone analysing an advertisement, the separated method is a checklist disguised as a warning: establish which of the four critiques a given claim belongs to before assessing it, and demand the evidence appropriate to that row, textual for the semiotic, accounting for the macroeconomic, documentary for the psychological, and nothing but the commodity form itself for the structural. For anyone teaching the subject, the taxonomy is a gift, since it converts a shouting match between dupes and sovereigns into four examinable arguments of unequal strength, and the weakest of the four is the most instructive, because its missing criterion teaches students what a completed argument would have to contain. For anyone reporting on the industry, the discipline is a defence against the two standing temptations of the beat, credulity toward the industry's own account of its creativity and credulity toward the critique's account of its power, both of which the evidence supports only in graded, specific and unglamorous forms. The magic system, Williams saw, is not dispelled by denunciation, because it feeds on real needs and real structures. It is dispelled, in the study if not yet in the street, by the slower work of distinction: saying exactly what is claimed, by whom the claiming is done, and what would have to be true for the claim to fail. That work, performed on advertising, is ideology critique in the only sense this series finally endorses, and the persona sections and the questions that follow equip the reader to continue it.

## For students and exam candidates

The single most valuable habit this topic offers an examination candidate is the separation itself, used as an essay architecture. A question on advertising and ideology invites the bundled answer, in which fetishism, manipulation and false needs blur into one denunciation, and markers reward the candidate who refuses the bundle. Open with the taxonomy: four critiques, four evidence bases, four degrees of strength. Devote the body to the two strongest, the structural argument from the commodity form and the macroeconomic argument from the sales effort, reading one advertisement semiotically as a worked specimen rather than gesturing at advertisements in general. Reserve the psychological critique for the evaluation, where the needs criterion gap becomes your highest value move: state Marcuse fairly, pose the question he could not answer, show the attempted repairs failing or converting into other arguments, and let your verdict grade the four claims instead of cheering for a side. Candidates lose marks here in predictable ways, by asserting effects the evidence does not support, by treating consumers as dupes against the record, and by quoting the magic system phrase without Williams's inversion about materialism, which is the detail that shows a marker the essay was read rather than remembered. Define every term before using it, attribute every argument to its author and date, and conclude with a graded judgement rather than a slogan.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The classroom exercise this topic was built for is a slow decoding, and it works because the semiotic method gives every student the same object and a procedure for disagreeing about it productively. Place one advertisement before the class, print or screen, and withhold commentary while students inventory its elements: image, copy, typography, the product's placement, the world the scene presupposes. Then run Williamson's sequence in the open, identifying the referent system, tracing the transfer, and listing the exclusions, asking at each stage what had to be left out for the association to hold, whose labour, which costs, what consequences. Only after the decoding is complete should the theoretical menu be introduced, at which point the four critiques can be distributed among groups, each required to state its claim about this same advertisement, name the evidence that would test it, and concede its vulnerability column without prompting. The discussion that follows teaches itself, because the groups discover that their claims do not compete on one field. Guard the exercise against its two failure modes: the session that becomes a competition in denunciation, where the teacher's politics mark the answers, and the session that becomes a celebration of cleverness, where decoding the trick is mistaken for escaping the structure. The taxonomy table is the handout that prevents both.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Reporting and researching this subject imposes a discipline of attribution, because almost every sentence in the field conceals a choice among the four critiques. Before citing any claim about advertising's power, establish which row it belongs to and demand that row's evidence: textual analysis for semiotic claims, accounts and expenditure series for macroeconomic ones, campaign documents and experiments for psychological ones, and nothing empirical at all for the structural claim, which is a thesis about the commodity form and is embarrassed, not supported, by being dressed in audience statistics. Treat industry sources as interested and critical sources as interested, and note that the trade's own estimates of its effectiveness are sales documents addressed to clients. Keep the case archive separate from the general theory: Bernays, the tobacco promotions and the platform targeting scandals convict as cases, and a report that promotes them into a theory of the audience has overreached in the manner this article anatomised. Historians will find the industry's internal literature, agency archives, trade press and the motivational researchers' papers, richer than the polemics written about them. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does Marxism analyse advertising?

Marxist analysis separates what popular criticism bundles. It distinguishes commodity fetishism, a structural appearance arising from commodity production itself, from the deliberate craft of advertising, which it studies semiotically in texts and historically in the industry's archives. It adds a macroeconomic account of the sales effort, in which advertising absorbs part of the social surplus, and it examines, sceptically, the claim that advertising manufactures false needs. Each claim is graded against the evidence appropriate to it, and the tradition's strongest conclusions concern structure and texts rather than the supposed gullibility of audiences.

### Q: Is advertising the same as commodity fetishism?

No, and the distinction is foundational. Commodity fetishism is the appearance, generated by commodity production itself, in which social relations between producers present themselves as properties of things; it has no author, no intention and no budget, and it would persist in full if every advertisement vanished. Advertising is a deliberate industry that attaches chosen meanings to goods for competitive purposes. The industry exploits the ground fetishism prepares, since commodities already carry condensed social meanings, but exploiting a structure and causing it are different relations, and confusing them flatters copywriters with a metaphysical power they do not possess.

### Q: What is the magic system argument?

Raymond Williams argued in his essay of 1960 that advertising operates as a magic system: it promises that objects will deliver satisfactions, love, status, youth, belonging, that the objects cannot in fact supply, much as ritual magic promises goods by means that cannot causally produce them. The magic persists because the needs invoked are genuine and chronically unmet by other means. Williams added the inversion for which the essay is remembered: a society governed by such magic is not too materialist but not materialist enough, since it teaches people to slight objects as objects and prize them as tokens of meanings instead.

### Q: Does advertising create false needs?

The honest answer is that the claim cannot be completed in its classic form. Marcuse argued that advanced industrial society manufactures needs whose satisfaction reconciles people to the order that produces them. The argument requires a criterion distinguishing true needs from false ones that does not simply override what people report wanting, and no such criterion has been supplied in seventy years, a defect this article names the needs criterion gap. Documented cases exist of campaigns deliberately cultivating insecurity, and wants are plainly formed by the market among other institutions. A general doctrine of false needs remains unproven.

### Q: Are consumers passive victims of advertising?

The evidence says no. Audiences display persuasion knowledge from a young age, decode selling intentions quickly, deploy scepticism, irony and parody as ordinary equipment, adopt skipping and blocking technologies eagerly, and adjudicate the industry's expensive failures by refusing to buy. Campaigns collapse constantly despite lavish budgets. None of this acquits the system at other levels: scepticism toward messages is compatible with accommodation to the environment of credit, fashion cycles and engineered obsolescence in which the messages sit, and vulnerability is unevenly distributed, with children and people at moments of insecurity far less armoured than the median adult shopper.

### Q: Why does capitalism need advertising at all?

Because generalised commodity production on a large scale must solve problems that selling then manages. Output must be realised as value through sale, rivals must be answered when products converge in function, new goods must be announced, and, in the account of Baran and Sweezy, a surplus generating economy must absorb output through an expanding sales effort of which advertising is the emblematic department. The industry's persistence through every wave of doubt about its persuasive power is itself evidence for this structural answer: firms buy advertising because unilateral silence is punished by competitors, whatever any single campaign achieves.

### Q: Is consumerism an ideology?

Only in a carefully specified sense. As a moral sneer at shoppers, the word is not an analysis, and as a doctrine nobody holds it in catechism form. What qualifies as ideology in the structural sense is the consumption default: the unargued public framing in which purchasable satisfaction is the visible shape of the good life, while satisfactions without buyers go unpictured. That framing misdescribes the relation between income and satisfaction and between production and consumption, and advertising is its most concentrated professional expression. The stronger claim, that individual consumers' wants are themselves false, requires a criterion that has never been supplied.

### Q: How does branding create value?

Strictly, in the labour theory's terms, branding does not create value in production; the sign is not a machine and makes nothing. What branding does is organise the realisation and distribution of value. A protected name and its accumulated associations steer demand toward one capital and away from rivals, stabilise that steering over time, support a price premium over functionally similar goods, and convert recognition into a balance sheet asset that can be sold, licensed or franchised. Branding is therefore best analysed as a technology of appropriation built upon production, which explains its luxuriance wherever products converge and the sign must carry the difference.

### Q: Is influencer marketing different in kind?

In its semiotic mechanism, no: meanings are transferred by juxtaposition from a borrowed referent system to a product, exactly as Judith Williamson described for print, except that the borrowed domain is a person's presented life and the concealment of the transfer runs deeper because promotion and content share one voice. In its political economy, the differences are real. The creator performs continuous labour producing a saleable self while depending on platforms that own distribution and set the terms of visibility, and the audience's attention and data are measured and sold with an intimacy broadcast never achieved. Different arrangement, same taxonomy.

### Q: Is surveillance capitalism a Marxist concept?

It is a neighbouring concept in disputed relation to Marxism. Shoshana Zuboff's account describes an economic order extracting behavioural surplus from users and refining it into prediction products sold to those who would shape behaviour. Many Marxist writers accept the phenomena while contesting the theory, arguing that the platforms behave as competing capitals seeking monopoly rent and advertising revenue, that data extraction is an intensification of accumulation rather than a new logic, and that presenting it as a rogue deviation flatters the ordinary capitalism it supposedly departs from. The dispute is substantive, about mechanisms, and honest exposition reports it as one.

### Q: How does advertising sell a way of life rather than only a product?

By the transfer mechanism the semiotic critique describes. The advertisement places the product inside a depicted world, a kitchen at a family hour, a coastline at dawn, an atelier of admired craftsmen, and the audience's existing feelings about that world migrate to the object placed within it. The product is rarely argued for; the life is shown, and the object appears as its natural equipment, the one element of the scene that happens to be for sale. Because the transfer is performed by the viewer's own knowledge, the way of life seems to emanate from the goods rather than from the art department that arranged the meeting.

### Q: What should a researcher separate when testing whether advertising creates needs?

Four separations, at minimum. Separate the claim about texts from the claim about minds, since decoding a message never measures an effect. Separate brand switching within a category from the creation of new wants, since the evidence for the first is strong and for the second weak. Separate documented cases, where internal campaign records show intentions to cultivate insecurity, from the general doctrine of false needs, which no accumulation of cases by itself completes. And separate the origin of a want from its legitimacy, since socially formed wants include unobjectionable ones and spontaneous wants include dubious ones.

### Q: Why does the false needs claim need a criterion it rarely states?

Because without a criterion the claim is unfalsifiable and authoritarian in the same motion. If the theorist may declare any expressed want false whenever it suits the theory, then the testimony of the people whose liberation is the theory's stated aim counts for nothing, and the distinction between manipulation and ordinary social formation of taste collapses. Candidate criteria, biological, historical, procedural, fail in instructive ways: they go silent on contested ground, argue in circles, or appoint a judge. Naming the missing criterion, the needs criterion gap, does not refute the suspicion that wants are engineered; it specifies what a completed proof would have to contain.

### Q: How do brands borrow the language of rebellion for commodities?

By treating dissent as a referent system like any other. Campaigns documented across decades have dressed soft drinks in the iconography of generational unrest, cast a computer buyer as the heroine smashing a totalitarian screen, and built sportswear identities on defiance and on athletes known for public stands. The borrowing works because the audience already honours the values being borrowed; advertising follows the culture's commitments at least as faithfully as it leads them. The practice demonstrates the commodity form's hospitality to any content that can be attached to a saleable object, and poses the open political question whether rebellion sold as style retains its force as practice.

### Q: What separates an ideology critique of advertising from dislike of consumer taste?

Method, evidence and the location of the charge. Dislike of taste judges shoppers and their pleasures against the critic's own basket, and needs no argument beyond distaste. An ideology critique specifies a mechanism, structural appearance, textual transfer, surplus absorption or documented targeting, states the evidence appropriate to that mechanism, grades its claim to that evidence, and directs its charge at arrangements rather than at the people living inside them. It can grant every pleasure in goods as genuine and still sustain its case, and it accepts in advance, through the needs criterion gap, the exact point where its own argument remains unfinished.
