The basic structure doctrine is the closest thing the Indian Constitution has to a second founding. On 24 April 1973, in Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala, a thirteen-judge bench of the Supreme Court held by the narrowest of margins that Parliament's power to amend the Constitution under Article 368 does not extend to destroying or damaging the Constitution's basic structure, the load-bearing frame of features without which the document would cease to be itself. Every account of Indian constitutional politics must pass through that verdict, and almost every account passes through it too quickly, treating the doctrine either as one entry in a line of amendment cases or as a trophy in an institutional contest between court and Parliament. This article defends a larger claim. Measured by the architecture it created and the half-century of constitutional argument it has organised, Kesavananda is the most consequential constitutional moment in India after the founding settlement of 1947 to 1950 itself, the settlement whose internal tensions the series has traced from [the founding three-tensions framework](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) onward. The doctrine matters not because judges won an argument in 1973, but because the verdict built a permanent structure of protection, review and tension inside which every later argument about amending the Constitution has had to be fought.

## Two Framings of the Basic Structure Doctrine, and a Third

Two established framings dominate the literature, and each rests on a genuine evidence base. The legal-doctrinal-history framing reads Kesavananda as a point on a curve of cases: Shankari Prasad in 1951, Sajjan Singh in 1965, Golaknath in 1967, the Twenty-Fourth and Twenty-Fifth Amendments of 1971, Kesavananda itself in 1973, and then the line of applications running through Minerva Mills in 1980, Waman Rao in 1981, I. R. Coelho in 2007 and the National Judicial Appointments Commission judgment of 2015. On this reading, the doctrine is a rule produced by litigation, intelligible through the reasoning of the judgments and the sequence of bench compositions that delivered them. The framing is careful, text-bound and indispensable, and Granville Austin's Working a Democratic Constitution (1999) shows how much narrative power it can carry when the case law is set inside the political history of the republic. What it cannot easily explain is why this one rule, among the thousands the Supreme Court has announced, reorganised the behaviour of governments, oppositions, commissions and litigants for five decades.

The judicial-supremacy framing starts from the opposite end. On this reading, Kesavananda is an act of institutional self-assertion: an unelected court, having lost the confrontation over property rights in the 1950s and 1960s, invented a limit on the elected Parliament's constituent power and appointed itself the limit's guardian. The framing has distinguished holders. H. M. Seervai, India's most formidable constitutional commentator of the twentieth century, turned against the verdict he had initially half-welcomed and attacked its reasoning in successive editions of his Constitutional Law of India. Senior advocate T. R. Andhyarujina, who had assisted the solicitor general's side of the Kesavananda courtroom, gave the institutional reading its most documented form in The Kesavananda Bharati Case (2011), an insider's account of a struggle for supremacy between the Supreme Court and Parliament. The political class supplied its own version: the verdict's opponents in 1973, and the authors of the Forty-Second Amendment in 1976, described the doctrine as a judicial veto on the people's representatives. This framing captures something real about motive and timing. What it cannot capture is content: it explains why a court might grasp such a power, but not why the power, once grasped, took the durable three-part shape it did, or why even governments commanding huge majorities have since argued inside the doctrine's categories rather than simply defying them.

The reading defended here begins from content rather than sequence or motive. The basic structure doctrine is a three-component construction. Its first component is protection beyond the text: the Constitution possesses a basic structure that the amending power cannot abrogate, although no clause of the document says so in terms. Its second component is judicial primacy in amendment adjudication: the courts, and finally the Supreme Court, decide whether a given amendment damages that structure, so an amendment carried by the special majorities of Article 368 can still fall. Its third component is sustained tension: the doctrine does not settle the contest between parliamentary sovereignty and constitutional supremacy but institutionalises it, so that each generation's amendment politics is conducted as an argument about where the boundary runs. A doctrine with those three components is not merely a rule or a power grab. It is a constitutional moment, a re-founding of the terms on which the founding settlement may be revised, and it operationalises, at the level of courts and amendments, the permanent tension between rights and development that runs through the entire Indian constitutional story.

The standard reference treatments illustrate the gap the moment reading fills. The encyclopaedia entries and the constitutional law textbooks alike organise the subject as a sequence: the amendment cases in chronological order, the lists of basic features reproduced from the opinions, the later applications appended as the line extends. Such treatments are accurate and inert. They can tell a reader what Sikri listed and what Coelho held, but they cannot explain why a proposition assembled from a seven-to-six split and a one-page summary came to organise the drafting practice of governments and the argumentative practice of courts for fifty years, or why the political class that denounced the doctrine in 1973 was, by 1978, restoring the Constitution around it rather than repealing it. Sequence is not consequence. The hub's claim begins where the chronologies stop: with the observation that Kesavananda changed what every other constitutional actor had to do next, and that a change of that kind, in a founding document's own amendment machinery, is a constitutional moment by any serviceable definition of the term.

### What exactly did the Kesavananda bench decide on 24 April 1973?

The thirteen judges held, by seven votes to six, that Parliament may amend any provision of the Constitution, including the fundamental rights, but may not alter the Constitution's basic structure or framework. The majority upheld the Twenty-Fourth Amendment in full and the Twenty-Fifth Amendment except for Article 31C's review-barring clause.

The bench also upheld the Twenty-Ninth Amendment, which had placed Kerala land reform statutes in the Ninth Schedule, so the verdict validated the bulk of the government's amendment programme even as it bounded the power behind it.

That holding was assembled from eleven separate opinions and compressed into a summary, headed as the view of the majority, which nine of the thirteen judges signed on the day of the verdict. The summary's first proposition recorded that Golaknath v State of Punjab stood overruled. Its second recorded that Article 368 does not enable Parliament to alter the basic structure or framework of the Constitution. Around those two sentences, the opinions disagreed about almost everything else: about which features counted as basic, about how much of Golaknath's reasoning survived, about the source of the limit in the word "amend" itself. The fragility of the construction is part of the record, and the pillar's next study examines the thirteen opinions one by one. For the hub's purpose, what matters is the proposition that nine signatures carried into constitutional history: the amending power is real, general and limited, and the limit is structural rather than textual.

The stakes of that proposition become clear against the sequence that produced it. Between 1951 and 1971, Parliament and the Supreme Court fought a twenty-year duel over a single question: could the constituent power of amendment reach the fundamental rights, and in particular the right to property, which stood in the path of land reform and nationalisation? The founding generation had itself reopened the question within months of the Constitution's commencement, when the Patna High Court's decision against the Bihar land reforms legislation pushed Jawaharlal Nehru's government to carry [the First Amendment of 1951](/2019/01/14/first-constitutional-amendment-1951/), which inserted Articles 31A and 31B and created the Ninth Schedule as a vault beyond judicial review. The duel that followed, case answering amendment and amendment answering case, is the genealogy the next section reconstructs, because the basic structure doctrine cannot be understood except as the settlement that duel finally produced.

## The Genealogy of the Doctrine: Shankari Prasad, Sajjan Singh and Golaknath

The first word belonged to a five-judge bench in Shankari Prasad Singh Deo v Union of India, decided on 5 October 1951. The petitioners, zamindars whose estates the Bihar, Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh land reform statutes had vested in the state, challenged the First Amendment itself, arguing that a constitutional amendment was a "law" within Article 13(2) and therefore void if it abridged fundamental rights. The bench, speaking through Chief Justice Harilal Kania's successor, Chief Justice M. Patanjali Sastri, rejected the argument on the distinction that governed the next fourteen years: the power to amend under Article 368 was a constituent power, exercised by Parliament in a different capacity from its ordinary legislative power, and amendments made under it were not "law" for the purposes of Article 13. The First Amendment stood. Land reform proceeded behind the shield of Articles 31A and 31B. The question appeared closed, and the founding settlement's internal bargain, rights guaranteed in Part III and social transformation mandated in Part IV, the bargain whose terms were set in [the directive principles and fundamental rights contest](/2018/12/23/directive-principles-vs-fundamental-rights/), appeared to have been given a workable amendment rule: Parliament could adjust the bargain by amendment, and the courts would not stop it.

The amendment habit formed early and across subjects. The State of Madras v Champakam Dorairajan judgment of 9 April 1951, which struck the Madras communal order in admissions for offending Articles 15 and 29, was answered within the same First Amendment that the land cases had provoked, by the insertion of Article 15(4) enabling special provision for backward classes; the Fourth Amendment of 1955 then widened Article 31A's shield and recast Article 31(2) after the courts had tested the compensation standard in the Bela Banerjee line of cases. By the middle of the decade a pattern was set that defined Indian constitutional politics for a generation: the court read Part III strictly, the government amended, the court tested the amendment against the amended text and usually sustained it, and the pressure accumulated in the one question the pattern could not absorb, whether amendment itself had a floor. The founding generation's confidence that amendment and adjudication could alternate indefinitely depended on each side accepting the other's last move. Golaknath was the court's refusal to accept the next move in advance, and the refusal's timing, after the Congress's electoral reverses of 1967 had weakened the government of the day, was not lost on the politicians who received it.

The second word came in Sajjan Singh v State of Rajasthan, decided on 30 October 1964 and reported in 1965, where a five-judge bench upheld the Seventeenth Amendment, which had added forty-four statutes to the Ninth Schedule, by a majority of three to two on the Article 13 point. The majority, led by Chief Justice P. B. Gajendragadkar, adhered to Shankari Prasad. The case's enduring significance lies in its doubts. Justice J. R. Mudholkar, concurring in the result, asked whether a constitution could be taken to contain features so basic that even the amending power could not change them, and pointed to the Preamble's promises as candidates. Justice M. Hidayatullah recorded his own unease at treating the fundamental rights as a plaything of special majorities. Here, eight years before Kesavananda, the basic structure idea exists in embryo inside the court's own reasoning, raised by judges who nevertheless felt bound by precedent to uphold the amendment before them. The genealogy matters for the adjudication this article must conduct: the doctrine of 1973 was not imported whole from foreign jurisprudence, although comparative scholarship, including the German academic Dietrich Conrad's work on amendment limits, circulated in the argument. It grew from an internal judicial anxiety that two members of the Sajjan Singh bench placed on the record and the majority left unanswered.

The third word reversed the field. In I. C. Golaknath v State of Punjab, decided on 27 February 1967, an eleven-judge bench held by six votes to five, through Chief Justice K. Subba Rao's lead opinion, that a constitutional amendment was indeed "law" within Article 13, that Parliament therefore lacked the power to amend Part III so as to take away or abridge fundamental rights, and that the First, Fourth and Seventeenth Amendments, though valid when enacted, had exhausted the power when exercised. Mindful of the chaos that retrospective invalidation would unleash, Subba Rao borrowed the American device of prospective overruling: the declaration operated from the date of the judgment forward, leaving past amendments intact and future amendments to Part III barred. Golaknath was a maximalist answer to the question Sajjan Singh's doubters had posed. It located the limit on amendment not in structure but in a textual classification, and it made the fundamental rights, alone among the Constitution's provisions, effectively unamendable in the direction of abridgement.

The political consequences were immediate and structural. The judgment froze the property clauses at the moment when Indira Gandhi's government was staking its programme on bank nationalisation and the abolition of privy purses, the programme whose institutional politics the series examined in [the bank nationalisation of 1969](/2019/04/01/bank-nationalisation-1969-politics/). When the Supreme Court struck down the bank nationalisation statute in R. C. Cooper v Union of India on 10 February 1970, and struck down the presidential order derecognising the princes in Madhav Rao Scindia v Union of India on 15 December 1970, the government read the three judgments as a single judicial blockade of its mandate. Cooper deserves a sentence of its own in the genealogy, because it showed the court's method at its most demanding: a bench of eleven struck the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance's statutory successor by ten votes to one, holding that compensation fixed by principles irrelevant to value was no compensation at all and that the statute's exclusion of the named banks' other business offended Article 14. Scindia, by nine votes to two, held that the president's mass derecognition of rulers exceeded the power Article 366(22) conferred and stripped the princes of guarantees the Constitution itself had written into Articles 291 and 362. Read together, the two verdicts told the government that neither drafting ingenuity nor executive order would route around Part III while Golaknath stood, and that the only remaining road ran through Article 368 and a reconsideration of Golaknath itself. The petitions that became Kesavananda were filed into exactly that impasse, by landholders and religious institutions whose property the Kerala and Mysore reform statutes had reached, and the government welcomed the vehicle: a bench large enough to bury Golaknath would settle the amendment question for a generation. The bench was indeed large enough. The settlement was the opposite of the one the government had commissioned. The mid-term election of March 1971, fought on the slogan of abolishing poverty, returned the Congress with the two-thirds strength in the Lok Sabha that amendment required, and the blockade was answered in the only forum the Constitution left open: Article 368 itself. The genealogy thus arrives at its hinge. Golaknath had tried to settle the amendment question by classification and had failed politically within four years. The settlement that endured would have to be built differently, on the architecture of the Constitution rather than on the taxonomy of its powers.

## The Founding Design: A Constitution Built to Be Amended

The doctrine's audacity is clearest against the founders' own intentions for amendment, because the Constituent Assembly designed Article 368 for flexibility and said so. The Assembly that debated the Draft Constitution between 1948 and 1949 had before it the two available models: the British, under which no parliament can bind its successors and constitutional change is ordinary legislation, and the American, under which amendment demands supermajorities of Congress and the states and the federal courts police the boundary between amendment and interpretation. The Indian design deliberately split the difference. Some provisions could be altered by simple majority outside Article 368 altogether; the general run required the special majority of Article 368; a federal core required state ratification in addition. Ambedkar, presenting the Draft Constitution on 4 November 1948, defended the gradation as a virtue, contrasting the Indian scheme's workability with the American Constitution's rigidity, under which, he observed, amendments had been few and the Supreme Court had been driven to expand the text by interpretation. The founders expected amendment to be a normal instrument of governance, and the early republic obliged: the First Amendment came within sixteen months of commencement, and twenty-four amendments had been carried by the time Kesavananda was argued.

The founders' flexibility had a philosophy, and naming it strengthens the hub's eventual adjudication by showing what the doctrine revised and what it preserved. The Assembly's leading members had watched colonial legislatures constrained by imperial vetoes and had concluded that a free India's Parliament must be trusted with the instruments of transformation; the Directive Principles, unenforceable by design, recorded the transformation's goals while the amendment power supplied its legal engine. Nehru's interventions in the Assembly and in the First Amendment debates of May and June 1951 return repeatedly to the theme that no judicial interpretation could be permitted to stand between a elected legislature and the abolition of zamindari, a theme stated most sharply in his letter to chief ministers and in his insistence that the Constitution's social purpose outranked its property text. The Constituent Assembly's own debates on the judiciary show the same settlement from the other side: Ambedkar's celebrated description of Article 32, on 9 December 1948, as the Constitution's very heart and soul assumed courts that would enforce rights against legislation, in a document whose rights the legislature could amend. The founders, in short, built both poles of the later tension into the original design and supplied no umpire for their collision beyond the political process itself. The doctrine of 1973 is the umpire the design eventually grew, which is why its emergence reads, in retrospect, less like an interruption of the founding than like the founding's delayed completion under the pressure of the founding's own contradictions.

That design choice carries a lesson the hub's adjudication must respect. The basic structure doctrine cannot be defended as the founders' stated intention, because the founders stated something close to the opposite: a flexible text, trusted to democratic majorities supervised by a ratification procedure for federal matters. The doctrine's defence must therefore rest where Krishnaswamy later placed it, on the structure the founders built rather than on the expectations they voiced, the distinction between the power that made the Constitution and the powers the Constitution makes. The Assembly sat as a constituent body, claiming to speak for a people constituting themselves; a Parliament elected under the Constitution sits as its creature, and the creature's warrant to revise the instrument of its own creation is the question the founders left, perhaps unavoidably, without an express answer. Article 368's text, read closely, shows the shape of the omission.

## The Text of Article 368 and the Silence at Its Centre

Article 368, as the Twenty-Fourth Amendment restated it, provides that Parliament may in exercise of its constituent power amend by way of addition, variation or repeal any provision of the Constitution in accordance with the procedure laid down in the article. The procedure is exacting and entirely procedural: initiation by bill in either House; passage in each House by a majority of total membership and two-thirds of members present and voting; ratification by the legislatures of not fewer than half the states where the amendment touches the federal provisions enumerated in the proviso, among them the election of the president, the extent of Union and state executive power, the judicature chapters, the Seventh Schedule lists, state representation in Parliament and Article 368 itself; and presidential assent, made obligatory by the Twenty-Fourth Amendment's substitution of "shall give his assent" for the earlier discretion. Two silences define the article. The first is substantive: no provision is declared unamendable, no eternity clause protects any feature, and the contrast with the German Basic Law of 1949, whose Article 79(3) expressly forbids amendments affecting human dignity and the federal and democratic order, is stark and was pressed in the Kesavananda argument. The second silence is institutional: the article does not say who judges whether the procedure's substance, as opposed to its arithmetic, has been respected, nor whether "amend" carries any internal measure of how much change a single exercise of the power, or a series of exercises, may accumulate.

The Kesavananda majority filled both silences from the same source, the identity of the thing being amended. The reasoning is sometimes caricatured as free-standing judicial philosophy; in the opinions it is presented as interpretation of the word the article uses. A repeal of the whole Constitution is not an amendment of it, Sikri reasoned, and between amendment and repeal there must lie a boundary, located where the document's identity gives out. Khanna's narrower version, which became the doctrine's operative centre, held that the power to amend does not include the power to abrogate the basic structure or framework, while leaving every particular provision, property included, open to change. The minority answered that the boundary, if it existed, had been left by the text to the political process, and that a court locating it in structure rather than words was legislating the eternity clause the founders had declined to write. Both readings are genuinely available on the text, which is why the case was decided seven to six and why the argument has never since closed. What the years after 1973 settled was not the philosophy but the jurisdiction: whatever the correct reading in the abstract, the reading adopted in Kesavananda is the one under which amendments are drafted, defended and judged, and a hub concerned with consequence rather than with first principles in isolation records that settlement as the fact to be explained.

## Parliament Answers: The Twenty-Fourth and Twenty-Fifth Amendments

The Twenty-Fourth Amendment, which received presidential assent on 5 November 1971, was drafted as a clause-by-clause reversal of Golaknath. It amended Article 13 to declare that nothing in that article applied to an amendment made under Article 368. It amended Article 368 itself to state, in terms, that Parliament may in the exercise of its constituent power amend by way of addition, variation or repeal any provision of the Constitution, and it converted the president's assent from a discretion into a duty: the amendment bill, when presented, shall receive assent. Every premise of Subba Rao's majority was negated by name. The amendment was the strongest assertion of parliamentary sovereignty in the Indian record, and it was carried by a Parliament whose governing party had just won an election fought partly against the judiciary's vetoes. Any honest statement of the parliamentary-supremacy case must begin here, with a democratically mandated assembly asserting, in the Constitution's own amendment language, that no part of the document stood beyond its reach.

The Twenty-Fifth Amendment, assented to on 20 April 1972, pressed the advantage into the property clauses. It substituted "amount" for "compensation" in Article 31(2), withdrawing the adequacy of payment from judicial scrutiny, and it inserted Article 31C, which gave laws enacted to secure the directive principles in Article 39(b) and 39(c), the equitable distribution of material resources and the prevention of concentration of wealth, immunity from challenge under Articles 14, 19 and 31, and further provided that no law containing a declaration that it was made for that purpose could be called in question on the ground that it did not in fact give effect to the policy. The declaration clause was the sharpest edge of the amendment: it sought to make the legislature's own characterisation of its statute conclusive against the courts. The Twenty-Sixth Amendment of December 1971 completed the programme by abolishing the privy purses and privileges of the former rulers, the settlement narrated in [the abolition of the privy purses](/2019/04/06/privy-purse-abolition-1971-india/). Together the three amendments posed the Kesavananda question in its purest form: if the amending power could override Article 13, redefine its own scope, immunise statutes by declaration and delete institutions wholesale, did any limit on it survive at all?

### Why did Parliament pass the Twenty-Fourth Amendment in 1971?

The amendment was Parliament's direct reversal of Golaknath. The 1967 verdict had held that amendments were law under Article 13 and could not abridge the fundamental rights, freezing the property clauses after the bank nationalisation and privy purse defeats of 1970. The amendment declared the amending power constituent and presidential assent obligatory.

It was carried after the March 1971 election victory, by a Parliament whose governing party had campaigned against the judicial vetoes, and it placed amendments outside Article 13 in terms.

The petitioners who carried the question back to court were, in the lead case, an unlikely standard-bearer for constitutional limits: Swami Kesavananda Bharati, the pontiff of the Edneer Math in Kasaragod, Kerala, whose challenge began as a dispute over the Kerala Land Reforms (Amendment) Act of 1969 and the placement of the math's lands under the reform statutes. Joined with petitions attacking the Twenty-Fourth, Twenty-Fifth and Twenty-Ninth Amendments, the case became the vehicle for the whole amendment controversy. The government of Kerala and the Union of India defended the amendments on the parliamentary-supremacy ground in its most uncompromising form: the amending power, submitted H. R. Gokhale as law minister in Parliament and argued in court by Attorney General Niren De, was plenary, and the remedy for its abuse lay with the electorate rather than the bench. The petitioners' lead counsel, Nani Palkhivala, argued the structure side: a power to amend, he contended, was a power to preserve a constitution's identity while changing its provisions, and a power to destroy the document's essential features was not amendment but abrogation, which Article 368 nowhere conferred. Between those two submissions, every premise of the Indian constitutional order was in play, and the bench convened to decide them was the largest ever assembled.

## Kesavananda: The Hearing, the Split and the Summary

Chief Justice S. M. Sikri constituted a bench of thirteen judges, the full strength of the court deployed to reconsider Golaknath, and the hearing ran from 31 October 1972 to 23 March 1973, through the winter in which the political temperature outside the courtroom was set by the aftermath of the Bangladesh war and the consolidation of Indira Gandhi's authority, the founding-generation drama of which forms [the founding triangulation context](/2018/12/17/patel-nehru-ambedkar-founding-triangulation/) for the state's self-understanding. The record of that hearing, preserved in the Supreme Court Registry and still the least-used primary material in the doctrine's vast literature, shows the argument narrowing month by month. Palkhivala's submissions, spread over weeks, built the implied-limits case from the text of Article 368, from the distinction between amendment and repeal of the whole, and from the Preamble's architecture. The Union's reply, led by Niren De, conceded nothing: asked by the bench whether the amending power could extend to abolishing the states, or to converting the republic into a monarchy, the law officers' position, as the opinions later recorded, treated such questions as political rather than judicial. That refusal to name any limit became, in the opinions of the eventual majority, the strongest internal evidence that a limit had to exist somewhere, because a power whose holders could name nothing beyond it was a power to unmake the Constitution by instalments.

The verdict of 24 April 1973 was delivered on Chief Justice Sikri's last day in office, and its arithmetic was as dramatic as its substance: seven to six for a limited amending power. Sikri's own opinion listed the supremacy of the Constitution, the republican and democratic form of government, the secular character of the Constitution, the separation of powers and the federal character among the basic features, and held that the amending power could not abrogate them. Justices J. M. Shelat and K. S. Hegde, in a joint opinion, added the dignity and freedom of the individual, the unity and integrity of the nation and the mandate to build a welfare state. Justices A. K. Mukherjea and P. Jaganmohan Reddy supplied the remaining majority votes, Reddy's opinion resting partly on the Preamble as the Constitution's identity card. Justice H. R. Khanna's opinion proved decisive and, in its reasoning, the most consequential of the thirteen: Khanna held that amendment excluded the abrogation of the basic structure or framework, but that the right to property was not part of that structure, a distinction that simultaneously created the doctrine and denied it to the property holders whose litigation had produced it. The six dissenters, led by Justice A. N. Ray and including Justices M. H. Beg, S. N. Dwivedi, Y. V. Chandrachud, K. K. Mathew and M. H. Beg's colleagues on the minority side, held the amending power unlimited in reach, subject to the procedure of Article 368 alone.

The bench's composition is part of the verdict's meaning and should be recorded plainly. Thirteen judges sat: Chief Justice S. M. Sikri and Justices J. M. Shelat, K. S. Hegde, A. N. Grover, A. N. Ray, P. Jaganmohan Reddy, D. G. Palekar, H. R. Khanna, K. K. Mathew, M. H. Beg, S. N. Dwivedi, A. K. Mukherjea and Y. V. Chandrachud. Between them they had argued, judged and administered the amendment controversy from every side; several had sat in the property cases of the preceding years; Chandrachud, the junior-most in the order of the opinions' eventual alignment, would as chief justice lead the Minerva Mills bench that defended the doctrine seven years later. The seven-to-six division cut across seniority, region and appointing government, which is one reason the result resisted the era's easy reduction to loyalty and defiance. The dissenters' position, that the text admitted no limit, was held by judges of undoubted independence, and the majority's position was assembled from judges whose agreement extended no further than the boundary itself. A doctrine born in so divided a house, on so narrow a count, might have been expected to dissolve at its first stress. The stresses came within months and years, supersession, Emergency, frontal amendment, and the count held. That the slimmest of majorities produced the settlement is not an embarrassment to the hub's claim; it is the standard biography of constitutional moments, which are rarely unanimous and never tidy.

### Which features did Chief Justice Sikri name as basic in 1973?

In his Kesavananda opinion, Chief Justice S. M. Sikri named the supremacy of the Constitution, the republican and democratic form of government, the secular character of the Constitution, the separation of powers between the three organs of state, and the federal character of the Constitution.

Other majority opinions added the dignity and freedom of the individual, the unity and integrity of the nation and the mandate to build a welfare state, so no single list commanded the whole majority, and the catalogue has been assembled from the opinions collectively ever since.

The disposition of the underlying disputes is worth a paragraph, because it is so easily lost beneath the doctrine's fame. The swami's own case ended in partial relief and partial defeat: the bench remanded the Kerala land reform questions, and the statutes' core survived, as land reform statutes generally survived the verdict that their challenge had provoked. The petitioners who had sought Golaknath's extension won the boundary and lost most of the territory in dispute, since the rights, property included, were returned to the amending power and the amendments they had attacked were substantially upheld. The Union, which had sought unlimited power, received a power broad enough for its entire legislative programme and bounded only at the architecture. Each side could plausibly claim the day, and each did. Constitutional moments often have this property of distributing victory so that no actor is driven to repudiate the settlement, and the property of the 1973 distribution is a large part of why the settlement was available to be built upon by all parties in the decades that followed rather than relitigated at the first change of government.

Two features of the verdict deserve emphasis because they govern everything the doctrine later became. The first is the fate of Golaknath: the majority overruled its holding that amendments were law under Article 13 and that Part III stood beyond amendment, so the fundamental rights returned to the amending power's reach, and the Twenty-Fourth Amendment was upheld in full. Kesavananda was therefore not a simple judicial victory over Parliament. The court surrendered Golaknath's textual fortress and rebuilt the limit on structural ground, trading a broad and brittle rule for a narrower and deeper one. The second feature is the treatment of Article 31C: the bench upheld the first limb of the Twenty-Fifth Amendment's property shield but struck the declaration clause, the provision that would have made the legislature's own say-so conclusive, by a majority that included judges otherwise divided. Judicial review, in other words, survived inside the very verdict that validated most of the government's amendment programme, and it survived precisely at the point where Parliament had tried to draft it out of existence. The next day, 25 April 1973, the government superseded the three senior-most judges of the majority line, Hegde, Shelat and A. N. Grover, and appointed Justice A. N. Ray, a dissenter, as chief justice, an act whose brazenness taught a generation of lawyers that the doctrine's survival would never be merely doctrinal. The scholarly and institutional war over what had happened in that courtroom, and over what the summary signed by nine judges had actually decided, is taken up below; first the hub must state, in citable form, the three-component framework the pillar will use.

## The Attempted Review of November 1975

The doctrine's narrowest escape came not in a reported judgment but in a proceeding that dissolved before it could decide anything. In November 1975, with the Emergency in force and the Raj Narain appeal freshly decided against the government's amendment, Chief Justice A. N. Ray constituted a thirteen-judge bench to review Kesavananda itself, on the Union's application in connected land reform matters. The bench sat for two days. Palkhivala, appearing against the review, argued that no party had shown the verdict unworkable, that the government's grievance was with the doctrine's consequences rather than its correctness, and that a review convened by a chief justice who had dissented in the original case, to reconsider a precedent his court had applied that same month, would damage the institution beyond the doctrine's worth. On the third day, 12 November 1975, the chief justice announced that the bench stood dissolved. No reasons were recorded. The episode, reconstructed by Andhyarujina and by the memoir literature of the bar, is the closest India has come to the doctrine's judicial repeal, and its manner matters for the hub's claim: the settlement of 1973 survived the Emergency not only because governments forbore but because, at the decisive internal moment, the argument against disturbing it prevailed inside the institution best placed to disturb it. Settlements that survive their own guardians' temptation to reopen them acquire the quality the law calls settled, and Kesavananda has never since faced a review bench.

## The Summary Signed by Nine Judges, and the Record Behind It

Between the eleven opinions and constitutional history stands a single sheet of paper: the summary, headed "the view by the majority in these writ petitions", which nine of the thirteen judges signed in court on 24 April 1973. Its nine propositions recorded the overruling of Golaknath, the validity of the Twenty-Fourth Amendment, the basic structure limit on Article 368, the partial validity of the Twenty-Fifth Amendment, the validity of the Twenty-Ninth Amendment and the remand of the Kerala land reform questions. The summary was Chief Justice Sikri's device for extracting a decidable result from opinions that shared a conclusion more readily than a rationale, and its drafting in the verdict's final days, attested by participants on both sides of the bar, has been the critics' favourite exhibit ever since: a doctrine of this magnitude, Seervai objected in substance, should not rest on a document whose signatories had not all held each of its propositions in the same sense. The objection is factually grounded and doctrinally less damaging than it appears. Later benches, in Raj Narain and Minerva Mills, did not treat the summary as a statute to be construed; they treated the majority opinions as the authority and the summary as their index, and Khanna's statement of the limit, the narrowest ground commanding the outcome, has functioned as the doctrine's canonical formulation.

The hearing record behind the summary deserves the attention scholars have largely denied it. The transcripts of the six months of argument, preserved in the Supreme Court Registry, run to thousands of pages in which the doctrine can be watched assembling: Palkhivala's early formulation of amendment as bounded by identity, the bench's repeated demand that the Union's counsel name a limit, and the gradual migration of judges toward the structural middle ground between Golaknath and unlimited power. Working with records of this fragility and value is an archival discipline in itself, of the kind described for source protection in practice in [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), and the pillar's Kesavananda study draws on it directly. For the hub, the record's lesson is narrower. The doctrine was not a coup prepared in chambers; it was the residue of an open argument in which the parliamentary side, offered repeated invitations to concede a lesser limit, declined them all, and thereby made the greater limit the only one on the table.

## The Three-Component Framework: The Basic Structure Substance Table

The framework this pillar cites is set out in the table below, the Three-Component Basic Structure Substance Table, which states each component of the doctrine, the ground on which the Kesavananda majority opinions built it, and the post-1973 application that most plainly exhibits it in operation. Later studies in the pillar take their individual cases in depth; the table is the shared map they work from.

| Component | Substance | Ground in the 1973 opinions | Exhibiting application |
|---|---|---|---|
| One: protection beyond the text | The Constitution has a basic structure that the amending power cannot abrogate, although no clause enumerates it | The majority opinions' shared holding that amendment excludes abrogation of the basic structure or framework | Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain, 7 November 1975, striking the Thirty-Ninth Amendment's election shield |
| Two: judicial primacy in amendment adjudication | The courts decide whether an amendment damages the basic structure, and a special-majority amendment can fall on that ground | The striking of Article 31C's declaration clause, which had tried to make the legislature's characterisation conclusive | Minerva Mills v Union of India, 31 July 1980, striking clauses 4 and 5 of Article 368 as inserted by the Forty-Second Amendment |
| Three: sustained tension with parliamentary sovereignty | The doctrine institutionalises rather than settles the contest between Article 368 and constitutional supremacy | The verdict's combination of upholding the Twenty-Fourth Amendment and limiting the power it asserted | I. R. Coelho v State of Tamil Nadu, 11 January 2007, extending review to Ninth Schedule laws placed after 24 April 1973 |

The table's discipline should be noticed. Each component is stated as a proposition about the constitutional architecture, not about the virtue of judges or the wisdom of parliaments. That is deliberate, and it is what distinguishes the most-consequential-moment reading from the judicial-supremacy framing: the doctrine's significance lies in the structure of argument it obliges every later actor to inhabit. A government that wishes to amend must draft against the possibility of basic structure review. A court that wishes to sustain an amendment must explain why the structure survives it. An opposition that wishes to resist an amendment acquires a constitutional vocabulary beyond the arithmetic of the division lobbies. The components are examined in turn.

## Component One: Protection Beyond the Text

The doctrine's first component is the proposition that constitutional protection can operate beyond the constitutional text. Article 368, as it stood in 1973, said that an amendment of the Constitution may be initiated by a bill in either House and, on passage by the prescribed majorities and presidential assent, the Constitution shall stand amended in accordance with the bill. It named no exceptions. The Kesavananda majority nevertheless held that the power, so expressed, stopped at the boundary of the basic structure. The reasoning, distributed across the majority opinions, worked from three materials. The first was the word "amend" itself: to amend a thing, Sikri reasoned, is to change it while it remains the same thing, and a change that destroys the thing's identity is not amendment but replacement, for which the Constitution provides no procedure short of a new founding. The second material was the Preamble, which the majority treated as the statement of the Constitution's identity: a document that begins by constituting India into a sovereign socialist, secular, democratic republic, the secular promise whose Assembly politics the series traced in [the secularism debates of the Constituent Assembly](/2018/12/28/secularism-constituent-assembly-debates/), could not be amended into a theocracy or a hereditary rulership without ceasing to be the document the Preamble announces. The third material was architecture: features such as federalism, separation of powers and judicial review are nowhere gathered into a single clause, yet each is produced by the arrangement of many clauses together, and an amendment power that could delete them one clause at a time could unmake the design while purporting only to adjust its parts.

The Preamble's role in the construction deserves separate statement, because Kesavananda changed the Preamble's legal status on the way to using it. In the Berubari Union reference of 1960, the Supreme Court had described the Preamble as a key to the Constitution's mind but not a part of the Constitution itself. The Kesavananda majority discarded that limitation: the Preamble, the bench held through Sikri and the Shelat and Hegde opinion, is a part of the Constitution, amendable like any other part and, like every other part, amendable only within the boundary of the structure it announces. The manoeuvre is characteristic of the whole verdict. A text the earlier law had treated as decorative preface became the identity document by which amendment is measured, so that the words "secular" and "socialist", inserted into the Preamble by the Forty-Second Amendment in 1976, entered a document whose Preamble the doctrine had already made load-bearing, and the secularism the Preamble names could thereafter be defended as structure in Bommai and elsewhere on the strength of 1973. Protection beyond the text was thus built partly from a text the court had first to promote, a detail the doctrinal-history framing records and only the moment framing explains: foundings promote their prefaces into law.

This component is the one the doctrine's critics have always found hardest to digest, and their objection deserves its strongest form. A limit that appears nowhere in the text, derived from the structure the text supposedly creates, hands the interpreter a power with no visible edge: if the basic structure is whatever the Constitution's identity requires, and the court alone discerns that identity, then the amendment power of the elected branches is held on judicial sufferance. That objection, pressed by Seervai in print and by the Ray-led minority in the case itself, is answered within the doctrine's own logic by the distinction the majority kept returning to: the limit is negative and architectural, not a charter for judicial policy. The court does not claim that the Constitution's structure prescribes correct outcomes on taxation, land ceilings or language policy. It claims only that some features are constitutive, in the sense that their removal would leave a different constitution occupying the old one's name, and that the amending power, being a power created by the Constitution, cannot be the instrument of the Constitution's own destruction. Whether that distinction can bear the weight placed on it is the standing argument of Indian constitutional law. What cannot be denied is the component's operational reality: since 1973, no serious defence of a challenged amendment has proceeded as if the text of Article 368 alone exhausted the question.

The component also explains the doctrine's peculiar relationship to the founding. The framers of 1949, deliberating in [the Constituent Assembly's founding settlement](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/), had built a constitution unusually easy to amend by the standards of federal documents, partly because a generation engaged in social transformation distrusted constitutional rigidity. B. R. Ambedkar, steering the Draft Constitution through the Assembly whose drafting machinery he chaired, defended the amendment provisions as a middle path between British parliamentary flexibility and American judicial supremacy, a defence reconstructed in [the drafting committee's work under Ambedkar](/2018/12/06/ambedkar-drafting-committee-constitution/). Kesavananda's first component revises that settlement in one direction only: flexibility remains the rule for the Constitution's provisions, including its rights provisions, but the settlement's own identity is withdrawn from the flexibility. The founding is thus protected against its heirs, by a doctrine the founders did not state, in the name of the thing the founders made. The audacity of that construction is the reason the article's claim calls 1973 a constitutional moment rather than a case: a rule interprets a founding, but a moment re-founds, and protection beyond the text is the point at which interpretation visibly becomes re-founding.

The lists of basic features, read across the decades, form a sedimentary record of the republic's anxieties, and the record repays attention. The 1973 lists are preoccupied with form: supremacy, separation, federalism, the republican and democratic character. Raj Narain added the electoral process and the rule of law at the moment a government had manipulated both. Minerva Mills added the harmony of Parts III and IV and the limitation of the amending power itself, at the moment a Parliament had tried to enthrone one part over the other and itself over both. Bommai in 1994 confirmed secularism in the register of centre-state power, in a decade when the secular settlement was under physical attack. Coelho organised the rights core, Articles 14, 19 and 21 in their interrelation, at the moment immunised legislation had again become the legislature's preferred instrument. The appointments cases confirmed judicial independence in the decade when appointments became the executive's preferred instrument. Each generation's addition names the threat that generation actually faced, and the doctrine's capacity to receive the addition without breaking is the first component's practical proof: protection beyond the text endures because the text of the list was never the protection. The architecture was, and the architecture is legible to each new bench in the threats of its own time.

A comparison sharpens what is distinctive about the Indian route. Germany's founders, writing the Basic Law in 1949 in the shadow of a republic that had voted itself into dictatorship by constitutional forms, placed the limit in the text: Article 79(3) forbids any amendment affecting the division of the federation into Länder, the guarantee of human dignity in Article 1 or the democratic and social federal principles of Article 20. The Indian founders, writing in the same years with the Weimar collapse equally available to them as a warning, wrote no such clause, and the difference was argued over in the Kesavananda courtroom, where the petitioners invoked comparative materials, including Dietrich Conrad's scholarship on the German debate, to show that amendment limits were a recognised device of postwar constitutionalism rather than an Indian eccentricity. The majority's construction achieved the eternity clause's function without its text, at the price of the clause's certainty: a German lawyer can read the limit, while an Indian lawyer must reconstruct it from the case law's accumulating lists. The doctrine's defenders treat that price as the unavoidable cost of a limit the founders omitted; its critics treat the omission as the founders' decision, which the court had no warrant to reverse. Both descriptions are accurate, and the choice between them is the choice the whole doctrine embodies.

## Component Two: Judicial Primacy in Amendment Adjudication

The second component assigns the boundary's custody. If the basic structure limits the amending power, some institution must say, in a contested case, on which side of the boundary a given amendment falls, and the Kesavananda settlement assigns that saying to the courts. The assignment was not abstract. It was exercised inside the verdict itself, against the Twenty-Fifth Amendment's declaration clause, where Parliament had attempted to reserve the characterisation question to itself: a law declared to implement Article 39(b) or 39(c) could not be questioned on the ground that it did not implement them. The majority's invalidation of that clause, recorded in the opinions of judges who agreed on little else, is the doctrine's true founding act in institutional terms. Parliament retained the power to shield property legislation from Articles 14, 19 and 31. It lost the power to make its own shield unexaminable. From that distinction, everything in the subsequent law of amendment review descends: the question in every later case is not whether judicial review of amendments exists, which 1973 settled, but how far its writ runs.

The applications built the primacy incrementally, and the pillar's case studies take them in turn; the hub records the load-bearing three. In Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain, decided on 7 November 1975, the doctrine was applied for the first time to strike a constitutional provision: the Thirty-Ninth Amendment's Article 329A, which had withdrawn the prime minister's election from judicial determination and retrospectively validated the election that the Allahabad High Court had set aside on 12 June 1975, in the judgment whose political consequences the series examined in [the Allahabad judgment of June 1975](/2019/04/17/allahabad-judgment-june-1975-indira/). Four of the five judges in Raj Narain held clause 4 of Article 329A destructive of basic features, variously named as free and fair elections, the rule of law, judicial review and the equality principle, while the bench nevertheless upheld the election itself under the amended election law. In Minerva Mills v Union of India, decided on 31 July 1980, the court struck clauses 4 and 5 of Article 368, inserted by the Forty-Second Amendment of 1976 to declare that no amendment could be called in question in any court and that the amending power was unlimited: the majority held that a limited power could not be used to make itself unlimited, and that judicial review was itself part of the structure the clauses attempted to abolish. In I. R. Coelho v State of Tamil Nadu, decided on 11 January 2007 by a nine-judge bench speaking through Chief Justice Y. K. Sabharwal, the primacy reached its widest extent: laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 are open to challenge where they damage the basic structure through the rights in Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21 read together, and the vault the First Amendment had built in 1951 was thereby fitted with a judicial door.

The primacy's working technique is severance, and its restraint is visible in the technique's use. In case after case the bench has struck the offending provision and sustained the remainder of the amendment: Article 31C lost its declaration clause in 1973 and kept its shield; Article 329A lost its clause 4 in 1975 while the election it concerned was decided under the amended election law; Article 368 lost its clauses 4 and 5 in 1980 while the Forty-Second Amendment's many other provisions stood or fell on their own merits in later litigation. Severance converts the doctrine from a veto on amendments into a scalpel applied to provisions, and it explains the otherwise puzzling statistics of the doctrine's career, in which the number of amendments enacted since 1973 exceeds one hundred while the number of provisions destroyed on structural grounds can be counted on one hand with fingers to spare. A supremacy bent on governing would not ration itself so. The rationing is strategic as well as principled, since each restrained exercise replenishes the authority the next exercise will spend, but its consistency across benches of very different composition suggests doctrine rather than tactics: the courts have behaved as custodians of a boundary they expect to defend rarely because its existence does the daily work.

Primacy, stated precisely, is narrower than supremacy, and the distinction is the hub's answer to the second framing. The courts hold no general veto over amendments: of the scores of amendments carried since 1973, the basic structure has invalidated a handful of provisions, and the doctrine's bite has fallen on clauses that attacked the adjudicating machinery itself, election adjudication withdrawn from courts, review barred by declaration, review abolished by self-entrenching amendment, immunised statutes placed beyond the rights they violated. The pattern is consistent enough to state as the component's working rule: judicial primacy in amendment adjudication has operated as a defence of the conditions of constitutional argument, the courts intervening where an amendment sought not merely to change the law but to remove the change from the reach of the Constitution's own testing apparatus. Critics reply, with force, that the guardians decide for themselves when the apparatus is threatened, and that the appointments jurisprudence, in which the court struck down the Ninety-Ninth Amendment and the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act on 16 October 2015, shows the primacy defending the judiciary's corporate interests under the structure's flag. That reply is examined in the scholarly adjudication below and is the subject of the pillar's study of the judges cases; it qualifies the component without dissolving it, because a primacy that can be abused is still a primacy whose existence every constitutional actor must price into its plans.

## The Grammar Spreads: The Structure Beyond Amendment Review

The second component's logic did not stay inside amendment cases, and its migration is part of the doctrine's consequence. In L. Chandra Kumar v Union of India, decided on 18 March 1997, a seven-judge bench held that the power of judicial review vested in the high courts under Article 226 and in the Supreme Court under Article 32 formed part of the basic structure, and that clauses of the Forty-Second Amendment's tribunal scheme which excluded that review were unconstitutional; tribunals could supplement the courts but could not supplant them. The Madras Bar Association line of cases, in 2010 and again in 2014, applied the same structural grammar to the design of the National Company Law Tribunals, testing their composition and independence against the separation of powers rather than against any single clause. In Roger Mathew v South Indian Bank, decided on 13 November 2019, a constitution bench struck down the tribunal rules framed under the Finance Act of 2017 and referred the money bill question to a larger bench, reasoning throughout in the vocabulary of judicial independence as structural fact. None of these cases reviewed a constitutional amendment in the Kesavananda sense, yet each is unintelligible without the premise Kesavananda installed: that the Constitution possesses an architecture which ordinary institutional engineering, like amendment, must respect. The supremacy framing reads this migration as mission creep, and the reading is available; the hub reads it as the predictable behaviour of a constitutional grammar, which, once established for the hardest case of amendment, becomes the natural instrument for every adjacent case in which institutional design threatens the adjudicative conditions the structure protects.

## Component Three: A Tension Institutionalised, Not Settled

The third component is the easiest to miss and the most important for the hub's claim. Kesavananda did not resolve the contest between parliamentary sovereignty and constitutional supremacy; it made the contest permanent and gave it a procedure. Consider what the verdict simultaneously did. It upheld the Twenty-Fourth Amendment, Parliament's declaration that its constituent power could reach any provision, and it limited that very power. It overruled Golaknath, freeing the fundamental rights for amendment, and it invented the doctrine that has since shadowed every major amendment. It was delivered seven to six, by a bench whose chief justice retired the same day, and its operative content had to be extracted from a summary that the judges signed in the courtroom's charged final hours. A settlement so constructed does not end an argument. It builds the arena in which the argument continues, with defined moves: amendment, challenge, structural adjudication, and, where the amendment survives, a new baseline from which the next amendment begins.

### How does a court test an amendment against the basic structure?

The working test asks whether the amendment damages or destroys a feature that the Constitution's architecture treats as basic, judged by the amendment's width and the depth of its effect on the feature rather than by the amendment's stated purpose. Courts strike only the offending provision where severance is possible.

The court identifies the feature from the text, the Preamble and the structure of the document read as a whole, then measures the abridgement against the feature's essence, asking whether the feature survives in recognisable form after the amendment has done its work.

The parliamentary side of the tension deserves statement at its strongest, because the doctrine's legitimacy depends on the strength of what it restrains. The Indian Parliament is not a creature of statute; under Article 368 it exercises a constituent power, the same genus of power that made the Constitution, delegated through the document to the people's representatives assembled in special majority with, for federal provisions, the ratification of half the states. The federal character of that procedure, ratification by state legislatures for the provisions listed in the proviso to Article 368, is examined in the series' account of [the federal design of the Indian Constitution](/2019/01/03/federalism-indian-constitution-explained/), and it underwrites the democratic argument: an amendment carried through that gauntlet expresses the constituent will with a pedigree no bench of thirteen can match. When that will is nevertheless reviewed against an unwritten structure, the reviewing court acts against the grain of the democratic premise, and knows it. The doctrine's defenders answer that the pedigree argument proves too much: it would equally validate an amendment abolishing elections, a consequence the Union's own counsel in 1972 and 1973 conspicuously declined to embrace in terms. Between the refusal to name a limit and the refusal to accept any, the tension stands, and the post-1973 record shows both institutions behaving as if the tension, rather than either pole, is the constitution of the Constitution. Governments draft around the doctrine; courts deploy it sparingly and mostly at the architecture's joints; oppositions invoke it as the grammar of resistance. The era after 2014, whose renegotiation of the founding tensions the series assessed in [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/), has tested the arena repeatedly without breaking it, which is the strongest available evidence that the arena, and not any single verdict inside it, is Kesavananda's real bequest.

The ratification proviso deserves notice as the tension's federal register, because it complicates any simple court-versus-Parliament account of the doctrine. For the provisions the proviso enumerates, an amendment is not Parliament's act alone: it requires the concurrence of at least half the state legislatures, bodies elected on their own mandates and frequently controlled by parties in opposition at the centre. The Ninety-Ninth Amendment carried that concurrence, securing ratification by sixteen states before its annulment in 2015, a fact its defenders pressed as the answer to the counter-majoritarian charge, and its annulment therefore stands as the doctrine's most democratically expensive exercise, a cost the hub has already entered in the ledger. The Goods and Services Tax amendment of 2016, the One Hundred and First, shows the same machinery succeeding within the doctrine's boundary: a restructuring of the fiscal federation of the first magnitude, carried through ratification and sustained, because it renegotiated the federal bargain through the bargain's own forms rather than abolishing the federal party to it. The contrast between 2015 and 2016 is the third component in operation, two amendments of comparable democratic pedigree meeting different fates on a structural question, and no account that reduces the doctrine to judicial will can explain why the second was never in danger. The architecture distinguishes between change within the federation and change to the federation's existence, and the distinction, not the identity of the judges applying it, did the work.

## The Doctrine and the Third Tension: Rights Against Development

The House Thesis of the series holds that Indian politics is organised by three permanent tensions, and the third, between rights and development, is the one Kesavananda operationalises at the constitutional-judicial level. The amendment duel of 1951 to 1973 was, in its substance, nothing else: land reform, bank nationalisation, privy purse abolition and resource redistribution were developmental programmes in the idiom of their time, and the fundamental rights to property and equality were the rights architecture they collided with. The state's lawyers argued development's case in the language of sovereignty: a Parliament elected to transform a poor country could not be thwarted by the property clauses of a constitution its own majority could amend. The petitioners argued the rights case in the language of limits: a Constitution whose guarantees dissolve at the touch of a special majority guarantees nothing. Each framing, in pure form, destroys the other pole. Unlimited amendment abolishes rights as constitutional facts; unamendable rights abolish democratic development as constitutional policy. Golaknath chose the second disaster, the pre-1973 Parliament flirted with the first, and the Forty-Second Amendment's clauses 4 and 5 attempted to enact the first in terms.

The basic structure settlement is the only position in the whole controversy that refuses both disasters, and that refusal is its analytical signature. By returning the fundamental rights to the amending power's reach, it kept democratic development constitutionally possible: the right to property itself was removed from Part III by the Forty-Fourth Amendment of 1978, an amendment no basic structure challenge could have defeated after Khanna's opinion had excluded property from the structure. By withdrawing the Constitution's identity from the amending power's reach, it kept rights constitutionally real: a developmental programme may adjust the rights architecture, but it may not abolish the conditions, elections, courts, federal states, secular citizenship, under which rights are claimed at all. The doctrine's handling of Article 31C across the decade traces the refusal in miniature. Kesavananda upheld the Article's first limb, the shield for Article 39(b) and 39(c) legislation, while striking its declaration clause. The Forty-Second Amendment then expanded the shield to all directive principles whatsoever, converting a bounded priority for redistributive law into a general subordination of Articles 14 and 19 to Part IV. Minerva Mills struck the expansion, and its reasoning is the third tension's canonical judicial statement: the Constitution, the majority held, rests on a harmony between fundamental rights and directive principles, and to give either absolute primacy over the other is to disturb the basic structure itself. Development could override particular rights claims within a bounded field; it could not be enthroned as the Constitution's general sovereign any more than property could. The reservation jurisprudence shows the same refusal at work in a neighbouring field, where the framework this series set out in [the constitutional reservation framework](/2020/02/25/constitutional-reservation-framework/) operates inside the boundary the doctrine polices, and where the economic criterion's arrival through the One Hundred and Third Amendment, assessed in [the EWS reservation amendment of 2019](/2020/03/29/2019-ews-reservation-amendment/), was framed by its defenders and its challengers alike in the doctrine's grammar of structure and essence. The third tension is therefore not resolved by the doctrine but given a constitutional-judicial register: development proceeds by amendment, rights resist by structure, and the court adjudicates the collision case by case. A doctrine that allocates a permanent tension to a permanent forum has done something categorically larger than winning a case, and it is on this ground, more than on any list of struck clauses, that the most-consequential-moment claim rests.

## The Scholarly War: Seervai, the Defenders and the Insider's Account

No doctrine in Indian law has been fought over in print as the basic structure has, and the hub adjudicates the fight rather than merely reporting it. The parliamentary-supremacy critique found its most rigorous voice in H. M. Seervai. Seervai's authority was unmatched: author of the Constitutional Law of India, the treatise through which generations read the document, and an advocate who had argued on the states' side in the amendment litigation. His considered position, hardened across the editions of the late 1970s and 1980s, was that Kesavananda was wrongly decided: that the majority's implied limit had no warrant in the text or in the framers' design, that the summary signed by nine judges could not bear the doctrinal weight later placed on a document agreed in haste, and that the court's theory licensed future benches to veto constitutional change on intuitions dressed as structure. The critique matters because it is not a politician's complaint. It is the textualist case at its highest level of craft, and any defence of the doctrine that cannot answer Seervai has not defended it.

The defence, at its strongest, is cumulative rather than single-authored. Granville Austin, in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), situated Kesavananda inside the political history of the republic and judged it by its fruits: the doctrine, Austin argued, gave the Constitution a means of self-preservation during the decades when its survival was a live question, and its restraint in application, one provision struck in the 1970s and the review-barring clauses in 1980, showed a court husbanding a weapon it rarely fired. Austin's judgment carried the weight of his earlier authority: his first book, The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation (1966), had given the founding its canonical scholarly narrative, and his description of the Constitution as a seamless web of democracy, secularism and social revolution supplied, in advance, the very idea of an identity that amendment might not tear. When the historian of the founding concluded, a third of a century later, that the doctrine of 1973 had preserved the web through its most dangerous decades, the defence acquired its most credible witness, a scholar whose subject was the Constitution's survival rather than the court's glory and who had no institutional loyalty to declare. Upendra Baxi, in Courage, Craft and Contention (1985), read the case within the Supreme Court's social biography, treating the verdict as one episode in the court's long negotiation between its rights-protecting self-image and its reluctance to confront state power directly, a reading that explains the split outcome, rights freed for amendment and structure withdrawn from it, as institutional craft rather than confusion. Sudhir Krishnaswamy's Democracy and Constitutionalism in India (2009) supplied the defence's most systematic theoretical form, arguing that the doctrine is defensible within democratic theory itself: a distinction between the constituent power that founds and the constituted power that amends runs through the Constitution's own design, and judicial enforcement of that distinction protects democracy's preconditions rather than frustrating its exercise. The insider literature cuts across the alignment. Andhyarujina's 2011 account, written by a participant who had watched the hearing from the government's side of the bar, documented the courtroom manoeuvres, the assembly of the summary and the supersession that followed, and concluded against the verdict on supremacy grounds; yet the same account preserves, in the government's own submissions, the refusal to name any limit on amendment that persuaded the majority a limit must exist.

Adjudicating this disagreement on the primary evidence, the hub finds for the defence, with the critics' two sharpest points conceded and priced in. The first concession is textual: the doctrine is an inference, not a quotation, and Seervai is right that its authority would be cleaner if the Constitution stated it. The reply is that the inference was forced by the case's own record: once Parliament asserted through the Twenty-Fourth Amendment, and the Union's counsel declined to disown in argument, a power to amend the republic into its opposite, the court faced a choice between an implied structural limit and an express licence for constitutional self-destruction, and implication was the smaller violence to the text. The second concession is institutional: Andhyarujina and the supremacy school are right that the bench's conduct in April 1973, the hurried summary, the contested arithmetic of who had held what, fell short of the gravity the doctrine claims. The reply is that doctrines are judged across their lives, and the half-century of applications, Raj Narain, Minerva Mills, Waman Rao, Coelho, shows later benches disciplining the inheritance, narrowing it to the defence of adjudicative and electoral conditions and declining invitations to widen it into a general policy veto. In disputes adjacent to the doctrine, the same discipline of holding-by-holding adjudication is visible in the court's handling of religious freedom, as the series showed in [the Sabarimala religious-secular adjudication](/2020/05/28/sabarimala-religious-secular-adjudication/) and in the settlement mapped by [the contested-settlement framework](/2020/04/09/indian-secularism-contested-settlement/). The critics describe what the doctrine might become in careless hands; the defenders describe what it has been in the record. The hub sides with the record.

## The Second Generation: The Doctrine in Scholarship After 2000

The scholarly war did not end with the treatise generation, and its second front confirms the hub's reading of the doctrine's standing. Gautam Bhatia's The Transformative Constitution (2019) placed Kesavananda inside a longer argument about the Constitution as an instrument of social transformation, reading the verdict's rights jurisprudence as one strand of a founding commitment that later courts, including the privacy bench of 2017, would take up; the book's architecture assumes throughout that 1973 is a hinge of the constitutional story rather than an episode in it. Madhav Khosla's India's Founding Moment (2020) approached from the founding side, reconstructing the codifying imagination of the Constituent Assembly, and in doing so supplied fresh reason to treat 1973 as the founding's sequel: a constitution designed as a comprehensive code for a society in transformation would predictably generate, at the point where amendment threatened the code's identity, a judicially articulated theory of what the code essentially was. Comparative scholarship carried the doctrine outward in the same decades, with the basic structure idea debated as a general device of constitutional design in jurisdictions from Bangladesh, whose courts adopted it in terms in 1989, to the Latin American debates on unconstitutional constitutional amendments. A doctrine that organises this much scholarship, across this many positions, has the sociology of a constitutional moment whatever its critics hold about its warrant: it is the question to which Indian constitutional thought keeps returning, and a hub charged with framing a pillar of twelve studies is entitled to treat that return as evidence.

## The Political Critique in Parliament's Own Voice

The supremacy critique is too often presented as a lawyer's argument and thereby weakened, because its most compelling form is political and was made by politicians at the time. When the Twenty-Fourth Amendment was debated in 1971, the government defended it in the language of democratic mandate: the March election had been fought on a programme the courts had blocked, and a Parliament returned with amendment strength claimed the right to remove the blockade by the Constitution's own procedure. H. R. Gokhale, piloting the amendment as law minister, framed judicial obstruction of land reform and nationalisation as the substitution of judicial for electoral judgment. During the Emergency the argument hardened into doctrine: the Swaran Singh Committee of 1976, whose recommendations shaped the Forty-Second Amendment, treated the basic structure theory as an impediment to the socio-economic transformation the Constitution's own directive principles commanded, and the amendment's clauses 4 and 5 of Article 368 were the committee's logic enacted. Even the restoration after 1977 kept a version of the critique alive: Morarji Desai's Janata government, though it repealed the Emergency's excesses through the Forty-Fourth Amendment, contained senior figures who regarded the doctrine as an anomaly to be tolerated rather than a settlement to be celebrated, and it was left to the Supreme Court in Minerva Mills, not to Parliament, to strike the clauses that had purported to abolish the limit.

The critique's democratic core survives every reply the doctrine's defenders make, and the hub records it without dilution. A special majority of both Houses, ratified where required by half the states, is the most demanding expression of popular will the Constitution knows short of a founding; to subject its product to annulment under a standard found nowhere in the text is, on any view, a counter-majoritarian act. The defenders' answer, that the majority's warrant derives from the Constitution and cannot extend to the Constitution's destruction, is logically strong and democratically incomplete, because it does not explain why the derivation's terms are for judges rather than for the amending body itself to state. The honest conclusion is that the doctrine rests on a judgment about institutional trust, rendered in 1973 by men who had watched an elected Parliament acquiesce in the steady enlargement of executive power, and sustained since by publics and oppositions who have repeatedly found the court's veto easier to forgive than the veto's absence would have been. The tension the third component names is therefore not a defect awaiting cure. It is the doctrine's continuing condition, renewed each time an amendment of consequence is drafted with one eye on the division lobbies and the other on the court.

## The Afterlife: The Doctrine as a Working Test

A doctrine's consequence is measured by what it obliges later actors to do, and by that measure the post-1973 trajectory is the hub's central evidence. Within two years of the verdict, the doctrine faced the most direct assault a constitutional device can face: the government it constrained held a two-thirds majority, faced an adverse election judgment, and governed under a proclaimed Emergency. The Thirty-Ninth Amendment of August 1975 withdrew the elections of the president, vice president, prime minister and speaker from the courts and retrospectively regularised the prime minister's own annulled election; its clause 4 was struck in Raj Narain on basic structure grounds even as the Emergency's machinery stood intact around the court. The episode is examined in depth in the pillar's study of the Raj Narain litigation; here it fixes a datum. At the moment of maximum political cost, a constitution bench used the two-year-old doctrine against the amendment of a government at the height of its power, and the government, which had packed the court's leadership in 1973, complied with the form of the judgment while retaining office under the election law the bench sustained. Neither the supremacy framing, which would predict defiance or capitulation, nor the doctrinal-history framing, which records the citation, explains that compound outcome. The moment framing does: both actors were performing inside an arena whose rules each had reasons to preserve.

The Emergency also supplied the doctrine's darkest counter-exhibit, and the hub does not flinch from it. In ADM Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla, decided on 28 April 1976, a constitution bench held by four to one that, the enforcement of Article 21 standing suspended, no person could move a court for habeas corpus against unlawful detention, however arbitrary the detention; Justice H. R. Khanna's lone dissent, which cost him the chief justiceship when he was superseded in January 1977, held that life and liberty did not derive from the Constitution and could not be extinguished by its suspension. The pillar's study of that verdict treats it at the length it deserves. For the hub, ADM Jabalpur establishes the limit of doctrinal consequence: a structure-limiting doctrine policed amendments, but it did not police interpretation, and a bench unwilling to defend liberty by construction was not rescued by a doctrine about amendment. The Forty-Second Amendment, assented to in December 1976, then attempted the frontal route, amending Article 368 to oust review and declare the power unlimited, and amending Article 31C to extend its shield to all directive principles. The Janata government's Forty-Fourth Amendment of 1978 rolled back much of the Emergency's constitutional work but conspicuously did not attempt to abolish the basic structure doctrine itself, and Minerva Mills in 1980 struck the Forty-Second's self-entrenching clauses using the doctrine, completing the sequence: the one government with both the motive and the majority to kill the doctrine chose instead to restore the Constitution around it. The political history of that restoration belongs to [the post-1975 Emergency context](/2019/04/23/emergency-1975-1977-reanalysed/) in the series' earlier pillar. One feature of the restoration deserves emphasis here because it is the third component operating through the electorate rather than the courts. The Janata Party's 1977 manifesto promised to restore the Constitution to its pre-Emergency position, and the victorious coalition held the parliamentary strength, in the Lok Sabha though not fully in the Rajya Sabha, to attempt a far-reaching revision. Its Law Minister, Shanti Bhushan, who had argued the Raj Narain case against the Thirty-Ninth Amendment, steered the Forty-Fourth Amendment through negotiation with the Congress opposition in the upper house, and the negotiations' surviving text left the basic structure doctrine, the judicial review it underwrites and the Minerva Mills questions all untouched, concentrating instead on the Emergency provisions, Article 352's grounds and safeguards, and the deletion of the property right. The restoration generation, which had suffered most directly from the doctrine's absence of effect during the Emergency and might have been expected to arm Parliament against courts as well as against executives, instead treated 1973 as part of the settlement to be restored. Electoral verdicts rarely ratify judicial doctrines in terms; by conduct, the verdict of March 1977 and the amendment of 1978 did.

The federal register supplied the doctrine's most operationally significant deployment of the 1990s. In S. R. Bommai v Union of India, decided on 11 March 1994, a nine-judge bench held that secularism formed part of the basic structure, with direct consequences for the Union's power under Article 356 to dismiss state governments: a state government pursuing policies contrary to the Constitution's secular character could expose itself to a proclamation, while proclamations themselves stood open to judicial review, including review of the material behind the president's satisfaction. Bommai carried the basic structure out of the amendment courtroom and into the working law of centre-state relations, where it disciplined the most abused provision of the Constitution more effectively in the following decades than any amendment had done; the proclamations' frequency fell, and the vocabulary of their defence changed. The Shah Bano controversy of the previous decade, whose parliamentary reversal the series examined in [the Shah Bano case and the 1986 Act](/2020/04/20/shah-bano-case-1986-act-india/), had already shown the secular feature's political charge; Bommai gave that charge doctrinal form. A doctrine that can govern both the validity of amendments and the survival of state governments is exercising the second component at full stretch, and the pillar's federalism-facing studies return to the case in that light.

Coelho's own facts show the doctrine disciplining a sympathetic cause rather than a hostile one, which is part of its authority. The case arose from the Tamil Nadu Backward Classes, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Reservation of Seats in Educational Institutions and of Appointments or Posts in the Services under the State) Act of 1993, which raised reservation in the state to sixty-nine per cent and was placed in the Ninth Schedule by the Seventy-Sixth Amendment to protect it from the fifty per cent ceiling the court had articulated in the Mandal judgment, Indra Sawhney, in 1992. The nine-judge bench did not strike the Tamil Nadu statute; it held the door of the Ninth Schedule open to review, laid down the rights and essence tests by which such statutes would be judged, and remitted the individual laws for examination under them. Reservation, the most democratically rooted of India's constitutional commitments, was thereby confirmed as subject to the structure like everything else, and the pillar's reservation-facing studies record how the Coelho framework has framed the sub-categorisation and census debates since. A doctrine willing to test its friends' statutes by the same measure as its enemies' amendments has the universality that distinguishes architecture from alliance.

The doctrine's second life, from the 1980s onward, converted it from an emergency-tested weapon into a routine instrument of constitutional adjudication, and the conversion is visible in the care with which later benches specified the test. Waman Rao v Union of India, decided in 1981, fixed the temporal boundary: amendments made before 24 April 1973 were not to be reopened on basic structure grounds, while those made after stood exposed. The nine-judge bench in Coelho in 2007 then supplied the operative method, the rights test and the essence-of-rights test, under which an immunised statute falls if it abrogates the essence of the golden triangle of Articles 14, 19 and 21 as those articles inform the structure. Later applications extended the doctrine's grammar beyond amendment review proper: the tribunal jurisprudence, from L. Chandra Kumar in 1997 to the Madras Bar Association line of cases, treated judicial review and the independence of the judiciary as structural features limiting ordinary legislation and institutional design alike. The appointments controversy carried the grammar to its most contested site when a constitution bench on 16 October 2015 struck the Ninety-Ninth Amendment for impairing judicial independence within the structure, restoring the collegium system the amendment had sought to replace; the pillar's study of the judges cases weighs that verdict's democratic costs and benefits. Meanwhile the doctrine's vocabulary migrated outward into ordinary constitutional argument, so that the challenge to the August 2019 reorganisation of Jammu and Kashmir, whose political mechanics the series reconstructed in [the Article 370 abrogation of August 2019](/2019/07/31/article-370-abrogation-august-2019/), was framed by its counsel substantially in the doctrine's language of federalism and structure, and the pillar's later studies take up the privacy judgment of 2017, which formally interred ADM Jabalpur, the Aadhaar judgment of 2018 and the Sabarimala judgment of the same year as the doctrine's extended family. A framework that began as seven judges' inference from the word "amend" had become, by the locked date of this study, the default syntax in which the largest questions of Indian public law are posed, a migration that parallels the institutional migration the series traced for the election watchdog in [the institution-under-stress framework](/2020/06/03/election-commission-institution-under-stress/).

The privacy judgment of 24 August 2017, Justice K. S. Puttaswamy v Union of India, in which a nine-judge bench unanimously recognised privacy as a fundamental right intrinsic to Part III, displayed the doctrine's grammar operating at full maturity in the rights register. Justice D. Y. Chandrachud's plurality opinion treated the Constitution's structure as the source from which the right's recognition flowed, describing the fundamental rights as the Constitution's commitment against majoritarian excess, and the bench formally overruled ADM Jabalpur's majority, with Chandrachud recording that the 1976 verdict's treatment of life and liberty stood discredited by its own reasoning. The son of the ADM Jabalpur majority's author thus wrote the judgment that buried it, an institutional fact the pillar's privacy study examines; for the hub, Puttaswamy matters as proof that the structural method of 1973, reading the document as an architecture whose commitments exceed any single clause, had become the court's ordinary way of deciding its largest questions, as much in the recognition of rights as in the limitation of amendments. The Aadhaar judgment of 26 September 2018 then applied the era's proportionality discipline within the structure Puttaswamy had described, and the Sabarimala judgment of 28 September 2018, whose religious-secular adjudication the series has treated in its Pillar 10 frame, carried the same method into the law of religious practice, from which the pillar's closing study takes the long view back to 1973.

### Where does the doctrine leave the amending power of a future Parliament?

It leaves the power wide and bounded. A future Parliament may amend any provision, including the fundamental rights, and may pursue any policy the amended text permits, but it may not destroy the document's basic features, among them free and fair elections, judicial review, federalism, secularism and the separation of powers.

The boundary is policed case by case, as amendments are challenged, and amendments that respect the architecture have been sustained far more often than struck, which is why the doctrine functions in practice as a drafting discipline for governments rather than as a standing veto.

## The Appointments Crisis: The Primacy Defends Its Own House

The doctrine's most contested modern application came on 16 October 2015, when a five-judge constitution bench struck down the Ninety-Ninth Amendment and the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act by four votes to one. The amendment, carried in August 2014 with near-unanimous parliamentary support and ratified by more than half the states, had replaced the collegium system of judicial appointments, itself a judicial construction of the Second Judges Case of 1993, with a six-member commission in which the judiciary held three seats. The majority held that the amendment impaired the independence of the judiciary, a basic feature, by diluting judicial primacy in appointments, and that the impairment damaged the structure. Justice J. Chelameswar's dissent rested substantially on the democratic objection this article has tracked throughout: the collegium's opacity was indefensible, the amendment's pedigree was impeccable, and the majority's reasoning converted the doctrine into a charter of judicial corporate interest. The episode is the pillar's judges-cases study in its own right; the hub registers its double edge. On one side, the verdict is the second component operating exactly as designed, an amendment tested against the structure and failing. On the other, it is the application in which the guardians' interest and the structure's interest are hardest to tell apart, and the one the supremacy school cites first. The hub's adjudication is that the verdict is defensible on the doctrine's own premises, since appointment capture is the historically demonstrated route by which executives have bent courts, in the supersessions of 1973 and 1977 above all, while the democratic cost Chelameswar named is real and remains unpaid: the appointments system's legitimacy deficit, which the amendment sought to cure, survives its cure's annulment.

## The Unevenness Objection, and Why the Claim Survives It

The strongest counter to the most-consequential-moment reading is not textual but empirical: the doctrine's applications have been uneven, and at several points the architecture it supposedly protects was eroded without the doctrine firing. The indictment is real and should be itemised. During the Emergency, the doctrine did not prevent the Thirty-Ninth Amendment's enactment, the Forty-Second Amendment's enactment, the supersession of judges in 1973 and 1977, or the ADM Jabalpur capitulation. In quieter decades, governments learned to achieve by ordinary legislation, by appointments practice and by institutional attrition what a frontal amendment might have risked, and the doctrine, addressed only to constitutional amendment and its near neighbours, watched avenues it does not patrol. The appointments verdict of 2015 can itself be entered on the debit side by those who read it as the primacy defending its own. A doctrine whose protection is this porous, the objection concludes, may be consequential as rhetoric while remaining modest as architecture.

The indictment can be pressed further, into the decades of the doctrine's supposed maturity. The supersession instrument, used in 1973 and 1977, taught executives that the composition of benches could be influenced without any amendment at all, through appointment and elevation decisions the doctrine does not reach. Governments of every complexion learned the lesson in milder forms: litigation strategy adapted to bench composition, constitutional challenges timed and framed for the audience the roster supplied. Ordinary legislation achieved quietly what amendments might have risked publicly, from the tribunalisation of adjudication, which the courts then had to police case by case, to statutory designs that shifted disputes away from the constitutional courts. After 2014, a government with the largest single-party majority in thirty years tested the architecture's unguarded avenues, in appointments delays, in the money bill route for measures of constitutional significance, and in the reorganisation of a state under president's rule, where the federal feature the doctrine protects was engaged by executive action rather than by amendment. Each episode can be narrated as the doctrine's irrelevance, and the supremacy school so narrates them. The hub's counter-reading is that the choice of avenue is itself the evidence: actors route around obstacles that exist. No government routes around a doctrine it believes to be rhetoric.

The objection mistakes the claim. The hub does not argue that the doctrine has uniformly succeeded in protecting the Constitution; it argues that the doctrine re-founded the terms on which constitutional change is attempted, defended and resisted, and that this re-founding is the consequential fact. The evidence for the claim is behavioural as much as doctrinal. Governments that could have ignored a merely rhetorical doctrine instead legislated around it, timed around it and argued inside it; the Janata restoration worked around it rather than through its repeal; the authors of the Ninety-Ninth Amendment chose the amendment route, submitting their appointments reform to the very review it then failed, rather than packing the court by statute. Even erosion confirms the frame: pressure on institutions in the decades after 1973 has characteristically been applied through channels the doctrine does not govern, which is precisely the behaviour of actors who regard the governed channel as closed. Uneven application is the normal condition of consequential constitutional devices; the American commerce clause and the German eternity clause alike have histories of advance and evasion. Consequence is measured by the displacement of all other actors' strategies, and by that measure no Indian constitutional development since 1950 displaces more than Kesavananda, not the Emergency amendments it eventually outlasted, not the economic reforms that proceeded under ordinary law, not the rights expansions of the public interest litigation era that presupposed the judicial authority Kesavananda secured. The claim stands on the architecture, and the architecture stands in the record of what every actor since 1973 has felt obliged to do.

## Measuring Consequence: What a Constitutional Moment Does

The claim that Kesavananda is the most consequential constitutional moment after the founding should be held to a measurable standard, and the hub states the standard before resting on it. A constitutional moment, as the term is used here, does three things. It redistributes authority among institutions in a way later actors cannot ignore. It supplies the vocabulary in which subsequent constitutional conflict is conducted. And it survives the conditions that produced it, binding actors who did not choose it and would not have enacted it. The founding of 1947 to 1950 did all three at the largest scale. Kesavananda does all three at the next scale down. Authority moved: the constituent power acquired a judicially policed boundary, and every government since 1973 has drafted its amendments in the boundary's shadow, including governments, in 1976 and 2014, commanding the full amendment arithmetic. Vocabulary moved: basic structure, width and depth of abridgement, essence of rights, are the working terms of Indian amendment politics, used as readily by the doctrine's parliamentary critics as by its judicial defenders. Survival is the plainest test of all: the doctrine outlasted the Emergency that tried to abolish it by amendment, the restorations that declined to abolish it by choice, and the appointments reform that tested it in 2015, and it binds a political generation for whom 1973 is history rather than memory.

The standard also disciplines the claim against inflation. Other verdicts have been more beloved, other amendments more transformative in material terms, and the pillar does not argue that 24 April 1973 is the finest day in the Supreme Court's history; ADM Jabalpur, three years later, is a strong candidate for its worst, and the same institution owns both. Consequence is a colder measure than merit. It asks what the constitutional system would look like had the moment not occurred, and the counterfactual here is not difficult to construct, because the materials for it were enacted and then struck: an Article 368 with clauses declaring the amending power unlimited and unreviewable, an election law whose outcomes the beneficiary's own amendment had predetermined, a Ninth Schedule filling steadily with statutes placed beyond the rights they infringed. That counterfactual republic, in which amendment is the only constitutional sovereign and its exercises are final, is recognisably buildable from the record of 1975 and 1976. The doctrine is the reason it was not built. A device that stands between the republic and an available alternative constitution of that character has consequence of the first order, whatever one's final judgment of its warrant.

Against that standard, the rival candidates for second place fall short on at least one limb. The Emergency itself redistributed authority drastically but supplied no durable vocabulary of legitimacy and was substantially reversed within three years. The public interest litigation expansion of the 1980s transformed access to courts but rested on the judicial authority the 1973 settlement had already secured, a dependence visible in the rights jurisprudence the pillar examines in its closing studies. The economic reforms after 1991 transformed the republic's material life while proceeding almost entirely under ordinary law, changing everything except the constitutional architecture. Kesavananda alone changed the architecture, named the change, and made the name compulsory. That is the whole of the hub's claim, and it is enough.

The nine studies that follow inhabit this frame case by case. The Kesavananda record itself, its thirteen opinions and its nine-signature summary, receives the close reading the hub deliberately deferred. The Emergency pair follows: the Raj Narain application, the doctrine's first strike, and the ADM Jabalpur failure, the doctrine's boundary marker. The appointments tetralogy carries the second component into its most contested territory. Coelho receives its own study as the doctrine's widest extension, and the long arc of Article 370 from 1954 to the post-2019 litigation tests the third component across sixty-five years. The privacy, Aadhaar and Sabarimala judgments close the pillar by showing the doctrine's grammar at work in the rights jurisprudence of the last decade, the afterlife of a verdict delivered on a chief justice's final day in 1973 by judges a third of whom did not live to see it become the Constitution's load-bearing wall. Running beneath the case studies, the pillar keeps one question in view, the question this hub has answered in the affirmative and each study re-tests against its own record: whether the settlement of 24 April 1973 deserves to be read as the republic's second founding, the moment at which the Constitution acquired, from within its own materials, the means of surviving its amenders. The studies that follow are free to complicate the answer. They are not free, on the evidence assembled here, to treat the question as closed in the negative. Two disciplines govern every study in the pillar, and the hub states them here so the case studies can proceed without repeating their warrants. The first is adjudication by holding: each judgment is stated by what it decided, on the votes it commanded, before any assessment of what it achieved, and scholarly positions enter attributed to their named holders at their strongest, from Seervai's textualism to Krishnaswamy's democratic defence. The second is the refusal of both available sentimentalities, the romance of the court as the Constitution's lone saviour and the romance of Parliament as the people's unmediated voice; the record of 1951 to 2020 flatters neither institution, and the pillar's claim is precisely that the doctrine's consequence lies in the disciplined distrust it obliges each to practise toward the other. Readers who hold the three components in view, protection beyond the text, judicial primacy in amendment adjudication, and a tension institutionalised rather than settled, hold the key the pillar uses on every lock that follows.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the basic-structure doctrine?

The basic-structure doctrine is the principle, established in Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala on 24 April 1973, that Parliament's power to amend the Constitution under Article 368 does not extend to damaging or destroying the Constitution's basic structure. The structure comprises the features that give the document its identity, including the supremacy of the Constitution, its republican and democratic form, its secular and federal character, the separation of powers, the rule of law, judicial review and the fundamental rights. Parliament may amend any provision, including the rights in Part III, but an amendment that abrogates one of these constitutive features exceeds the amending power and is void to that extent.

### Q: Why is Kesavananda the most-consequential constitutional moment?

Kesavananda earns that standing because it re-founded the terms on which the Constitution may be changed, a function second only to the founding of 1947 to 1950 itself. The verdict of 24 April 1973 simultaneously freed the fundamental rights for amendment, by overruling Golaknath, and withdrew the Constitution's identity from the amending power's reach, thereby allocating the permanent tension between rights and democratic development to a standing judicial forum. Every later actor has had to work inside that allocation: governments draft amendments against basic structure review, courts test amendments against the structure, and oppositions frame resistance in its vocabulary, from Minerva Mills in 1980 to the appointments judgment of 2015.

### Q: What is Article 368?

Article 368 is the Constitution's amendment clause, titled the power of Parliament to amend the Constitution and the procedure therefor. It provides that an amendment may be initiated only by a bill introduced in either House of Parliament, that the bill must be passed in each House by a majority of the total membership of that House and by two-thirds of the members present and voting, and that specified federal provisions additionally require ratification by the legislatures of not fewer than half the states. After passage and, where required, ratification, the bill is presented to the president, who shall give assent, a duty the Twenty-Fourth Amendment of 1971 made obligatory.

### Q: Can Parliament amend the Constitution?

Parliament can amend the Constitution extensively but not without limit. Under Article 368 it may amend by addition, variation or repeal any provision, including the fundamental rights, provided it commands the prescribed special majorities in both Houses and, for the entrenched federal provisions, ratification by half the state legislatures. The Kesavananda verdict of 24 April 1973 confirmed that breadth, upholding the Twenty-Fourth Amendment and overruling Golaknath's bar on amending Part III. The same verdict fixed the boundary: the amending power cannot be used to damage or destroy the Constitution's basic structure, and an amendment crossing that boundary, such as clause 4 of Article 329A in 1975 or the self-entrenching clauses struck in Minerva Mills in 1980, is void.

### Q: What is judicial review of amendments?

Judicial review of amendments is the power the courts exercise, under the basic structure settlement, to test a constitutional amendment against the Constitution's basic features and to invalidate the amendment, or its offending provision, where the test fails. It differs from ordinary judicial review of legislation because its benchmark is not the constitutional text, which the amendment has itself changed, but the structure the text embodies. The power was asserted within Kesavananda itself when the bench struck the declaration clause of Article 31C, and it was exercised against an amendment for the first time in Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain in 1975, then against the Forty-Second Amendment's Article 368 clauses in Minerva Mills in 1980.

### Q: What are the components of basic structure?

No single authoritative catalogue exists, because the Kesavananda majority opinions listed the features in overlapping terms and later benches have added to the list case by case. Chief Justice S. M. Sikri named the supremacy of the Constitution, the republican and democratic form of government, the Constitution's secular character, the separation of powers and its federal character. Justices Shelat and Hegde added the dignity and freedom of the individual, the unity and integrity of the nation and the welfare state mandate. Later verdicts confirmed free and fair elections, the rule of law, judicial review, the balance between fundamental rights and directive principles, secularism and federalism, and the independence of the judiciary as structural features.

### Q: Why is basic structure doctrine controversial?

The doctrine is controversial because it limits a democratically pedigreed power by an unwritten standard administered by unelected judges. Critics, most rigorously H. M. Seervai in his Constitutional Law of India, argue that the Constitution's text confers the amending power without the limit the court inferred, that the summary signed by nine judges in April 1973 was too hastily assembled to found so large a doctrine, and that the device invites future benches to veto constitutional change on structural intuitions. Defenders reply that the inference was forced once Parliament claimed a power to amend the republic into its opposite, and that the doctrine's sparing use across five decades shows restraint rather than supremacy.

### Q: What is prospective overruling, and why did the Golaknath bench use it?

Prospective overruling is the judicial device by which a court declares a new rule for the future while leaving past transactions undisturbed. Chief Justice K. Subba Rao's majority in Golaknath v State of Punjab, on 27 February 1967, borrowed the device from American practice because the alternative was chaos: holding amendments to be law under Article 13 threatened the validity of the First, Fourth and Seventeenth Amendments and of the land reform settlement built on them across two decades. The bench therefore declared that Parliament lacked the power to amend Part III in abridgement of fundamental rights from the date of the judgment forward, while the amendments already enacted remained effective for the past.

### Q: What did Sajjan Singh decide in 1965, and which judge first raised the basic features doubt?

Sajjan Singh v State of Rajasthan, decided on 30 October 1964 and reported in 1965, upheld the Seventeenth Amendment, which had placed forty-four further statutes in the Ninth Schedule, adhering to Shankari Prasad by a majority of three to two on the Article 13 question. Its lasting importance is the doubt recorded inside the majority's own camp. Justice J. R. Mudholkar, concurring in the result, asked whether the Constitution contained basic features that even the amending power could not alter, pointing to the Preamble's promises, and Justice M. Hidayatullah expressed parallel unease about fundamental rights resting on special majorities. Mudholkar's question is the doctrine's first judicial statement, eight years before Kesavananda.

### Q: What is the difference between Golaknath and Kesavananda Bharati?

The two verdicts answer the same question with opposite constructions. Golaknath v State of Punjab, decided on 27 February 1967 by six votes to five, held that an amendment is law under Article 13 and that Parliament therefore could not amend Part III to abridge fundamental rights, protecting the rights textually and prospectively. Kesavananda, decided on 24 April 1973 by seven votes to six, overruled that holding, restored the rights to the amending power's reach and upheld the Twenty-Fourth Amendment, but limited the power structurally instead: any provision may be amended, yet the Constitution's basic structure may not be abrogated. Golaknath froze a part; Kesavananda bounded the whole.

### Q: What role did Nani Palkhivala play as counsel in Kesavananda?

Nani Palkhivala was lead counsel for the petitioners in Kesavananda Bharati, and his submissions across the hearing, which ran from 31 October 1972 to 23 March 1973, supplied the architecture of the eventual majority position. He argued that the power to amend is a power to preserve a constitution's identity while changing its provisions, that amendment excludes abrogation, and that the fundamental rights and the Preamble's promises mark the identity the power cannot touch. Palkhivala's advocacy, preserved in the Supreme Court Registry's hearing record, is the clearest example in Indian legal history of counsel's argument becoming constitutional doctrine within a single case.

### Q: Why did H. M. Seervai argue that Kesavananda was wrongly decided?

H. M. Seervai, author of the Constitutional Law of India and the most authoritative Indian constitutional commentator of his generation, hardened into opposition across the treatise's later editions. His case was textual and institutional: the Constitution confers the amending power in Article 368 without the structural limit the majority inferred, the framers' design trusted the amendment procedure rather than judicial guardianship, and the summary signed by nine judges on 24 April 1973, assembled in the verdict's final hours, could not support the doctrinal edifice later benches built on it. For Seervai, the verdict licensed courts to veto constitutional change on intuitions presented as structure, a power no text had granted.

### Q: What did T. R. Andhyarujina's 2011 book add to the Kesavananda record?

T. R. Andhyarujina's The Kesavananda Bharati Case, published in 2011, is the doctrine's insider history: its author had assisted the government's side of the 1972 to 1973 hearing as a junior to the solicitor general, and he reconstructed the courtroom contest from the record and from participants' recollections. The book documents the hearing's manoeuvres, the assembly of the nine-judge summary and the supersession of senior judges on 25 April 1973, and it argues the supremacy case against the verdict. Its unintended service to the doctrine is archival: it preserves the government's refusal, in argument, to name any limit on the amending power, the very refusal the majority opinions treated as decisive.

### Q: What does Sudhir Krishnaswamy argue about the doctrine in his 2009 study?

In Democracy and Constitutionalism in India (2009), Sudhir Krishnaswamy offers the doctrine's most systematic democratic defence. He argues that the Constitution's own design distinguishes the original constituent power that founded the document from the derivative amending power the document creates, and that a derivative power cannot legitimately unmake the conditions of the authority from which it derives. On that reading, judicial enforcement of the basic structure protects democracy's preconditions, elections, courts, federal units and rights, against capture by a temporary special majority, rather than frustrating democratic choice. The argument answers the counter-majoritarian objection on democratic theory's own ground, which is why the study anchors the defence.

### Q: How do judges identify a feature of the basic structure in a new case?

Judges work from the decided materials rather than from intuition. A feature qualifies where the Kesavananda opinions or later verdicts have recognised it, or where it can be shown to be constitutive, in the sense that the Constitution's arrangement of clauses presupposes it and its removal would leave a different document bearing the old name. In application cases the bench asks after width and effect: how deeply the amendment cuts into the feature, and whether the feature's essence survives. The Coelho bench of 2007 formalised the inquiry for immunised statutes as the rights test and the essence-of-rights test, measured against Articles 14, 19 and 21 read together.

### Q: How does the basic-structure doctrine differ from ordinary judicial review?

Ordinary judicial review measures legislation against the constitutional text: a statute that offends a fundamental right or exceeds a legislative list falls because the text forbids it. Review of a constitutional amendment cannot work that way, because the amendment has rewritten the text against which it would be measured. Basic structure review therefore measures the amendment against the Constitution's architecture, the identity produced by the text as a whole, and asks whether the change amends the Constitution or replaces it under the amendment form. The distinction explains both the doctrine's rarity in use and its depth when used: it is review of the constituent power itself, not of power exercised under the Constitution.

### Q: Has any court outside India adopted a basic-structure style limit on constitutional amendment?

The doctrine has travelled, most directly to Bangladesh, where the Supreme Court's Appellate Division in Anwar Hossain Chowdhury v Bangladesh in 1989 struck down the Eighth Amendment's dispersal of the High Court Division's benches, holding that Parliament's amending power could not destroy the Constitution's basic structures, and citing Kesavananda expressly. The underlying idea has older comparative roots in the German Basic Law's eternity clause of 1949, which places human dignity and the democratic and federal order beyond amendment by text, and in the German scholarship on implied amendment limits that circulated in the Indian argument through Dietrich Conrad's work. India's version remains distinctive for resting the limit on structure inferred by judges rather than on an eternity clause enacted by founders.

### Q: Why is federalism counted among the basic features of the Constitution?

Federalism appears in every major Kesavananda list, beginning with Chief Justice Sikri's, because the Constitution's entire distribution of power presupposes states that the Union cannot legislate out of existence. The Seventh Schedule divides legislative fields between the Union and the states, Article 368 itself requires state ratification for amendments touching the federal provisions, and the states' existence is the premise of institutions from the Rajya Sabha to the finance commission. An amending power that could abolish the states or convert the Union into a unitary state at a special majority's pleasure would not be amending the federal Constitution of 1950 but replacing it, which is the doctrine's test.

### Q: Can a referendum or a new constituent assembly remove a basic feature?

Within the Constitution's own legal order, no procedure supplied by the document, including a referendum, which the Constitution nowhere provides for, can enlarge the amending power beyond its structural limit, because every procedure the document authorises derives its authority from the document itself. A genuinely new constituent assembly stands outside that logic: it would not amend the Constitution but replace it, an act of refounding whose legitimacy would be political and revolutionary rather than constitutional. The Kesavananda majority's reasoning implies exactly this division. The amending power is bounded precisely because it is conferred; what no clause confers is the power to refound, and refounding cannot borrow the amendment procedure's authority.

### Q: How did the doctrine survive the Emergency of 1975 to 1977?

The doctrine survived because its enemies chose to fight it inside the constitutional forms. The Thirty-Ninth Amendment's election shield was struck on basic structure grounds in Raj Narain on 7 November 1975, and the government complied with the judgment's form. The Forty-Second Amendment of 1976 tried the frontal route, inserting clauses into Article 368 to bar review and declare the amending power unlimited, yet the Janata government's Forty-Fourth Amendment of 1978, which undid much of the Emergency's constitutional work, left the doctrine untouched, and Minerva Mills struck the Forty-Second's clauses on 31 July 1980. Survival by the opponents' forbearance, completed by judicial enforcement, is the doctrine's Emergency record.
