Indian secularism has been praised as a constitutional invention without parallel and condemned as a borrowed creed that never fitted the society it governed, and the two verdicts have organised the scholarly argument for half a century. Rajeev Bhargava, in The Promise of India's Secular Democracy (2010), reads the constitutional order as a distinct architecture of principled distance between state and religion. T.N. Madan, in Modern Myths, Living Truths (1997), and Ashis Nandy, in his anti-secularist essays of the 1980s, read the same order as a Western import that misconceives how religion lives in India. This article defends a third reading against both: Indian secularism is a contested settlement, a body of constitutional commitments whose meaning has been renegotiated at dated moments from the locking of the Babri Masjid in December 1949 to the citizenship contestation of 2019 and 2020, and the machinery of that renegotiation is the substance of the settlement rather than evidence of its collapse.

The claim requires groundwork, because both rival readings rest on formidable evidence. Bhargava's reading explains why the Indian state administers Hindu temples through statute, recognises a separate Muslim family law, funds minority educational institutions and reserves a power to reform religious practice from within, all without ceasing to describe itself as secular. The Madan and Nandy reading explains why that same state has repeatedly failed to hold its own stated line: a prime minister's government overturning a Supreme Court judgment on a divorced Muslim woman's maintenance in 1986, a disputed mosque demolished in December 1992 under the eyes of the state that had pledged to protect it, a citizenship statute in December 2019 that sorted claimants by religion for the first time in the republic's history. A reading of Indian secularism that cannot hold both bodies of evidence at once is a reading that has discarded half the record. The contested-settlement reading is built to hold both. It treats the constitutional text of 1949 and 1950 as a settlement among commitments that pull in different directions, it treats the post-1947 decades as a sequence of renegotiations of that settlement at identifiable moments, and it treats the courts, the elections and the street mobilisations through which renegotiation happens as the settlement's working machinery. What follows sets out the two master readings at their strongest, reads the constitutional text closely, walks through the renegotiation moments, adjudicates the disagreement, and answers the hardest objection, which is that the contestation itself may be closing.

## The Two Master Readings of Indian Secularism

The scholarly field that this pillar inherits was mapped, in its enduring form, by three bodies of work produced between 1963 and 2010. Donald Eugene Smith, an American political scientist, published India as a Secular State in 1963, thirteen years after the Constitution commenced, and gave the founding generation's project its first systematic defence: Smith argued that a secular state required freedom of religion, citizenship rights detached from religious affiliation, and the absence of a state religion, and he judged the Indian text to meet the test with qualifications he stated openly. The qualifications mattered to his successors. Smith recorded that the Constitution permitted state intervention in religious practice, protected cow slaughter legislation under its directive principles and left family law divided by religion, and he treated each as a deviation that Indian conditions might excuse but could not dissolve. The sui generis reading and the failed-Western reading both descend from Smith's ledger, one side generalising his defence, the other generalising his reservations.

Bhargava's principled distance, stated across essays from the 1990s and gathered in The Promise of India's Secular Democracy (2010), begins from a refusal of the two models that dominate Western theory. The first model, strict separation on the pattern associated with the United States, forbids the state from aiding or intervening in religion at all. The second, the established-church pattern of parts of Europe, privileges one religion in law. Bhargava argued that Indian constitutional practice fits neither, because the Indian state keeps a distance from every religion that is principled rather than fixed: the distance may widen or narrow case by case, governed by the constitutional values of freedom and equality, so the state may fund a minority school, decline to fund a temple, administer a shrine's finances, abolish a practice it judges oppressive, and support a reform movement inside a faith, without inconsistency, provided each engagement or abstention serves the stated values. On this reading the Hindu Code Acts of 1955 and 1956, which rewrote Hindu marriage, succession, adoption and maintenance law while leaving Muslim family law to a different legislative timetable, were not hypocrisy but principled distance in operation: the state intervened where a reform constituency and a legislative majority existed, and held back where they did not. Bhargava's reading gives Indian secularism a grammar. It explains the text's otherwise puzzling combination of a freedom-of-religion guarantee in Article 25, a state power to regulate secular activity associated with religion and to throw open Hindu temples in Article 25(2), denominational autonomy in Article 26, a bar on religious taxes in Article 27, graded rules for religious instruction in Article 28, minority cultural and educational rights in Articles 29 and 30, and an aspiration toward a uniform civil code placed among the unenforceable Directive Principles in Article 44.

Madan's counter-argument, built across essays from the 1980s and collected in Modern Myths, Living Truths (1997), attacks the premise that such a grammar can govern Indian life. Madan, a sociologist of religion, held that secularism in its South Asian setting is an impracticable credo because the region's religions are totalising ways of life that do not concede the private sphere the secular doctrine requires them to inhabit. Where Bhargava sees a state keeping principled distance among faiths, Madan sees a doctrine imported by a Westernised elite, written into a constitution by men and women formed in that elite's institutions, and thereafter sustained by courts and commissions while the society outside conducted its affairs in religious idioms the doctrine could not translate. Madan's sharpest formulation was sociological rather than legal: secularism, he wrote, is the dream of a minority that has tried to shape the majority's life in its own image, and the dream fails because the majority's life is lived religiously. The politician who speaks the language of faith to voters is not, on this reading, corrupting a working secular order; the politician is revealing that the order never governed the society it claimed to describe.

Nandy's essays pressed the critique further and in a different direction. In The Politics of Secularism and the Recovery of Religious Tolerance (1985) and the anti-secularist manifesto that followed it, Nandy, a political psychologist, argued that secularism as an ideology of statecraft arrived in India as part of the colonial-modern package, that it treats religion primarily as a problem of violence and backwardness to be managed by a rational state, and that in doing so it discredits the resources within India's religious traditions themselves, the everyday tolerances by which Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs, Christians and others had shared neighbourhoods, festivals and shrines for centuries. Nandy's counter-proposal was not a religious state. It was a recovery of what he called the traditional, faith-grounded tolerance of ordinary believers, which he set against the secular intellectual's tolerance, a tolerance he described as rootless and, at the limit, contemptuous of the believers it claimed to protect. Where Madan doubted that secularism could work, Nandy doubted that it deserved to, and he located the violence of the 1980s partly in the vacuum left where living traditions of coexistence had been delegitimised by a state doctrine that promised to manage religion and repeatedly failed at the task.

The two readings disagree about nearly everything except the location of the argument. Both treat Indian secularism as a doctrine whose truth is settled at the level of design: for Bhargava the design is coherent and distinct, for Madan and Nandy the design is misconceived and alien. Both, in consequence, read the post-1947 record as a test the doctrine either passes or fails. The contested-settlement reading relocates the argument. It holds that the founders did not enact a doctrine at all, in the sense of a single principle from which arrangements follow; they enacted a settlement, a negotiated allocation of powers, immunities and aspirations among parties who disagreed with one another inside the Constituent Assembly itself, and the settlement's subsequent history is not a test result but the settlement continuing to operate. The evidence for that relocation begins where both readings begin, with the text the Assembly produced between November 1948 and November 1949.

## The Constitutional Text: Articles 25 to 30 and Article 44

The religion clauses of the Constitution were debated in the Constituent Assembly chiefly in late November and early December 1948, when the Assembly took up the draft articles that became Articles 25 to 28, and again in the 1949 sessions that settled the minority provisions. The clauses were the Assembly's working answer to [the religion-state founding tension](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) that the founding generation had carried since the 1920s. The debates, preserved in Volume VII of the Constituent Assembly Debates, show a body that knew it was allocating rather than deducing. Article 25 guarantees freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality and health and to the other fundamental rights. The word propagate survived a concerted attempt to remove it. Tajamul Husain, a Muslim member from Bihar, moved in December 1948 that religion be confined to a private relation between a person and the person's creator, with propagation and public practice excluded from the guarantee; the Assembly rejected his amendment after K.M. Munshi, a member of the Drafting Committee, defended the wider wording as the price of a settlement every community could sign. Munshi's intervention is the founding generation's clearest statement of method: the clause was framed, he told the Assembly, as a compromise that recognised how religion was actually practised in India, in public and in community, rather than how a stricter doctrine would prefer it to be practised.

Article 25 then qualifies its own guarantee in the same breath. Clause (2)(a) preserves the state's power to regulate economic, financial, political or other secular activity associated with religious practice. Clause (2)(b) preserves the power to legislate for social welfare and reform and to throw open Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus. B.R. Ambedkar, steering the draft as chairman of the Drafting Committee and Law Minister, explained the breadth of the regulatory power with a candour that later courts quoted for decades: religious conceptions in India, he told the Assembly on 2 December 1948, cover every aspect of life from birth to death, and if the state were barred from legislating on anything a religion touched, Parliament would be left with almost nothing it could govern. The sentence is the settlement in miniature. The freedom is real, and the state's reserved power over the freedom's social perimeter is equally real, and the text does not rank the two in advance. It leaves the ranking to future legislatures and courts, which is to say it builds the contestation into the document.

### What did Ambedkar tell the Assembly about a uniform civil code in November 1948?

Ambedkar, speaking on 23 November 1948 for the draft article that became Article 44, told the Assembly that a uniform civil code need not be imposed on unwilling communities at the outset, and that Parliament could begin by applying such a code only to citizens who declared themselves willing to be bound by it.

He coupled the assurance with a claim about power: the authority to unify family law had to be written into the Constitution even if its exercise waited generations, because a state that lacked the power would never acquire the occasion to use it justly, and a future Parliament moved by a reform constituency should not have to amend the founding text before it could legislate.

The exchange that produced Article 44 shows the same allocation at work. The draft placed a uniform civil code among the Directive Principles, binding on no court and enforceable against no legislature. Muslim members including Mohammad Ismail and B. Pocker Sahib moved amendments in November 1948 to protect personal law from the article's reach, arguing that a community's family law, observed for centuries, could not be made a target of state unification without breaking the settlement the minority had been promised. Munshi and Ambedkar resisted the amendments and accepted the placement: the code would stand in the text as a stated aspiration of the republic, and its realisation would wait on politics. Ambedkar's assurance, recorded in the same debate, was that the article conferred a power, not a command, and that no government would be so foolish as to exercise the power against a community's settled opposition. Defenders of a strong secular reading cite the article as proof that the founders intended eventual uniformity. Defenders of the plural reading cite the assurances as proof that the founders promised the opposite. The text, read with its debates, supports the settlement account better than either: the founders wrote the aspiration and the restraint into the same clause and left their reconciliation to their successors.

Articles 26 to 30 complete the allocation on the institutional side. Article 26 gives every religious denomination the right to establish and maintain institutions, to manage its own affairs in matters of religion, and to own and administer property, again subject to public order, morality and health. Article 27 bars the state from compelling any person to pay taxes whose proceeds are specifically appropriated for the promotion or maintenance of any particular religion. Article 28 grades religious instruction by the state's distance from the institution: barred in institutions wholly maintained out of state funds, permitted on a voluntary basis in state-administered trust institutions and in state-recognised institutions receiving aid. Article 29 protects any section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture in conserving it. Article 30 gives religious and linguistic minorities the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice and bars discrimination in state aid. The detailed record of those sittings, and the reading of them as [the post-1947 plural-civilisational settlement](/2018/12/28/secularism-constituent-assembly-debates/), is treated in this series' study of the Assembly debates themselves. A constitution intending strict separation would not have written Article 30's protected sphere for minority institutions alongside Article 25(2)'s reserved power to reform majority institutions. A constitution intending an established faith would not have written Article 27. The text is a bundle of allocations among parties the Assembly could name: the reforming state, the denominations, the minorities, the individual conscience. That is the first component of the contested-settlement framework, and the framework's findable form is set out in Table 1 below.

The Assembly's handling of the word secular itself confirms the method. On 15 November 1948, K.T. Shah moved to amend the description of India in the draft's opening article to read a secular, federal, socialist union. Ambedkar opposed the amendment, arguing that to write a social philosophy into the constitutional text would be to bind future generations to one generation's preferences and to destroy the democratic premise that each electorate chooses its own arrangements. The amendment fell. The word entered the Preamble only on 3 January 1977, when the 42nd Amendment of 1976, passed during the Emergency by Indira Gandhi's government on the recommendations associated with the Swaran Singh Committee, inserted secular and socialist into the Preamble's description of the republic. The sequence matters for the adjudication that follows. The settlement's substance was enacted in 1949 without the label; the label was added twenty-seven years later by a Parliament elected in 1971 and extended beyond its term, during a period when fundamental rights stood suspended. A doctrine-centred reading must treat the label's late arrival as an embarrassment. The settlement reading treats it as data: the allocations were the settlement, the label was one more object of contestation, and the Supreme Court's later elevation of secularism into the basic structure gave the label a juridical weight its authors in 1976 could not have conferred on their own.


## Multiplicity as Content: A Settlement of Contradictory Commitments

The first component of the framework is the claim that the settlement's content is multiplicity itself. Read the operative arrangements side by side and no single principle generates them. The state administers many of Hinduism's largest temples. The Madras Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments Act of 1951, replaced by the Tamil Nadu Act of 1959, placed temple administration under a government commissioner with powers over budgets, appointments and alienation of temple property, a statutory regime whose Travancore and Andhra counterparts did comparable work, and the Tirumala Tirupati Devasthanams came under a dedicated Andhra Pradesh statute in 1987. No equivalent commission administers mosques, churches or gurdwaras. The same state that runs the temple trusts left Muslim family law under the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act of 1937, a colonial statute that directs courts to apply Shariat rules to marriage, maintenance and inheritance for Muslims, and left Christian marriage and divorce under the Indian Divorce Act of 1869 until Parliament reformed that statute in 2001. The same state rewrote Hindu family law root and branch in the [Hindu Code Acts of 1955 and 1956](/2018/12/12/hindu-code-bill-politics-1947-1956/), against ferocious opposition that included the President of the republic, Rajendra Prasad, and then declined for decades to legislate a comparable code for any other community. Bhargava's principled distance describes this pattern as a policy. The settlement reading describes it as the record of successive allocations, each produced by the balance of organised pressure, electoral arithmetic and judicial doctrine at the moment it was made, and none derivable from the others.

The judiciary built its own layer of the multiplicity in the Shirur Mutt case of 1954, Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments, Madras v Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Sri Shirur Mutt, where the Supreme Court held that a denomination's protection under Article 26 covered its religious practices, and that what counted as a religious practice was to be judged by whether the practice was essential to the religion concerned. The essential practices doctrine handed the courts the settlement's most consequential continuing power: the power to decide, case by case, which parts of a faith the Constitution protects absolutely and which parts the state may regulate as secular activity under Article 25(2)(a). Every later renegotiation by judgment, from temple entry to the Sabarimala verdict of 28 September 2018, has run through that gate. A doctrine-centred account must either celebrate the gate as principled distance working or condemn it as judges playing theologian, a charge pressed by scholars including Pratap Bhanu Mehta and by Justice Indu Malhotra in her Sabarimala dissent. The settlement account is simpler: the Assembly's text reserved the boundary question without answering it, the Court answered it with a test in 1954, and the test's outputs are renegotiations, revisable by later benches, as the nine-judge reference arising out of the Sabarimala review keeps open.

### How did the Supreme Court rule on cow slaughter bans in Mohd. Hanif Quareshi in 1958?

The Constitution Bench in Mohd. Hanif Quareshi v State of Bihar, decided in 1958, upheld total bans on the slaughter of cows and calves as a reasonable pursuit of Article 48's directive, while striking down total bans on bulls and bullocks that had ceased to be useful, as a disproportionate burden on the butchers' trade.

The judgment's structure, protection for the milch and draught animal and proportionality for the spent one, governed the field until the seven-judge bench in State of Gujarat v Mirzapur Moti Kureshi Kassab Jamat in 2005 upheld Gujarat's total prohibition and read Article 48 with an emphasis the 1958 bench had withheld, a renegotiation inside the judicial forum of the same allocation the Assembly had left to the Directive Principles.

The cow in the constitutional text illustrates multiplicity from the Directive side. Article 48 directs the state to organise agriculture and animal husbandry on modern and scientific lines and to endeavour to prohibit the slaughter of cows and calves. The direction sits in Part IV beside Article 44's civil code: two aspirations, one cherished by the Hindu majority's representatives in the Assembly and one pressed in the name of national uniformity, both made unenforceable in court, both left to legislatures. K.M. Munshi and Seth Govind Das had pressed cow protection in the Assembly with an openness about its religious meaning that embarrassed colleagues who preferred the economic idiom of cattle wealth, and the placement in the Directive Principles was the compromise between the two idioms. The settlement reading notes the symmetry that doctrine-centred readings miss. The founders handled the majority's sacred animal and the minority's family law with the same instrument, a stated direction without a judicial remedy, and the seventy years since have renegotiated the two directives at opposite speeds, one through state slaughter statutes and street enforcement, the other through a single Goan inheritance, a Uttarakhand statute of 2024 that made that state the first in the republic to enact a uniform code of its own, and repeated national debate without a national code.

The basic structure cases then fixed the settlement's outer walls. In Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala, decided on 24 April 1973 by a thirteen-judge bench that split seven to six, the Supreme Court held that Parliament's amending power under Article 368 does not extend to damaging the Constitution's basic structure, and Chief Justice S.M. Sikri's enumeration of basic features included the secular character of the Constitution. The holding did two things at once. It placed secularism beyond the reach of ordinary amendment, giving the settlement a juridical floor that no parliamentary majority could lawfully remove. It also left the content of that secular character to be specified by later benches, which is to say it constitutionalised the contestation while fencing its outer boundary. When the 42nd Amendment wrote the word secular into the Preamble in 1976, it added a label to a structure the Court had already named; when the Janata Parliament's 44th Amendment of 1978 attempted to define the term in the constitutional text as equal respect for all religions, the Rajya Sabha struck the definition out, and the label remained undefined by statute to this day in the sense that no enacted definition governs it. The floor is judicial, the content is contested, and the contest proceeds inside the fence. That arrangement is unintelligible as a doctrine and entirely intelligible as a settlement.

The nine-judge bench in S.R. Bommai v Union of India, decided on 11 March 1994, supplied the specification Kesavananda had postponed. The case arose from the dismissal of state governments under Article 356, including the Bharatiya Janata Party governments dismissed after the Ayodhya demolition of December 1992, and the bench held that secularism is a basic feature of the Constitution, that a state government which acts contrary to the constitutional commitment to secularism exposes itself to dismissal under Article 356, and that the President's satisfaction in such a case is open to judicial review. Whatever one's judgment of the dismissals, the doctrinal point for this framework is the mechanism: the settlement's meaning was renegotiated, in the aftermath of its gravest street defeat, by a court converting a political catastrophe into a rule about the terms on which governments may hold office. The parallel with [the constitutional reservation framework](/2020/02/25/constitutional-reservation-framework/) is instructive, because there too a layered text, a sequence of amendments and a nine-judge verdict in Indra Sawhney in 1992 produced an order that no single founding principle generates. In both domains the Indian method is identical: allocate in the text, fence in the case law, and renegotiate in between.

## Renegotiation by Freeze and by Reversal: Ayodhya 1949 and Shah Bano 1986

The second component of the framework is continuous renegotiation, and its first exhibit predates the Constitution's commencement by five weeks. On the night of 22 and 23 December 1949, idols of Ram were placed inside the Babri Masjid at Ayodhya, the sixteenth-century mosque whose site Hindu organisations claimed as the birthplace of the deity. Worshippers gathered at dawn in the belief, encouraged by the event's organisers, that the deity had appeared miraculously. The district administration of Faizabad, under the district magistrate K.K.K. Nayar, declined to remove the idols, judging that removal would provoke disorder graver than the trespass, and the provincial government of Govind Ballabh Pant in Lucknow let the refusal stand. Jawaharlal Nehru, the Prime Minister, pressed in writing for the idols' removal and for action against officials who had connived, and warned Pant that the episode would damage India's standing and its treatment of minorities; the removal did not happen. A Faizabad court order in the weeks that followed attached the property and appointed a receiver, the gates were locked to general entry, and a Hindu priest was permitted to continue worship of the installed idols under the receivership. The pillar's own study of that December takes up the episode's documentary record in full; the hub's concern is what the episode did to the settlement.

What it did was renegotiate the relation between the state and a disputed sacred site from administration to freeze. The executive at three levels, district, province and Union, had the legal instruments to restore the mosque and chose, at each level for stated reasons of order, inheritance or prudence, not to use them. The judiciary then converted an accomplished trespass into a managed status quo that endured, with the locks marginally loosened in 1986, until the demolition of 1992. Neither the sui generis reading nor the failed-Western reading digests the episode comfortably. For Bhargava's account, 1949 is an embarrassment of enforcement, a failure of the state to keep the distance its principles required. For the Madan and Nandy account, 1949 is proof that the doctrine never governed the society. The settlement account reads the same facts as the settlement working in its characteristic register: a conflict the text had not foreseen was allocated, by default and by design mixed together, to the forum that would hold it longest, the courts, and the allocation itself became a term of the settlement for forty-three years. The freeze was not the settlement failing to happen. The freeze was the settlement, in the form the balance of 1949 produced.

The Shah Bano episode renegotiated a different joint of the settlement, the boundary between a uniform law of maintenance and the protected sphere of Muslim personal law, and it did so through the legislature rather than the courts' custody. Shah Bano, a woman of Indore divorced after more than four decades of marriage, sought maintenance from her former husband, Mohammad Ahmed Khan, under Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, a religion-neutral provision aimed at preventing destitution. A five-judge bench of the Supreme Court, speaking through Chief Justice Y.V. Chandrachud, held on 23 April 1985 that Section 125 applied to her, that the Quran itself, properly read, obliged a husband to provide for a divorced wife unable to maintain herself, and the judgment added, in passages that travelled far beyond the holding, that a uniform civil code under Article 44 would help the cause of national integration and that it was a matter of regret the article had remained a dead letter. The judgment was, in form, a modest maintenance order. In the settlement's terms it was a judicial push on two allocated questions at once: whether the general criminal law could reach inside the personal law's territory, and whether the judiciary would press the legislature toward the Article 44 aspiration the Assembly had deliberately left unenforceable.

The response came from the two constituencies the allocation had protected and exposed. Organisations speaking for Muslim religious leadership, prominently the All India Muslim Personal Law Board, founded in 1973 and by 1985 the community's most organised voice on family law, read the judgment as a breach of the founding bargain: personal law had been left to the community, the court had overridden it in the name of a secular code provision, and the Chief Justice's excursions into Quranic interpretation and the uniform code revealed the destination. Members of Parliament from the community pressed the government of Rajiv Gandhi, whose Congress held the largest majority in the republic's history after the December 1984 election, to reverse the judgment by statute. The government first defended the judgment through its minister Arif Mohammed Khan, who argued in Parliament that the judgment accorded with Islamic law itself, and then reversed its position. Khan resigned from the ministry. Parliament enacted the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act in May 1986, confining a former husband's obligation to the iddat period following divorce and shifting longer support to the woman's relatives and the state waqf boards. The pillar's dedicated study of the episode examines the Act's text and the Danial Latifi judgment of 2001, which upheld the Act while reading the iddat obligation generously enough to restore much of the maintenance the Act had appeared to remove.

For the framework, the sequence 1985 to 1986 to 2001 is the cleanest demonstration that renegotiation is the settlement's normal condition. A court renegotiated the boundary in favour of the uniform provision. An elected legislature, responding to organised community pressure and electoral calculation in proportions historians still dispute, renegotiated it back in favour of the protected sphere. A later court renegotiated the reversal's meaning without striking it down. Each move was made by an institution the Constitution itself supplied, each was defended by its makers in the settlement's own vocabulary of rights, autonomy and equality, and the boundary that resulted was none of the three positions any single actor had sought. Bhargava's reading can describe the outcome as principled distance badly calibrated in 1986; Madan's can describe it as the doctrine's hollowness exposed. Both descriptions treat the sequence as a verdict on a design. The settlement reading treats it as the design's biography, and notes that the same three-step of judgment, statute and reinterpretation recurs across the pillar, in temple administration, in cow protection and in the citizenship contestation with which this article closes.

One further feature of 1986 deserves the hub's emphasis, because it disciplines the stance this pillar takes toward every contested episode it treats. The actors of 1986 did not divide into the secular and the communal along the lines later commentary prefers. The judgment's defenders included Muslim reformers and the government's own Muslim minister; the Act's defenders included parliamentarians who argued, in terms, that they were protecting a minority's constitutional rights under Articles 25 and 26 against majoritarian uniformity wearing a secular mask; the Act's opponents included Hindu nationalist politicians who demanded the uniform code in 1986 as an instrument of national integration and secularists who demanded it as an instrument of gender equality. Any narration that sorts these positions into virtue and prejudice before examining them has abandoned analysis for allegiance. The settlement framework's discipline is to record each position at the strength its holders gave it, to identify which allocation each position sought to move, and to let the adjudication among scholarly readings, Bhargava against Madan and Nandy against the framework defended here, proceed on that record.


## Renegotiation by Demolition and by Enforcement: 1992 and Cow Protection

The third renegotiation on the framework's list is the one that came nearest to breaking the settlement outright. On 6 December 1992, a crowd of kar sevaks assembled at Ayodhya by the Vishva Hindu Parishad, the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh and the Bharatiya Janata Party in the Ram Janmabhoomi campaign demolished the Babri Masjid over the course of a single day, in the presence of the movement's senior leadership and under the authority of a state government, Kalyan Singh's BJP ministry in Uttar Pradesh, that had assured the Supreme Court the mosque would be protected. The Union government of P.V. Narasimha Rao dismissed the state ministry that evening, and Parliament and the courts spent the following decades processing the consequences: the Liberhan Commission, appointed on 16 December 1992 under Justice M.S. Liberhan, reported on 30 June 2009 after seventeen years, and its findings on responsibility, together with the criminal proceedings that ended in acquittals by the special court in September 2020, are examined in the pillar's study of the demolition and the commission. [The politics of the demolition day itself](/2019/10/16/1992-babri-demolition-politics/) has been treated earlier in this series from the movement's side; the hub's concern is the settlement's response.

The response, characteristically, was a renegotiation conducted in three registers at once. In the judicial register, the Supreme Court in M. Ismail Faruqui v Union of India, decided on 24 October 1994, upheld the Union's acquisition of the Ayodhya site under the Acquisition of Certain Area at Ayodhya Act of 1993, while a passage in the majority judgment observing that a mosque is not an essential part of the practice of Islam supplied Hindu litigants a sentence they quoted for twenty-five years and Muslim litigants a grievance of equal longevity. In the constitutional register, the Bommai bench converted the demolition's aftermath into the doctrine described in the previous section: a state government acting against secularism could be dismissed, and the dismissal reviewed. In the electoral register, the demolition's authors were punished and rewarded in alternation, the BJP losing the state elections of 1993 in Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh and Himachal Pradesh and rising within six years to lead the Union government, its 1998 and 1999 coalitions formed on an explicit agreement to shelve the temple, the uniform civil code and Article 370 for the life of those governments. A settlement whose terms can be shelved by coalition agreement and unshelved by a later majority is not a doctrine in any useful sense. It is a standing allocation under continuous renegotiation, and 1992, the settlement's worst day, produced not its abolition but its most consequential fence, the Bommai rule, within two years.

### What principle did the Supreme Court state in M. Ismail Faruqui in 1994?

The majority in M. Ismail Faruqui v Union of India, decided on 24 October 1994, upheld Parliament's acquisition of the disputed Ayodhya area under the 1993 Act, and observed that prayer in a mosque is not an essential part of Islam unless the place itself carries a particular religious significance for the faith.

The five-judge bench in the 2019 title judgment later declined to let that observation disturb the rights it found, holding that the Faruqui reasoning, delivered in an acquisition reference, could not decide questions of title between the parties before it, and the observation's career, quoted for twenty-five years by one side and resented by the other, closed where it had begun, inside the settlement's own case law.

The fourth renegotiation on the list is the only one conducted chiefly by enforcement rather than by text or judgment: the cow-protection politics that followed the general election of 2014. The constitutional allocation, as the Quareshi judgment of 1958 recorded, left slaughter regulation to the states under cover of Article 48's directive, and the resulting map was always uneven, with total-prohibition states, regulated-slaughter states and states in the Northeast and in Kerala and West Bengal where beef consumption by sections of the population kept the trade lawful. What changed after 2014 was the register of enforcement and the political meaning attached to it. The Maharashtra Animal Preservation (Amendment) Act, which received the President's assent in March 2015 on a bill first passed two decades earlier, extended the state's slaughter ban from cows to bulls and bullocks and criminalised possession of beef; Haryana enacted a stringent statute in November 2015; the Union's environment ministry notified livestock-market rules in May 2017 that would have barred the sale of cattle for slaughter in animal markets nationally, before the Madras High Court stayed them and the Supreme Court suspended their operation. On 28 September 2015, in Bisada village in the Dadri district of Uttar Pradesh, a mob killed Mohammad Akhlaq at his home on a rumour that his family had stored beef, an event the official record processed as murder and that entered the national argument as the emblem of a new enforcement climate conducted outside the statutes altogether. The Supreme Court's response in Tehseen S. Poonawalla v Union of India, on 17 July 2018, directed every state to appoint nodal officers against mob violence and described lynching as a crime the state had a constitutional duty to prevent, a direction the Court repeated when fresh petitions reached it in 2023.

The settlement reading handles this material with the same discipline it applies to 1986. The defenders of stricter cow protection state their case in the text's own terms: Article 48 is a constitutional direction, Hindu sentiment on the cow is a matter of record reaching back to the Assembly debates, and state legislatures legislating on a State List subject are exercising an allocated power, not usurping one. The critics state theirs in equally textual terms: enforcement that falls on Muslim and Dalit traders, transporters and households converts a directive about animal husbandry into a regime of suspicion against named communities, and the gap between statute and street enforcement is where the settlement's guarantees of equality and livelihood are actually lost. Both statements identify real allocations in the text. The framework's contribution is to show that the contest between them is not an interruption of the settlement's working but an instance of it, conducted, as the framework predicts, simultaneously in legislatures, in courts and in the street, with each forum's output altering the terms on which the others act. The pillar's study of cow protection takes up the state statutes and the case record in detail; the hub records the mechanism.


## The Contestation-Architecture: Courts, Elections and Mobilisations

The third component of the framework names the machinery. Renegotiation does not happen in the abstract; it happens in three forums, each with its own tempo, and the settlement's content at any date is the sum of what the three forums last did to the allocations of 1949. The judicial forum works by precedent and operates on the scale of decades. Its foundational instrument, as the multiplicity section recorded, is the essential practices doctrine of 1954, and its characteristic product is the boundary judgment that resets a term of the settlement for every party at once. Shayara Bano v Union of India, decided on 22 August 2017, set aside the practice of instant triple talaq by a majority of three to two on a five-judge bench whose members reached the result by different routes, two judges holding the practice unconstitutional as arbitrary, one holding it un-Islamic and therefore outside the protection the personal law claimed, and two dissenting judges holding it a matter of faith the court should leave to the legislature. Parliament then enacted the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act in 2019, criminalising the pronouncement the judgment had voided. The sequence repeated the 1985 to 1986 pattern with the polarities reversed: this time the court's push and the legislature's follow-through moved the boundary in the same direction, and the community organisations that had mobilised against the court in 1986 mobilised against the statute in 2019 on the ground that a civil wrong had been converted, for one community only, into a crime. The framework does not ask the reader to cheer either sequence. It asks the reader to notice that the machinery, the forums and the moves are identical, and that a reading of Indian secularism which treats one sequence as the doctrine working and the other as the doctrine failing has mistaken its sympathies for an analysis.

The Sabarimala judgment of 28 September 2018, Indian Young Lawyers Association v State of Kerala, shows the judicial forum working at the settlement's most sensitive joint, the boundary between an individual woman's Article 25 right and a denomination's Article 26 autonomy. A five-judge bench held four to one that the exclusion of women between the ages of ten and fifty from the Sabarimala temple was not an essential practice protected by the Constitution and that the denomination's claim could not override the individual devotee's right; Justice Indu Malhotra, dissenting alone, held that in a pluralist polity the courts should not rationalise religious practice at all, and that the essential practices doctrine itself invited the judiciary into theology. The review petitions were referred in November 2019 to a larger bench, and the questions framed for that reference reach beyond one temple to the doctrine's application across faiths. The pillar's closing study takes the judgment as its subject in the religion-politics frame; the hub notes the mechanism, which is that a single boundary judgment at one shrine reopened the foundational test of 1954 for the entire settlement, and that the political response, mass protest in Kerala, an electoral penalty debated ever since, and review litigation, ran through the other two forums in parallel. Researchers reconstructing these sequences from the printed debate volumes and the provincial gazettes work through large scanned files, and a scholar comparing the Constituent Assembly record against a judgment's annexures will often keep both open through an [office file viewer for working with Excel, DOCX and PPTX records](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) rather than trust a summary, because in this field the operative sentence is usually the one the summaries omit.

### When did Goa get its uniform civil code?

Goa's uniform civil code is an inheritance rather than an enactment: the Portuguese Civil Code of 1867 continued in force after Goa's liberation in December 1961, and the Goa, Daman and Diu Administration Act of 1962 preserved the territory's existing laws until amended, so one substantially common code has governed Goans of every religion since.

The code's survival under Indian sovereignty, amended piecemeal by later legislatures rather than replaced, made Goa the standing exhibit in every national debate on Article 44: cited by the code's advocates as proof that a common family law can govern a religiously mixed population without friction, and by its critics as a colonial survival whose own inequalities, in its rules on the communion of assets and its limited recognition of certain customary usages, complicate the exhibit considerably.

The electoral forum works by majority and operates on the scale of the single Parliament. Its characteristic product is the statute or the shelving: the Muslim Women Act of 1986 from a Congress majority, the acquisition Act of 1993 from a Congress minority government legislating after a demolition, the coalition agreements of 1998 and 1999 in which the BJP set aside the three demands its movement had made central, as the price of governing with partners who would not accept them. The electoral forum also produces the settlement's rhetoric, and the rhetoric is evidence. When the Congress of the 1980s both reversed the Shah Bano judgment and permitted the unlocking of the Ayodhya site in February 1986 and the shilanyas of November 1989, its critics in the BJP named the pattern pseudo-secularism, a charge examined in the next section. When the BJP's governments after 2014 legislated the triple talaq prohibition, implemented the citizenship statute and presided over the Ayodhya temple's construction on the title judgment's foundation, its critics named the pattern majoritarian. The framework records both charges as moves within the electoral forum, each aimed at re-describing the settlement so that the next renegotiation starts from the charger's preferred baseline. Description is itself a term of the settlement, fought over like the others.

The third forum is organised mobilisation, which works by presence and operates on the scale of the campaign. The Ayodhya movement between 1984 and 1992, from the Vishva Hindu Parishad's first dharma sansads through the Ram shila pujans of 1989 and L.K. Advani's rath yatra of September and October 1990, is the forum's largest modern instance on one side of the settlement; the protests against the Citizenship (Amendment) Act between December 2019 and March 2020, of which [the CAA and NRC contestation of 2019 and 2020](/2019/08/05/caa-nrc-contestation-2019-2020/) is this series' earlier study, are its largest recent instance on another, with students, Muslim women at Shaheen Bagh and their supporters reading the Constitution's Preamble aloud at protest sites as a deliberate claim that the settlement's text belonged to them. Mobilisation rarely writes law directly. What it does is set the prices the other two forums pay: the Ayodhya movement made the temple a question no Parliament could leave alone and no court could decide cheaply, and the citizenship protests made the 2019 statute's implementation a question no executive could complete quietly, the rules under the Act being notified only in March 2024. A doctrine-centred reading sees mobilisation as pressure on the settlement from outside. The settlement reading sees it as one of the settlement's own chambers, the one without a roof, in permanent session.

## A Backward Look: What the Settlement Inherited

The framework's backward look is brief, because the pillar's first articles own the founding record, but the hub must register that the Assembly of 1948 did not invent its allocations from nothing. The Motilal Nehru Report of 1928, the Congress's first draft constitution for a free India, had already proposed joint electorates with reserved seats for minorities, a catalogue of fundamental rights including freedom of conscience, and a state without an established religion, and the Karachi Resolution of March 1931 committed the Congress to religious neutrality of the state in terms the delegates understood as a pledge to minorities. The allocations were then stress-tested by [the constitutional consequences of Partition](/2019/01/08/partition-constitutional-consequences-india/), which destroyed the political basis on which separate electorates and weightage had rested and left the Assembly of 1948 legislating for a Muslim minority reduced to roughly a tenth of the population and a Hindu majority whose spokesmen could present uniformity as nationalism and protection as generosity in the same speech. The Advisory Committee on Minorities, chaired by Vallabhbhai Patel, working within [the founding triangulation context](/2018/12/17/patel-nehru-ambedkar-founding-triangulation/) of Patel's administrative nationalism, Nehru's secular statecraft and Ambedkar's rights architecture, recommended in May 1949 that reserved seats for religious minorities in legislatures be abolished, and the Assembly accepted the recommendation, Patel telling the house that those who wished to divide the country further should find another place. The decision completed the founding allocation on representation: protection would run through rights and institutions, Articles 25 to 30, not through reserved political presence. Critics of the settlement from the minority side have treated May 1949 as the moment the majority's terms were set; its defenders treat it as the moment the settlement became a citizens' constitution rather than a communities' compact. The framework records it as the last founding renegotiation, the one that fixed the baseline the post-1947 forums inherited.


## The Vocabulary of Contestation: Equal Respect, Positive Secularism, Pseudo-Secularism

A settlement that is perpetually renegotiated acquires a vocabulary in which the renegotiations are proposed, and three phrases carry most of the load. The oldest is sarva dharma samabhava, equal respect for all religions, a phrase with Gandhian roots that the Nehruvian state adopted as its vernacular alternative to the imported word secularism. Its defenders, then and since, hold that it describes the Indian practice more honestly than separation does: the Indian state garlands the processions of every faith, its presidents and prime ministers attend the rituals of several, and its citizens experience the state not as a wall but as a presence at every community's threshold, respectful in intention if uneven in performance. Its critics answer that equal respect is precisely what the text does not promise, since the text permits the state to reform one community's law and administer another's temples, and that the phrase launders asymmetry into harmony. The Janata government's attempt in 1978 to write the phrase into the Constitution as the definition of secular failed in the Rajya Sabha, and the phrase survives as rhetoric rather than law, which the framework counts as its proper place: it is one of the baselines the parties propose, not a description of the allocation that exists.

The second phrase is positive secularism, written into the Bharatiya Janata Party's founding programme at its Bombay session of April 1980, when the party of Atal Bihari Vajpayee and L.K. Advani presented itself as heir to Gandhian socialism and to a secularism it defined against the Congress's. Positive secularism, in the party's formulation, meant sarva dharma samabhava practised without favour, and its negative counterpart in BJP polemic was the third phrase, pseudo-secularism, the charge that Congress secularism was a technique for assembling minority votes by conceding to conservative community leaderships whatever they demanded, from the Shah Bano reversal to the ban on Salman Rushdie's The Satanic Verses in October 1988, while denying comparable concessions to the majority. Advani carried the charge through the Ayodhya years and into the party's 1990s manifestos. Congress and left responses returned the charge in kind, holding that pseudo-secularism was a coinage designed to make equal citizenship sound like appeasement and to prepare the ground for a Hindu majoritarian order. The framework takes no side in the exchange because the exchange is its evidence: three phrases, each coined to re-describe the settlement, each partially true to the record its coiners selected, and the contest among the phrases is the electoral forum doing its ordinary work. What the hub insists on, against all three vocabularies, is the discipline of returning from the phrase to the allocation: which power, which immunity, which aspiration moved, in which forum, on which date.

A fourth voice in the vocabulary belongs to the communist left, whose parties held for most of the republic's history that secularism meant the strict separation of religion from the state and the equal protection of every faith's practice, and whose practice in office, in West Bengal and Kerala, ran closer to the settlement's actual allocations than to the separationist formula, administering temple boards, negotiating procession routes and accommodating community institutions like every other incumbent. The left's polemical contribution was the mirror of the BJP's: where Advani's party charged the Congress with pseudo-secularism for conceding too much to minority leaderships, the left charged the Congress with soft majoritarianism for conceding too much the other way, from the Ayodhya unlocking of 1986 onward. That the same Congress record could sustain both charges, each documented, is the vocabulary section's last piece of evidence for the framework: a government administering a contested settlement will always be describable, from opposite baselines, as betraying it in opposite directions, and the descriptions will both be true to the allocations each baseline selects.

## Adjudication: Where Each Master Reading Holds and Where It Stops

The adjudication can be stated precisely, and it follows the discipline of [the three-reading adjudication framework](/2020/02/14/mandal-decisive-moment-indian-politics/) that this series applied to the Mandal moment: name what each reading explains, name what it cannot, and let the framework that explains the most stand. Bhargava's sui generis reading explains the text. No other account makes sense of why a constitution would combine Article 25's guarantee with Article 25(2)'s reform power, Article 30's minority institutions with Article 27's tax bar, and an unenforceable civil code with an unenforceable cow directive, except Bhargava's claim that the Indian state was designed to engage religions at a distance set by principle rather than by a wall. Where the reading stops is at the word designed. The debates of November and December 1948 show men and women allocating under pressure, accepting Munshi's compromise on propagation, Ambedkar's assurances on the code, Patel's abolition of reserved seats, with the coherence Bhargava celebrates assembled retrospectively from outcomes the makers did not jointly intend. Principled distance is a rational reconstruction of the settlement's better moments, and like all rational reconstructions it must treat 1949, 1986 and 1992 as lapses from the design, external accidents befalling a sound architecture. A framework that must call the settlement's most consequential episodes accidents has located its principle in the wrong place.

The Madan and Nandy reading explains the society. No other account makes sense of why the settlement's terms keep being set by actors who speak religious idioms fluently and secular idioms haltingly, why the state's own agents, from the Faizabad district magistrate of 1949 to the Uttar Pradesh government of 1992, repeatedly declined to perform the doctrine they were sworn to, or why the most effective mass politics of the post-1947 period, the Ayodhya movement on one side and the citizenship protests on another, drew their energy from religious belonging and from a constitutional text brandished as a sacred object in its own right. Where the reading stops is at its inference from mismatch to failure. Madan's claim that secularism is impracticable in South Asia and Nandy's that it deserves replacement by the tolerance of the traditions both predict a settlement that either collapses into a religious state or survives as a hollow formula recited by courts. Neither has occurred across seven decades in which the settlement has instead done continuous work: it has supplied the terms in which a divorced woman's maintenance, a temple's entry rules, a mosque's title, a procession's route and a citizen's papers are argued, and it has supplied them to all parties, including the parties who denounce it. The Ayodhya movement's own lawyers argued title under the settlement's property law for twenty-seven years before the Supreme Court's judgment of 9 November 2019 awarded the disputed site for a temple through a trust the Union was directed to create, a judgment whose reasoning ran on limitation, possession and the Archaeological Survey's report, the settlement's most prosaic instruments. A failed import does not usually process its own supersession through its own forms for three decades. The framework's verdict is that Madan and Nandy mistook the settlement's dependence on contestation for a symptom of its unreality, where the dependence is the reality.

The contested-settlement reading, finally, must answer for its own weakest point, which is that a framework explaining every outcome as renegotiation risks explaining nothing, since no event could falsify it. The answer is that the framework makes dated, checkable claims. It claims the founding text allocates without ranking, which the debates verify or refute. It claims the renegotiations cluster in the three forums and follow the sequence of push, counter-push and codification, which the Shah Bano and triple talaq sequences verify in mirror image. It claims the outer fence, the basic structure, holds while content moves, which the fate of the 42nd Amendment's label and the 44th Amendment's failed definition jointly illustrate. And it claims, against the closure objection taken up next, that the contestation continues through the present shifts rather than ending in them. Where the reading genuinely differs from its rivals is in what it asks the reader to admire or deplore. Bhargava asks admiration for a design, Madan and Nandy ask regret for a mismatch. The settlement reading asks attention to a practice: a republic of many faiths arguing, in forums its constitution built, about the terms on which those faiths share a state, and producing, in the argument itself, the only secularism India has ever had. The comparison with [the tripartite-disentanglement framework](/2019/09/02/hindutva-three-distinct-ideologies/) is instructive, because that analysis likewise replaced a single-label argument with an anatomy of positions in motion, and with [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/), which reads the post-2014 government as renegotiating founding settlements rather than abolishing them. Across this series the pattern recurs because the polity recurs to it: India's durable arrangements are settlements, and their durability is the durability of an argument that has somewhere to go.

## The Closure Objection: Renegotiation after 2014

The strongest counter to the framework is the argument that the post-2014 decade is not another renegotiation but a closing of the account. Its evidence is formidable and must be stated at full strength. In August 2019 the Union government, acting through [the Article 370 abrogation of August 2019](/2019/07/31/article-370-abrogation-august-2019/), ended Jammu and Kashmir's special status and reorganised the state into two Union territories, removing the one constitutional arrangement in which a Muslim-majority unit's accession terms had been entrenched. In December 2019 Parliament enacted the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, introducing religion as a criterion in citizenship law for the first time, fast-tracking naturalisation for Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi and Christian migrants from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan who had entered by the end of 2014, and leaving Muslim migrants from the same countries to the ordinary process, an asymmetry the Act's defenders grounded in the theocratic character of the three named states and its critics grounded in the equality guarantee of Article 14. On 22 January 2024 the Prime Minister consecrated the Ram temple at Ayodhya on the site the 2019 judgment had awarded, in a state ceremony broadcast nationally, an act the temple movement's heirs read as the settlement of the Ayodhya question on their terms and its critics read as the state's formal embrace of one faith's triumph. Around these set pieces, litigation over the Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act of 1991, which froze the religious character of all other sites as of 15 August 1947, pressed at Gyanvapi in Varanasi and the Shahi Idgah in Mathura toward reopening settlements the 1991 Act had declared closed. On this evidence the closure argument concludes that the contestation-architecture the hub describes has been captured by one party to it, and that what continues is not contestation but consolidation.

The framework's answer distinguishes, as the brief for this pillar requires, between a settlement's terms shifting and its architecture closing. The terms have shifted, sharply, and the hub does not minimise the shift: the baseline from which renegotiation proceeds in 2024 is not the baseline of 2004 or of 1984, on citizenship, on Kashmir, on the public presence of Hindu ritual in state ceremony. But the architecture through which the shift occurred, and through which its opponents continue to act, is the settlement's own. The citizenship statute was challenged on Article 14 grounds in the Supreme Court within days of enactment, and the challenge, with the government's defence, remained the operative legal contest over the Act through its implementation in March 2024. The Article 370 action was tested for five days of hearings and upheld on 11 December 2023 by a five-judge bench that simultaneously directed the restoration of statehood and elections in Jammu and Kashmir, a direction with which the Union complied by holding assembly elections in the autumn of 2024. The Places of Worship litigation proceeds, on the record of the hearings held so far, inside a statute the courts have not struck and a 2019 judgment that called the Act a legislative instrument of the settlement's own values. Even the consecration of January 2024 stood on the foundation of a title judgment obtained through twenty-seven years of the settlement's property litigation. Closure would mean the forums no longer bite: a court that always finds for the government, an election that cannot change the terms, a mobilisation that cannot raise the price. The record shows forums biting unevenly and sometimes late, which is the settlement's historical norm rather than its negation, for the forums bit no faster for Shah Bano in 1986 or for the mosque in 1949. The framework therefore holds through the objection, with one concession recorded for the pillar's later articles to test: if the forums' outputs were to cease, over a run of cases and elections, to move any allocation in any direction but one, the contested-settlement reading would have to yield to the closure reading, and the hub's framework is written to make that test applicable rather than to evade it.

## The Pillar's Map: Ten Studies of One Settlement

This hub opens a pillar of ten studies, and the framework's use is best shown by the work it assigns them. The study of the December 1949 lock-up takes the first renegotiation at documentary depth, through the district record, the Prime Minister's correspondence and the receivership's papers. The study of the Shah Bano episode takes the second, through the judgment, the Act and the Latifi reinterpretation. Three studies carry the Ayodhya sequence at the length it demands: the movement that built the mobilisation forum between 1984 and 1992, the demolition and the Liberhan Commission that spent seventeen years naming responsibility for it, and the Places of Worship Act whose freezing of every other disputed site is the demolition's legislative shadow. The study of Gujarat in 2002 examines the settlement's gravest failure of protection and the investigations that followed it, on the same discipline of record applied to 1949 and 1992. The study of cow protection examines the enforcement renegotiation, the study of the citizenship statute the legislative one, and the study of Sabarimala the judicial one, each in the religion-politics frame the hub has built and each free to find against the hub's framework where its record requires. Every one of these subjects reaches the general reader wrapped in a verdict already supplied by the reader's own side. The pillar's promise, set by this hub, is to supply instead the record, the allocations and the dates, and to let the verdicts be argued where the settlement itself argues them, in the open and on evidence.

The method deserves one statement of its own, because it governs how the ten studies handle material on which Indian public argument is least disciplined. Each study states the episode's chronology from the primary record first, the commission reports, the judgments, the statutes and the dated correspondence, before it admits any party's characterisation of the episode, and it renders each characterisation in the strongest form its holders gave it, attributed to the named holder and the named occasion. Where the record itself is disputed, as it is for the crowd sizes of December 1992 and the casualties of 2002, the study says so and gives the competing figures with their sources rather than silently adopting one. Where a scholarly disagreement structures the subject, on secularism's meaning here, on the demolition's responsibility in the Liberhan study, on the citizenship statute's equality defect in its own study, the article adjudicates the disagreement on the evidence it has assembled rather than reporting it as a balance of opinions. The hub asks the reader to hold the pillar to that method article by article, and to notice that the method is the settlement's own: allocate the questions, name the parties, date the moves, and let the argument proceed in forums where the record can be checked. A reader who finishes the pillar with a changed verdict on any single episode will have been persuaded the way the republic itself is persuaded, slowly, on documents, against resistance. A reader who finishes it with the same verdicts held more exactly will have received what a reference work owes. Either outcome is the hub's purpose, and the framework defended here, that the contestation is the content, is offered in the same spirit: as the reading that survives the most evidence, open to replacement by any reading that survives more.


## The Settlement in the States: One Text, Many Balances

Because religious endowments, public order and much of the law of religious institutions sit in the State and Concurrent Lists, the settlement is renegotiated in twenty-eight state capitals as well as in New Delhi, and the balances struck there differ enough to refute any account that treats Indian secularism as a single national posture. Punjab carries the oldest statutory artifact of the entire field: the Sikh Gurdwaras Act of 1925, a colonial statute won by the Akali movement's agitations, which vested the management of historic gurdwaras in the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee, a body elected by Sikh voters on a religious franchise and recognised in law as the community's governing parliament for its shrines. The republic retained the arrangement untouched after 1950, and the SGPC's elections, its control of gurdwara revenues and its interventions in Punjab politics have proceeded ever since under a statute that creates, for one community in one state, an elected religious legislature with statutory personality. No doctrine of separation generates such a body. A settlement among communities, struck in 1925 and renewed by retention in 1950, does.

Tamil Nadu works the opposite side of the street. The Dravidian movement that has governed the state, in its two party forms, since 1967 built its politics on a rationalist critique of Brahminical religion associated with Periyar E.V. Ramasamy and the Self-Respect Movement, and the state's Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments administration, the most extensive temple bureaucracy in the country, governs thousands of temples whose priests' appointment the state has repeatedly tried to open beyond hereditary Brahmin lines. The Supreme Court upheld the abolition of hereditary priesthood in Seshammal v State of Tamil Nadu in 1972 while protecting the agama rules of worship, and the state's attempts in 2021 to appoint trained priests of all castes to temples produced fresh litigation on the same seam. Here the settlement's reform power under Article 25(2) is wielded by a government whose founding ideology is sceptical of the religion it administers, a configuration no other large state reproduces and that the national vocabulary of secularism describes very badly.

West Bengal under the Trinamool Congress after 2011 developed yet another balance, organised around state patronage of public festivity. The state government paid monthly allowances to imams and muezzins through the waqf board from 2012 until the Calcutta High Court struck the payments down in 2013 as an impermissible use of public funds, and from 2018 the same government made grants to community Durga Puja committees, rising year by year and defended by the Chief Minister as support for the state's largest public festival, a practice the Calcutta High Court declined to forbid while questioning its stated rationale. The sequence, subsidy to a minority's clergy struck down and subsidy to a majority's festival sustained, is the settlement's state-level arithmetic in its purest form, and it is produced not by doctrine but by the electoral forum pricing two constituencies in one state. Kerala offers the counterpoint of a settlement administered by alternating fronts of left and Congress-led coalitions for four decades, where the state's temple boards for Travancore and Cochin, inherited from the princely order, made the government the respondent when the Sabarimala judgment landed in 2018, and where the left government's attempt to implement the judgment against mass protest was followed by its return to office in 2021, the first incumbent re-election in the state in forty-four years, a result analysts attributed in part to welfare delivery outweighing the temple question. The hub draws one conclusion from the tour. Any theory of Indian secularism that cannot travel from Amritsar to Chennai to Kolkata to Thiruvananthapuram without changing its terms is a theory of a slogan. The settlement framework travels because its terms are allocations, and allocations are local by construction.

## Conversion and the Private Boundary: Where the Settlement Guards the Individual

One joint of the settlement has been renegotiated in a single direction for fifty years, and it concerns the individual's exit from a faith. Article 25's guarantee includes the word propagate, retained in 1948 against Tajamul Husain's amendment, and the word promised missionaries of every religion a constitutional space for persuasion. The states began fencing that space with the Orissa Freedom of Religion Act of 1967 and the Madhya Pradesh Dharma Swatantrya Adhiniyam of 1968, statutes that criminalised conversion by force, fraud or allurement and required official intimation of conversions performed. In Rev. Stainislaus v State of Madhya Pradesh, decided in January 1977, a Constitution Bench upheld both statutes, holding that the right to propagate did not include a right to convert another person, since conversion impinged on the freedom of conscience of the person converted. The holding is one of the settlement's most criticised, by scholars who note that it protects conscience by restricting the persuader rather than the coercion, and it remains the governing authority. The electoral forum then widened the fence in the present century: Arunachal Pradesh had legislated as early as 1978, Gujarat in 2003, Himachal Pradesh in 2006 and again in 2019, Uttarakhand in 2018, Uttar Pradesh in 2020 and 2021, Madhya Pradesh again in 2021, and Karnataka in 2022, with several of the newer statutes adding marriage to the conversion question by voiding marriages found to have been contracted for conversion and reversing the burden of proof onto the person accused. The newer statutes' treatment by the courts was still unfolding through challenges in the Gujarat and Himachal high courts and petitions in the Supreme Court.

Against this single-direction fence the judicial forum has built, in the same decades, a counter-allocation around intimate choice. In Shafin Jahan v Asokan K.M., decided in April 2018, the Supreme Court restored the marriage of Hadiya, a Kerala woman who had converted to Islam and married a Muslim man, after the Kerala High Court had annulled the marriage in proceedings begun by her father, and the Court held that the choices of faith and partner lie at the core of the liberty and dignity the Constitution protects, beyond the reach of family, community and state alike. The two lines of authority, Stainislaus and Shafin Jahan, coexist in the settlement without synthesis: the state may police the persuader's methods and even the marriage's purpose under statutes of widening severity, and the same state's highest court will strike down a family's and a high court's attempt to undo one woman's chosen faith and marriage. Doctrine-centred readings call this incoherence. The settlement reading calls it the allocation working at two joints at once, the community's boundary guarded in one forum and the individual's exit guarded in another, with the friction between the two lines of cases marking the precise spot where the next renegotiation will occur.

## The Comparative Mirror: What Indian Debates Borrow and Misborrow

The Western in the failed-Western charge deserves one section of precision, because the two Western settlements against which India is measured are themselves contested settlements of long standing, a fact the Indian argument routinely forgets. The French law of 9 December 1905 separated churches and state in a republic that had fought its Catholic Church through the Dreyfus years, and French laïcité has been renegotiated ever since, through the 2004 statute barring conspicuous religious symbols in public schools and the litigation and polemic that followed each adjustment. The American First Amendment's two religion clauses, against establishment and for free exercise, have been in productive tension since the Supreme Court nationalised them in the 1940s, with the wall of separation metaphor from Everson v Board of Education in 1947 invoked by both sides of every subsequent case. Bhargava's comparative point in The Promise of India's Secular Democracy was that India's framers, watching these systems, deliberately chose a third grammar because the Indian problem differed: the state's task was not only to keep out of the churches but to reform religions whose practices governed birth, marriage, food, dress and death for a billion people, and a wall would have disabled the reform the founders wanted. Madan's comparative point ran the other way, that the very idea of the state as referee among religions presumes a society that experiences religion as one compartment of life, which South Asia's does not.

The settlement framework absorbs both points by refusing the comparison's premise. France and the United States are not doctrines against which India fails or from which it differs; they are settlements of the same kind, older in their current forms, narrower in the range of life their texts allocate, and equally subject to renegotiation in courts and elections, as the French headscarf sequence and the American litigation over public funding of religious schools in the 2020s each show. The renegotiation in the older settlements is not a scholarly courtesy but a matter of recent record. France, having separated its churches in 1905 and barred conspicuous religious dress from its public schools in 2004, returned to the field with the statute of 24 August 2021 reinforcing respect for the principles of the Republic, which subjected religious associations to fresh declaration and funding controls after the murder of the teacher Samuel Paty in October 2020, a renegotiation conducted, exactly as in India, by statute in the aftermath of a trauma and contested in the name of the settlement's own founding values. The United States Supreme Court, in the school-funding cases of the early 2020s, moved its establishment jurisprudence toward requiring states that fund private education to fund religious schools on equal terms, reversing the practical meaning of separation for a generation of litigants without amending a word of the First Amendment. Neither republic describes these episodes as the failure of its secularism. Each describes them as its secularism being worked out, which is the description this article claims for India.

What distinguishes the Indian settlement is not a unique essence, the sui generis claim in its strong form, and not a borrowed essence, the failed-Western claim, but a unique load: no other constitutional order attempts to run a reform power over the majority's religion, a protected sphere for minority institutions, a uniform code deferred, a sacred animal deferred the other way, and an elected religious parliament for one community's shrines, all inside one text, all at once. The load explains the contestation's volume. A settlement carrying that much weight, among that many parties, in a democracy that votes constantly, will be renegotiated constantly, and the renegotiation is the sound of the structure bearing its designed load rather than cracking under an accidental one.


## The Hindutva Challenge as a Renegotiation Bid

Every settlement eventually faces a party that proposes not to renegotiate its terms but to replace its foundation, and the hub's framework is incomplete without stating that bid at its strongest, as the pillar's stance requires. The bid's intellectual charter is V.D. Savarkar's Hindutva of 1923, which defined the Hindu not by creed but by the conjunction of a common land held as fatherland and holy land together, a definition under which Muslims and Christians, whose holy lands lie outside India, could belong to the Indian nation fully only by a cultural identification the text demanded and doubted. M.S. Golwalkar's writings for the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh carried the argument into an organisational programme. The constitutional claim built on this foundation, as BJP and Sangh writers have stated it across the decades since 1980, runs as follows. The settlement of 1949, they argue, was never neutral among civilisations: it reformed the majority's law and temples while immunising the minorities' laws from the same reform, it reserved to the state the administration of Hindu shrines while leaving mosques and churches autonomous, and it taught the majority to experience its own religious identity as a disqualification in public life. On this account the uniform civil code, the temple's liberation from state administration and the end of religion-based citizenship preferences are not communal demands but the settlement's own promises, Articles 44 and 25 read without embarrassment, and the charge of pseudo-secularism names the gap between the text and its Congress-era operation.

The counter-position, stated at its strongest by the settlement's defenders in the Congress, the left parties and much of the constitutional academy, answers each limb. The reform asymmetry, they argue, followed the Assembly's own logic that the state reforms first where a reform movement inside the community has prepared the ground, as the Hindu reform movements of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries had, and the remedy for asymmetry is to extend reform by consent and legislation, as the 2019 triple talaq statute claimed to do, not to dissolve minority protections into uniformity administered by the majority's state. Temple administration, they argue, entered the statute book because Hindu endowments were vast public trusts riddled with hereditary misappropriation that the colonial courts themselves had supervised since the nineteenth century, and the denominations protected by Article 26 include Hindu maths, as Shirur Mutt itself established. The citizenship preferences of 2019, they argue, cross the line the other protections never crossed, because they distribute a right of the republic by religion rather than regulating a community's internal affairs. Between the bid and the counter-position the framework does not arbitrate a winner; it identifies the terrain. Both sides argue from the text of 1949, both claim its promises against its practice, and the forums of court, election and mobilisation have been processing the bid, demand by demand, since 1989: the temple demand resolved into a title judgment in 2019, the code demand enacted in one small state in 2024 and pending nationally, the citizenship demand enacted in 2019 and litigated since. A bid processed piecemeal through the settlement's own machinery, over thirty-five years, with each demand separately priced, is the strongest evidence the record offers that the settlement's architecture, and not any doctrine, is the durable object. Whether the piecemeal processing ends in a revised settlement or in the bid's full replacement of the foundation is the question the pillar's remaining articles, and the republic's next decades, will answer on evidence this hub cannot prejudge.

## Counting Faith: The Census, the Sachar Committee and the Politics of Numbers

One archive underwrites every modern argument about the settlement, and it is the census. The colonial enumerations from 1871 and 1872 onward counted Indians by religion with a completeness the republic retained even as it abandoned the colonial count by caste after 1931, so that the decennial census became the one official instrument through which the nation's religious composition is authoritatively known. The numbers have never been inert. The census of 2001, whose religion tables were released in September 2004, produced a national controversy when the first presentation of the data appeared to show Muslim population growth at a rate the Registrar General's office then had to contextualise with fertility differentials and corrections, an episode in which a statistical release required a public clarification within days. The religion tables of the 2011 census were released in August 2015, recording Hindus at 79.8 percent of the population and Muslims at 14.2 percent, and were received, predictably, as ammunition by every party to the settlement's argument: by those who read a slow differential in growth rates as a civilisational emergency requiring legislative answer, and by those who read the same tables, with their falling growth rates for every community including a Muslim decadal growth declining faster than the Hindu rate, as proof that demography was converging and the emergency was manufactured.

The Sachar Committee, appointed by the Manmohan Singh government in March 2005 under Justice Rajinder Sachar and reporting in November 2006, converted the counting question into the settlement's most consequential data exercise. The committee documented, from census, survey and official records, that Indian Muslims lagged the national averages in literacy, in formal employment, in representation in the civil services, the police and the judiciary, and in access to credit and to public infrastructure in Muslim-concentration districts, and it recommended an Equal Opportunity Commission and a diversity index for public institutions. The report's significance for the settlement is structural. It established that a community could hold every formal right the text confers, Articles 25 to 30 entire, and still stand measurably outside the republic's material distribution, and it forced the settlement's argument onto new ground: whether the allocations of 1949, framed as rights and immunities, required supplementing by the distributive instruments the reservation framework had built for caste. The Ranganath Misra Commission of 2007 recommended a ten percent reservation for minorities in government employment, a recommendation no government enacted, and the question remains where the committee left it, as one of the settlement's open files. The framework notes the pattern one final time. A commission's evidence, an unimplemented recommendation, a file that stays open: this is not the settlement failing to decide. It is the settlement's way of holding a question whose price, in the electoral forum, no government has yet chosen to pay, and the openness of the file is itself a term on which the contestation continues.


## The Personal Law Archipelago: Family Law as the Settlement's Quiet Core

Beneath the headline contests, the settlement's largest standing allocation is the archipelago of personal laws, the separate family-law regimes through which Indians marry, divorce, inherit and adopt according to their religion. The map was inherited from the colonial order and retained deliberately. Hindu law, reformed and codified by the four Acts of 1955 and 1956, governs Hindus and, by statutory definition, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs, with the Anand Marriage Act of 1909 separately recognising Sikh marriage rites. Muslim law runs through the Shariat Application Act of 1937 and the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act of 1939, supplemented after 1986 by the Muslim Women Act and after 2019 by the triple talaq statute. Christian marriage and divorce ran under the Indian Christian Marriage Act of 1872 and the Indian Divorce Act of 1869 until Parliament finally equalised the divorce grounds between Christian husbands and wives in 2001, a reform Christian organisations had sought for decades against their own conservative wings as much as against the state. Parsi law stands in the Parsi Marriage and Divorce Act of 1936, with its distinctive institution of Parsi matrimonial courts sitting with community delegates. Beside the archipelago the republic keeps one bridge to a common shore: the Special Marriage Act of 1954, descendant of an 1872 statute, under which any two Indians may marry in a civil ceremony regardless of religion, accepting in return a succession regime under the Indian Succession Act rather than their personal laws.

The archipelago is where the settlement's asymmetry charge and its autonomy defence meet in their most concrete form, because family law touches every citizen and its inequalities are borne overwhelmingly by women inside each system. The reform sequence has therefore run community by community, at speeds set by each community's internal politics: the Hindu code over the opposition of the republic's first President, the Christian divorce reform after a century, the Muslim adjustments by judgment in 1985, reversal in 1986, reinterpretation in 2001 and prohibition in 2017 and 2019, each step argued in the settlement's vocabulary by the women who brought the cases, Shah Bano in Indore, Shayara Bano in Uttarakhand, and by the organisations that resisted them. The judiciary has meanwhile cut across the archipelago where a practice collided with a right held in common: in Joseph Shine v Union of India, on 27 September 2018, a five-judge bench struck down the adultery offence in Section 497 of the Indian Penal Code, a provision that had treated a wife as her husband's property interest, in a judgment whose reasoning on equality and autonomy reached every personal law at once without naming any of them. The Law Commission's consultation paper on reform of family law, issued on 31 August 2018, advised that a uniform civil code was neither necessary nor desirable at that stage and recommended reforming each system from within toward gender equality, a position the twenty-second Law Commission reopened in June 2023 by inviting fresh public responses on the code. The two commissions, five years apart, mark the archipelago's present position: the Article 44 aspiration intact in the text, the expert machinery of the state unable to certify that the country wants it cashed, and the actual movement toward common standards proceeding, as it has since 1955, one community, one statute and one judgment at a time.

## Processions, Loudspeakers and Classrooms: The Settlement's Everyday Frontier

The settlement's least theorised frontier is the street, where the allocations are tested not by benches and Parliaments but by district administrations deciding, week by week, whose procession takes which route past whose shrine, at what volume, and under what police escort. The licensing of religious processions on contested routes is the oldest continuous exercise of the public-order qualification written into Article 25, and its failures punctuate the record from the Bhagalpur violence of 1989, which began around a Ramshila procession's route, to the route disputes that district magistrates still arbitrate each festival season. The law's posture was stated by the courts in a long line of cases treating processions as protected assembly subject to regulation in the interest of order, a formula that hands the operative decision to the local officer and makes the settlement's everyday content a function of district administration. Bhargava's principled distance and Madan's impracticable credo both pass over this layer, yet for most citizens the settlement is experienced precisely here, in the negotiated choreography of the Muharram route, the Ganesh immersion, the Ram Navami procession and the police lines that hold their intersections.

Sound carried the frontier into the courts. In Church of God (Full Gospel) in India v K.K.R. Majestic Colony Welfare Association, decided in 2000, the Supreme Court held that no religion prescribes prayers performed through voice amplifiers disturbing the peace of others, and that the right to practise religion does not include a right to impose sound on unwilling listeners; the noise pollution directions of 2005 fixed decibel limits and a night-hours silence that applies to every faith's loudspeakers and to none in particular. The rules lay dormant as general law until politics reactivated them, most sharply in Maharashtra in April and May 2022, when the Maharashtra Navnirman Sena's campaign against loudspeakers at mosques, answered by the state government's enforcement of the standing rules against all religious sites, converted a fifteen-year-old environmental judgment into a live renegotiation of the audible terms on which faiths share a city's air. The Karnataka hijab litigation of 2022 ran the same frontier through the classroom: after a government order of 5 February 2022 prescribed uniforms in pre-university colleges and some institutions barred students wearing hijab, the Karnataka High Court held on 15 March 2022 that the hijab was not an essential practice of Islam and that the uniform prescription was a reasonable restriction, and a two-judge bench of the Supreme Court divided in October 2022, one judge upholding the bar and one holding that the state's action failed the test of constitutional freedom, leaving the question to a larger bench. The hub records the episode, which falls within the post-2022 contestations the pillar's brief directs it to address, as the frontier's purest recent product: a district-level dress dispute that climbed, within eight months, through every forum the settlement owns and arrived, undecided, at the question the essential practices doctrine has carried since 1954, namely who may say what a faith requires. One further state experiment belongs to the same frontier: Maharashtra's Prevention and Eradication of Human Sacrifice and Other Inhuman, Evil and Aghori Practices and Black Magic Act of 2013, enacted in the weeks after the murder of its chief campaigner, the rationalist Narendra Dabholkar, on 20 August 2013, which criminalised a schedule of exploitative practices while protecting religious observance as such, the Article 25(2) reform power exercised by a state legislature against practices its own majority community produced. The frontier cases share a moral for the framework. The settlement is not only renegotiated in the great set pieces the newspapers remember. It is renegotiated continuously, at the scale of a loudspeaker and a school gate, and the accumulation of those small settlements is a large part of what the word secularism denotes in Indian life.

## Hurt Sentiment and the Law: The Settlement's Speech Frontier

A second everyday frontier runs through speech about religion, and its governing instrument is older than the Constitution. Section 295A of the Indian Penal Code was enacted in 1927, after the Lahore High Court's acquittal of the publisher of the Rangila Rasul pamphlet, a scurrilous tract on the Prophet Muhammad, exposed the absence of any offence covering deliberate outrage to religious feelings; the colonial legislature answered with a section punishing deliberate and malicious acts intended to outrage the religious feelings of any class of citizens. The Supreme Court upheld the section in Ramji Lal Modi v State of Uttar Pradesh in 1957, reading it as confined to aggravated insults perpetrated with the requisite intention and sustained in the interest of public order under Article 19(2). The section's career since then is a settlement history in itself. It has been the vehicle through which offended constituencies of every faith have sought the state's protection against books, paintings, films and speeches, and its practical operation has repeatedly inverted its stated design: the process, arrest, investigation and the threat of disorder, rather than the trial, does the work, so that the effective rule is set by the constituency most willing to mobilise, a rule scholars of the field, including its historians of the blasphemy question in South Asia, have described as a standing invitation to competitive outrage.

Three episodes mark the frontier's modern positions. In October 1988 India became the first country to bar Salman Rushdie's The Satanic Verses, the Rajiv Gandhi government prohibiting its import under the Customs Act after protests led by Muslim parliamentarians and before most of the world had heard of the book; the ban was defended as a pre-emption of disorder and attacked, by the book's defenders and by the settlement's liberal wing, as the state pricing one community's sentiment above the citizen's right to read, and the episode entered the pseudo-secularism charge sheet within the year. In February 2014 Penguin India agreed, in settlement of a civil suit brought by a Hindu educational trust, to withdraw and pulp Wendy Doniger's The Hindus: An Alternative History, a scholarly work of a decade's standing, without any court having found against it, a capitulation its critics across the world read as proof that the frontier is governed not by adjudicated rights but by the publisher's calculation of risk. In January 2015 the Tamil writer Perumal Murugan announced that the author in him was dead, after protests by caste and religious organisations in his home district over his novel Madhorubhagan drove him to an apology brokered in a state-convened peace meeting; the Madras High Court, in a judgment of July 2016 that quashed the proceedings against him and dismissed the settlement extracted from him, held that the state has a duty to protect the artist against the mob rather than to broker the artist's surrender, in language that remains the frontier's strongest judicial statement. The three episodes distribute the forums exactly as the framework predicts: the executive acting pre-emptively in 1988, the market capitulating without a judgment in 2014, and a court reclaiming the allocation in 2016, each output standing until the next constituency tests it. A doctrine of secularism has nothing particular to say about any of this, since none of it turns on the state's distance from religion. A settlement framework recognises the pattern at sight: speech about faith is one more allocation, between the speaker's Article 19(1)(a) freedom, the believer's claim to protection from deliberate outrage, and the state's interest in order, and like every other allocation in this article it is held not by being settled but by being continuously, expensively and publicly re-argued.

## Minority Institutions in Practice: Article 30 and the Education Cases

The allocation that touches the largest number of citizens after family law is education, and Article 30's guarantee, that religious and linguistic minorities may establish and administer educational institutions of their choice with no discrimination in state aid, has generated the settlement's most continuous line of case law. The line began with the Kerala Education Bill reference, on which the Supreme Court advised in 1958 that the state may regulate minority institutions in the interest of educational standards but may not regulate them out of their minority character, a formula every later case has restated and rebalanced. In St. Xavier's College v State of Gujarat in 1974 the Court struck down university provisions that would have converted a Jesuit college's governing body into a state-nominated one, holding that the right to administer is the substance of the guarantee. The eleven-judge bench in T.M.A. Pai Foundation v State of Karnataka, decided on 31 October 2002, restated the whole field: minorities hold the right to establish institutions of their choice including professional colleges, unaided institutions may set their own fee and admission arrangements subject to fairness, and the state may regulate in the national interest without annihilating the minority character, a balance the seven-judge bench in P.A. Inamdar in 2005 applied against state-imposed quotas in unaided professional colleges. The result is an allocation unknown to doctrine: a constitutionally protected sector of minority-run education, from village madrasas and mission schools to medical colleges, operating inside a regulatory state whose writ runs to standards, fairness and non-discrimination and stops, by judicial order, at the institution's character.

Two live fronts show the allocation still moving. The first is Aligarh Muslim University. In S. Azeez Basha v Union of India in 1967 the Supreme Court held that the university, having been established by a central statute of 1920, was not an institution established by the Muslim minority and so stood outside Article 30's protection, a holding Parliament attempted to answer by amending the university's Act in 1981 to declare its minority character, an amendment the Allahabad High Court struck down in 2005. On 8 November 2024 a seven-judge bench of the Supreme Court overruled Azeez Basha's test, holding that an institution does not lose minority character because its administration was formalised by statute, and remitted the university's status to a regular bench on the facts of its founding, a judgment that reset a fifty-seven-year-old term of the settlement in the minority's favour while leaving its application to a further round of the same machinery. The second front is the madrasa sector in the states. Assam's legislature voted in December 2020 to convert state-run madrasas into general schools, a measure its government defended as secularising public education and its critics attacked as the state withdrawing from Muslim institutions a recognition it continued to give others; Uttar Pradesh surveyed its unrecognised madrasas in 2022 amid litigation over the state's madrasa Act, and in October 2024 the Supreme Court stayed a national child-rights body's recommendation that state funding to madrasas be stopped, pending the hearing of challenges to the Uttar Pradesh statute, which a bench then substantially upheld in November 2024 while striking down its higher-education provisions. The sequence across one year, a seven-judge reset on the minority side and a madrasa statute largely sustained on the regulatory side, is the education allocation doing its ordinary work: neither the annihilation the minority fears nor the immunity its maximalists claim, but a line redrawn by named benches on named dates, which is to say, a settlement continuing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Indian secularism?

Indian secularism is the set of arrangements by which the Indian Constitution governs the relation between the state and religion: a guarantee of freedom of conscience and religious practice in Article 25, a reserved state power to regulate and reform religious practice, denominational autonomy in Article 26, a bar on taxes for any particular religion in Article 27, graded rules on religious instruction in Article 28, minority rights over culture and educational institutions in Articles 29 and 30, and a uniform civil code held as an unenforceable aspiration in Article 44. The word secular entered the Preamble only in 1976, and the Supreme Court treats secularism as part of the Constitution's basic structure.

### Q: Who is Rajeev Bhargava?

Rajeev Bhargava is an Indian political theorist, long associated with the Centre for the Study of Developing Societies in Delhi, whose work on secularism is the most influential scholarly defence of the Indian model. In essays from the 1990s and in The Promise of India's Secular Democracy (2010), Bhargava argued that Indian secularism operates through principled distance, a flexible relation in which the state may engage with or abstain from each religion case by case, guided by the values of freedom and equality, rather than observing the strict separation of the American model or the established church of parts of Europe. His reading treats the Indian arrangement as a distinct and defensible constitutional architecture.

### Q: What is principled distance?

Principled distance is Rajeev Bhargava's name for the relation between state and religion that he finds in the Indian Constitution. Unlike a fixed wall of separation, the distance is set case by case by principle: the state may intervene in a religion's practices to end an oppression, as it did through the Hindu Code Acts of 1955 and 1956, may support a minority's institutions under Article 30, may administer temples under statute, and may abstain where intervention would violate freedom or equality. Engagement and abstention are both permitted, provided the governing reason is a constitutional value rather than favour or hostility toward the faith concerned.

### Q: What is the failed-Western secularism critique?

The failed-Western critique is the position associated with the sociologist T.N. Madan and the political psychologist Ashis Nandy, holding that secularism in India is a Western import unsuited to Indian conditions. Madan, in Modern Myths, Living Truths (1997), argued that secularism is an impracticable credo in South Asia because the region's religions are totalising ways of life that refuse confinement to a private sphere. Nandy, in his anti-secularist essays of the 1980s, argued that the secular state discredits the tolerance carried inside religious traditions themselves and manages religion as a problem while repeatedly failing at the task. Both conclude that the doctrine, however elegant, does not describe or govern Indian society.

### Q: Is Indian secularism a Western import?

The import charge is the core of the Madan and Nandy critique, and the historical record cuts both ways. The vocabulary of secularism, the separation of religious and political authority, and the rights idiom of Articles 25 to 30 descend from European and American constitutional history, and the founding generation was formed in those literatures. The arrangements themselves, however, have no Western template: state administration of one religion's temples, statutory protection for minority educational institutions, an elected religious body for Sikh shrines under a 1925 Act, and a deferred uniform civil code form a combination no Western constitution contains. Critics call the result an import that failed to naturalise; defenders call it a translation that produced a distinct settlement.

### Q: What is the contested-settlement reading?

The contested-settlement reading, defended in this article, holds that Indian secularism is neither a unique doctrine in Rajeev Bhargava's sense nor a failed Western import in T.N. Madan's and Ashis Nandy's sense, but a settlement: a bundle of allocations among the reforming state, the denominations, the minorities and the individual conscience, written into Articles 25 to 30 and Article 44 without being ranked in advance. On this reading the settlement's meaning has been renegotiated continuously at dated moments, from the Babri Masjid locking of December 1949 through the Shah Bano reversal of 1986, the demolition of 1992 and the citizenship contestation of 2019, and the courts, elections and mobilisations that conduct the renegotiation are the settlement's substance.

### Q: How does Indian secularism differ from Western secularism?

The standard contrasts are three. Against the American model of separation, the Indian state may intervene inside religious practice, administer temples and fund minority institutions, which a strict wall forbids. Against the French model of laïcité, the Indian state does not expel religious symbols and observance from public life and maintains no general bar on religious dress in public institutions. Against both, the Indian text couples its freedom guarantees with a reform power and with directive aspirations, a uniform civil code and cow protection, that point in opposing directions. Scholars dispute whether these differences amount to a distinct model, as Rajeev Bhargava argues, or to an incoherent borrowing, as his critics argue.

### Q: What does it mean to call Indian secularism a settlement rather than a doctrine?

A doctrine generates arrangements from a principle, so its history is a record of fidelity or betrayal. A settlement allocates powers, immunities and aspirations among parties who disagree, so its history is a record of renegotiation. Calling Indian secularism a settlement means reading Articles 25 to 30 and Article 44 as allocations the Constituent Assembly made among the reforming state, the denominations, the minorities and the individual conscience, in sittings where the makers themselves disagreed, and reading the post-1947 decades as those allocations being reset at dated moments by courts, legislatures and movements. The test of a settlement is not whether it is obeyed but whether its machinery keeps processing the disputes it was built to hold.

### Q: What are the four renegotiation moments in the contested-settlement framework?

The framework names four. In December 1949 the executive and the courts froze the Babri Masjid dispute at Ayodhya by lock and receivership, renegotiating the state's relation to a disputed sacred site. In 1986 Parliament, through the Muslim Women Act, reversed the Supreme Court's Shah Bano judgment of 1985, renegotiating the boundary between general maintenance law and Muslim personal law. In December 1992 the demolition of the Babri Masjid and the constitutional response in the Bommai judgment of 1994 renegotiated the relation between religious mobilisation and state protection. After 2014, state cow-protection statutes and their street enforcement renegotiated the relation between a Directive Principle, Article 48, and the livelihoods and safety of the communities the enforcement touched.

### Q: How does the contested-settlement reading answer Rajeev Bhargava?

The reading accepts Bhargava's central observation and disputes his inference. It accepts that the Indian arrangements form a pattern no Western model generates, and that principled distance describes the pattern's better moments with precision. It disputes that the pattern was designed as a coherent doctrine, pointing to the Constituent Assembly debates of November and December 1948, where propagation survived by compromise, the uniform civil code was deferred by assurance, and reserved seats for minorities were abolished in May 1949 by a committee's recommendation rather than by deduction from a principle. Where Bhargava must treat 1949, 1986 and 1992 as lapses from a sound design, the settlement reading treats them as the design's ordinary operation, and counts that difference as the framework's advantage.

### Q: How does the contested-settlement reading answer T.N. Madan and Ashis Nandy?

The reading accepts the critics' sociology and disputes their conclusion. It accepts that religion in India is lived publicly and communally, that secularism's vocabulary arrived through colonial and Western channels, and that state agents from the Faizabad district magistrate of 1949 onward have repeatedly declined to perform the doctrine they were sworn to uphold. It disputes that mismatch between doctrine and society amounts to failure, because the settlement has done continuous work for seven decades: it supplies the terms in which maintenance, temple entry, mosque title, processions and citizenship are argued, and it supplies them to the critics' own side as readily as to the state's, as when the Ayodhya movement litigated title through the settlement's property law for twenty-seven years.

### Q: What did Ashis Nandy offer as an alternative to secularism?

Nandy proposed recovering the tolerance that lives inside India's religious traditions themselves. In The Politics of Secularism and the Recovery of Religious Tolerance (1985) and the anti-secularist writings that followed, he distinguished the faith of ordinary believers, which in his account carried centuries of practical coexistence among Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs, Christians and others sharing neighbourhoods, festivals and shrines, from religion as an ideology mobilised by parties and states. The secular intellectual's tolerance he judged rootless by comparison, a doctrine of the state that delegitimised the traditions' own resources and then failed to manage the violence it claimed to prevent. His alternative was not a religious state but a politics that would trust the believers' inherited tolerance over the managers' imported creed.

### Q: What did Donald Eugene Smith argue in India as a Secular State?

Donald Eugene Smith, an American political scientist, published India as a Secular State in 1963, the first systematic scholarly assessment of the founding settlement. Smith held that a secular state requires freedom of religion, citizenship unconditioned by religious affiliation, and the absence of an established religion, and he judged the Indian Constitution to meet the test. He stated his qualifications openly: the Constitution permitted state intervention in religious practice, protected denominational institutions, left family law divided by religion and carried cow protection among its directives. Later scholars divided along his ledger, the sui generis reading generalising his defence into Bhargava's principled distance, and the critics generalising his reservations into the failed-Western charge.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide in the triple talaq case of 2017?

In Shayara Bano v Union of India, decided on 22 August 2017, a five-judge bench set aside instant triple talaq, the practice by which a Muslim husband could divorce by a single pronouncement, by a majority of three to two. Two judges held the practice manifestly arbitrary and therefore unconstitutional under Article 14; a third held it un-Islamic and outside the personal law's protection; the two dissenting judges held it a matter of faith for the legislature rather than the court. Parliament followed with the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act of 2019, which declared the pronouncement void and made it a criminal offence, a step the judgment's supporters praised and its critics, including Muslim organisations, attacked as criminalising one community's civil wrong.

### Q: How do Indian states come to administer Hindu temples?

Temple administration descends from colonial supervision of Hindu endowments, which the Madras and Bombay governments had regulated since the early nineteenth century on the ground that temple trusts held vast public wealth under hereditary managers. After 1950 the states generalised the supervision under Article 25(2) and their legislative powers: the Madras Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments Act of 1951, replaced by the Tamil Nadu Act of 1959, created a government department with authority over temple budgets, appointments and property, and Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Kerala and other states built comparable regimes, including statutory boards for Tirupati and the Travancore and Cochin Devaswom Boards. The Supreme Court upheld the model in the Shirur Mutt case of 1954, distinguishing a denomination's protected religious practice from the secular administration of its property.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide in the Stainislaus case of 1977?

In Rev. Stainislaus v State of Madhya Pradesh, decided in January 1977, a Constitution Bench upheld the Orissa Freedom of Religion Act of 1967 and the Madhya Pradesh statute of 1968, which criminalise conversion by force, fraud or allurement. The Court held that the right to propagate religion in Article 25 does not include a right to convert another person, reasoning that conversion of another impinges on that person's freedom of conscience, which the same article protects. The judgment remains the governing authority on anti-conversion legislation and one of the most criticised in the settlement's record, since scholars argue it protects conscience by restricting the persuader rather than the coercion, but later benches have not displaced it.

### Q: What was the Hajj subsidy, and why did it end in 2018?

The Hajj subsidy was the Union government's contribution, administered through the Haj Committee of India and the national carrier, toward the airfare of Indian Muslims travelling to Mecca for the pilgrimage, a support whose origins lay in arrangements of the early republic and whose cost rose to hundreds of crores of rupees a year. In 2012 the Supreme Court directed that the subsidy be phased out within ten years. The Union government ended it in January 2018, ahead of that schedule, with the Minister for Minority Affairs stating that the funds would be redirected to the education of Muslim girls and women. Critics of the subsidy, including many Muslim organisations and parliamentarians, had long argued it benefited the airline more than the pilgrims and carried a patronage the community had not sought.

### Q: What is the waqf system, and how does the Indian state supervise it?

A waqf is a permanent dedication of property under Islamic law for religious or charitable purposes, and India holds one of the largest stocks of waqf property in the world, in mosques, graveyards, shrines and endowed land. The state supervises the system through the Waqf Act, first enacted in 1954 and replaced by a comprehensive Act in 1995, which created the Central Waqf Council and a waqf board for each state with powers to register waqf properties, audit their accounts and remove mutawallis, the managers, for misconduct, with disputes heard by waqf tribunals. The arrangement is one of the settlement's characteristic allocations: a religious institution of great antiquity administered under a modern statute, whose boards' performance and whose property record remain contested in the courts and in Parliament.

### Q: What did the Sachar Committee report of 2006 establish about Indian Muslims?

The committee chaired by Justice Rajinder Sachar, appointed in March 2005 and reporting in November 2006, documented from census, survey and official records that Indian Muslims lagged national averages across the material indices of citizenship: literacy and years of schooling, representation in the civil services, the police, the judiciary and public employment, access to bank credit, and the provision of schools, water and electricity in districts of Muslim concentration. The report recommended an Equal Opportunity Commission and a diversity index for public institutions. Its structural finding was that a community holding every formal right in Articles 25 to 30 could still stand outside the republic's material distribution, a finding that pushed the secularism argument from rights toward distribution.

### Q: Where did the charge of pseudo-secularism come from?

The charge was coined in Bharatiya Janata Party polemic against the Congress governments of the 1980s and popularised by L.K. Advani through the Ayodhya years. It named a pattern its authors assembled from the record: the reversal of the Shah Bano judgment by the Muslim Women Act of 1986, the unlocking of the Ayodhya site in February 1986 and the shilanyas permitted in November 1989 under the same Congress government, and the ban on Salman Rushdie's The Satanic Verses in October 1988, each read as a concession traded for a community's votes. Against pseudo-secularism the party set its own founding formula of 1980, positive secularism, meaning equal respect for all religions without favour. Congress and left writers returned the charge as a device for making equal citizenship sound like appeasement.
