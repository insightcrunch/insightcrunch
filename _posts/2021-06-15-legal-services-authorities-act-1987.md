The Legal Services Authorities Act 1987 rests on a bargain that most of its users never see stated plainly. In exchange for a lawyer paid by the State and a forum that can end a dispute in a single sitting, the Act asks the litigant to surrender something the ordinary court system treats as sacred: the right to carry on fighting. A person who accepts free legal services accepts representation of a quality the system does not guarantee. A person who settles at a Lok Adalat accepts an award against which no appeal lies to any court. A person who applies to a Permanent Lok Adalat accepts that a body which began by conciliating may end by deciding the dispute against them, and that the door of the ordinary court closed behind them when the application was filed. The statute is generous and final at the same time, and it cannot be understood, or used well, unless both halves of that sentence are kept in view.

![The Legal Services Authorities Act 1987, showing who is entitled to free legal services and how Lok Adalats work - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-67.webp)

That double character is the subject of this article. The Act does two things that no other Indian statute does together. It converts the constitutional promise of free legal aid into an enforceable statutory entitlement, administered by a permanent judiciary-led machinery that runs from the Supreme Court down to the taluk. And it gives statutory form to the Lok Adalat, the people's court, together with its harder-edged sibling, the Permanent Lok Adalat for public utility services. The first half of the statute is about getting a citizen into the legal system. The second half is about getting disputes out of it. The tension between those two purposes, entry and exit, access and closure, runs through every chapter of the Act and through every criticism that has been made of it.

A reader who finishes this article should be able to answer the practical questions the statute actually generates. Who is entitled to free legal services, in full, category by category, and does entitlement depend on income at all for most of the listed classes? Where does an application go, who decides it, and on what test? What is the difference between the lawyer the Act provides and the lawyer a litigant hires? What exactly happens at a Lok Adalat, what can it decide, and why is its award the one decision in the Indian legal system from which no appeal lies? And what is a Permanent Lok Adalat, the body that shares the name but not the method, since it may impose a decision on parties who never agreed to one? Those questions, answered precisely, are the payload. The institutional history, the case law on finality, and the argument about quality are the frame that makes the answers intelligible.

## The promise before the statute: Article 39A and the road to 1987

The Act was not Parliament's first thought on the subject. It was the point at which several decades of constitutional text, judicial pressure, and committee recommendation finally hardened into machinery.

The constitutional text came first. Article 39A was inserted into the Directive Principles of State Policy by the Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976, one of the changes carried on the recommendations of the Swaran Singh Committee, and it directs the State to secure that the operation of the legal system promotes justice on a basis of equal opportunity, and in particular to provide free legal aid, by suitable legislation or schemes or in any other way, so that opportunities for securing justice are not denied to any citizen by reason of economic or other disabilities. The language matters. A directive principle is not enforceable in court, and Article 39A deliberately leaves the instrument open: legislation, schemes, or any other way. For nearly two decades the State chose schemes. The Act is what happened when Parliament at last chose legislation, and the preamble of the Act tracks Article 39A almost word for word, which is why the statute reads as the directive principle given organs and a budget.

The courts, meanwhile, had been building a parallel and enforceable route to the same destination through Article 21. In M.H. Hoskot v. State of Maharashtra, decided in 1978, the Supreme Court treated free legal services for a prisoner who could not engage a lawyer as part of the fair procedure that Article 21 guarantees. In Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar, the proceedings that began in 1979 on behalf of undertrial prisoners who had spent longer in custody awaiting trial than the maximum sentence for the charges against them, the Court read the right to a speedy trial and to free legal aid into Article 21 itself, holding that a procedure which keeps a person in prison for years without trial and without representation is not reasonable, just or fair. Khatri v. State of Bihar, in 1981, added the operational duty: a magistrate or sessions judge is obliged to inform an accused person who cannot afford counsel of the right to free legal services at State cost, and the obligation does not depend on the accused asking. Suk Das v. Union Territory of Arunachal Pradesh, in 1986, supplied the consequence: a conviction recorded where the accused was denied the offer of free legal assistance, in a trial where he faced imprisonment, was vitiated. Taken together, these decisions meant that by the middle of the nineteen eighties the right to legal aid in criminal proceedings had a constitutional floor beneath it. What it did not have was a delivery system. A right declared in a judgment still needs an office, a budget line, a panel of lawyers, and a person whose job it is to notice that an unrepresented undertrial exists.

The committee route supplied the blueprint and exposed the delivery problem. A committee chaired by Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer reported in 1973 under the title Processual Justice to the People, arguing that legal aid in India had to be organised as a public service reaching beyond courtroom representation to advice, conciliation and preventive law. A committee chaired by Justice P.N. Bhagwati followed in 1977 with the report National Juridicare: Equal Justice, Social Justice, which recommended a statutory, nationwide structure of legal aid committees supported by public funds. The Union Government constituted the Committee for Implementing Legal Aid Schemes in 1980, with Justice Bhagwati as its chairman, to run legal aid programmes on an executive footing across the country. The schemes that followed did real work: legal aid and advice boards in the States, Lok Adalats run as experiments, aid extended to categories of litigants. But executive schemes share three weaknesses that the record of those years displays. They depend on annual administrative goodwill and can be restructured or starved without any legislature voting on the change. They vary from State to State without any floor of uniformity, so the content of a citizen's right to assistance changed at a State border. And they create no entitlement a citizen can demand: a scheme confers a benefit at discretion, while a statute confers a claim. The case for the 1987 Act was, at bottom, the case for converting discretion into entitlement.

The fundamental rights chapter supplied a narrower but sharper guarantee of its own. Article 22(1) gives a person who is arrested the right to consult, and to be defended by, a legal practitioner of his choice. For an arrested person without money the choice is empty unless someone else pays, and the gap between the Article's promise and the prisoner's means is precisely the gap the legal aid machinery was built to close. The courts noticed the gap early and repeatedly, and it is no accident that so much of the foundational legal aid case law concerns undertrials: the arrested person is the citizen most completely in the State's power and least able to reach a lawyer unaided.

Two further strands fed into the statute. The Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 already contained, in Section 304, a provision for legal aid to an accused at State expense in trials before a court of session where the accused lacked sufficient means, with power for the High Court to extend the arrangement to other classes of trial. That provision was narrow, confined to one procedural setting, and dependent on rules framed by each High Court, but it established the principle inside the procedural codes that the State pays for the defence of the undefended. Its successor in the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 carries the same principle in Section 341. Its limits were equally instructive. The ordinary run of criminal trials takes place before magistrates, not sessions courts, and an accused in a magistrate's court charged with an offence carrying imprisonment had no statutory claim under the Code itself; whatever reached him arrived through the executive schemes and the constitutional case law, unevenly. A general entitlement Act, applying to every court and both civil and criminal business, was the obvious completion of the structure, and the preamble of the 1987 Act is drafted in exactly that general register. The second strand was the Lok Adalat experiment itself. The first modern Lok Adalat was held in Gujarat in 1982, in the Junagadh district, as a camp at which motor accident claims and petty matters were settled in a day by persuasion, and the sittings spread through the nineteen eighties as a voluntary, non-statutory forum in which serving and retired judges, lawyers and social workers persuaded parties to settle pending cases on the spot. Gujarat's legal aid board and the State judiciary made the experiment a programme, other States copied it, and the Committee for Implementing Legal Aid Schemes promoted it nationally, so that by the time Parliament legislated, the Lok Adalat was not a proposal to be debated in the abstract but a going concern whose methods, strengths and limits were observable facts of legal life. The experiment demonstrated an appetite for settlement-based disposal that the formal system could not satisfy, but a Lok Adalat award before 1987 had no statutory force of its own; a settlement had to be recorded as a compromise in the pending suit to bind anyone. The Act absorbed both strands: it generalised the legal aid principle across civil and criminal matters and every level of court, and it gave the Lok Adalat's award the force of a decree.

## Enactment, amendment before commencement, and the eight-year delay

The Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 is Act 39 of 1987. Its long title states its two objects in a single sentence: to constitute legal services authorities to provide free and competent legal services to the weaker sections of society, so that opportunities for securing justice are not denied to any citizen by reason of economic or other disabilities, and to organise Lok Adalats to secure that the operation of the legal system promotes justice on a basis of equal opportunity. The Act extends to the whole of India. Its structure, as it stands after amendment, runs to seven chapters. Chapter I contains the preliminaries and definitions. Chapter II constitutes the National Legal Services Authority. Chapter III constitutes the State Legal Services Authorities, together with the High Court Legal Services Committees and the District and Taluk machinery. Chapter IV, the shortest and in human terms the most important, states who is entitled to legal services and on what conditions. Chapter V deals with finance: grants, and the National, State and District Legal Aid Funds. Chapter VI contains the Lok Adalats. Chapter VIA, inserted later, contains the Permanent Lok Adalats. Chapter VII holds the miscellaneous provisions, including the rule-making powers.

Between enactment and commencement, the Act was overtaken by its own amendment. Parliament passed the Legal Services Authorities (Amendment) Act, 1994, Act 59 of 1994, which recast parts of the original text before the original text had spent a single day in force: it refined the definitions, supplied the Supreme Court Legal Services Committee and the High Court Legal Services Committee in Sections 3A and 8A, and inserted the Taluk Legal Services Committees in Section 11B, completing the four-level structure the Act is known by. The delay itself is part of the statute's meaning. A legal aid statute that waits eight years for commencement, and is amended in the interval, records the distance between the ease of declaring a welfare entitlement and the difficulty of funding and staffing it. The pattern recurs across Indian social legislation, and this series has met it repeatedly: enactment is a legislative act, but commencement is an administrative one, and the second does not follow automatically from the first.

### When did the Legal Services Authorities Act come into force?

The whole Act except Chapter III was brought into force on 9 November 1995 by notification S.O. 893(E), and the National Legal Services Authority was constituted on 5 December 1995. Chapter III, which contains the State Authority provisions, was the stated exception in the commencement notification.

The date has acquired a commemorative life of its own: 9 November is observed as National Legal Services Day, marked by the legal services institutions with awareness programmes, which is a fitting use for the anniversary of a statute whose largest practical problem is that the people entitled under it often do not know it exists. The exception for Chapter III is worth pausing on, because it is commonly flattened in summary accounts into the statement that the Act commenced in 1995, full stop. Section 1(3) permits different dates to be appointed for different provisions and for different States, and the 1995 notification used that power to hold back the chapter constituting the State Authorities while the national tier and the entitlement and Lok Adalat chapters took effect. The architecture therefore came alive in sequence rather than at once, which is the normal biography of a statute that builds institutions rather than merely prohibiting conduct.

The 1994 amendment rewards a closer look, since it is the real founding document of the institution as it operates. The Act of 1987, as enacted, sketched the national and State tiers and the Lok Adalat, but the committee structure that connects the system to the individual courts was incomplete: the apex and the High Courts had no dedicated committees in the text, and the taluk, the level closest to the rural litigant, had no institution at all. The Amendment Act of 1994 supplied all three, and also recast the definitions with which the Act works, including the definition of legal service that fixes the content of the entitlement. The sequence carries a generalisable lesson about Indian welfare legislation of the period. The first enactment establishes the principle and the headline institutions and passes with the credit for both; the amendment, unglamorous and unremembered, builds the parts of the machine that touch the public; and commencement waits for the second text, because commencing the first would have created authorities with no committees through which to work. Readers of this series will recognise the pattern from statutes far removed from legal aid, and it is the correct frame for the eight-year delay: the delay was not eight years of neglect of a finished design, but the interval in which the design was finished.

A second amendment completed the Act's present shape. The Legal Services Authorities (Amendment) Act, 2002, Act 37 of 2002, inserted Chapter VIA, Sections 22A to 22E, creating the Permanent Lok Adalats for public utility services. That insertion is examined in its own part of this article, because it quietly changed the character of the statute: until 2002, every outcome the Act produced rested on consent, and after 2002 one of its forums could decide against a party who had never consented to its decision. A reader tracking the argument of this article, that the Act trades appellate rights for speed and access, should register 2002 as the year the trade became explicit in the text.

## The machinery: a judiciary-led hierarchy in four tiers

The Act builds its delivery system as a hierarchy of authorities and committees that mirrors the court structure it serves. The wider map of courts, tribunals and forums within which this machinery sits is drawn in the series guide to [the judicial system laws of India](/2021/04/01/judicial-system-laws-india-guide/), and the legal services institutions are best understood as a parallel structure laid alongside that map, one institution for each level of court, staffed substantially by the judiciary itself.

At the apex stands the National Legal Services Authority, constituted under Section 3. Its design announces the Act's governing idea. The Chief Justice of India is its Patron-in-Chief. Its Executive Chairman is a serving or retired judge of the Supreme Court nominated by the President in consultation with the Chief Justice of India, and its membership draws in the senior administration of justice and of government, including the secretaries responsible for law and for expenditure. A Member Secretary, drawn from the Indian Judicial Service of a senior rank, runs its administration. Section 4 assigns the Central Authority the functions that belong to a national tier: laying down policies and principles for making legal services available under the Act, framing the most effective and economical schemes for legal services, disbursing funds to the State Authorities, running legal literacy and public awareness programmes, encouraging settlement of disputes by negotiation, arbitration and conciliation, and evaluating the implementation of its own schemes. The national authority is, in other words, a policy and standards body with a budget, not a case-handling office. No citizen applies to it for a lawyer. Its presence is felt one level down, in the schemes and directions that govern what the lower tiers must do.

Section 3A constitutes the Supreme Court Legal Services Committee, which carries the Act's work into the one forum the district machinery cannot reach: litigation before the Supreme Court itself. The Committee, chaired by a sitting judge of the Supreme Court, regulates legal services for cases before that Court, administers applications from litigants entitled under the Act, and applies the income ceiling prescribed for Supreme Court matters, which is separately fixed from the State ceilings and stands at five lakh rupees. The existence of a distinct committee with a distinct ceiling for the apex court is a small provision with a large implication: entitlement under the Act follows the litigant up the appellate ladder, and the statute anticipates that a person aided in a district court may need aid again, from a different body, applying a different income figure, in Delhi.

The State Legal Services Authority, constituted under Section 6 for every State, replicates the national design at the level where most legal services are organised. The Chief Justice of the High Court is its Patron-in-Chief; a serving or retired High Court judge nominated in consultation with the Chief Justice is its Executive Chairman; the District Authorities within the State work under its direction. Its functions under Section 7 are the operational core of the Act in each State: giving effect to the policies of the Central Authority, giving legal service to persons who satisfy the criteria laid down in the Act, conducting and organising Lok Adalats, undertaking preventive and strategic legal aid programmes, and running legal literacy work. Section 8 requires the State Authority to act in coordination with government agencies, non-governmental voluntary organisations, universities and other bodies engaged in promoting legal services and literacy. Section 8A constitutes a High Court Legal Services Committee for every High Court, chaired by a sitting judge of that Court, to do for High Court litigation what the Supreme Court Committee does at the apex.

The District Legal Services Authority, constituted under Section 9 in every district, is where the Act meets the public. Its chairman is the District Judge, and its secretary, a judicial officer of the subordinate judiciary posted full time to the work, runs its office. Its functions under Section 10 include coordinating the activities of the Taluk Committees and other legal services bodies in the district, organising Lok Adalats within the district, and performing the functions fixed for it by the State Authority. The district authority maintains the panels of advocates from whom legal aid counsel are assigned, runs the front office at which applications are received, maintains legal services clinics, supervises para-legal volunteers, and administers the District Legal Aid Fund under Chapter V. If the Act has a single point of contact with the citizen, it is this office, usually located in or beside the district court complex. Section 11 requires the District Authority, like the State Authority above it, to work in coordination with local agencies and voluntary bodies rather than as a sealed bureaucracy.

Between the tiers, and easy to overlook in a structural account, sits the cadre that makes the tiers function: the secretaries. The Central Authority has a Member Secretary of senior judicial rank, each State Authority a Member Secretary, and each District Authority a secretary who is a full-time judicial officer of the subordinate judiciary, and in the daily life of the institution it is the secretary, not the patron or the chairman, who is the authority for most purposes an applicant or a panel lawyer will ever encounter. The secretary receives and decides applications, maintains the panels, convenes the Lok Adalats, supervises the front office, the clinics and the para-legal volunteers, administers the fund at that level, and answers upward for the district's returns. The design consequence is that the institution's quality map is, in substance, a map of its district secretaries: a district with an able secretary posted for a reasonable tenure has a working legal aid system whatever the State average, and a district in which the secretaryship rotates as a charge held in passing has a notice board. Judicial officers bring to the post the authority of the court and the confidence of the bar, which the work needs, and the institution pays for those advantages with the discontinuity of judicial transfers, a rhythm set by the judiciary's own administrative needs rather than by the legal services calendar. No amendment has attempted to professionalise the cadre into a separate service, and the choice, made by default and renewed by practice, keeps the institution judicial in character at the cost of making its continuity depend on postings it does not control.

The fourth tier is the Taluk Legal Services Committee, constituted under Section 11B for each taluk or mandal, or for a group of them. The senior civil judge operating within the committee's jurisdiction is its ex officio chairman. Its functions are to coordinate the activities of legal services in the taluk, organise Lok Adalats within its jurisdiction, and perform the functions assigned by the District Authority. The taluk tier matters out of proportion to its size, because distance is one of the disabilities the Act exists to counteract: a litigant in a village far from the district headquarters has a legal services institution, however small, within reach of a local journey rather than a day's travel.

### Who heads the National Legal Services Authority?

The Chief Justice of India is the Patron-in-Chief of the National Legal Services Authority under Section 3, and a serving or retired Supreme Court judge nominated by the President in consultation with the Chief Justice serves as its Executive Chairman and directs its working.

The judiciary-led character of the whole structure deserves analysis rather than mere description, because it is a deliberate institutional choice with consequences in both directions. The argument for it is strong. Legal aid schemes run as ordinary executive programmes had suffered from the weakness that the institutions meant to receive the aided litigant, the courts, treated the scheme as an outside imposition; a machinery chaired by judges carries authority inside the court system, can convene Lok Adalats that courts will feed with cases, and can commit the subordinate judiciary's administrative machinery to the work. The presence of the Chief Justice of India and the Chief Justices of the High Courts at the head of the respective tiers gives the legal services movement a constitutional visibility no department of government could supply. The argument on the other side is structural. The same institution that must decide cases impartially also administers the settlement machinery that disposes of them, and the pressure to show disposals, which is real and measurable, is generated inside the judiciary itself; critics of Lok Adalat practice make exactly this point when they describe settlement pressure at single-day sittings. There is also a capacity cost: district judges and senior civil judges carry legal services administration on top of their judicial work, supported by a small secretariat, and the quality of the local institution tracks the energy of individual officers more than any national standard. The Act's answer to both criticisms is not separation but transparency of design: the settlement forum decides nothing without consent, and the entitlement chapter gives the citizen a claim that does not depend on official enthusiasm. Whether that answer suffices is taken up in the assessment at the end of this article.

Chapter V, the finance chapter, deserves a paragraph because money is where entitlement statutes quietly fail. Section 14 provides for grants by the Central Government to the Central Authority. Section 15 constitutes the National Legal Aid Fund, into which grants, donations and sums received under court orders are credited and from which the costs of legal services, including the expenses of the State Authorities, are met. Sections 16 and 17 replicate the arrangement at State and district level with the State Legal Aid Fund and the District Legal Aid Fund. Section 18 requires accounts and audit. The structure is sound and the flow is logical, Centre to State to district, but the adequacy of the sums that travel down it has been the subject of sustained criticism, recorded later in this article, and a reader should hold the distinction the chapter itself creates: the Act guarantees the channel, not the volume that passes through it. The channel metaphor should be pressed one step further, because the finance chapter explains a recurring feature of the institution's public record, the coexistence of unspent balances in some authorities with threadbare provision in others. Money reaches a district authority against schemes and returns; a district with an energetic secretary and a functioning clinic network draws down funds that a dormant district leaves idle, and the deficiency is then administrative rather than budgetary. Assessments of legal aid spending that stop at the size of the total allocation therefore miss the mechanism. The Act built a pipe of the right shape. What flows through it depends on the Union and State budgets at the top and on institutional energy at the bottom, and both variables sit outside the statute's control, which is the deepest structural fact about the whole enterprise.

## Entitlement: the eight doors in Section 12

Chapter IV consists of two sections, and the first of them, Section 12, is the most valuable text in the Act for the ordinary reader, because it answers the question every potential user asks first: does this apply to me? The section provides that every State Authority, District Authority, Supreme Court Legal Services Committee, High Court Legal Services Committee and Taluk Committee shall be deemed to be under an obligation to give legal services to a person who satisfies the criteria it lists, and it then lists the classes of person entitled. The classes must be set out in full, because summary accounts habitually truncate them and the truncations always omit the categories readers least expect to find.

A member of a Scheduled Caste or a Scheduled Tribe is entitled, without any inquiry into income. A victim of trafficking in human beings or of begar, as referred to in Article 23 of the Constitution, is entitled. A woman is entitled, and a child is entitled: the statute makes no distinction between a woman of means and a woman without means, and the entitlement of a child attaches to the child's status, not to the parents' resources. A person who is mentally ill or otherwise disabled, in the Act's own words, is entitled. A person under circumstances of undeserved want is entitled, and the section illustrates the phrase with a catalogue of calamity: being a victim of a mass disaster, of ethnic violence, of caste atrocity, of flood, drought, earthquake or industrial disaster. An industrial workman is entitled. A person in custody is entitled, and the section spells out that custody includes custody in a protective home of the kind maintained for women under the legislation against immoral traffic, in a juvenile home or observation home for children in conflict with the law or in need of care, and in a psychiatric hospital or psychiatric nursing home. The breadth of the custody category is the provision's quiet radicalism: it reaches persons whose liberty is restricted by welfare and medical institutions as well as by prisons, on the reasoning that a person who cannot leave a building cannot walk to a lawyer either.

The eighth door is the income door, and it is the only one that turns on means. A person in receipt of annual income less than the figure the clause specifies is entitled, where the case is before a court other than the Supreme Court, and less than the separately specified figure where the case is before the Supreme Court. The clause as enacted wrote the figures into the statute itself, nine thousand rupees for cases before other courts and twelve thousand rupees for the Supreme Court, and then empowered each government to prescribe a higher amount: the State Government for its courts, the Central Government for the Supreme Court. The prescribed figures have long since displaced the enacted ones. The ceiling for Supreme Court matters stands at five lakh rupees of annual income. The State ceilings vary, and vary widely, because each State Government prescribes for its own courts and revisions have proceeded at different speeds in different States. The single most common error in published accounts of this Act is the statement of one income limit for the whole country; there is none, and a reader who needs the figure must take it from the legal services authority of the State whose court is seized of the matter. The design is defensible on federal principle, since the cost of living and the fiscal capacity of States differ, but it produces a genuine inequality of access between States which the Act accepts as the price of State prescription.

Two features of Section 12 transform it from a list into an entitlement. The first is that entitlement in the seven status categories does not depend on income at all. A Scheduled Caste litigant of substantial means is entitled as a matter of statutory text; a woman defending a maintenance claim is entitled whatever her salary; an undertrial in custody is entitled because he is in custody. The means test governs only those who enter through the eighth door. Public understanding lags the text here, and much awareness work by the legal services institutions consists of correcting the assumption that legal aid is only for the destitute. The second feature is that the section is cast as a duty. The authorities shall be deemed to be under an obligation to give legal services to a person satisfying the criteria. Section 13 then states the conditions on which the duty is engaged, and the conditions are deliberately light.

Section 13 provides that a person satisfying the Section 12 criteria shall be entitled to legal services if the authority concerned is satisfied that the person has a prima facie case to prosecute or to defend, and that the person's means, where means are in issue, may be established by an affidavit, which the authority shall accept as sufficient unless it has reason to disbelieve it. Each element repays attention. The prima facie case condition is a screening condition, not a merits trial: it exists so that public funds are not committed to claims that disclose no arguable case at all, and it is applied by the authority, in practice by the secretary of the District Authority or the committee concerned, on the papers presented. The affidavit rule is an access rule disguised as an evidence rule. Requiring documentary proof of income from the class of persons least likely to possess salary slips, bank records or tax filings would defeat the section; the Act therefore reverses the ordinary burden and directs the authority to accept the applicant's sworn statement unless there is positive reason to doubt it. An applicant whose statement is disbelieved, or whose case is judged to disclose no prima facie merit, is not left without recourse: the regulations governing the authorities provide for the decision to be revisited within the institution, and the refusal chapter of this article's frequently asked questions takes up the practical position. What the Act does not contain is any power in the authority to refuse a qualifying applicant on the ground that its budget for the year is exhausted. The entitlement is drafted against the State, and scarcity is handled in practice by queue, panel strength and quality, which is one of the routes by which the quality problem enters a system whose legal design is generous.

What, once granted, does the entitlement buy? Section 2(1)(c) defines legal service to include the rendering of any service in the conduct of any case or other legal proceeding before any court or other authority or tribunal, and the giving of advice on any legal matter. Regulations and schemes made under the Act fill the definition out into the recognised modes of aid: representation by an advocate in court proceedings, payment of court fees and process fees and the other charges payable in connection with the proceedings, the cost of drafting and preparing documents, certified copies and paper books, and legal advice short of litigation. The modes matter because legal aid is often imagined as a single thing, a lawyer in a courtroom, when the statute contemplates a spectrum running from a consultation at a legal services clinic, through help with an application or a notice, to full representation at trial and on appeal. Advice is frequently the highest-value service in the set: a person told at the outset that a claim belongs in a consumer commission, or that a dispute is headed for a Permanent Lok Adalat whether they wish it or not, has been saved the costliest errors litigation offers.

One function of the authorities sits between advice and representation and should not pass unnoticed, because Section 7 names it and it distinguishes this institution from a mere assigning office: preventive and strategic legal aid. Preventive work aims at the dispute before it hardens, the legal literacy camp that tells a village about succession rights before the family partition turns litigious, the clinic intervention that produces a corrected land record or a released welfare payment without any filing at all. Strategic work selects the case that will decide a question for a class: a recurring illegal deduction by employers of a category of workers, a welfare benefit denied on a uniform misreading, a pattern of remand practice in a district. The authorities are empowered to support litigation of that character, and the national authority's schemes direct district offices to identify such patterns in their intake. The concept matters to an assessment of the Act because it shows the framers' institution escaping the case-by-case model in both directions at once, upstream into prevention and upward into precedent, even if the resources devoted to these functions remain the first casualty of a pressed district budget.

Entitlement is personal, not partisan, and one consequence follows that surprises lay readers: both sides of the same dispute may be aided by the same authority at the same time. A woman claiming maintenance and the industrial workman defending the claim may each satisfy Section 12 on independent grounds, and where each applies and shows a prima facie position, the District Authority assigns different panel counsel to each, the institution funding the contest it exists to make fair rather than funding a side. The arrangement is the entitlement logic carried through honestly: the Act's concern is that no party be unrepresented for want of means, not that the authority pre-judge which party deserves help, and the prima facie screen, applied to each application separately, is the only filter the statute interposes. In criminal proceedings the same logic appears in a sterner form, since the victim's entitlement to representation and compensation assistance coexists with the accused's entitlement to a defence, and the authority administers both without embarrassment, the two functions being discharged through different counsel and, where the defence counsel system operates, through different offices altogether. An institution that can fund both the prosecution's witness and the prisoner's defence in the same week has understood its own statute: it is infrastructure for adversarial justice, not a party to it.

The criminal side of the entitlement carries a further layer from the case law. The Supreme Court's decisions under Article 21, described in the opening part of this article, oblige courts to inform an unrepresented accused of the right to free representation, and the legal services machinery is the instrument through which that obligation is discharged: remand advocates are posted at courts to appear for persons produced in custody, duty counsel arrangements cover the first production, and panels supply trial counsel. Legal aid under the Act is also not confined to defendants and accused persons. Victims of crime are entitled within the categories, legal services are provided in proceedings for compensation, and the authorities run specialised schemes for classes the statute's framers could see needed targeted delivery, among them victims of trafficking, workers in the unorganised sector, and persons affected by drug abuse, each administered through the standard machinery rather than by separate bodies.

## The regulations beneath the Act: panels, retainers and monitoring

A short statute that creates an entitlement and a hierarchy leaves the texture of the service to subordinate legislation, and in this field the texture is where quality is decided. The National Legal Services Authority has framed regulations on the subjects that matter most to an aided litigant: the free and competent legal services regulations of 2010, the legal services clinics regulations of 2011, and the framework for selecting and monitoring the lawyers through whom aid is delivered.

The panel is the basic unit of supply. Each authority maintains panels of advocates for the courts and tribunals within its charge, constituted for fixed terms through a selection process that weighs experience and standing at the bar, with separate panels in practice for civil, criminal, family and tribunal work. The aided litigant does not choose from the panel; the authority assigns, ordinarily through its secretary, and the assignment carries the brief, such papers as exist, and the fee the authority's approved rates prescribe. The rates are fixed by each State Authority and are the single most consequential number in the system, since they determine which segment of the profession finds panel work worth taking. On assignment, the panel advocate owes the aided client the same professional duties owed to any client, and the client is entitled to be kept informed, to have the case conducted with diligence, and to complain to the authority if the conduct falls short. The monitoring and mentoring committees constituted under the regulations exist to receive the system's own information about itself: they review the progress of aided cases, call for reports from panel counsel, and are the forum in which a pattern of neglect by an individual lawyer can be identified and the lawyer removed from the panel.

Alongside the panel sit two refinements that experience forced on the design. Retainer lawyers are engaged by district authorities on a monthly retainer to staff the front office, attend the jail clinic, appear as remand counsel, and take up urgent matters that cannot await panel assignment; the retainer converts the first forty-eight hours of a case, often its decisive hours, from an administrative interval into a served one. Duty counsel arrangements at the magisterial courts perform the same function at the point of first production. Neither refinement appears in the Act itself, and both are best understood as the institution repairing, by scheme and regulation, the interval problems that a panel-only reading of the statute would leave exposed. A researcher who reads only the Act will miss them entirely, which is the general lesson of this field: the statute is the skeleton, and the service lives in the regulations.

## Applying for legal aid: the route in practice

The application route is designed to be walkable by a person without assistance, which is a harder design problem than it sounds. An application may be made to the legal services institution at the level matching the forum of the case: to the District Legal Services Authority for matters in the district courts and below, to the Taluk Committee for matters within its reach, to the High Court Legal Services Committee for matters before the High Court, and to the Supreme Court Legal Services Committee for matters before the Supreme Court. In practice the district front office is the door most applicants use, and a district authority that receives an application belonging to another level forwards it rather than turning the applicant away. Applications are also received through the online portals maintained by the national and State authorities, through legal services clinics, and through para-legal volunteers working in villages, courts, prisons and police stations, who are trained to help an applicant complete the papers and to carry the application to the authority.

The front office deserves description, because it is the institution's chosen answer to the problem that a rights-bearing citizen may still be defeated by a counter, a queue and a form. Front offices in the district court complexes are staffed by retainer lawyers and para-legal volunteers whose function is to receive applicants, explain the criteria, assist with the application and the means affidavit, and track the file until counsel is assigned. The application itself is short. It identifies the applicant, states the category of entitlement or the income position, and describes the matter for which aid is sought. On receipt, the secretary of the authority examines it against Sections 12 and 13, and where the applicant qualifies and a prima facie case appears, legal service is sanctioned and an advocate from the authority's panel is assigned to the matter. The assignment is the moment at which the paper entitlement becomes a professional relationship, and the quality of what follows depends on machinery examined later in this article: the panel system, its remuneration, and the monitoring committees that review the conduct of aided cases.

A grant of aid is not irrevocable, and the conditions of its continuance are part of an honest account of the route. The regulations under which the authorities work contemplate the withdrawal or modification of legal services where the aided person's means improve beyond the ceiling, where the person engages an advocate privately for the same matter, where the prima facie foundation on which aid was granted collapses in the course of the proceedings, or where the aided person obstructs the conduct of the case. Withdrawal is not supposed to strand a litigant mid-hearing: the authority's practice is to let the assigned counsel complete the stage in hand or to substitute counsel, and the affected person can represent against the withdrawal to the authority that ordered it. The aided litigant also carries obligations that mirror the entitlement, to cooperate with the assigned counsel, to attend when required, and to keep the authority informed of any change in means or address, obligations whose breach explains a share of the cases in which aided representation fails in practice and the failure is entered in the record against the institution rather than against the file. The certificate of eligibility, the assignment order and the counsel's reports together form the paper trail of an aided case, and a litigant who understands that the trail exists can use it: the authority cannot act on a complaint about a case it cannot reconstruct, and the litigant who has kept dates and names, in the manner suggested earlier in this article, gives the monitoring machinery something to grip.

The authorities also do work that arrives before any dispute exists, and one branch of it carries a cross-link the reader should follow. Under Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, a plaintiff contemplating a commercial dispute in which no urgent interim relief is sought must first exhaust pre-institution mediation, and that mediation is conducted by the legal services authorities under this Act, a role examined in the series article on [the Commercial Courts Act 2015](/2021/06/01/commercial-courts-act-2015-explained/). The episode is instructive about the Act's standing in the legal system: when Parliament needed a standing, district-level, publicly accountable mediation machinery for a new statutory precondition, it did not build one. It pointed at the institutions this Act had already built, and the district authorities acquired a commercial function their framers had not contemplated.

A practical word on papers. An applicant who keeps the documents of the matter together, the notices received, the orders passed, the identity and category proof relied on, and a dated note of every hearing and every assurance given, is easier to help and harder to lose in the system. Those who handle sensitive files professionally observe the same discipline; the habit of keeping a case file complete, ordered and private, of the kind described in accounts of [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), is worth copying by any litigant whose papers include material they would not want circulated. The point is not the tool but the practice: the authority can act on a file it can read, and a litigant who arrives with the whole story in order gets the benefit of the authority's time at the first meeting rather than the third. This article is legal information about how the Act works, not advice on any individual matter; a person with a live case and a limitation period running should put the facts before the district authority or an advocate without delay, because no general account can weigh the merits of a particular claim.

### Where is the Taluk Legal Services Committee placed in the structure?

The Taluk Legal Services Committee, constituted under Section 11B, is the fourth and lowest tier of the legal services structure, below the National, State and District Authorities. The senior civil judge within its jurisdiction chairs it, and it coordinates legal services and organises Lok Adalats in the taluk.

## The service in operation: clinics, compensation and the prison interface

The entitlement chapter states the right and the application route delivers it to an individual, but a large part of the institution's daily work consists of standing services that operate whether or not any particular application is ever filed. Three of them show the Act's reach better than any organigram.

The first is the legal services clinic. Under the clinic model, which the National Legal Services Authority regulated in its Legal Services Clinics Regulations of 2011, basic legal services are dispensed from fixed points in villages, in court complexes, in law colleges and universities, and in other places of public congregation, staffed by para-legal volunteers and panel lawyers working to a roster. The clinic is conceived on the analogy of a primary health centre: it gives advice, drafts applications and notices, helps with the paperwork of welfare entitlements and identity documents, refers what it cannot resolve to the District Authority, and follows up. Its significance is diagnostic as much as remedial, since a functioning clinic network tells the district authority where disputes are forming, among whom, and about what, long before they become litigation. The village clinic also answers the most persistent structural objection to the whole design, that a court-complex institution serves only those already inside the court system. A clinic in a panchayat building is inside a different system, the citizen's own, and that is where the statute's framers wanted the first contact to occur.

The second standing service is the prison interface. Persons in custody are a Section 12 category in their own right, and the authorities operationalise the category through legal services clinics in jails, through remand advocates deputed to courts where undertrials are produced, and through the duty the case law lays on every magistrate to ask the unrepresented accused whether he wants counsel at State cost. The work at this interface is disproportionately about time rather than about verdicts: bail applications not moved, sureties not arranged, trials stalled for want of production, sentences completed without the prisoner knowing the appeal window has closed. Successive editions of Prison Statistics India, published by the National Crime Records Bureau, record undertrials as a large majority of the prison population, and that single pattern explains why the legal services establishment treats the undertrial as its central criminal-justice client. A system in which most prisoners have not been convicted is a system in which the quality of pre-trial representation is not a welfare adjunct but a principal determinant of how much liberty the State takes from the unconvicted. The Act's custody category, drafted before the statistics were commonly cited in argument, reads in retrospect as its most prescient line.

The third service is financial rather than representational. The victim compensation schemes framed by State Governments under the criminal procedure codes, Section 357A of the Code of 1973 and its successor provision, are administered through the legal services authorities: the District Authority, on the recommendation of the court or on application, inquires into the claim and awards compensation to victims of crime and their dependents, including in cases where the offender is not traced or no trial takes place. The arrangement is characteristic of the institution's growth. Parliament and the State Governments, needing a district-level body able to receive applications, verify circumstances and disburse public money with judicial supervision, repeatedly reached for the machinery this Act had built, exactly as the Commercial Courts Act later did for pre-institution mediation. The legal services authority has become the default welfare interface of the justice system, a role well beyond the two objects in its long title, and one that loads the district office with work its staffing assumptions never anticipated.

Around these standing services the national authority has framed targeted schemes for classes whose difficulties are specific: schemes for victims of trafficking and commercial sexual exploitation, for workers in the unorganised sector, for children, for victims of mass disaster and industrial disaster, for persons affected by drug abuse, and for persons with mental illness and intellectual disabilities, administered through the standard State and district machinery rather than by parallel bodies. The schemes share a method: identify the class, name the typical legal problems it faces, assign the district authority concrete duties of outreach, camps and follow-up, and measure the work. Their existence qualifies any simple verdict on the Act. The statute itself is short and general; the living institution is the accumulation of schemes, regulations and protocols beneath it, and it is there, rather than in the text of 1987, that most questions about what legal aid does are actually answered.

## Three applicants: the Act tested against its own promise

The One Test of this article is practical, and it is fairly applied to three composite applicants whose situations recur in the institution's daily intake. They are illustrations of the machinery, not reports of decided cases, and each shows a different joint in the design.

The first applicant is a woman in a district town whose husband has stopped maintaining the household and against whom she wishes to claim maintenance, while also facing a civil suit over the small property in which she lives. She is entitled twice over, as a woman under Section 12 and, on the facts, very likely through the income door as well, and no one at the front office will ask her to prove poverty before asking what her case is. Her application, taken at the District Authority's front office with the help of a retainer lawyer, discloses a prima facie case, and counsel from the panel is assigned to the maintenance proceedings. The property suit, in which the opposing party is a relative and the real dispute is over shares and possession, is of the class a court or the authority may steer toward a Lok Adalat, where she will be advised, by the same counsel the Act provided, on whether the figure and the terms offered are ones she can accept knowing that acceptance is final. Her experience of the Act is its two halves in sequence: an entitlement that got her represented, and a settlement forum whose finality her representative existed to explain before she consented. If the second half operates without the first, if she reaches the sitting unadvised, the design has failed at exactly the point the statute intended the halves to interlock.

The second applicant is an undertrial in a district jail, arrested on a charge triable by a magistrate, whose family cannot brief a lawyer. The Code's own legal aid provision, Section 304, would not have reached him, since his trial is not before a court of session. The Act reaches him through the custody category, and the machinery reaches him through three contacts rather than one: the magistrate at first production, obliged by the case law to inform him of the right; the remand advocate at the court, who can move his bail application at the next hearing rather than the next month; and the jail legal services clinic, where a para-legal volunteer records his case details and carries them to the District Authority for the assignment of trial counsel. His entitlement is the clearest in the statute and its delivery the most dependent on other people's routines, which is why the defence counsel system, when it covers his district, changes his position in kind: his lawyer is then a salaried member of an office whose whole docket is men in his position, and the file does not wait for a panel assignment to begin moving.

The third applicant is the dependant family of a man killed by a truck on a national highway, pursuing compensation against the vehicle's insurer. No one in the family needs to be told they are poor enough, since the claim's path runs through the second half of the Act rather than the first. The claim petition before the Motor Accidents Claims Tribunal is referred, or the insurer's standing practice brings it, to a Lok Adalat sitting, where the insurer's representative holds a mandate to settle within a computed band and the tribunal's presiding officer sits as bench chairman. The family is offered a sum computed on the structured approach the courts apply to such claims, discounted in negotiation for immediacy and certainty. If they accept, the award is a decree, the money follows on the decree's timeline, and no appeal can disturb it in either direction; if they refuse, the claim returns to the tribunal and the contested route resumes, years included. Everything in the institution's defence, and everything in its critics' case, is visible in that single offer: a bird in the hand against a larger, later, uncertain entitlement, with the quality of the moment's advice deciding which description the settlement deserves.

## Lok Adalats: the settlement engine of Chapter VI

The Lok Adalat, the people's court, is the institution for which the Act is publicly known, and Chapter VI, Sections 19 to 22, is its statutory home. The idea predates the statute, as the account of the Gujarat experiments shows, but the chapter converted a persuasive gathering into a forum whose output is a decree. Understanding the chapter requires holding apart three questions that public discussion habitually merges: what a Lok Adalat is, what it may take up, and what its decision does.

Section 19 provides for the organisation of Lok Adalats. Every legal services authority and committee in the hierarchy, the Supreme Court Committee, the High Court Committees, the State, District and Taluk bodies, may organise Lok Adalats at such intervals and places and for exercising jurisdiction in respect of such areas as it thinks fit. A Lok Adalat bench is constituted of a serving or retired judicial officer as chairman together with other persons, ordinarily a member of the legal profession and a social worker, the number and composition being determined by the organising authority. The bench is therefore not a court in judicial robes but a settlement panel with a judge at its head, and that composition is the design in miniature: enough judicial authority to command confidence in the outcome, enough non-judicial presence to keep the conversation a negotiation rather than a hearing.

Section 20 governs cognizance: how a matter arrives before the bench. There are two streams. The first is the stream of cases pending before a court. A court before which a matter is pending may refer it to a Lok Adalat where the court is satisfied, on the material before it, that there are chances of settlement, or where the matter is one which the court considers appropriate to be taken cognizance of by a Lok Adalat, ordinarily after giving the parties a reasonable opportunity of being heard; the procedural codes reinforce the stream, since Section 89 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 directs a civil court that sees a possibility of settlement to formulate the terms and refer the dispute to one of the listed modes, Lok Adalat among them. The second stream is the stream of matters not yet before any court: a party to a dispute may apply to the organising authority for the dispute to be taken up at the pre-litigation stage, and the authority, after hearing the parties, may refer it to a Lok Adalat. The second stream is the quieter revolution. A dispute that has never been filed, a bank's recovery claim, an insurer's repudiated motor claim, a quarrel over a boundary or a maintenance amount, can acquire a binding resolution without a plaint, a court fee or a cause list, because the statute allows the settlement forum to be seized of the dispute directly.

The referral stream acquired a judicial grammar of its own through Section 89 of the Code of Civil Procedure. In Afcons Infrastructure Limited v. Cherian Varkey Construction Company, decided in 2010, the Supreme Court worked through the referral provision in detail, explaining the modes it lists, identifying the classes of case ordinarily suited to settlement processes and the classes ordinarily unsuited, among them cases involving serious allegations of fraud, fabrication or impersonation, claims against minors and persons under disability requiring the court's protection, and prosecutions for non-compoundable offences. The judgment matters to this Act because it disciplined the first stream: a court referring a case to a Lok Adalat is expected to have formed a view that the matter is of the settleable kind, and the parties' opportunity of being heard, which Section 20 contemplates, is the litigant's safeguard against a reference made by routine. A reference is not a disposal. The case returns untouched if the parties do not agree, and the Afcons framework explains why the great majority of referred cases that do settle are those in which the only genuine dispute was always about how much and when, rather than about whether anything was owed at all.

The jurisdiction itself is stated in Section 19(5): a Lok Adalat has jurisdiction to determine and to arrive at a compromise or settlement between the parties to a dispute in respect of any case pending before, or any matter falling within the jurisdiction of but not brought before, any court for which the Lok Adalat is organised. One exclusion follows, and it is absolute: the Lok Adalat has no jurisdiction in respect of any matter relating to an offence not compoundable under any law. The compoundability line runs through the criminal process: offences that the criminal procedure code permits the parties to compound, with or without the permission of the court, may be settled at a Lok Adalat and the settlement closes them; offences the code does not permit to be compounded cannot be touched, however willing the parties. The line explains a large share of the Lok Adalat list at any sitting, since compoundable criminal matters, including the mass of cheque dishonour prosecutions under the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, are among its staple categories. It also marks the boundary of the settlement idea itself. Some wrongs the law treats as public, and their disposal is not the parties' to bargain away; the debates over offences within families, examined for one provision in the series article on [the judicial response to Section 498A](/2016/03/01/section-498a-judicial-response/), illustrate why cruelty in marriage, which is not compoundable, cannot be settled at a Lok Adalat even where connected civil and maintenance questions can be and regularly are.

The boundary is worth a further paragraph, because it is commonly misunderstood as a boundary on settlement itself. The exclusion binds the Lok Adalat, not the legal system. The High Courts, exercising inherent power to quash criminal proceedings, have long accepted settlements even in some matters that cannot be compounded, where the dispute is essentially private in character and continuation of the prosecution would serve no purpose: the Supreme Court recognised the practice in B.S. Joshi v. State of Haryana, in 2003, in the matrimonial context, and disciplined it in Gian Singh v. State of Punjab, in 2012, by distinguishing offences with a private or civil flavour from heinous and serious offences and offences under special statutes, which settlement cannot close. The contrast is instructive about institutional design. Quashing on settlement is an exercise of constitutional-court discretion, case by case, on a full record. The Lok Adalat is a mass forum, and Parliament withheld the non-compoundable field from it entirely rather than entrust the distinction to a settlement bench working through a long list in a day. The line is crude, and deliberately so: a forum built for volume is given only the categories that can be safely processed at volume.

The compoundable field itself is wider than the word criminal suggests to a lay reader, because the largest single contributor to the criminal side of the Lok Adalat list is a commercial offence. Dishonour of a cheque is punishable under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, and Section 147 of that Act makes every offence in it compoundable, so a prosecution that fills magistrates' courts in enormous numbers is, by legislative design, a settleable claim for a liquidated sum with compensation. The fit with the Lok Adalat method is exact: liability usually turns on documents, the real dispute is quantum and time to pay, and both sides gain from a dated, executable compromise over a contested trial. Motor accident compensation supplies the other great staple. Claims before the Motor Accidents Claims Tribunals turn on negligence and on the computation of compensation, insurers hold the paying side of nearly every claim, and a settlement culture developed in which the insurer's representatives attend sittings with authority to compromise within bands. The Lok Adalat did not invent either practice; it gave both a statutory room, a presiding judicial officer, and an output with the force of a decree.

What happens at the sitting is governed by the character of the forum. Section 20 directs the Lok Adalat, where a matter is referred to it, to proceed to dispose of it and arrive at a compromise or settlement between the parties, guided by the principles of justice, equity and fair play, and Section 22 clothes the bench with specified powers of a civil court for the purposes of summoning witnesses, discovery, receiving evidence on affidavit and requisitioning records, while deeming its proceedings to be judicial proceedings for defined purposes. In operation the sitting is informal: no pleadings are exchanged before the bench, no evidence is recorded in the forensic sense, the parties speak, the bench tests the distance between them, experienced benches shuttle figures and terms between the rooms, and an insurance company's representative arrives with a settlement mandate and a ceiling. Where a compromise is reached, its terms are recorded and the award is drawn on them. Where no compromise is reached in a referred case, the record is returned to the court from which it came and the case proceeds from the stage it had reached, the parties having lost a day and nothing else; where no compromise is reached in a pre-litigation matter, the parties are advised to seek their remedy in court. Nothing said in an unsuccessful attempt binds anyone, and the failure costs no rights.

The award is the subject of Section 21, and Section 21 is the provision on which the whole institution turns. Every award of a Lok Adalat is deemed to be a decree of a civil court or, as the case may be, an order of any other court, and where a compromise or settlement has been arrived at in a case referred from a court, the court fee paid in that case is refunded in the manner provided under the Court Fees Act, 1870. Every award is final and binding on all the parties, and no appeal lies to any court against the award. The deeming of the award as a decree means it is executable like any decree: a party who does not perform a settlement can be taken to execution, and the settlement is not a mere contract vulnerable to a fresh suit on the original cause. The finality is the price, and it is charged deliberately. The statute's reasoning, visible in the design, is that a consent outcome needs no appellate correction because the parties are its authors; an appeal against one's own agreement would convert the settlement forum into a first round of litigation and destroy the finality that makes settlement attractive to both sides, above all to the side with the deeper pocket and the longer patience.

### What kinds of disputes do National Lok Adalats mostly settle?

National Lok Adalats, organised across the country on a single day under the direction of the National Legal Services Authority, mostly settle compoundable criminal matters, cheque dishonour cases, motor accident compensation claims, bank recovery matters, labour disputes and matrimonial and family questions, together with large volumes of pre-litigation disputes.

The scale of the institution deserves a measured paragraph, because its figures are its argument and its vulnerability. The national Lok Adalat, a single-day sitting held simultaneously from the taluk level to the Supreme Court under the calendar fixed by the national authority, is the largest settlement exercise in the Indian legal system: on the figures the National Legal Services Authority publishes for its sittings, individual national sittings have taken up matters running into crores and recorded settlements numbering, on the authority's own count for a 2024 sitting, more than a crore of matters disposed of when pending and pre-litigation cases are counted together. The composition of the disposed list matters more than its size. Compoundable criminal cases, cheque dishonour prosecutions, motor accident claims, bank recovery, traffic challans and matrimonial disputes dominate, which is to say the Lok Adalat harvests the categories where liability is usually clear, quantum is the only real question, and delay is the principal injustice. State and district Lok Adalats sit between the national sittings, and mobile Lok Adalats carry the forum to areas far from court complexes. A reader should still hold the figures at the distance the institution's own critics insist on: a disposal count measures closure, and the same annual reports that celebrate volume also record settlements whose terms no one audited. That argument is joined in the assessment part of this article.

The institution's formats have multiplied within the chapter's spare text, and a reader meeting the term in the wild should be able to place the variety in front of them. The regular Lok Adalat is the episodic sitting organised by a State, district or taluk institution for its area, taking referred and pre-litigation matters together. The National Lok Adalat superimposes a single date and a common subject emphasis on all of them at once, so that the entire hierarchy sits on the same day. Mega and special sittings adapt the model to a single subject or a single institution's backlog, motor accident claims being the classic instance. Mobile Lok Adalats carry a bench to places the court system does not reach, in a vehicle fitted as an office, and dispose of the small civil and compoundable business of a locality on the spot. The e-Lok Adalat, which grew out of the period in which the pandemic closed court complexes, conducts the sitting by video conference where the applicable arrangements permit, and is best understood not as a separate statutory category, since Chapter VI creates none, but as a mode of holding the same sitting. Across all formats the statutory core is constant: the same bench form, the same jurisdiction, the same requirement of consent, and the same Section 21 award at the end. The formats are logistics. The law is one.

One further feature of the chapter is easily missed and practically important: no fee is charged for taking a matter to a Lok Adalat, and the refund of court fee on settlement of a referred case turns the settlement into a financially positive act for a plaintiff who has already paid to file. The economics explain the insurers' and banks' enthusiasm, which has its own critics, but for the individual claimant in a motor accident case the arithmetic is simple. Years of contested litigation over quantum, with interest accruing in theory and costs accruing in fact, are exchanged for an agreed sum payable at once, executable if unpaid, and never reopenable. Whether that exchange is fair in an individual case depends on the advice the claimant had when consenting, which is why the legal aid half of this statute and the Lok Adalat half cannot be prised apart: the same District Authority that organises the sitting also supplies the counsel who can tell a claimant whether the figure on the table is a settlement or a surrender.

## At the sitting: the Lok Adalat from the litigant's side of the table

The statutory account of a Lok Adalat is complete without a word about what the day feels like to the person whose case is on the list, and the omission matters because the forum's legitimacy is decided in that experience. The litigant's day begins with a list far longer than any court's, fixed for a sitting announced in advance and published through the courts, the authorities and, for institutional parties, their standing counsel. The bench sits in a courtroom or a hall in the court complex, the parties are called in rough order, and the negotiation happens in the open, in the presence of the other waiting litigants, a informality that cuts both ways: it strips the encounter of forensic mystery, and it denies the parties privacy for figures they might prefer to keep from their neighbours. Institutional defendants dominate the room's rhythm. An insurer's officer or a bank's recovery manager attends with a written mandate, a band within which settlement is authorised on the spot and beyond which the file must travel upward, and the experienced bench knows the bands as well as the officers do, so that much of the day's real work is a three-cornered conversation among the bench, the mandate-holder and the claimant about where inside the band the case belongs.

For the claimant, the decisive preparation is arithmetical and documentary rather than rhetorical. The bench will work from the papers: in a motor accident claim, the injury record, the income material and the structured computation the tribunals apply; in a bank matter, the statement of account and the borrower's proposal for instalments; in a family matter, the parties' own sense of a livable arrangement, translated by the bench into figures and dates. A claimant who arrives with the computation done, and with advice on the figure below which the contested route is worth its years, negotiates as an equal in the only currency the room respects, which is the credible alternative to agreement. A claimant who arrives without either is negotiating against two professionals, the other side's officer and, in effect, the day's disposal pressure. The terms agreed are recorded in the award the same day or shortly after, orders for payment carry dates, settlements payable in instalments record the consequence of default, and the award's decree character means that default is answered with execution proceedings in the ordinary court, not with a fresh negotiation. Nothing about the process requires the litigant to speak in legal language, and the bench's questions are directed as much to comprehension as to consent, since a settlement recorded over a party who plainly did not follow the terms is precisely the material from which writ petitions are later made. The sitting's defenders and its critics are describing the same room. It is a room in which a prepared party with advice does well, sometimes better than litigation would ever have done, and an unprepared party without advice signs away an appeal for a figure no one tested. The statute built the room and supplied the adviser in adjoining sections. The whole question is whether the two met before the signature.

## Permanent Lok Adalats: the forum that may decide

Chapter VIA, inserted by the Amendment Act of 2002, created the Permanent Lok Adalat and in doing so created the most instructive contrast in the statute. The institution shares the Lok Adalat name and the settlement ambition, and differs from it in the two respects that matter most: it is confined to one class of disputes, and it is not confined to consent.

The pressure that produced the 2002 amendment is worth recovering, because it explains the design's peculiarities. The episodic Lok Adalat had demonstrated, over fifteen years of sittings, that a large class of disputes with public utilities and insurers settled readily when a judicial figure chaired the negotiation, but the sittings were occasional, the utilities' representatives attended with limited mandates, and a dispute that arose the week after a sitting waited months for the next one. A standing body, permanently available, with a defined subject field and officers who would develop expertise in it, was the natural institutional answer, and the parliamentary choice of the time was to build it inside the Lok Adalat statute rather than as a separate tribunal law. The adjudicatory power was the amendment's bold stroke and its point of controversy from the beginning: the Law Commission's and the committees' settlement philosophy had always rested on consent, and a forum that could impose an outcome was attacked, including by the Bar Council of India in the litigation the courts later resolved, as a court in substance without a court's safeguards or a court's appeal. Parliament's answer, visible in the text, was to bound the experiment on every side: one subject class, a pecuniary ceiling, a pre-litigation gate, a judge-grade chairman, and natural justice as the procedural floor. Chapter VIA is therefore best read as a controlled departure from the consent principle, fenced about with the conditions that made the departure arguable.

Section 22A supplies the definitions, and the operative one is public utility service. The term covers air, road and water transport services for passengers or goods; postal, telegraph and telephone services; the supply of power, light or water to the public by any establishment; systems of public conservancy and sanitation; services in hospitals and dispensaries; and insurance services, together with any other service the Central Government or a State Government may notify in the public interest. The list is a map of the everyday monopolies and near-monopolies of Indian life, the services a citizen cannot realistically boycott and whose provider often answers complaints with a standard form. The design premise is that disputes with such providers are structurally unequal, repetitive and usually small, and that a specialised standing forum will handle them better than either the ordinary courts or a one-day settlement sitting.

Section 22B provides for the establishment of Permanent Lok Adalats by the Central Authority or the State Authorities, as the case may be, at such places and for such areas as may be notified. Each Permanent Lok Adalat consists of a chairman who is or has been a district judge, an additional district judge, or has held judicial office higher in rank than a district judge, and two other persons having adequate experience in public utility services, nominated by the government concerned on the recommendation of the establishing authority. The body is permanent in the administrative sense: it sits continuously at its notified place, unlike the episodic Lok Adalat, and a dispute may be brought to it on any working day. Section 22D frees its procedure from the strict requirements of the Code of Civil Procedure and the Indian Evidence Act, binding it instead to the principles of natural justice, objectivity, fair play and equity, and empowering it to specify its own procedure.

The procedure in Section 22C is where the statute's centre of gravity shifts. Any party to a dispute may, before the dispute is brought before any court, apply to the Permanent Lok Adalat for its settlement. Two consequences attach at the threshold. The first is a bar: the Permanent Lok Adalat has no jurisdiction over a matter relating to an offence not compoundable under law, and it has no jurisdiction where the value of the property in dispute exceeds the pecuniary limit, fixed by the section at ten lakh rupees and raised by the Central Government, in consultation with the Central Authority, by notification to one crore rupees under S.O. 803(E) of 20 March 2015. The second consequence is the closing of the court door: once an application is made to the Permanent Lok Adalat, no party to the application shall invoke the jurisdiction of any court in the same dispute. The applicant therefore chooses the forum in a way the ordinary plaintiff does not, since the choice, once exercised, excludes the alternative.

On receiving the application, the Permanent Lok Adalat first conducts conciliation proceedings, assisting the parties to reach an agreement in a manner independent and impartial, and where the parties reach an agreement it passes an award on its terms. The divergence from Chapter VI comes in Section 22C(8): where the parties fail to reach an agreement, the Permanent Lok Adalat shall, if the dispute does not relate to any offence, decide the dispute on its merits. The body that began as a conciliator ends as an adjudicator, on the same file, without the parties being asked a second time whether they consent to be bound. Section 22E then completes the design: every award of a Permanent Lok Adalat, whether on agreed terms or on a decision on merits, is final and binding on the parties, is deemed to be a decree of a civil court, is to be made by a majority of the persons constituting the bench, and no appeal lies against it. The series of propositions, stated in sequence, is stark. A citizen with a grievance against an electricity distributor, an insurer or a transport undertaking may invoke a forum that is free, quick and procedurally informal; by invoking it, the citizen gives up the courts; and the forum, if conciliation fails, will impose an outcome the citizen cannot appeal. The statute is candid about the exchange, and the exchange is defensible precisely to the extent that its terms are understood before the application is signed, which is why exposition of this chapter, in plain terms, is a public function and not a scholarly luxury.

The working of a Permanent Lok Adalat application repays a slower description, because the sequence determines the party's rights at each step. The applicant files the application with the Permanent Lok Adalat for the area, stating the dispute with the public utility service and the relief sought, before any court has been approached on it. The registry examines the application against the threshold conditions: the subject must be a public utility service, the dispute must not relate to a non-compoundable offence, and the value of the property in dispute must fall within the pecuniary ceiling. Notice goes to the other side, ordinarily the utility or the insurer, which files its response. The bench then conducts conciliation, and the section obliges it to assist the parties in an independent and impartial manner to reach a settlement, including by formulating the possible terms of a settlement and putting them to the parties for their observations; a settlement so reached is signed and embodied in an award. Only on the failure of that process does the adjudicatory power awaken, and when it awakens the bench decides on the material before it, unbound by the technical rules of pleading and proof that govern a civil trial, though bound by natural justice, which means bound to hear both sides and to decide on the record rather than on impression. The award is then drawn, signed by the majority of the bench, transmitted to the parties, and, if not performed, executed through the civil court of local jurisdiction as a decree of that court. A party who receives an adverse merits award and believes the bench exceeded its jurisdiction, decided a matter beyond the pecuniary ceiling, or denied a hearing, has the writ remedy and no other; a party whose complaint is only that the decision is wrong on the merits has no remedy at all, which is the sharp end of the design.

One overlap deserves notice because it generates real confusion in practice. Many disputes with public utility services, a defective electricity bill, a repudiated insurance claim, a telephone service failure, also fall within the consumer commissions' jurisdiction under the consumer protection legislation, and an aggrieved consumer may have both doors open. The doors are not equivalent. The consumer commission is a court-substitute entered by complaint, which adjudicates after a contested process and whose orders are appealable through the consumer hierarchy. The Permanent Lok Adalat is entered by an application that closes the court door, attempts settlement first, and ends, if settlement fails, in an unappealable decision. A consumer who files before the Permanent Lok Adalat cannot then carry the same dispute to the commission, and the choice between a slower appealable route and a faster final one is a genuine strategic decision on which advice is worth taking before, not after, the application is signed. The existence of the choice is itself an argument for the legal aid half of the statute: the person least equipped to weigh it is the person the Act exists to serve, and the district authority's front office is the place where the weighing can be done with help.

### What is the pecuniary limit of a Permanent Lok Adalat?

A Permanent Lok Adalat may not take up a dispute where the value of the property in dispute exceeds the prescribed pecuniary limit, which the Act fixed at ten lakh rupees and which the Central Government raised to one crore rupees by notification in 2015.

One structural criticism of the design should be recorded in the institution's own section, because it goes to the working of conciliation rather than to constitutionality. Where the same bench conciliates and then adjudicates, a party negotiating in the conciliation phase knows that the persons hearing its concessions, its bottom line and its private assessment of weakness are the persons who will decide the dispute if the negotiation fails. Dispute-resolution theory has a name for the arrangement, med-arb, and a standard objection to it: candour in the settlement phase is purchased at the price of exposure in the decision phase, so rational parties guard their statements, withhold their real flexibility, and conciliate at a fraction of the openness the process presupposes. The objection applies with reduced force to the Permanent Lok Adalat, since its conciliation works from filed pleadings and documents more than from confessional disclosure, and the Supreme Court's description of the procedure as conciliation-cum-arbitration in Interglobe Aviation accepts the hybridity as the design rather than as a defect in it. But the objection identifies the honest cost of the 2002 experiment, and it explains a pattern practitioners report: the Permanent Lok Adalat's settlement rate is not its measure, since a substantial part of its work was always going to end in the merits decisions its critics said made it a court. Judged as a settlement forum it is compromised by its own second power; judged as a fast, cheap, expert court for utility disputes it is coherent. The courts, in upholding it, judged it the second way.

The constitutional challenge to this design arrived as expected, and the courts upheld it, in reasoning that clarified what the institution is. In United India Insurance Company Limited v. Ajay Sinha, decided in 2008, the Supreme Court examined the Permanent Lok Adalat's power where conciliation had failed and confirmed that the body is not confined to recording agreements: under the scheme of Chapter VIA it may decide the dispute on merits, the adjudicatory element being written into the section rather than implied. In Interglobe Aviation Limited v. N. Satchidanand, decided in 2011, the Court described the Permanent Lok Adalat's procedure with a phrase that has stuck, conciliation-cum-arbitration, and treated the body as a hybrid whose award, though not appealable, remains open to the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts. In Bar Council of India v. Union of India, decided in 2012, the Court rejected the challenge to Chapter VIA itself: the absence of an appeal was no constitutional defect, since the right of appeal is a creature of statute and not an inherent right, and the writ remedy against an award rendered without jurisdiction or in breach of natural justice remained available. The line of cases establishes the institution as the courts wish it understood: a genuine adjudicatory tribunal for a bounded class of disputes, created knowingly, and policed at its edges by judicial review rather than softened at its centre by an appeal.

## The Legal Services Authorities Act among the statutes: where its entitlement meets other laws

No statute in the access-to-justice field operates alone, and the Legal Services Authorities Act's position is best fixed by its junctions with the neighbouring laws that assume its existence.

The junction with the criminal procedure codes has been noted: Section 304 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, and Section 341 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, place the duty of providing counsel on the court in the specified trials, while this Act places the entitlement in the person and the machinery in the authorities, so that the two provisions interlock rather than overlap, the court identifying the unrepresented accused and the authority supplying and paying the advocate. The junction with the mental health legislation is express: the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 recognises the right of a person with mental illness to free legal services, and the providers of that service are the authorities under this Act, whose Section 12 category of persons in psychiatric custody was drafted thirty years before the 2017 statute cashed it. The junction with the juvenile justice legislation is operational: children in conflict with the law are entitled under Section 12 as children and as persons in custody, and the legal services authorities supply the counsel and the legal services clinics through which the boards and committees under that system encounter represented rather than unrepresented children. The junction with the legislation against domestic violence runs through its protection officers and service providers, whose statutory duties include informing a woman of her right to free legal aid under this Act and connecting her to the district authority, so that the first legal fact a complainant under that statute learns may be the existence of this one. The junction with the law on atrocities against Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes is written into Section 12 itself, in the undeserved want clause's express naming of caste atrocity, and the special statute's own victim-protection framework operates alongside the entitlement that naming creates.

The pattern across these junctions is consistent and worth naming. Later statutes repeatedly treat the legal services authorities as existing infrastructure, a standing answer to the question every welfare law eventually asks: who, in the district, on the ground, will actually do this? That is a tribute to the 1987 design and a strain on it, since each junction adds work without adding a corresponding tier to the structure. A reader assessing the institution's performance should therefore count its full brief, representational, settlement, compensatory, mediatory and outreach functions together, before judging the resources assigned to it, which were framed for the first two.

## The courts on finality: no appeal, but consent is policed

The bar on appeal against a Lok Adalat award is short in the text and has generated a body of case law out of proportion to its length, because parties who settle and then repent are a constant of litigation. The jurisprudence has settled into a stable position with two limbs, and an accurate account of the Act must state both, since the second limb is the answer to the most common misunderstanding of the first.

The first limb is that the bar means what it says. In State of Punjab v. Jalour Singh, decided in 2008, the Supreme Court considered a challenge to a Lok Adalat award in a land acquisition reference and held that an award of a Lok Adalat is not appealable, that it is final and binding, and that the only remedy available to a party aggrieved by it is to approach the High Court under Article 226 or Article 227 of the Constitution. The reasoning goes to the nature of the instrument: a Lok Adalat has no power to hear and decide a case on its merits at all, its award merely records the compromise the parties themselves reached, and an order recording a compromise is not the kind of adjudication from which an appeal can be constructed. A party who signed the settlement has nothing to appeal against except their own consent. The Court in P.T. Thomas v. Thomas Job, decided in 2005, had earlier explained the award's character from the other side: it is deemed a decree, it can be executed, and its force derives from the agreement it embodies, which is why the award binds with the strength of a judgment while lacking a judgment's vulnerability to appellate correction.

The second limb is that consent is the award's foundation, and a foundation can be examined. Where a settlement was not in truth voluntary, where a party's signature was procured by fraud or misrepresentation, where the bench recorded an award on terms a party never agreed to, or where the Lok Adalat purported to decide a matter outside its jurisdiction, including a non-compoundable offence, the writ jurisdiction of the High Court is available, and High Courts have set aside awards on such grounds. The distinction the courts maintain is precise: the merits of the bargain are unreviewable, the existence of the bargain is not. This is a well-judged line. It preserves the finality that makes the forum useful, since a settlement that could be re-argued on its merits in a writ petition would be final in name only, while refusing to treat a signature as conclusive where the signature itself is the thing in dispute. The practical corollary belongs in any honest exposition: a party at a Lok Adalat should understand, before consenting, that regret is not a ground of challenge. The moment for advice, for calculation and for second thoughts is the sitting itself, and the legal services machinery's own counsel, supplied under the entitlement chapter, is the intended safeguard at that moment.

A corollary of the consent foundation is that an ordinary Lok Adalat which strays into deciding produces nothing at all. High Courts have repeatedly set aside purported awards in which the bench, faced with an absent or unwilling party, proceeded to record an outcome on the merits, treating the instrument as a nullity rather than as an erroneous award, because a Lok Adalat has no merits jurisdiction to exercise erroneously. The point disciplines benches as much as parties: the chairman's authority at the sitting is the authority to broker, to adjourn, or to return the record, and a bench that announces a decision has exceeded the statute in a way no subsequent consent can cure. The enforceability of a genuine award received the Supreme Court's attention in K.N. Govindan Kutty Menon v. C.D. Shaji, decided in 2012, arising from a cheque dishonour prosecution settled at a Lok Adalat: the Court held that the award is deemed to be a decree of a civil court and can be executed as such, so that a settlement of even a criminal-compoundable matter issues in a civilly executable instrument, and the complainant is not remitted to a fresh proceeding to realise what was agreed. Read together, the two lines of authority complete the picture of Section 21: consent in, decree out, and nothing in between that resembles a judgment.

The same analytical structure governs the Permanent Lok Adalat, with the addition that its merits decisions, being adjudications, attract writ scrutiny on the conventional grounds of jurisdictional error and breach of natural justice, as the Interglobe Aviation and Bar Council of India decisions record. In neither forum did the courts manufacture an appeal the statute had withheld. What they preserved is review of legality, which is a different and narrower thing, and the difference is the settled law on the subject.

## Three forums, one spectrum: Lok Adalat, Permanent Lok Adalat, mediation

The two chapters of this Act, read with the mediation framework that Parliament enacted later, disclose a spectrum along which Indian dispute resolution arranges itself, and locating each forum on the spectrum is the most useful single thing a reader can carry away from the comparison. The variable that orders the spectrum is consent: how much of the outcome the parties control, and what happens to a party who will not agree.

At one end stands mediation, in which a neutral assists the parties to negotiate and the neutral has no power to impose anything. A mediation that fails ends the process and leaves the parties exactly where they stood, free to litigate; a mediation that succeeds produces a settlement agreement, which the Mediation Act, 2023 then clothes with the force of a decree, in the framework examined in the series article on [the Mediation Act 2023](/2021/07/01/mediation-act-2023-explained/). Control of outcome is total and appellate questions do not arise, because there is nothing to appeal against but one's own signature. In the middle stands the Lok Adalat of Chapter VI. Consent is equally necessary there, since the bench cannot decide, but the consequences of consenting are heavier: the settlement is recorded as an award deemed to be a decree, the court fee is refunded, execution follows on default, and no appeal lies. The parties still author the outcome; they merely author it in a form that cannot be unsaid. At the far end stands the Permanent Lok Adalat of Chapter VIA, where consent is attempted first and then dispensed with: the forum conciliates, and failing agreement it decides, bars the courts from the moment of application, and gives no appeal from its decision. The citizen's control over the outcome is at its lowest precisely where the forum's power is at its highest, and the statute confines that arrangement to the bounded field of public utility services and a pecuniary ceiling, as if acknowledging that the far end of the spectrum is tolerable only in a small room.

The comparison yields the namable claim of this article, which a reader can test against the text: a settlement that cannot be appealed and an adjudication that cannot be avoided are housed in one statute, and they are the same idea at two temperatures. Chapter VI trusts consent completely and protects its product in full. Chapter VIA trusts a specialised tribunal completely and protects its product in terms just as unqualified. In both, finality is the currency with which speed and cheapness are bought, and in both the writ jurisdiction is the courts' retained safeguard against the abuse of the mechanism rather than against its ordinary operation. A legislator designing settlement machinery, and a litigant choosing a forum, are both really choosing where on this spectrum a dispute should sit, and the Act is most instructive when read as a menu of such choices rather than as a miscellaneous collection of benevolent provisions.

## Quality, criticism, and the defence counsel answer

An entitlement is only as real as the service delivered under it, and the criticism of legal aid quality is the oldest and most serious criticism the institution faces. It should be stated in its strong form, as its practitioners and researchers state it, and then set against what the institution has done in answer, since the stance of this series requires the argument at full strength on both sides.

The criticism runs as follows. Panel advocates, from whom aided litigants are supplied, are remunerated at rates fixed by the authorities that bear little relation to market fees for comparable work, and the rates in many States have historically been low enough that experienced practitioners decline panel work, leaving aided cases disproportionately to the youngest members of the bar. Selection and assignment have often been slow, with counsel appointed at a late stage of a criminal case, sometimes after the evidence has begun, which converts the constitutional promise of representation into the presence of a lawyer rather than the conduct of a defence. The aided litigant, unlike the paying client, cannot choose counsel and cannot change counsel for loss of confidence with anything like a client's freedom. Assessments of the justice system, including the successive editions of the India Justice Report, have repeatedly recorded that public spending on legal aid is a minute share of justice expenditure and that large numbers of persons entitled under the Act do not know of it, a finding consistent with the utilisation pattern: the categories of Section 12 describe a population counted in tens of crores, while the number who receive legal services in a year is a small fraction of it. On this account, the Act promises a right and delivers a lottery in which the prize depends on the district, the panel and the individual lawyer drawn.

Behind the quality criticism sits a demand problem of a particular shape, and it deserves separate statement because it qualifies the institution's defence that it serves all who come. Most of the entitled never come. The distance between the entitled population and the served population is not explained by refusal, since refusals are rare and mostly confined to the prima facie case screen, but by ignorance of the entitlement, by distance from the district headquarters, by the reasonable belief of a person in custody or in a village that offices in court complexes are not for them, and by the rational calculation of litigants who have watched aided cases conducted indifferently and prefer to borrow money for a chosen lawyer. Outreach, the clinic network, the para-legal volunteers and the awareness calendar all exist because the institution knows this; the criticism is that the outreach effort is funded as an afterthought relative to the size of the unmet need it acknowledges. A right that must be advertised to exist in practice is not thereby a lesser right, but its administrators are properly judged on the advertising as well as on the adjudication of applications, and on the institution's own reporting the gap remains the largest single number in the field.

The institution's answers are of three kinds, and they are not negligible. The first is regulatory: the National Legal Services Authority's regulations on free and competent legal services prescribe standards for panel selection, require panels to be constituted with regard to experience, and institute monitoring and mentoring committees charged with watching the progress of aided cases and the conduct of panel counsel, so that non-performance has somewhere to be reported. The second is structural, and it concerns the bar itself. The panel system draws on the enrolled profession administered under [the Advocates Act 1961](/2021/04/15/advocates-act-1961-explained/), whose own provisions charge the Bar Councils with organising legal aid, and the duty of the individual advocate to the client who cannot pay is written into the profession's rules of conduct; the legal aid system and the professional system are designed to interlock, even where the interlocking creaks. The third answer is the most consequential, and it amounts to a change of model for criminal defence.

The Legal Aid Defence Counsel System, introduced by the National Legal Services Authority in 2022, replaces reliance on part-time panel assignment for criminal matters with full-time salaried defence counsel engaged exclusively for legal aid work, organised in dedicated offices at district headquarters on lines consciously modelled on the public defender systems of other jurisdictions. The scheme began as a pilot in selected districts and was extended in phases across the districts of the country. Its chief defence counsel and deputies handle the criminal docket of the aided population from remand through trial to appeal, with support staff, so that the accused person's lawyer is an identifiable office with continuity and files, rather than a name drawn from a list for a single hearing. The reasoning is precisely the reasoning of the critics: a salaried, specialised, supervised defender service answers the three standing complaints of late assignment, divided attention and absent accountability in one design. The scheme has also drawn organised criticism from sections of the bar, whose objection is that a State-funded defence office diverts criminal work from independent practitioners and builds a parallel service; that objection, and the institution's answer that the quality of an undertrial's defence cannot be left to the accidents of panel economics, state the genuine and unresolved policy dispute the scheme embodies. What is not in dispute is the direction of travel: the legal services establishment itself concluded that the panel model, unamended, was not delivering the competent services its own statute promised, and 2022 is the year it acted on that conclusion.

The Lok Adalat criticism belongs in the same ledger, since it is the other half of the Act's reputation. The case for the sittings, made by the institution and by the many litigants who leave them with money in hand, is that volume is itself a form of justice where the alternative is a decade of adjournments; that a motor accident claimant compensated in a day has been served better than a claimant vindicated in a decade; that settlement restores relationships litigation corrodes, notably in family and neighbourhood disputes; and that the disposal of compoundable and petty matters frees court time for the serious docket. The case against, made by legal aid practitioners and by researchers who have observed the sittings, is that the single-day format with long lists creates structural pressure to settle; that insurance and bank representatives negotiate from institutional strength against unrepresented claimants; that a party may sign without grasping that no appeal lies; and that official figures count settlements without weighing their adequacy, so that closure masquerades as justice in the statistics. Both cases are true of different sittings, which is why the honest verdict is institutional rather than anecdotal: the mechanism is sound where its two safeguards operate, informed consent and access to independent advice at the sitting, and unsound where they do not, and the safeguard machinery belongs to the legal aid half of the Act. The statute's two halves stand or fall together in practice, exactly as its long title joins them.

## Assessment: what the Act achieves and where it leaves its users exposed

Taken as a whole, the Act's achievements are structural and permanent. It made legal aid a statutory entitlement with a defined beneficiary class broader than public intuition supposes, and it built a delivery institution in every district and taluk instead of leaving the right to be gathered from judgments. It gave the settlement movement a statutory engine whose output is a decree, and it used that engine to dispose of a volume of small and middling disputes that the court system demonstrably could not process at the same speed. Its Permanent Lok Adalat experiment, upheld by the courts, remains the boldest statutory essay in adjudication without appeal anywhere in Indian law, and its confinement to public utility services and a monetary ceiling shows a legislature aware that it was handling something strong. The judiciary-led design, whatever its tensions, kept the institution inside the justice system's own chain of responsibility rather than in a department's out-tray.

Its exposures are equally identifiable. One of them is jurisprudential and rarely stated plainly: the Act gives the aided litigant an entitlement against the authority but no enforceable standard of the service itself. A paying client whose advocate neglects the case has the ordinary remedies of a client, complaint to the Bar Council, a consumer or civil claim in an appropriate case, and above all the sanction of withdrawal. An aided litigant whose panel counsel neglects the case can complain to the authority, and the monitoring machinery can act against the lawyer, but the litigant has no statutory claim that the aid be competent of the quality the long title promises, and no damages route for its absence; the promise of competent service in the preamble is enforced, if at all, administratively. The constitutional case law fills part of the gap in criminal trials, where representation so deficient as to deny a fair trial can vitiate the result, but in civil aid the litigant's protection remains the authority's supervision and nothing harder. A statute that converted discretion into entitlement stopped one step short: it made the lawyer a right and the lawyer's diligence a hope. The defence counsel reform is significant partly because it attacks this gap organisationally, by making the provider an office whose work can be supervised as a body of work, rather than a list of individuals each beyond practical supervision.

The entitlement is broad and the budget thin, and the distance between the two is paid in quality and in awareness rather than in any formal denial, which makes the shortfall harder to litigate and easier to ignore. The income ceiling's variation between States means the content of a national statutory right changes at a State border, a federal untidiness the Act chose and has never revisited. The Lok Adalat's finality, sound in principle, depends for its legitimacy on advice and voluntariness at the sitting, and the system cannot audit either at the scale at which it operates. The Permanent Lok Adalat places a heavy consequence, the loss of the courts, on a single signature on an application form, and the quality of the explanation that accompanies that signature is not standardised. None of these exposures is an argument against the statute. They are the points at which its users are least protected, and a reader who knows them can use the machinery deliberately: claim the entitlement in terms, take the advice before the settlement, and treat the Permanent Lok Adalat application as the jurisdictional decision it is.

Three questions about the Act remain genuinely open, and an assessment should end by naming them rather than by resolving what the record has not resolved. The first is whether the system will ever price its own promise: as long as panel remuneration in many States sits far below the market, the quality floor of the entitlement is set by an administrative rate, and the defence counsel experiment, which escapes the rate by paying salaries instead, may prove to be the general future or an enclave within a panel system that continues unchanged around it. The second is whether the income door will be harmonised: the State ceilings have drifted so far apart that the real content of a national entitlement varies by geography in a way no policy document defends, and a floor ceiling prescribed nationally, below which no State could go, would be a modest amendment with a large equalising effect, if any legislature chooses to spend its capital on it. The third is whether the Permanent Lok Adalat model expands: the notification power in Section 22A lets governments add services to its field without fresh legislation, and each addition extends adjudication without appeal into new territory, so the discipline of the 2002 settlement, bounded subject, bounded value, pre-litigation gate, will be tested by increments rather than by a single debate. On each question the statute's text gives the tools and withholds the answer, which is why the institution repays continued attention rather than a settled verdict.

The series thesis has been that mechanisms designed for access can operate as limitations on it, and this Act states the trade in its text more openly than most. It widened the gate into the legal system and narrowed the road out of a settlement to nothing, in the same chapters and for the same citizen. Understood on those terms, it is one of the most instructive statutes in the Indian book: a law that trusts consent, distrusts delay, and leaves its readers, as it leaves its users, with a clear view of what was exchanged for what.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who is entitled to free legal services?

Section 12 of the Act entitles a member of a Scheduled Caste or Scheduled Tribe; a victim of trafficking in human beings or begar; a woman or a child; a person who is mentally ill or otherwise disabled; a victim of undeserved want such as a mass disaster, ethnic violence, caste atrocity, flood, drought, earthquake or industrial disaster; an industrial workman; a person in custody, including in a protective home, a juvenile home or a psychiatric hospital; and a person whose annual income falls below the ceiling prescribed for the court concerned. Only the income category is means-tested; the status categories carry no income condition.

### Q: How is legal aid organised across the country?

The Act builds four tiers. The National Legal Services Authority under Section 3, headed by the Chief Justice of India as Patron-in-Chief, sets policy and frames schemes. A State Legal Services Authority under Section 6 serves each State under the patronage of the Chief Justice of the High Court. A District Legal Services Authority under Section 9 serves every district under the District Judge. Taluk Legal Services Committees under Section 11B work at the taluk level. Separate committees serve the Supreme Court and each High Court. The district authority is the office a citizen ordinarily deals with.

### Q: What is a Lok Adalat and what can it decide?

A Lok Adalat is a settlement forum organised by the legal services authorities under Chapter VI of the Act, presided over by a judicial officer with other members. It does not decide cases on merits. It takes up cases pending before a court and disputes not yet filed, and it helps the parties reach a compromise or settlement, which is recorded as its award. Its jurisdiction covers civil matters, compoundable criminal matters and a wide range of claims including motor accident compensation and bank recovery. It cannot take up any matter relating to an offence that is not compoundable under law.

### Q: Can a Lok Adalat award be appealed?

No. Section 21 provides that every award of a Lok Adalat is final and binding on all parties and that no appeal lies to any court against it. The Supreme Court confirmed this position in State of Punjab v. Jalour Singh, holding that the award only records the parties' own compromise and is therefore not appealable. The one route that remains is a writ petition to the High Court under Article 226 or 227, available only on narrow grounds such as fraud, absence of genuine consent, or the Lok Adalat having acted outside its jurisdiction, not for re-arguing the merits of the settlement.

### Q: How is a Permanent Lok Adalat different?

A Permanent Lok Adalat, established under Chapter VIA for public utility services such as transport, power, water, postal and insurance services, is a standing body rather than a one-day sitting. An application to it can be made only before the dispute goes to court, and once made it bars the parties from approaching a court on the same dispute. It first attempts conciliation like a Lok Adalat, but if the parties fail to agree it may decide the dispute on its merits under Section 22C(8), which an ordinary Lok Adalat can never do. Its award is final, binding and not appealable.

### Q: How does a person apply for legal aid?

An application is made to the legal services institution matching the forum of the case, in most cases the District Legal Services Authority, whose front office in the district court complex receives applicants and helps them complete the papers. Applications are also received online through the portals of the national and State authorities, and through legal services clinics and para-legal volunteers. The applicant states the category of entitlement or files an affidavit of income, which the authority must accept unless it has reason to disbelieve it. The authority then tests whether a prima facie case exists and assigns a panel advocate.

### Q: What is the Legal Aid Defence Counsel system?

The Legal Aid Defence Counsel System is a scheme introduced by the National Legal Services Authority in 2022 for criminal defence, modelled on public defender systems. Instead of assigning each aided accused to a part-time panel advocate, it engages full-time salaried defence counsel in dedicated district offices who handle the criminal legal aid docket from remand through trial and appeal. It was launched as a pilot in selected districts and extended in phases across the country. The design answers the standing criticism of panel legal aid: late assignment, divided attention and weak continuity in the defence of accused persons entitled to representation at State cost.

### Q: What criticism does legal aid attract?

The standing criticism, made by practitioners and researchers, is that panel advocates are paid far below market rates, that counsel is often assigned late in a criminal case, that the aided litigant cannot choose a lawyer, and that public spending on legal aid is a very small share of justice expenditure while awareness of entitlement remains low among those who qualify. A related criticism concerns Lok Adalats: single-day sittings with long lists can pressure parties into settlements, and disposal figures measure closure rather than the quality of outcomes. The institution's responses include monitoring committees, panel standards and the defence counsel system.

### Q: Is free legal aid a fundamental right in India?

The Act makes legal aid a statutory entitlement, and the courts had earlier grounded it in the Constitution. In decisions including M.H. Hoskot v. State of Maharashtra, Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar and Suk Das v. Union Territory of Arunachal Pradesh, the Supreme Court read free legal services for a person who cannot afford counsel into the guarantee of a fair, just and reasonable procedure under Article 21, particularly in criminal proceedings where liberty is at stake. Article 39A, a directive principle inserted in 1976, directs the State to provide free legal aid. The two sources together make the right enforceable in criminal defence and statutory elsewhere.

### Q: What does free legal services actually cover?

Legal service under Section 2(1)(c) includes representation in any case or legal proceeding before a court, tribunal or other authority, and advice on any legal matter. In practice the aid granted covers the advocate's services, court fees and process fees, the cost of preparing and filing documents, certified copies and paper books, and the other charges payable in connection with the proceeding. It extends beyond the courtroom: advice at a legal services clinic, help with applications and notices, and pre-litigation assistance all fall within the scheme. The precise package in a case is fixed when the authority sanctions aid on the application.

### Q: Does free legal aid cover an appeal?

Yes, provided the applicant remains within a Section 12 category and the authority is satisfied that a prima facie case exists for prosecuting or defending the appeal, since Section 13 speaks of a case to prosecute or to defend without confining aid to trials. A person aided in a lower court applies to the institution matching the appellate forum: the District Authority within the district judiciary, the High Court Legal Services Committee for the High Court, and the Supreme Court Legal Services Committee for the Supreme Court, where the separately prescribed income ceiling of five lakh rupees applies to applicants who enter by the income route.

### Q: What income limit applies for free legal aid?

There is no single national figure, and any source stating one for the whole country is wrong. Section 12 makes a person entitled whose annual income is below the ceiling prescribed by the appropriate government: the State Government prescribes for cases before courts other than the Supreme Court, and the figures differ from State to State and are revised at different times. For cases before the Supreme Court, the prescribed ceiling stands at five lakh rupees. Most applicants do not need the figure at all, because the status categories in Section 12, such as women, children, Scheduled Castes and persons in custody, carry no income test.

### Q: How does a case reach a Lok Adalat?

A case reaches a Lok Adalat by one of two routes. A court before which the case is pending may refer it, where the court sees a possibility of settlement or considers the matter appropriate for the forum, after giving the parties an opportunity of being heard; Section 89 of the Code of Civil Procedure also directs civil courts to consider this mode. Alternatively, in a dispute not yet filed, a party may apply to the legal services authority organising the Lok Adalat, which may refer the matter after hearing the parties. No fee is charged on either route, and settlement of a referred case brings a refund of the court fee paid.

### Q: Which cases cannot be taken up by a Lok Adalat?

The statutory exclusion is any matter relating to an offence not compoundable under law: a Lok Adalat has no jurisdiction over it however willing the parties are, since the disposal of a non-compoundable offence is not the parties' to bargain away. Beyond that exclusion, the forum is structurally unsuited to matters in which there is no room for negotiated compromise, and a bench will not record a settlement on a matter outside the jurisdiction of the court for which the Lok Adalat is organised. Pending cases unsuited to settlement are simply returned to the referring court and proceed from the stage they had reached.

### Q: Is a Lok Adalat award enforceable?

Yes. Section 21 deems every award of a Lok Adalat to be a decree of a civil court, or an order of the court concerned, and an award that is not performed can be put into execution like any other decree, without a fresh suit on the original claim. This is one of the features that distinguish a Lok Adalat settlement from an ordinary private compromise: the parties' agreement is converted by the statute into an enforceable judicial instrument at the moment it is recorded. The same enforceability applies to an award of a Permanent Lok Adalat, which Section 22E likewise deems to be a decree.

### Q: Can a party refuse to settle at a Lok Adalat?

Yes. An ordinary Lok Adalat has no power to decide a dispute, and a settlement comes into existence only when both parties agree to it. A party who considers the terms on offer inadequate may decline them, and the consequence is limited: a case referred from a court goes back to that court and continues from the stage it had reached, and a pre-litigation matter is closed with the parties free to pursue their remedies. No inference is drawn and no cost penalty attaches to refusal. The position differs at a Permanent Lok Adalat, where failure to agree leads to a decision on merits.

### Q: What counts as a public utility service under the Act?

Section 22A defines public utility service for the purposes of the Permanent Lok Adalat chapter. It covers transport of passengers or goods by air, road or water; postal, telegraph and telephone services; the supply of power, light or water to the public by any establishment; systems of public conservancy or sanitation; services in hospitals or dispensaries; and insurance services. The Central Government or a State Government may notify additional services in the public interest. Disputes with such providers, from electricity billing to repudiated insurance claims, form the Permanent Lok Adalat's field, subject to its pecuniary limit and to the exclusion of non-compoundable offences.

### Q: How is a Lok Adalat different from mediation?

Both are settlement processes, but their machinery and their failure modes differ. Mediation uses a neutral mediator who facilitates negotiation and can impose nothing; if it fails, the parties return to litigation untouched, and a successful settlement takes effect under the mediation framework. A Lok Adalat is a statutory forum chaired by a judicial officer and organised by a legal services authority, whose bench actively works toward compromise; its settlement is recorded as an award deemed to be a court decree, carries a refund of court fee for referred cases, and cannot be appealed. The Lok Adalat's outcome is therefore heavier: identical in source, the parties' consent, but judicial in effect.

### Q: What can a person do if legal aid is refused?

Legal services can be refused where the authority is not satisfied that a prima facie case exists, or where the applicant falls outside Section 12. The refusal is an administrative decision of the institution, and the authority's own regulations and structure provide the first remedies: the applicant can place fuller material before the authority and seek reconsideration by the secretary or the chairman of the district or State institution, whose decision governs the panel machinery. In criminal matters the trial court itself bears a constitutional duty, recorded in the Khatri line of cases, to see that an undefended accused is offered representation, and the point can be raised before that court directly.

### Q: Who are para-legal volunteers and what do they do?

Para-legal volunteers are trained community workers deployed by the legal services authorities to carry legal awareness and basic assistance into villages, urban settlements, courts, prisons and police stations. They are not advocates and cannot appear for a litigant, but they explain entitlements, help people complete applications for legal aid, accompany applicants to front offices, identify persons in custody or distress who need the authority's attention, and assist at legal services clinics and Lok Adalats. They are the ground-level link between a statute administered from court complexes and a population that would otherwise never reach the counter, and they work under the supervision of the District Authority.
