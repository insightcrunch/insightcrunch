Gandhi's 1920 reorganisation of the Congress is usually filed under leadership: the moment a charismatic strategist captured a debating society and turned it toward mass struggle. The filing misses the durable event. At Nagpur in December 1920 the Indian National Congress adopted a new constitution, and that constitution, the Nagpur Constitution, rebuilt the organisation as a mass-membership architecture of Provincial Congress Committees drawn on linguistic lines, District Congress Committees as the working unit, primary committees at the base and a four-anna membership fee as the door. The claim defended here is architectural: Gandhi's 1920 reorganisation built mass-membership architecture, and the architecture provided the institutional backbone for the Phase Two Congress across the twenty-seven years that followed.

## December 1920 at Nagpur: A Session That Rebuilt the Congress

The thirty-fifth session of the Indian National Congress met at Nagpur from 26 to 31 December 1920, and the Congress's own synopsis of its plenary sessions records the scale of the gathering: 14,583 delegates, a reception committee chaired by Seth Jamnalal Bajaj, and general secretaries named Motilal Nehru, C. Rajagopalachari and M. A. Ansari. C. Vijayaraghavachariar, a veteran of the Salem bar and of the Congress's older petitioning generation, presided. The city itself carried an argument. Nagpur, capital of the Central Provinces, sat at the subcontinent's geographic centre, a railway junction of the Great Indian Peninsula line where the Marathi and Hindi regions met, and the choice of venue placed the session beyond the presidency capitals whose elites had hosted the party's gatherings for a generation. The reception organisation under Jamnalal Bajaj, the industrialist who had attached himself to Gandhi's cause, ran the session as an exhibition of the order being proposed: a delegates' camp laid out as a small disciplined town, volunteers managing kitchens, sanitation and queues, the proceedings conducted for a gathering numbered in the tens of thousands rather than the hundreds of the old conferences. Delegates who had travelled to earlier sessions as guests of a host city's notables encountered instead a machine that fed, housed and marshalled them by roster. The session's political business was the ratification of non-cooperation, first adopted at the Calcutta special session of 4 to 9 September 1920, where Lala Lajpat Rai had presided and Gandhi had moved the programme past a divided house by 1,886 votes to 884. At Nagpur, Chittaranjan Das of Bengal, who had fought the programme at Calcutta over the boycott of the legislative councils, himself moved the non-cooperation resolution. Mohammad Ali Jinnah, who held to constitutional methods, opposed the new creed and left the Congress at Nagpur, a departure the session records without ceremony. The political drama is documented in every standard history. The constitutional business, transacted in the same hall across the same week, is the subject here.

### Who presided over the Nagpur session of the Congress in 1920?

C. Vijayaraghavachariar of Salem presided over the thirty-fifth session at Nagpur in December 1920. A senior lawyer of the older Congress generation, he held the chair as Gandhi directed the session's real business: ratifying non-cooperation and adopting the new Congress constitution that reorganised the party on linguistic and mass-membership lines.

The Nagpur Constitution was not a resolution. Resolutions declared intentions and expired with the session; a constitution allocated authority, defined membership and fixed the units through which every later campaign would be staffed, financed and disciplined. The delegates who adopted it changed four things at once. They redrew the party's provincial map on linguistic lines. They made the district, rather than the province or the annual session, the working level of the organisation. They opened formal membership to any adult who paid four annas a year and enrolled through a primary committee in a village or an urban ward. And they placed a fifteen-member Working Committee over the whole structure, served by an All India Congress Committee raised to 350 members, so that the party possessed an executive that sat between sessions instead of dissolving into correspondence until the next December. Each of these changes has a section of its own below, because each carries a distinct institutional consequence that the post-1920 record tests.

The constitution's own passage through the session deserves a paragraph, because it shows the old party voting its own replacement into existence. The draft was prepared in the weeks before Nagpur through the session's constitution work and Gandhi's consultations with the secretaries named above, debated in the Subjects Committee, and adopted in open session alongside the non-cooperation ratification, so that the delegates who endorsed the movement in one vote built the movement's house in the next. Opposition concentrated on the creed's new wording and the council boycott, not on the committee architecture, which passed with the assent of men who disagreed about nearly everything else in that hall, Motilal Nehru and Das among them, a detail the architectural reading notes with emphasis: the structure was acceptable even to the strategy's opponents because each faction could see its own future inside a machine of provinces, districts and registers.

The documentary basis for the architectural reading is unusually firm. The primary text is the Nagpur Congress Constitution of 1920 itself, printed in the Congress's records and reproduced in Gandhi's Collected Works, volumes 19 and 20, which cover the drafting and the first year of operation. The All India Congress Committee's Working Committee minutes for 1920 to 1922, preserved at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, record the machine being switched on: provincial committees reconstituted, district committees recognised, membership returns demanded. The secondary literature named in this article's adjudication, Judith Brown's Gandhi's Rise to Power (1972), Sumit Sarkar's Modern India 1885-1947 (1983) and Bipan Chandra and his co-authors in India's Struggle for Independence (1988), supplies the scholarly disagreement that the final section weighs. Gandhi's promise at Nagpur, that swaraj could arrive within a year if the programme were completed, frames the period's urgency; the constitution, rather than the promise, is what survived the year.

## The Congress Gandhi Inherited: An Annual Gathering Rather Than a Machine

To measure what Nagpur changed, the antecedent architecture needs its own precision. The Congress founded in December 1885, whose first president was Womesh Chandra Bonnerjee at Bombay, operated for its first two decades as an annual deliberative gathering of educated professionals. Its provincial committees mirrored the administrative map of British India, Bengal, Madras, Bombay, the United Provinces, the Punjab, the Central Provinces, because the organisation's working life ran through the lawyers, journalists and landlords who staffed those provinces' public life. Between sessions the party barely existed as an apparatus. A British committee in London, a journal, India, and the personal networks of its leaders carried its presence through the year. The politics matched the structure: [the Moderate-petition-politics architecture as antecedent](/2019/11/01/1885-founding-congress-moderate-phase/) rested on memorials, resolutions and deputations, instruments wielded by men who met once a year, drafted carefully and dispersed.

The structure survived its first great rupture without changing its nature. The Surat split of December 1907, where the Moderate and Extremist camps broke apart over the presidency and the four Calcutta resolutions of 1906, was a quarrel inside a sessions-based party about the temper of its demands, and [the 1907 architectural rupture as immediate antecedent](/2019/11/07/tilak-extremists-1907-surat-split/) left the underlying machinery untouched: the Extremists excluded, the annual session continued, the provincial committees still mapped onto the Raj's provinces, membership still an informal affiliation of the politically literate rather than an enrolled, fee-paying, card-carrying status. The Lucknow reunion of December 1916 and the Congress-League scheme restored unity of leadership. The Home Rule Leagues of Bal Gangadhar Tilak and Annie Besant, organised from 1916, demonstrated a different organisational possibility, branches, membership and year-round propaganda, and their pressure showed in the Congress's wartime temper. Gandhi himself took over Besant's league in April 1920 and renamed it the Swarajya Sabha. Yet on the eve of Nagpur the Congress constitution still described an organisation whose centre of gravity was a December week.

The Amritsar session of December 1919, held in the city of the massacre eight months after the event and presided over by Motilal Nehru, marks the transitional architecture's limit. Emotion and attendance were abundant; the Congress condemned the Punjab wrongs and demanded redress in resolutions of genuine power. Yet the gathering remained what its predecessors had been, a deliberative assembly without the standing machinery to convert its resolutions into a year of administered action in the districts, and the gap between the Amritsar resolutions and the country's organisational reality was the space into which Gandhi's programme stepped during 1920. The Khilafat conferences and the Satyagraha Sabha's pledge networks were already building parallel organisation outside the Congress frame, a duplication the Nagpur settlement ended by giving the Congress itself the machinery its rivals for the nation's energy had improvised.

Three deficits defined that inheritance, and the Nagpur drafting answers each in turn. The first deficit was territorial: a provincial committee for undivided Bengal or for the Madras Presidency addressed populations speaking several languages through an elite that worked in English, so mobilisation stopped at the district town. The second deficit was vertical: below the province there was no constitutionally recognised rung, no district committee with defined duties, no primary committee in a village or ward, and therefore no ladder by which an ordinary adherent could enter, pay, meet and be counted. The third deficit was temporal: the party woke for its session and slept for the remaining fifty weeks, leaving campaigns to improvisation by provincial bosses. A movement preparing to boycott councils, courts, colleges and foreign cloth across a subcontinent could not run on that skeleton. The reorganisation of December 1920 is best read as a point-by-point repair of these three deficits, drafted by men who had spent 1919 and 1920 discovering, in the Rowlatt satyagraha, the Punjab wrongs campaign and the Khilafat alliance, exactly where the old skeleton snapped under load.

## Innovation One: Provincial Congress Committees Drawn on Linguistic Lines

The Nagpur Constitution's boldest stroke was cartographic. The Congress divided its own India into twenty-one provincial committees organised by language, discarding the Raj's administrative provinces as the template for party geography. Andhra received a committee distinct from Tamil-speaking Madras. Karnataka, Kerala country under the name Malabar, Maharashtra apart from the Bombay city committee, Gujarat, Sindh, Bihar, Utkal for the Odia-speaking districts, and a Punjab committee for the Punjabi-speaking region all appeared as units of the party map, alongside committees for Bengal, Assam, the United Provinces, the Central Provinces in Hindi and Marathi divisions, the North-West Frontier, Delhi, Ajmer-Merwara and Berar. The precise roster matters less than the principle the roster enacted: the unit of Congress organisation would be the population a worker could actually address, in the language that population spoke at home, rather than the jurisdiction a British secretariat happened to administer.

The principle had a prehistory that the Nagpur drafting generalised. Demand for an Andhra committee separate from Madras had been pressed since the Andhra Mahajana Sangam conferences of the preceding decade, and the Congress had conceded a separate Andhra circle before 1920, an exception that proved the rule's weakness. Telugu-speaking delegates at successive sessions had to conduct their politics inside a Madras committee dominated by Tamil-speaking Madras city lawyers, and the friction was structural rather than personal. Gandhi's own campaigning from 1915 onward, at Champaran in Bihar in 1917, at Kheda and Ahmedabad in Gujarat in 1918, had taught the same lesson from the field: mobilisation moved at the speed of the vernacular, and an organisation drawn on secretariat lines would always arrive late to its own meetings. The Nagpur settlement converted scattered concessions into a universal rule, applied to every region at once, including regions such as Sindh and Utkal whose claims rested on language communities that the Raj's map folded into Bombay and Bihar respectively.

### How did the linguistic PCCs differ from the provinces of British India?

The Raj's provinces were revenue and administrative jurisdictions, drawn for governance and often multilingual: the Madras Presidency held Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam and Kannada speakers under one secretariat. The Nagpur committees were drawn for mobilisation, one language community to one committee, so Andhra, Karnataka and Kerala received Congress provinces decades before India received linguistic states.

The long consequence deserves emphasis because it runs in one direction. When the Indian Republic reorganised its states on linguistic lines, with the States Reorganisation Commission under Fazl Ali appointed in December 1953 and [the post-1956 linguistic-state reorganisation as later parallel](/2019/02/10/states-reorganisation-act-1956-india/) redrawing the federal map from November 1956, the state boundaries followed a geography that Congress workers had inhabited for thirty-five years. A generation of politicians had been trained, promoted and disciplined inside linguistic units; the demand for linguistic states was pressed hardest by men whose entire party careers had unfolded within an Andhra, a Karnataka or a Maharashtra committee. The Nagpur map did not cause the 1956 settlement, and the causal chain runs through the Dhar Commission, the JVP report and the fast of Potti Sriramulu in December 1952. What Nagpur supplied was the experienced cadre and the normalised expectation that language was the natural unit of political organisation, an expectation the party's own constitution had taught since 1920. Architectural choices made for mobilisation hardened, across three decades, into the common sense of federal design.

The individual cases show the rule doing different work in different regions. Andhra's committee converted a cultural demand into an organisational fact: the Telugu districts had agitated for separation from the Madras committee through the Andhra Mahajana movement for years, and the Nagpur settlement let Telugu workers build a province of their own in which the district organisations of the Godavari, Krishna and Guntur country became famous nurseries of the party's organisers. Sindh, folded into the Bombay Presidency for every administrative purpose, received a Congress province of its own on the strength of the Sindhi language, and its committee gave Karachi and Hyderabad a direct line to the centre that the Bombay secretariat had never offered. Utkal gathered the Odia-speaking districts out of three separate jurisdictions, Bihar, Madras and the Central Provinces, into a single party province, performing at committee level a unification the Raj did not attempt for Odisha until 1936. The Hindi-speaking Mahakoshal committee and the Marathi-speaking committee of the Central Provinces divided a single British province between two language communities, demonstrating that the Nagpur cartographers were prepared to cut against the administrative grain in both directions, merging where language united and splitting where it divided.

The linguistic provinces also relocated the party's sovereign gathering. The pre-1920 sessions had rotated among the presidency capitals and the great provincial cities, Bombay, Calcutta, Madras, Lahore, Lucknow, places where an elite host class could house and feed a conference of lawyers. After Nagpur the session itself began travelling into the party's new geography: Kakinada on the Andhra coast in December 1923 under Maulana Muhammad Ali's presidency, Belgaum in the Karnataka country in December 1924, Gauhati in Assam in December 1926 under Srinivasa Iyengar, and, at the line's end, Faizpur village in December 1936. Each venue was a demonstration that the host province's committee could build a session city from its own resources, volunteers and funds, and the reception committees read like rolls of the new provincial leaderships. The geography of sovereignty mattered to the architecture's meaning: a party whose highest body met in a mofussil town under canvas had declared, in the plainest logistical terms, that authority flowed from the provinces upward rather than radiating from three metropolitan salons.

The linguistic provinces also altered the party's internal language. The pre-1920 Congress transacted its national business overwhelmingly in English, the tongue of its lawyer leadership, and its provincial work in a translation of that business. A committee constituted for a language region naturally worked in that region's language: Andhra's proceedings in Telugu, Gujarat's in Gujarati, Maharashtra's in Marathi, with Hindustani pressed by Gandhi as the link language of the centre and the annual session's debates increasingly conducted with interpretation for delegates who had no English. The shift was administrative before it was symbolic. Registers, receipts, circulars and conference resolutions in the vernacular multiplied the class of people who could hold party office, because the qualification for a district secretary became knowledge of his district rather than command of the secretariat's tongue. The mass party's famous social broadening, its intake of small-town traders, schoolteachers, peasants' sons and vernacular journalists, was mediated by this linguistic machinery as much as by the four-anna fee, and the two innovations reinforced each other: the fee admitted the member, the language admitted him to the meeting.

## Innovation Two: District Congress Committees as the Working Unit

A linguistic province remained, on its own, too large to do work. The second innovation fixed the level at which the party's business would actually be transacted: the district. The Nagpur Constitution recognised District Congress Committees as the standard operational unit beneath each provincial committee, with town and taluka committees beneath the district in the countryside and ward committees in the cities, the Congress's synopsis of the Nagpur changes listing ward committees among the session's organisational decisions. Each district committee carried defined responsibilities: enrolling members through the primary committees, collecting the membership subscription and the party's funds, convening district conferences, and executing the campaigns the Working Committee announced, whether the boycott of foreign cloth in 1921 or the salt laws in 1930.

The institutional consequence was a permanent address. Before 1920, a villager who wished to join the national movement faced a journey to a provincial capital and an introduction to a notable. After 1920, the movement maintained an office, a secretary and a register in his own district town, and a primary committee in or near his own village. The district became the rung where the party's two scarcest resources were manufactured: reliable local information flowing upward, about prices, officials, grievances and mood, and disciplined local execution flowing downward, about meetings, pickets, processions and boycotts. The Working Committee minutes of 1920 to 1922, preserved at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, show the centre learning to use the new rung within months, demanding district-wise membership returns, arbitrating district-level factional disputes and addressing circulars to district secretaries by name. A party that can count its districts can plan a campaign the way a staff plans a march, province by province and district by district, and the great Gandhian campaigns of the period were planned in exactly that grammar.

The district committee's reach extended, in the movement's first year, into the administration of justice itself. The boycott of the law courts under the non-cooperation programme required an alternative forum for the disputes the courts would no longer hear from Congress adherents, and the programme directed the committees to establish arbitration boards and panchayats in the districts and towns. Across several provinces in 1921 these Congress panchayats heard civil disputes, enforced their awards through the movement's moral authority and reported through the district committees, giving the party an adjudicatory presence in local life that no Indian political organisation had previously attempted. The experiment faded as the movement ebbed after 1922, and its awards never acquired legal force, but its institutional meaning survives the fading: the district committee was conceived by its designers as a general-purpose organ of local governance in waiting, not as a mere election or agitation bureau, and the villagers who carried their boundary disputes to a Congress panchayat in 1921 had grasped the design's ambition as clearly as the drafters had.

The district conference was the unit's characteristic institution. Provincial and district political conferences, a form the pre-1920 decades had used sporadically, became regular fixtures of the party's year under the new constitution, convening the primary committees' workers to hear reports, settle accounts, elect office-bearers and receive the centre's programme from visiting leaders. The conference circuit gave the movement a calendar richer than the annual session: a district worker's year held the primary committee's meetings, the district conference, the provincial conference and, for the few, the December session, each level reporting to the next. When campaigns came, the circuit doubled as the mobilisation timetable, and when repression came, the same circuit, driven indoors and into smaller rooms, preserved the chain of acquaintance on which the next campaign would be built. The district conference also served as the party's court of first instance for local faction, and the provincial committees' surviving correspondence is heavy with conference disputes, rival delegate lists and contested elections, the ordinary litigation of a mass organisation that took its own procedures seriously enough to fight over them.

District organisation also redistributed power inside the party, a consequence the drafters understood and accepted. Provincial bosses of the older type, whose authority rested on their control of the annual session's delegate lists and their standing in the provincial capital, faced a new class of district organisers whose authority rested on enrolled members and functioning committees. The district secretary who could deliver five thousand four-anna members and a disciplined picket line possessed a claim on the party's attention that no amount of session oratory could match. Across the 1920s this new class supplied the party's middle leadership: men apprenticed in district committees rose to provincial executives and, after 1937, to provincial ministries. The architecture thereby reproduced itself, because each cohort of district workers had been formed by the structure and defended it as the natural order of Congress life.

## Innovation Three: Primary Committees and the Four-Anna Door

The third innovation reached the bottom of the ladder. The Nagpur Constitution provided for primary committees at the level of the village and the urban locality, and it fixed the price of belonging at four annas a year. The clause can be quoted exactly, because the Belgaum session of December 1924 replaced it and printed the original text in its amendment: every person not disqualified under the constitution's Article IV, and paying a subscription of four annas per year, was entitled to become a member of any organisation controlled by the Provincial Congress Committees. The sentence is preserved in the Collected Works, volume 19, at page 192, cited in the Belgaum resolution's own footnote. Four annas amounted to a quarter of a rupee, one sixteenth of a rupee to the anna, a sum set against the earnings of an agricultural labourer rather than against the incomes of the pleaders who had staffed the old Congress.

The sum acquires its meaning against the period's prices. Four annas stood at roughly a day's wage for an agricultural labourer in many districts, as the standard accounts of the movement note, and at a small fraction of the incomes of the barristers and mill-owners who had underwritten the old Congress. It was priced, in other words, at the level of a working family's sacrifice rather than a schoolboy's pocket money or a patron's subscription, high enough to be felt and recorded, low enough to be paid in every village the committees reached. The contrast with the party's own older tariffs is instructive: the annual session's delegate fees and the costs of session travel had always filtered attendance toward the prosperous, and it was the Belgaum assembly of 1924, acting inside the new order's logic, that cut the delegate fee from ten rupees to one.

The calibration was deliberate and the drafting record shows its two-sided logic. A fee of zero would have made membership a mood, untestable and uncountable, and a movement preparing boycotts needed registers it could trust when it asked members to surrender titles, withdraw children from government schools or court arrest. A fee at the older levels, which had priced participation toward the professional classes, would have kept the mass party a fiction. Four annas bought a receipt, a register entry and a primary committee obliged to meet, which together converted sympathy into an auditable organisational fact. Gandhi's financing practice since the South African campaigns had run on the same principle, small sums collected from large numbers through printed appeal and public accounting, and the Tilak Swaraj Fund, launched at Nagpur in Bal Gangadhar Tilak's memory after his death on 1 August 1920, applied the principle at campaign scale.

The membership drive that followed gives the architecture its first stress test, and the test is documented in targets rather than in boasts. Meeting at Vijayawada in April 1921, the Working Committee fixed the Bezwada programme: one crore of rupees for the Tilak Swaraj Fund by 30 June 1921, one crore of members enrolled on the Congress registers, and twenty lakh charkhas set to work. The figures were aspirational and the returns fell short of the crore lines, a shortfall Sumit Sarkar records with care in Modern India 1885-1947 (1983), noting the unevenness of provincial returns and the inflation that crept into district reporting when targets pressed. The shortfall does not acquit the old architecture or convict the new one. What the Bezwada targets reveal is a party thinking in enrolled millions for the first time in its existence, and possessing, in the primary committees and the four-anna receipt, the only instrument by which such a target could even be stated without absurdity. The pre-1920 Congress, counting its adherents in the low thousands and its delegates in the low thousands as well, could not have announced a crore of members because it had built no door through which a crore could enter.

## The Economics of the Four-Anna Party: Funds, Quotas and Public Accounts

The four-anna clause changed the party's finances as profoundly as its franchise, and the financial architecture deserves separate treatment because it explains how the committees lived. The pre-1920 Congress had been financed by patronage: prosperous merchants and lawyers in the session's host city bore the costs of the annual gathering, wealthy sympathisers answered special appeals, and the organisation's permanent expenses were small because the organisation itself was small. That model could not fund standing committees in twenty-one provinces and several hundred districts, full-time organisers on stipends, a central office with paid secretaries, printing, postage and the legal defence of arrested workers. The Nagpur settlement replaced patronage with subscription finance at mass scale. Four annas from each of a lakh of members yielded 25,000 rupees; from ten lakhs, two and a half lakh rupees, recurring annually and forecastable in a budget. The membership fee was therefore not a token of belonging laid over the real finance of donation. It was the fiscal constitution of the new party, designed so that the machine's running costs were carried by the enrolled many rather than negotiated with the wealthy few.

The Tilak Swaraj Fund demonstrated the same plumbing at campaign scale within the constitution's first year. Announced in Tilak's memory after his death on 1 August 1920 and driven through 1921 against the Bezwada target of one crore of rupees by 30 June 1921, the fund was collected by quota: the centre assessed the provinces, the provinces assessed their districts, and the districts collected through primary committees and local workers, issuing receipts and publishing totals. Bombay's contribution of some 37.5 lakh rupees led the returns and earned the city Gandhi's public praise. The fund paid for the movement's working costs, the national schools, the khadi programme's organisation, the relief of workers' families and the Congress's own offices, converting political sympathy into an administered budget for the first time in the party's history. The accounting discipline mattered to the design's author: Gandhi's South African funds had been rendered in print, rupee by rupee, in the columns of Indian Opinion, and the Congress's funds under the new dispensation were likewise reported in the party's press, including Young India and Navajivan, as a standing answer to the charge that mass collections invited leakage.

Two consequences followed for the party's internal balance of power. The first was the quiet emancipation of the committees from the notables. A district secretary whose office ran on receipted subscriptions and an audited quota owed his position to the register rather than to a patron's favour, and the Working Committee's insistence on returns, visible throughout its minutes of 1920 to 1922, gave the centre a financial instrument of discipline parallel to its political one: a committee that failed to collect and remit could be reorganised, and the threat was used. The second consequence was a new politics of the receipt book. Because representation and prestige inside the party tracked enrolment, the integrity of the registers became a contested asset, and disputes over inflated or bogus membership recur in the party's internal record from the 1920s to the celebrated quarrels over delegate rolls in 1939. The four-anna receipt, humble as it looks beside the era's manifestos, was the point where the mass party's idealism met its audit, and the meeting shaped the organisation as deeply as any resolution.

## Innovation Four: A Party That Never Adjourned

The first three innovations rebuilt the party's base and map. The fourth rebuilt its calendar. The Nagpur Constitution created a Working Committee of fifteen members as the party's standing executive and raised the All India Congress Committee to 350 members as the party's standing legislature between sessions. The Congress's own synopsis of the 1920 session compresses the change into a line: the All India Congress Committee's membership raised to 350, a Working Committee of fifteen appointed. Behind the line stands a transfer of sovereignty. The annual session retained the power to declare the party's creed and programme, and its Subjects Committee continued to draft the resolutions the plenary debated. Between sessions, however, authority to interpret the programme, discipline the committees, manage the funds and answer events passed to a small executive that could meet in any month, at any place, on a few days of notice.

### How many members did the All India Congress Committee have after the Nagpur changes?

The Nagpur Constitution raised the All India Congress Committee to 350 members, drawn from the provincial committees in proportion to population, and set a Working Committee of fifteen above it as the standing executive. Between annual sessions the 350 supplied the party's legislature and the fifteen its government, a two-tier centre the old Congress never possessed.

The presidency itself was repositioned by the new order, a detail the session's own choice illustrates. The annual president had been, in the old party, the year's presiding notable, honoured for a week and retired to his province. Under the Nagpur working, the presidency became a one-year magistracy at the head of a continuous executive, its holder chairing the year's deliberations of the Working Committee and embodying the party before the government and the country until the next session replaced him. Vijayaraghavachariar was succeeded in the office by a rotation that itself enacted the architecture's geography: Hakim Ajmal Khan at Ahmedabad in 1921, Das at Gaya in 1922, Maulana Muhammad Ali at Kakinada in 1923, Gandhi at Belgaum in 1924, Sarojini Naidu at Kanpur in 1925, Srinivasa Iyengar at Gauhati in 1926, a succession that carried the party's crown through the provinces and communities in turn, binding each region's committee to the centre by the honour of supplying its president. The office's rotation was not in the constitution's letter as a quota; it was the constitution's spirit operating through the ladder, since a president thereafter needed the assent of a party organised in provinces whose committees expected the honour to circulate.

The practical difference appeared within the new constitution's first hundred days. When the Working Committee met at Vijayawada in April 1921 to fix the Bezwada targets, an executive of the party was setting enrolment and finance goals for the whole country in the middle of the party's year, a scene the pre-1920 constitution had no machinery to produce. When the movement's crisis arrived in February 1922, the same executive, meeting at Bardoli on 11 and 12 February, could suspend the mass civil disobedience campaign for the entire organisation within a week of the Chauri Chaura killings, and the suspension held in every province because the order travelled down a chain of committees that owed their places to the constitution. Year-round presence, the phrase the brief for this architecture uses, means exactly this: the party's decisions, whether of advance or of retreat, became enforceable in February and August as well as in the December of the session. Cadre presence was the human form of the same fact, full-time workers attached to provincial and district committees, supported by party funds and the constructive programme's institutions, for whom the Congress was an occupation rather than a season.

## The Committee Ladder: How Representation Climbed from the Village to the Centre

The Nagpur Constitution was a ladder as well as a map, and its rungs were connected by election and apportionment so that authority could be traced from the primary member to the Working Committee without a break. At the base, the enrolled members of a primary committee elected its office-bearers and sent representatives upward to the town, taluka and district committees. District committees answered to the provincial committee, which supervised their recognition, their elections and their returns. The provincial committees in turn elected the All India Congress Committee, its 350 seats apportioned among the provinces in proportion to population, so that populous Bihar and the United Provinces carried weight at the centre that Berar or Ajmer-Merwara did not, a deliberate departure from the older habit of treating each province's delegation as a diplomatic mission among equals. Delegates to the annual session were chosen through the same provincial machinery, and the session's Subjects Committee, drawn from the central leadership and the host province, drafted the resolutions the plenary adopted. The Working Committee of fifteen sat at the ladder's top, chosen from the All India Congress Committee's ranks with the president's hand decisive in its composition in the practice of the period.

The ladder's design solved a problem the old party had never faced because it had never attempted sustained mass action: how to make a decision taken in one room binding on a million enrolled strangers. The answer the constitution gave was representational continuity. A district secretary disciplined by his provincial committee could not plead that the Bardoli suspension of February 1922 was Delhi's affair, because his own mandate derived through the same chain that issued the order; a provincial committee resisting a Working Committee circular faced the constitutional fact that its own seats at the centre were apportioned and recognised under the same text. The chain also ran upward with information. Primary committees reported enrolment and grievance to districts, districts consolidated returns for provinces, and the centre's picture of the country, displayed in the Working Committee's minutes of 1920 to 1922 as it demanded district-wise figures and arbitrated local disputes, was assembled from the ladder's rungs rather than from newspaper clippings and touring impressions.

The ladder also educated. A worker who enrolled members in a village primary committee, kept its register, carried its quota to the district town and sat in the district conference acquired the complete curriculum of the movement's administration before he ever addressed a large meeting. The Congress's celebrated provincial leaders of the 1930s and 1940s were produced by this curriculum in cohort after cohort, and its effects outlasted the movement: the parliamentary party of 1937 and the Constituent Assembly's Congress benches after 1946 were staffed overwhelmingly by men whose political formation had occurred on the Nagpur ladder's middle rungs. Constitutions rarely advertise their pedagogic function, yet the Nagpur text's deepest long-run product may be neither its campaigns nor its funds but the trained political class it manufactured as a by-product of routine committee work.

## Where the Architecture Came From: South Africa, Champaran, Kheda, Khilafat

Architectures have biographies, and the Nagpur design's biography runs through Gandhi's two decades of organisational apprenticeship before 1920. The Natal Indian Congress, founded at Durban in August 1894, taught the young lawyer the rudiments he never abandoned: a named membership, a modest subscription, an executive committee, and a printed organ, Indian Opinion from 1903, binding scattered adherents into a counted body. The Phoenix Settlement of 1904 and Tolstoy Farm of 1910 added the residential cadre idea, workers living under discipline at the movement's expense. The Transvaal campaigns of 1906 to 1914 then tested the instruments at scale, marches staffed by enrolled satyagrahis whose names, villages and families the organisation knew. When Gandhi redrew the Indian party's map in 1920, he was scaling a toolkit assembled across twenty years, not improvising in a single December.

The South African record contains one more rehearsal that the Nagpur text silently incorporates. The great march of November 1913, when some two thousand striking miners and their families crossed from Natal into the Transvaal in defiance of the pass laws, was organised from lists: the strikers of the Newcastle coalfields were enrolled through the strike committees, their dependants registered for relief, the march's columns marshalled by named captains answerable for named followers. Gandhi's own accounts of the march dwell on the logistics of feeding and counting a multitude on the road, and the campaign's success in extracting the Indian Relief Act of 1914 rested on that administratable multitude. Seven years later the Nagpur Constitution gave the Indian party the same instruments at continental scale, the register, the receipt, the responsible local officer, the centre that could count what it commanded. The Ahmedabad mill-hands' organisation completed the Indian side of the apprenticeship in the very year of Nagpur: the Textile Labour Association, founded in 1920 out of the 1918 strike's settlement, enrolled the mill workers of Ahmedabad on subscription registers with elected representatives in each mill, a union architecture of dues and delegates that Gandhi treated as a model of the organised constituency, proof at the scale of a single industry that enrolled membership and disciplined representation could be built among the poorest workers of an Indian city.

The Indian apprenticeship of 1915 to 1920 supplied the specifically Indian components. At Champaran in 1917, Gandhi's inquiry into the tinkathia indigo system was executed through a network of local volunteers who recorded the statements of thousands of raiyats village by village, an early demonstration that district and village level organisation could carry a campaign's evidentiary load. At Kheda in 1918 the no-tax campaign ran through village committees and pledged signatories in the district's villages, with Vallabhbhai Patel emerging as the organiser of the district machine. At Ahmedabad in 1918 the mill-hands' strike added the urban ward and the labour constituency. The Rowlatt satyagraha of April 1919, organised through the Satyagraha Sabha, taught the complementary lesson of what happened when mobilisation outran organisation: the violence of April 1919 and the Punjab catastrophe that followed, including the Jallianwala Bagh massacre of 13 April 1919, entered the Nagpur drafting as a standing warning that mass energy without enrolled, disciplined committees was a danger to the movement itself. The Khilafat alliance of 1919 and 1920, built with the Ali brothers and the Central Khilafat Committee, demonstrated coalition architecture, two organisations with distinct registers cooperating through a common programme, and it carried the Congress's new machinery into districts where the party had never previously reached.

Two further episodes of the apprenticeship deserve entry in the record, because each supplied a mechanism Nagpur generalised. The first is Gandhi's year of preparation after his return to India in January 1915, when Gopal Krishna Gokhale extracted from him a promise to spend a year travelling and observing before pronouncing on Indian politics. The tour of 1915, conducted by rail through every province and recorded in the third-class compartments he insisted on using, gave Gandhi the district-eye view of India that his constitution later institutionalised: he had seen the country at the level where the party did not yet exist, and the Nagpur map reads, in part, as a plan to colonise that level. The second episode is the Satyagraha Sabha of 1919, the body formed at Bombay in February of that year to organise resistance to the Rowlatt Bills. The Sabha's instrument was the signed pledge, a printed form on which the satyagrahi enrolled his name, address and undertaking, and the Sabha collected pledges through local committees in a recognisable rehearsal for the four-anna register: enrolment as a deliberate, documented act, counted centrally, binding the signer to discipline. The pledge campaign of 1919 collapsed into the April violence and the Punjab repression, and the lesson Gandhi drew, that enrolment must precede exhortation and that registers without permanent committees could not hold discipline, is written into the Nagpur Constitution's insistence on standing district and primary organs beneath the pledge's successor, the membership receipt.

Read against this record, each Nagpur innovation has a traceable source in Gandhi's practice. The linguistic committee answers the South African and Gujarati lesson that people are organised in the language of their homes. The district unit generalises Champaran and Kheda. The primary committee and the four-anna receipt extend the Satyagraha Sabha's pledge and the small-subscription financing of the South African funds to a national register. The standing executive converts the campaign committee, which Gandhi had formed and dissolved for each struggle since 1906, into a permanent organ of the party. Judith Brown's Gandhi's Rise to Power (1972), the canonical study of these years, documents the apprenticeship in detail even where its interpretation privileges strategy over structure; the apprenticeship is the evidence that the structure was designed, and designed by the man whose campaigns had generated its requirements.

## The Drafting Question: Gandhi's Hand and His Colleagues' Hands

The strongest counter to the architectural reading is biographical, and it deserves its strongest form. The Nagpur text was not written by Gandhi alone at a single desk. The session's general secretaries, Motilal Nehru, C. Rajagopalachari and M. A. Ansari, carried the organisational labour of the period. Rajagopalachari, in Madras, became the foremost administrator of the new provincial order in the Tamil country. Jawaharlal Nehru served the United Provinces committee as an organiser and learned the district trade in the western districts of his province. Vallabhbhai Patel built the Gujarat machine that became the party's model district organisation. The constitution's formal drafting passed through committee work at and before the session, and its implementation across twenty-one provinces was, unavoidably, the work of hundreds of provincial and district hands. On this evidence the counter-reading concludes that the architecture was a collective product for which the leadership framing, with Gandhi as first among strategists, remains the adequate account.

### Why did C. R. Das move the non-cooperation resolution at Nagpur after opposing it at Calcutta?

Das had opposed Gandhi's programme at Calcutta in September 1920 chiefly over the boycott of the councils, fearing the surrender of Bengal's strongest arena. By December his provincial consultations convinced him the country would follow Gandhi regardless, and Das chose leadership of the movement over opposition to it, moving the resolution himself.

The drafting's paper trail supports the attribution of the design. Gandhi expounded his organisational intentions in print through the autumn of 1920, using the columns of Young India to argue for a Congress remade as a working body of enrolled members and responsible committees, and the constitution's text travelled to Nagpur as a prepared scheme rather than as a session improvisation, debated in the Subjects Committee and carried in open session within the session's week. The treatise form of the document matters to the adjudication: constitutions of this completeness, with their articles numbered and their fee clauses preserved for later amendment at Belgaum to cite by volume and page, are produced by a drafting mind working to a design over months, not by a secretariat improvising between resolutions. The secretaries of the session, Rajagopalachari pre-eminent among them as an administrator, were the design's executors and improvers, and the provinces' organisers its builders, but the drawing itself bears the marks catalogued in this article's apprenticeship section, each mechanism traceable to a Gandhi campaign of the preceding fifteen years.

The counter proves less than it claims, for two reasons grounded in the record rather than in reverence. The first is the authorship of the design's distinctive choices. The four innovations are not the generic furniture any committee of 1920 would have produced. Linguistic provinces contradicted the administrative common sense of every senior lawyer in the hall, including the session's own president, and they reproduce Gandhi's organisational convictions formed in South Africa and Gujarat. The four-anna fee contradicted the fiscal instincts of a party accustomed to financing itself from wealthy patrons, and it reproduces Gandhi's small-sum financing doctrine. The drafting is credited in the standard accounts, and in the placement of the constitution's text within Gandhi's Collected Works, volumes 19 and 20, to Gandhi's own hand and dictation in the session's preparatory work, with his lieutenants revising and administering rather than originating the scheme. The second reason is the division of labour the record shows after adoption. The colleagues named in the counter were not the architecture's rivals; they were its first provincial franchisees, and their eminence inside the post-1920 party derived in large part from the committees they built under the Nagpur rules. A reading that credits Gandhi with the strategy and his colleagues with the organisation must still explain why the organisation's design choices bear the stamp of Gandhi's prior practice at every distinctive point. The architectural reading explains it directly: the strategist and the architect were the same man, and the colleagues were the builders who made his drawings stand in twenty-one provinces.

## The Machine at Work, 1921 and 1922: Non-Cooperation Through the New Circuits

The non-cooperation campaign of 1921 was the first Indian political movement administered as a national programme through standing committees, and the Nagpur circuits carried loads the old party could not have lifted. The boycott of foreign cloth required pickets at cloth merchants' shops in thousands of localities, organised by district and town committees and staffed by enrolled volunteers whose names stood on primary committee registers. The surrender of titles and the withdrawal of students from government institutions required local verification and local persuasion, work done by the same committees. The national schools and colleges founded outside the official system, from the Gujarat Vidyapith and the Kashi Vidyapith to the Jamia Millia Islamia, were financed and governed through the movement's funds and committees rather than through private charity alone. The pattern is worth pausing on, because it shows committees acting as a shadow administration: Gandhi founded the Gujarat Vidyapith at Ahmedabad in October 1920; the Jamia Millia Islamia was founded at Aligarh in October 1920 by the Khilafat movement's educationists and later moved to Delhi; the Kashi Vidyapith followed at Benares in February 1921 and the Bihar Vidyapith in the same season. Each institution enrolled students withdrawn from government colleges under the boycott, employed teachers the official system had dismissed or who had resigned under the programme, and drew its budget from the movement's funds administered through committee structures. A party that can run universities on its subscription income has crossed the line from protest organisation to parallel polity, and the crossing was executed by district and provincial committees applying the Nagpur text to education as readily as to picketing. The Tilak Swaraj Fund's collection, against the Bezwada targets fixed in April 1921, ran through provincial quotas assigned to district committees, the party's new fiscal plumbing carrying money upward from the primary committee to the centre for the first time in the party's history.

The campaign's second year showed the circuits carrying a boycott of the highest symbolic order. The visit of the Prince of Wales, landing at Bombay on 17 November 1921, was met by a hartal administered through the provincial and district committees across India; at Bombay the welcome crowds and the boycott processions collided in days of rioting that killed more than fifty people, and Gandhi fasted for five days in penance and repair, working through the same committee network to restore the discipline the boycott had been designed to display. The Ahmedabad session of December 1921, with Hakim Ajmal Khan in the chair and the president-elect, C. R. Das, in prison, registered the movement's confidence in its machinery by investing Gandhi with sole executive authority for the campaign's conduct, a delegation of power by a deliberative body to one man that only makes sense inside a constitution: the session could lend its sovereignty because the constitution assured its return.

The campaign's end tested the architecture harder than its advance had done. On 5 February 1922, at Chauri Chaura in Gorakhpur district, a procession fired on by police burned the police station and killed twenty-two policemen. Gandhi, treating the killings as proof that the country's discipline had not matched its enrolment, pressed the Working Committee at Bardoli on 11 and 12 February 1922 to suspend mass civil disobedience across India. The episode is usually told as a drama of one man's conscience. Organisationally, it is a demonstration of the Nagpur chain of command: a decision taken by fifteen men in a Gujarat town was transmitted through twenty-one provincial committees and their district organs, and a movement of millions stood down within days, baffling a government prepared for escalation. No committee of the pre-1920 party could have issued such an order with any expectation of obedience, because no such chain existed. Gandhi was arrested at Ahmedabad on 10 March 1922, tried before Judge C. N. Broomfield on 18 March, and sentenced to six years of imprisonment, and the machine he had built spent the following two years operating without its architect at liberty, which is the final proof that what Nagpur built was an institution and not a retinue.

## Holding the Frame, 1922 to 1929: Swarajists, No-Changers and the Belgaum Amendments

The decade's middle years subjected the architecture to the strain of strategic disagreement, and the architecture's survival of the Swarajist controversy is among the strongest evidence for the architectural reading. At the Gaya session of December 1922, presided over by C. R. Das, the proposal to enter the legislative councils was defeated, and Das with Motilal Nehru resigned their offices and founded the Congress-Khilafat Swarajya Party, the Swaraj Party, on 1 January 1923, to contest elections and obstruct the councils from within. In the November 1923 elections the Swarajists won forty-two seats in the Central Legislative Assembly and a majority in the Central Provinces council, becoming the largest single bloc in the imperial legislature. The opposing No-Changer camp, Rajagopalachari, Vallabhbhai Patel, Rajendra Prasad and M. A. Ansari among its leaders, held to boycott and the constructive programme of khadi, national education and the removal of untouchability. The dispute was the sharpest strategic quarrel since Surat, and at Surat in 1907 a quarrel of this rank had split the party for nine years. After Nagpur it did not split the party at all, because the constitution supplied what 1907 lacked: a Working Committee that could arbitrate, provincial committees within which both camps held office, and a membership register that belonged to the party rather than to either faction. The Swaraj Party contested elections as an acknowledged wing and was folded formally into the Congress order at Belgaum, inside the architecture rather than against it.

Belgaum also shows the constitution being amended by its own procedures, the mark of a working instrument rather than a sacred text. The thirty-ninth session met in December 1924 under Gandhi's presidency, the only Congress session he ever presided over, and it ratified the Gandhi-Swarajist settlement and rewrote the membership article. The four-anna subscription was supplemented, and for committee purposes effectively replaced, by the spinning franchise: a contribution of 20,000 yards of evenly spun yarn each year, payable in monthly instalments of 2,000 yards, with hand-spun and hand-woven khaddar required of committee members in their Congress capacity. The amendment printed the 1920 text it displaced, preserving the four-anna clause for the record in the Collected Works, volume 25. Gandhi's presidential term ran from December 1924 to April 1925, and his Belgaum address redirected the party's standing machinery toward the constructive programme, Hindu-Muslim unity, the removal of untouchability and village industry, tasks addressed to committees that sat all year rather than to a crowd that gathered once.

### What did the Belgaum session of 1924 change in Congress membership?

At Belgaum in December 1924, the only session Gandhi presided over, the Congress added the spinning franchise to its constitution: members qualified through 20,000 yards of self-spun yarn a year, paid in monthly instalments of 2,000 yards, and committee members were required to wear khaddar for Congress business.

The district machine's campaigning quality in these years is best sampled at Borsad, in Kaira district of Gujarat, where the government in 1923 imposed a punitive tax on the district's villages to pay for additional police against a wave of dacoities. The Gujarat provincial committee, directed by Vallabhbhai Patel through the district and taluka committees, organised a no-tax satyagraha in which village after village refused the levy, surrendered nothing to attachment proceedings and held discipline for three months, until the government withdrew the tax in January 1924 and returned the confiscated property. Borsad was a district campaign, conceived at district scale, staffed by taluka and village committees, financed by the province and won without a single all-India call, the Nagpur architecture operating as routine rather than as mobilisation. The settlement made Patel's reputation as the party's organiser and fixed the Gujarat model, the disciplined district, as the standard against which other provinces' committees were measured in the party's internal conversation.

The decade also produced the cadre's uniformed auxiliary. The Hindustani Seva Dal, organised from 1923 under N. S. Hardikar after the Kakinada session's call for a trained volunteer corps, drilled the party's young volunteers in camps across the provinces, supplying the disciplined marshals who managed the great gatherings and the picket lines of later campaigns. The Seva Dal's significance for the architectural argument is its constitutional position: a volunteer army housed inside a committee party, trained by a central board, deployed by provincial committees, the year-round-cadre-presence of the Nagpur design given a drill square and a bugle. Where the old Congress had relied on the hospitality of local notables to manage its crowds, the new party trained its own.

The closing years of the decade ran on the same chassis. At Kanpur in December 1925 Sarojini Naidu presided, the first Indian woman to hold the office, her election itself a product of the widened organisation the Nagpur registers had created, since the constituencies that sustained her candidature, the provincial committees and their conference circuits, had admitted women as enrolled members and office-bearers in numbers the old party never approached. The Madras session of December 1927, with the Simon Commission boycott and the first independence resolution moved by Jawaharlal Nehru, was administered through the provincial and district committees as a standing campaign. The Bardoli satyagraha of 1928, where Vallabhbhai Patel's district organisation in Surat district sustained a no-tax campaign to a settlement on its own terms, displayed the Gujarat district machine at its mature strength. At Lahore in December 1929, Jawaharlal Nehru presiding, the Congress declared Purna Swaraj as its goal on the bank of the Ravi and fixed 26 January 1930 as Independence Day, and the declaration was carried to the country not by proclamation alone but through the committee network that read the pledge in thousands of local meetings. The creed had been rewritten at Nagpur; at Lahore it was rewritten again, by the same machinery, without institutional crisis.

## The Standing Executive in Action: The Working Committee as a Shadow Cabinet

The fourth innovation's consequences are best audited in the Working Committee's own career, because the committee became, across the 1920s and 1930s, a species of institution the old Congress had never contained: a shadow cabinet in permanent session. Its composition read as a council of the movement's principals, Gandhi's lieutenants of the Nagpur secretariat, the provincial builders, the party's parliamentarians after 1923, and its deliberations set the line that the provincial committees executed and the annual sessions retrospectively blessed. The professionalisation of the centre accompanied the concentration: the office of general secretary of the All India Congress Committee, an administrative post in the new constitution, became a career in itself. Jawaharlal Nehru served as a general secretary in the 1920s and learned the machine from its switchboard. J. B. Kripalani then held the general secretaryship from 1934 to 1946, the longest administrative tenure of the era, running the central office, the provincial correspondence and the party's electoral machinery through the ministry period, the war and the final negotiations, a continuity of administration inconceivable in the era when the party's centre had been a president's private secretariat renewed each December.

Two episodes of the committee's maturity display its constitutional character under the period's highest pressures. In 1937, after the provincial election victories, the question of office acceptance divided the party's left from its leadership, and the settlement was reached not by session or by plebiscite but inside the executive architecture: the All India Congress Committee at Delhi in March 1937 authorised office acceptance on conditions, and the Congress Parliamentary Board, constituted under Vallabhbhai Patel's chairmanship with Maulana Azad and Rajendra Prasad among its members, supervised the ministries thereafter, selecting chief ministers, arbitrating provincial disputes and holding the governments to the party's programme. A movement party had grown, inside its standing executive, a second executive for its governments, and both were Nagpur's children: the fifteen of 1920 are the institutional ancestors of the board that disciplined the ministries of 1937 to 1939. In 1942 the same committee carried the movement's gravest decisions at executive speed: the Working Committee at Wardha on 14 July 1942 passed the draft resolution demanding the British withdrawal, the All India Congress Committee at Bombay ratified it on 8 August as the Quit India resolution, and within hours of the ratification the committee's members were in prison, leaving the movement to the lower rungs exactly as the architecture's design contemplated. Negotiations with the Cripps Mission in March and April 1942 had likewise run through the committee, with Congress president Maulana Azad conducting the talks and the committee deciding the rejection. A party whose executive could negotiate with a British cabinet mission, supervise eight provincial governments and launch a rebellion on a month's deliberation had become a state in rehearsal, and the rehearsal stage was built in December 1920.

## The Architecture Under Load, 1930 to 1947: Satyagraha, Office, War and Quit India

The Salt Satyagraha of 1930 was the Nagpur machine's masterpiece of logistics. Gandhi's march from Sabarmati Ashram on 12 March 1930 with seventy-eight chosen followers, reaching Dandi on 5 April and breaking the salt law on 6 April, was the visible spearhead; the campaign behind it was committee work, and its authorisation had followed the constitution's forms: the Lahore session had empowered the All India Congress Committee, the central committee had charged the Working Committee, and the Working Committee at Sabarmati in February 1930 authorised Gandhi to fix the campaign's opening, a chain of delegation from sovereign session to marching column that ran entirely through Nagpur's rungs. Provincial committees designated the coastal points and inland salt sources for parallel law-breaking, district committees organised the volunteers, the manufacture of contraband salt, the boycott pickets and the relief of arrested workers' families, and the repression that followed, with arrests counted in the tens of thousands, was absorbed by an organisation able to replace arrested office-bearers from its own registers. The Karachi session of March 1931, meeting after the Gandhi-Irwin Pact of 5 March 1931, adopted the resolution on Fundamental Rights and the National Economic Programme, drafting in committee the constitutional commitments that connected the movement party to [the founding triangulation](/2018/12/17/patel-nehru-ambedkar-founding-triangulation/) of the later Constituent Assembly period. The second civil disobedience campaign of 1932 to 1934, launched into ordinances and mass arrests, strained the committees severely and drove much of the party's life underground or into the constructive programme's institutions, yet the provincial and district frames were intact when the Government of India Act of 1935 opened provincial elections.

The intervening years added further proofs of the frame's flexibility. In October 1934 the Congress Socialist Party was founded inside the Congress by Jayaprakash Narayan, Acharya Narendra Deva and their associates, organising the party's left as a recognised current working through the same committees rather than as a rival party outside them, an absorption of ideological plurality the Nagpur text's broad creed and open membership made administratively routine. At Faizpur in December 1936 the annual session met for the first time in a village, Jawaharlal Nehru presiding, and adopted an agrarian programme, a choice of venue that only a party with genuine village-level organs could have executed as anything but theatre. At Tripuri in March 1939 the architecture processed its gravest internal challenge of the decade: Subhas Chandra Bose, standing for a second presidential term against the leadership's preference, defeated Pattabhi Sitaramayya by 1,580 votes to 1,377 in the delegates' poll, Gandhi declared the defeat his own, twelve members of the Working Committee resigned, and Bose, unable to form an executive that commanded the organisation, resigned the presidency in April 1939 and formed the Forward Bloc the following month. The episode is remembered as a crisis of personalities. In constitutional terms it is the Nagpur machinery working exactly as designed under maximum load: an election held on enrolled rolls, a result accepted, an executive impasse resolved by resignation rather than by schism, and the dissident leader departing to build his own organisation outside a party whose internal verdict he could not overturn.

The electoral test of 1937 converted the movement's architecture into a governing one without redesign. In the provincial elections held under the 1935 Act, the Congress contested 1,161 of the 1,585 provincial assembly seats and won 716, forming ministries in seven provinces in July 1937 and in Assam in 1938, eight in all. The candidates were selected, the campaigns staffed and the victorious legislators organised as a parliamentary party by the same provincial and district committees built for boycott and satyagraha, a transfer of function that a sessions-based party could not have executed. When the Congress ministries resigned in October and November 1939 over Viceroy Linlithgow's declaration of war without consultation, the party returned to opposition through the same machinery, ran the individual satyagraha campaign opened by Vinoba Bhave on 17 October 1940, and in August 1942 staked everything on the Quit India resolution adopted at Bombay on 8 August 1942. The arrest of the entire Working Committee on 9 August 1942 decapitated the party in a morning, and the movement that followed, underground networks, parallel governments at Ballia, Tamluk and Satara, the clandestine Congress Radio, ran on district and local cadres formed in the Nagpur structure's lower rungs, the only stratum of the party the police could not arrest in a single operation. The architecture's intake had also changed the party's composition in ways the enrolment clauses quietly governed. The four-anna register made no distinction of sex, and women entered the post-1920 Congress as enrolled members, committee workers and campaign leaders in a progression the old party's sociology had blocked: the picketing of liquor and foreign-cloth shops in 1921 and again in 1930 was led in city after city by women's volunteer groups organised through the local committees, Sarojini Naidu led the raid on the Dharasana salt works in May 1930 after Gandhi's arrest, and Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay's organising carried the committee form into constituencies the male leadership had not reached. The point is structural. A sessions-based party of lawyers had no rung on which a woman volunteer could stand; the Nagpur ladder, from primary committee upward, supplied the rungs, and the movement's celebrated broadening of its social base followed the ladder's shape.

The elections of 1945 and 1946, the interim government of September 1946 and the transfer of power on 15 August 1947 completed the twenty-seven years the Nagpur Constitution had framed. The architecture had carried a movement through boycott, satyagraha, office, resignation, prison and insurrection, and handed a governing party to the Republic's founders.

## The Four-Innovation Map: The Artifact of the Nagpur Settlement

The architectural reading stands or falls on whether the four innovations can be stated as a single citable map, each provision tied to the institutional consequence it produced across the twenty-seven years. That map is set out in Table 1: The Four Architectural Innovations of the Nagpur Constitution, 1920 to 1947, and readers citing the Nagpur settlement for its organisational substance should cite the article by this table's name.

| Innovation | Nagpur provision, December 1920 | Institutional consequence, 1920 to 1947 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Linguistic Provincial Congress Committees | Twenty-one PCCs drawn on language lines in place of the Raj's administrative provinces | Vernacular mobilisation from 1921; the cadre geography that normalised linguistic states by 1956 |
| District Congress Committees | The district made the standard operational unit, with town, taluka and ward committees beneath it | A permanent party address in every district; campaign logistics and the middle leadership of the movement |
| Primary committees and the four-anna fee | Village and locality committees enrolling any adult at four annas a year under Article VII | Membership as an audited register; the Bezwada targets of 1921 stated in crores; small-sum party finance |
| Standing executive and enlarged AICC | Working Committee of fifteen; All India Congress Committee raised to 350 | Year-round decision and discipline, from the Bardoli suspension of February 1922 to the Quit India direction of August 1942 |

The map also explains the Bezwada programme's arithmetic, which otherwise reads as rhetoric. The crore of members presupposed row three, the register and the receipt; their collection in a single season presupposed row two, districts capable of receiving quotas; their supervision in the vernacular presupposed row one, provinces drawn where the workers' language ran; and the fixing of the targets themselves, by an executive meeting in April for a deadline in June, presupposed row four. Read the columns in reverse and the table becomes a causal chain: the standing executive could set crore targets because the districts could collect for them, the districts could collect because the primaries could enrol, and the primaries could enrol because the fee was priced for the villager and the committee spoke his tongue.

The table's fourth row carries the map's central claim in miniature. Every celebrated episode of the period that the leadership framing attributes to Gandhi's personal authority, the Bardoli stand-down of 1922, the Belgaum settlement of 1924, the Lahore declaration of 1929, the ministry resignations of 1939, was in form an act of the standing executive or the enlarged central committee operating between sessions, transmitted through the provincial and district rungs the other three rows describe. Remove any row and the others falter: linguistic provinces without districts remain letterheads, districts without enrolled primary members remain notables' clubs, enrolment without a standing executive remains a crowd that gathers and disperses. The Nagpur drafting is a system, and the system is the achievement.

## What the Provincial Reports Show: Implementation, 1921 to 1925

The least-used evidence for the Nagpur settlement is the paperwork it generated. The provincial committees' organisational reports for 1921 to 1925, preserved in part at the All India Congress Committee's archives and in regional Congress collections, record the constitution being installed at different speeds and in different shapes across the twenty-one provinces, and they correct the tidy picture a Delhi-centred narrative suggests. Andhra's committee built district organisations rapidly on the back of the earlier Andhra movement's networks. Gujarat, under Vallabhbhai Patel's provincial leadership, developed the taluka-level discipline that Bardoli displayed in 1928. Bengal presented the recurring difficulty of a province where C. R. Das commanded a machine of his own construction, and the reports show the centre negotiating with a provincial organisation strong enough to bargain. The United Provinces reports show town and ward committees carrying the party into the qasbahs, with enrolment fluctuating sharply as campaigns rose and fell. Researchers reading these returns in the digitised provincial files through [an office file viewer for Excel, DOCX and PPTX records](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) encounter the architecture as its operators saw it: a register of committees formed, lapsed and re-formed, of fees collected and organisers appointed.

Two provincial vignettes from the reports illustrate the range. In the Tamil country, C. Rajagopalachari, a general secretary of the Nagpur session itself, organised the Tamil Nadu committee around the constructive programme's institutions, ashrams, khadi depots and prohibition campaigns, so that the province's committees retained a working membership between campaigns at the price of a quieter mass profile than Bengal's. In Karnataka, the unification of Kannada-speaking districts drawn from Bombay, Madras and Hyderabad jurisdictions into one linguistic committee made the party the first all-Karnataka institution of any kind, and the committee's conferences of the early 1920s doubled as a forum of the Karnataka unification movement a generation before the state existed. The reports from both provinces show the centre reading provincial returns with a manager's eye, querying districts whose enrolment collapsed, recognising new committees, and disaffiliating bodies that existed only as letterheads, the unglamorous jurisprudence of a constitution being kept alive by enforcement.

A third vignette, from the United Provinces, shows the reports doing corrective work. The province's returns in the movement's first year distinguished the western districts, where town committees and the Khilafat alliance's networks gave the party an urban skeleton, from the eastern districts of peasant mobilisation around Gorakhpur and Basti, where enrolment ran ahead of committee discipline with the consequences Chauri Chaura exposed in February 1922. The centre read the contrast in its own paperwork and answered it in kind, pressing the eastern districts' organisers on verification of rolls and the raising of taluka committees where primary enrolment had outrun structure. The episode belongs in any honest account of the architecture's first years: the registers did not merely record the movement, they diagnosed it, and the diagnosis occasionally arrived in time to matter.

The reports also register the architecture's honest limits, which the architectural reading must absorb rather than hide. Enrolment surged with campaigns and ebbed between them; the crore targets of the Bezwada programme were not met; some district committees existed chiefly on paper in quiet years; the spinning franchise of 1924, by substituting yarn for cash, narrowed the active membership in provinces where the constructive programme's organisation was thin. Sumit Sarkar's Modern India 1885-1947 (1983) remains the sharpest secondary guide to this unevenness, and his account of the gap between the movement's peaks and its organisational troughs disciplines any triumphalism. Yet the troughs are themselves the evidence of a structure: a party whose membership ebbs is a party that possesses membership as a measurable stock, recorded by committees that persist through the ebb and rebuild at the next call. The pre-1920 Congress had no troughs because it had no stock to measure.

## Counting the Mass Party: Registers, Receipts and the Membership Problem

A mass-membership architecture invites a question its predecessors never had to answer: how many members, counted how, on whose word? The Nagpur system's answer was documentary. Enrolment generated paper: the member's receipt for four annas, the primary committee's register, the district's consolidated return, the province's report to the centre. The chain meant that the party's claims about its own size were, in principle, auditable, and the Working Committee's minutes of the early 1920s show the centre auditing them, pressing provinces for district-wise breakups and treating collapsing returns as an organisational symptom rather than a public-relations inconvenience. The Bezwada targets of April 1921 were stated in crores precisely because the register system made a crore a meaningful unit of account: one crore of members meant one crore of receipts issued through named committees, a claim the provinces could be ordered to evidence.

The honesty of the paper chain varied, and the variation is part of the record. Sarkar's Modern India 1885-1947 (1983) warns against the returns of the movement's peak months, when district organisers under quota pressure enrolled names faster than committees could verify them, and the party's own internal debates returned repeatedly to bogus and inflated membership, a controversy that reached its height around the contested delegate rolls of the late 1930s. The architectural reading draws the opposite moral from the one the leadership framing suggests. Inflated registers are a pathology of register-based parties; a movement held together by charisma alone has no rolls to falsify and no quotas to game. That the Congress argued about the integrity of its membership figures for twenty-seven years is evidence that membership figures mattered, that office, representation and campaign planning ran on them, and that the organisation Nagpur built was the kind of thing that could have an integrity problem at all. The primary committees' registers, imperfect as they were, gave Indian politics its first continuously maintained count of a party's adherents, and every subsequent Indian party that adopted the membership book, the receipt and the district return was working, knowingly or not, in the Nagpur form.

## Adjudication: Leadership, Architecture, and the Evidence of Twenty-Seven Years

The named disagreement can finally be weighed. The political-strategic-leadership reading, in its strongest scholarly forms, holds that Gandhi's contribution to the post-1920 Congress was the strategy of non-cooperation, the moral authority that held a fractious coalition together, and the tactical judgement that launched and halted campaigns; on this reading the organisational changes of 1920 were the routine modernisation any mass movement required, executed by his lieutenants. Judith Brown's Gandhi's Rise to Power (1972) gives the reading its finest evidence, reconstructing Gandhi's construction of a political base among Gujarat's Patidars, the merchants and the Khilafat constituency between 1915 and 1922. Bipan Chandra and his co-authors, in India's Struggle for Independence (1988), present the movement's strategy of struggle and truce within which the organisational work sits as an enabling condition. Neither account is weak, and this article's verdict does not require them to be.

Sarkar's account supplies the adjudication's hardest evidence and must be faced directly. Modern India 1885-1947 (1983) is unsparing about the movement's limits: enrolment that ebbed between campaigns, a membership whose social depth in the countryside was shallower than the registers implied, and a leadership that repeatedly chose negotiated pauses over the mass energies it had summoned. A defender of the leadership reading might argue that these limits expose the architecture as a façade behind which personal authority did the real work. The evidence cuts the other way on inspection. The ebbing enrolment was measured, lamented and corrected through the committees' own returns, which is to say the organisation audited its distance from its architect's ambitions and published the audit internally. The negotiated pauses were executed through the constitution's chain, at Bardoli in 1922 and through the Gandhi-Irwin settlement of March 1931, without the party fragmenting on either occasion, although each pause disappointed a constituency large enough to have broken a merely charismatic following. Sarkar's Congress is an organisation whose failures are institutional in form, shortfalls against its own registers, targets and procedures, and an institution is precisely what his evidence keeps describing, even where his sympathies lie with the crowds the institution sometimes restrained.

The verdict turns on a test the leadership reading fails and the architectural reading passes: survival of the leader's absence and of the strategy's reversal. Gandhi was in prison from March 1922 to February 1924, withdrew from active Congress leadership after 1924, resigned his own membership of the party in 1934, and was murdered in January 1948; the strategy of non-cooperation was suspended in 1922, reversed by the Swarajists in 1923, replaced by civil disobedience in 1930, by office acceptance in 1937 and by open rebellion in 1942. Through every absence and every reversal, the linguistic provinces, the district committees, the primary registers and the standing executive persisted, amended at Belgaum by their own procedures and otherwise unreplaced, until the party they constituted took power in 1947. Institutions are what remain when strategies change and leaders leave; by that test the decisive Gandhi-era construction was the Nagpur architecture. The comparator across the same city makes the point from the opposite camp: [the RSS cadre architecture as 1925 contemporaneous comparator](/2019/09/13/kb-hedgewar-founding-rss-1925/), founded by K. B. Hedgewar at Nagpur in September 1925 around the daily shakha, likewise treated disciplined organisation as the durable political instrument, and the two Nagpur architectures, the committee and the shakha, competed for the century on the shared premise that structure outlasts slogan.

The leadership reading's subsidiary claim, that the Nagpur changes were the routine modernisation any mass movement of the era required, also fails on comparison. The era offered several templates for political organisation, and the Congress's contemporaries chose differently. The Muslim League retained into the 1930s a structure of annual sessions, a council and a small enrolled elite, and its mass phase, when it came after 1937, had to be improvised on a frame never designed for it. The Communist Party of India, organised in the 1920s, adopted the opposite template, the small disciplined cell of selected cadres, formidable in a union or a kisan sabha and constitutionally incapable of enrolling a crore of fee-paying members. The RSS after 1925 built the shakha, a cadre instrument of daily drill and ideological training that deliberately refused mass enrolment as a dilution of discipline. Against these alternatives, the Nagpur template, open enrolment at a labourer's fee, territorial committees in the vernacular, elected ladders and a standing executive, was a distinctive and contestable choice, not an inevitability. It privileged breadth over intensity and counting over selection, accepting the bogus-register scandals and the ebbing enrolments as the price of a party that could plausibly claim to contain the nation. That the choice was made, against the instincts of most of the men in the Nagpur hall and against the practice of every rival organisation of the decade, returns the adjudication to its author: distinctive choices have designers, and the design's fingerprints are the apprenticeship's fingerprints.

One refinement of the leadership evidence strengthens the verdict rather than weakening it. Brown's reconstruction shows Gandhi, between 1915 and 1922, deliberately constructing his political base through organisations, the Gujarat Sabha, the Home Rule League he captured and renamed, the Khilafat alliance's committees, before he ever held the Congress's machinery. A strategist whose method was the patient construction of committees, registers and funds in every arena he entered did not cease to be an organisational thinker on reaching Nagpur; the constitution is the same method written at national scale. The leadership reading, pressed to its own evidence, keeps producing the architect. What it cannot produce is an alternative author for the design's distinctive choices, and in adjudication the absence of an alternative author matters: neither the session's president, a constitutionalist of the old school, nor the Bengal and Bombay leaderships, absorbed in the strategy quarrel, had any record of advocating linguistic party provinces, a four-anna franchise or a standing executive of fifteen before Gandhi's draft embodied all three in a single text.

The counterfactual seals the adjudication. Imagine the non-cooperation programme of 1921 entrusted to the constitution of 1919: provincial committees on the Raj's map, no district rung, no enrolled membership, no standing executive, the party's sovereignty parked in a December session. The boycott of the councils could have been declared, as earlier boycotts had been declared since 1905, and would then have depended on the voluntary exertions of provincial notables without registers to staff pickets, quotas to finance schools or a committee empowered to suspend the campaign when it turned violent in February 1922. The probable outcomes are written in the pre-1920 record: a brilliant season of agitation, a slow dispersal, a reunion of leaders at the next session to draft the next resolution. That the actual sequence instead produced a party able to fight the elections of 1923, 1926, 1934, 1937 and 1946 on one constitution, and to govern provinces and then a dominion on the cadre that constitution trained, is the fact the leadership reading leaves unexplained and the architectural reading exists to explain.

Within the long account of the party's life, the Nagpur settlement marks the consummation of Phase Two in [the four-phase organisational framework](/2019/10/27/indian-national-congress-long-arc-1885-2020/), the phase in which the Congress became a mass organisation with a governing executive, distinct in kind from the deliberative Phase One body of 1885 to 1919 and the template for the governing Phase Three that followed independence. Its consequences run forward into [the founding-period long-arc context](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/), because the men who staffed the Constituent Assembly's party, and the provincial machines that delivered the Republic's first elections, were products of the committee ladder Nagpur built. The leadership framing tells us why the crowds came in 1921. The architectural reading tells us why, having come, they could be counted, financed, disciplined, halted, redirected into councils and ministries, and still be standing in 1947. Of the two questions, the second is the one the next twenty-seven years kept asking, and Nagpur is its answer.

## The Nagpur Form in the Party's Later Constitutions

The final evidence for the architectural reading is the behaviour of the party's constitution-makers after 1947, who were free to redesign their inheritance and chose instead to keep it. The Indian National Congress framed a new constitution for the independence era, adopted in 1948 as the party separated itself from the state it had won, and the revision revised the creed, the flag and the membership terms while preserving the Nagpur skeleton entire: provincial committees still organised on the linguistic geography of 1920, district committees beneath them, primary and block level units at the base, an All India Congress Committee above the provinces and a Working Committee of the same order of size at the top. The names themselves were carried over unchanged, and they remain the party's constitutional vocabulary in the twenty-first century: a Karnataka Pradesh Congress Committee, a Tamil Nadu Congress Committee and a District Congress Committee in any Indian district are offices in direct descent from the Nagpur text, the linguistic map of 1920 fossilised into the permanent geography of the party. Successor organisations learned the form as well. The parties that displaced the Congress in the states after 1967, and the national rivals that rose after 1989, built themselves as central committees, state units and district units with enrolled membership and receipted subscriptions, because the Nagpur template had become, through twenty-seven years of demonstration, the default answer to the question of how an Indian political party is shaped. Gandhi's December 1920 week at Nagpur therefore closes the argument this article has defended. The strategy of that week, non-cooperation, was suspended within fourteen months and never resumed in its original form. The architecture of that week was amended, inherited, imitated and inhabited for a century. Even the exceptions to its map proved its force. Bombay remained a bilingual state until 1960 and Punjab a bilingual province until 1966, against the linguistic grain, and in both cases the Congress's own provincial organisation had long been divided on Nagpur's lines, a Maharashtra committee and a Gujarat committee inside one Bombay state, so that the party map of 1920 stood as the standing reproach and eventual template for the state map that followed. The linguistic PCCs of 1920 had taught the country to imagine its politics in language provinces a full generation before the Republic's cartographers drew them, and the imagining was done not in editorials but in the monthly routine of committees that met, enrolled and reported in the mother tongue of their districts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the 1920 Nagpur Congress?

The 1920 Nagpur Congress was the thirty-fifth annual session of the Indian National Congress, held at Nagpur from 26 to 31 December 1920 under the presidency of C. Vijayaraghavachariar, with 14,583 delegates recorded in the Congress's own synopsis. The session ratified the non-cooperation programme first adopted at the Calcutta special session of September 1920, with C. R. Das moving the resolution he had opposed three months earlier. Its enduring act was constitutional: the session adopted the Nagpur Constitution, which reorganised the Congress on linguistic provincial lines, created district and primary committees, fixed membership at four annas a year and installed a fifteen-member Working Committee as a standing executive.

### Q: How did Gandhi reorganise the Congress?

Gandhi reorganised the Congress at Nagpur in December 1920 by replacing its sessions-based structure with a mass-membership architecture. Provincial committees were redrawn on linguistic lines, twenty-one in place of the Raj's administrative provinces. District Congress Committees became the standard operational unit, with town, taluka and ward committees beneath them. Primary committees at village and locality level enrolled any adult who paid four annas a year. A Working Committee of fifteen and an All India Congress Committee raised to 350 members gave the party a standing executive and legislature between sessions. Gandhi's drafting translated his South African, Champaran, Kheda and Khilafat organisational experience into the party's constitution, while colleagues including C. Rajagopalachari and Jawaharlal Nehru administered its installation across the provinces.

### Q: What is the Nagpur Constitution of the Congress?

The Nagpur Constitution is the constitution of the Indian National Congress adopted at the Nagpur session in December 1920, printed in the Congress's records and reproduced in Gandhi's Collected Works, volumes 19 and 20. It rewrote the party's creed as the attainment of swaraj by peaceful and legitimate means, replacing the older formula of self-government by constitutional means. It organised the party into linguistic provincial committees, district committees and primary committees, fixed the membership subscription at four annas a year in its Article VII, enlarged the All India Congress Committee to 350 members and created the fifteen-member Working Committee. Amended at Belgaum in 1924, it framed the party's working until independence in 1947.

### Q: What are Provincial Congress Committees?

Provincial Congress Committees, or PCCs, are the provincial-level organs of the Indian National Congress. Under the Nagpur Constitution of December 1920 they were reorganised on linguistic lines, twenty-one committees for the major language regions, so that Andhra, Karnataka, Kerala country as Malabar, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Sindh, Bihar and Utkal received committees distinct from the multilingual presidencies and provinces of British India. Each PCC supervised the district committees in its territory, conducted provincial conferences, managed provincial funds and elections to the All India Congress Committee, and executed the campaigns the Working Committee announced. The PCC map of 1920 anticipated the linguistic reorganisation of Indian states carried out in 1956.

### Q: What was the four-anna membership fee?

The four-anna membership fee was the annual subscription fixed by the Nagpur Constitution of 1920 for membership of the Indian National Congress. Article VII provided that every person not disqualified under Article IV, paying a subscription of four annas per year, was entitled to become a member of any organisation controlled by the Provincial Congress Committees. Four annas equalled a quarter of a rupee, a sum calibrated to the earnings of labourers and peasants rather than to professional incomes, and the fee bought a receipt and a register entry through a primary committee. At Belgaum in December 1924 the clause was supplemented by the spinning franchise of 20,000 yards of yarn a year.

### Q: How did Gandhi build the mass Congress?

Gandhi built the mass Congress through the architecture adopted at Nagpur in December 1920 and the enrolment machinery it created. The four-anna fee and the primary committees opened a door at village and ward level through which adherents could be enrolled, receipted and counted. District committees staffed the door, provincial committees on linguistic lines supervised the districts, and the Working Committee set national targets, most famously the Bezwada programme of April 1921, which aimed at one crore of members and one crore of rupees for the Tilak Swaraj Fund. Gandhi's method, formed in South Africa and in the Champaran, Kheda and Ahmedabad campaigns, treated membership as an auditable organisational stock rather than as crowd sympathy, and the campaigns of 1921 were administered through the registers the constitution created.

### Q: When did linguistic-state organisation begin in Indian politics?

Linguistic organisation in Indian politics began as a party structure at the Nagpur session of December 1920, when the Indian National Congress redrew its provincial committees on language lines, creating twenty-one committees for the major linguistic regions in place of the British administrative provinces. The demand had a prehistory in the Andhra movement, and a separate Andhra circle had been conceded before 1920, but Nagpur generalised the principle across India at a single stroke. Congress politicians thereafter built careers inside linguistic units for thirty-five years before the States Reorganisation Act of 1956 redrew the Indian Union's own map on the same principle, following the States Reorganisation Commission appointed in December 1953 under Fazl Ali.

### Q: Who was C. Vijayaraghavachariar?

C. Vijayaraghavachariar was a senior lawyer of Salem in the Madras Presidency and a veteran of the Congress's older generation, who presided over the thirty-fifth session of the Indian National Congress at Nagpur in December 1920. His presidency embodied continuity: a leader formed in the petitioning era held the chair while the session ratified non-cooperation, changed the party's creed to the attainment of swaraj by peaceful and legitimate means and adopted the Nagpur Constitution. The reception committee at Nagpur was chaired by Seth Jamnalal Bajaj, and the session's general secretaries were Motilal Nehru, C. Rajagopalachari and M. A. Ansari, the lieutenants who administered the new order Vijayaraghavachariar's session had enacted.

### Q: What happened at the Calcutta Special Session of September 1920?

The Calcutta Special Session of the Indian National Congress met from 4 to 9 September 1920 under the presidency of Lala Lajpat Rai, weeks after the death of Bal Gangadhar Tilak on 1 August 1920. Gandhi moved the non-cooperation programme, proposing the surrender of titles, the boycott of law courts, government schools and the reformed councils, and the boycott of foreign goods, in response to the Punjab wrongs and the Khilafat question. The house was divided, with C. R. Das and others resisting the council boycott, and the programme carried by 1,886 votes to 884. The Nagpur session of December 1920 ratified the Calcutta decision, with Das himself moving the resolution.

### Q: What was the Tilak Swaraj Fund?

The Tilak Swaraj Fund was the Indian National Congress's central campaign fund, launched in memory of Bal Gangadhar Tilak, who died on 1 August 1920, and announced in connection with the Nagpur session of December 1920 to finance the non-cooperation movement. The Working Committee, meeting at Vijayawada in April 1921, set the fund a target of one crore of rupees to be collected by 30 June 1921, alongside targets of one crore of enrolled members and twenty lakh charkhas. Collections ran through the new provincial and district committees against assigned quotas, making the fund the first exercise of the Nagpur architecture's fiscal plumbing. Bombay's contribution of some 37.5 lakh rupees led the provincial returns.

### Q: What did the Working Committee do between Congress sessions?

The Working Committee created at Nagpur in December 1920 acted as the standing executive of the Indian National Congress between annual sessions. Its fifteen members interpreted the session's programme, issued circulars to the provincial committees, fixed campaign targets such as the Bezwada enrolment and fund goals of April 1921, arbitrated disputes inside provincial and district organisations, managed the party's central funds and answered emergencies. Its authority was most visible in retreat: at Bardoli on 11 and 12 February 1922 the committee suspended mass civil disobedience for the whole country after Chauri Chaura, and the order held in every province. The committee met as events required, giving the party continuous government for the first time.

### Q: How did the Nagpur Constitution change the Congress creed?

The Nagpur Constitution changed the Congress creed by replacing the party's stated method of advance. The older creed committed the Congress to the attainment of self-government within the British Empire by constitutional means, a formula that confined legitimate action to petitions, resolutions and council proceedings. The text adopted in December 1920 set the goal as the attainment of swaraj by peaceful and legitimate means. The substitution of one word, peaceful for constitutional, carried the constitutional sanction for extra-parliamentary mass struggle: boycotts, picketing and civil disobedience could be read as peaceful and legitimate although they stood outside the official constitution. Mohammad Ali Jinnah, committed to the older formula, opposed the change at Nagpur and left the Congress.

### Q: What were ward committees in the Congress structure of 1920?

Ward committees were the urban base units of the Congress structure created by the Nagpur Constitution of December 1920, the city counterpart of the village primary committees. The Congress's synopsis of the Nagpur changes lists ward committees among the session's organisational decisions. A ward committee enrolled members in a defined city ward at the four-anna subscription, maintained the local register, organised picketing of foreign cloth shops and liquor shops during the boycott campaigns, and fed delegates and funds upward to the town and district committees. Through ward committees the party acquired a permanent presence in the mohallas and bazaars of Indian cities, where the pre-1920 Congress had appeared only at election time or during a visiting leader's tour.

### Q: How did district committees run the boycott campaigns of 1921?

District Congress Committees ran the boycott campaigns of 1921 as the operational level of the non-cooperation movement. Each district committee translated the Working Committee's programme into local assignments: pickets at foreign cloth merchants' premises, promotion of khadi and the charkha, collection of the Tilak Swaraj Fund against the district's quota, enrolment of four-anna members through primary committees, and the organisation of national schools outside the official system. The committees reported membership and fund returns upward to the provincial committees, whose consolidated figures reached the All India Congress Committee. The Working Committee minutes of 1920 to 1922 show the centre demanding district-wise returns and arbitrating district disputes, evidence that the district rung carried the campaign's administrative weight.

### Q: Why did Mohammad Ali Jinnah leave the Congress at Nagpur?

Mohammad Ali Jinnah left the Indian National Congress at the Nagpur session of December 1920 because the session committed the party to methods he rejected. Jinnah held to constitutional and legal struggle and opposed both the non-cooperation programme and the Nagpur Constitution's new creed, which replaced self-government by constitutional means with the attainment of swaraj by peaceful and legitimate means, a formula that sanctioned mass action outside the legislatures. He also resisted the party's Khilafat entanglement and the religious idiom entering its campaigns. Having failed to carry the session against Gandhi's programme, Jinnah withdrew from the Congress, retaining his independent position in the Central Legislative Assembly and in Muslim politics.

### Q: What was the Bezwada programme of April 1921?

The Bezwada programme was the set of organisational targets adopted by the Congress Working Committee at Vijayawada, also rendered Bezwada, in April 1921, four months after the Nagpur reorganisation. The committee fixed three goals for the non-cooperation campaign: the collection of one crore of rupees for the Tilak Swaraj Fund by 30 June 1921, the enrolment of one crore of members on the Congress registers, and the introduction of twenty lakh charkhas to spread hand-spinning and khadi. The targets were assigned through the provincial committees to the districts. Returns fell short of the crore lines, as Sumit Sarkar records in Modern India 1885-1947 (1983), but the programme shows the new architecture thinking in enrolled millions for the first time.

### Q: How did the Swaraj Party dispute test the Nagpur Constitution?

The Swaraj Party dispute tested the Nagpur Constitution by placing the party's sharpest strategic quarrel since 1907 inside the new machinery. At Gaya in December 1922 the council-entry proposal of C. R. Das and Motilal Nehru was defeated; the two leaders founded the Swaraj Party on 1 January 1923 and won forty-two seats in the Central Legislative Assembly that November. The No-Changers defended boycott and the constructive programme. Unlike Surat in 1907, the quarrel produced no lasting split, because the Working Committee could arbitrate, both camps held office within the provincial committees, and the membership register belonged to the party. The settlement was ratified at Belgaum in December 1924, folding the Swarajists into the Congress order.

### Q: What did the provincial organisational reports of 1921 to 1925 record?

The provincial organisational reports of 1921 to 1925, preserved in part at the All India Congress Committee's archives and in regional Congress collections, recorded the installation of the Nagpur Constitution province by province. They registered committees formed, lapsed and re-formed at district and primary level, membership enrolments against the four-anna subscription, fund collections against quota, and the appointment of organisers. Their picture is uneven: Andhra and Gujarat built dense district networks early, Bengal's organisation bargained with a centre it partly resisted, and enrolment everywhere surged with campaigns and ebbed between them. For historians the reports are the least-used primary record of how the 1920 architecture actually worked on the ground.

### Q: How long did the Nagpur Constitution remain in force?

The Nagpur Constitution remained the working constitution of the Indian National Congress from December 1920 until independence in 1947, a span of twenty-seven years. It was amended within its own procedures, most notably at Belgaum in December 1924, when the spinning franchise supplemented the four-anna subscription and the Swarajist settlement was ratified, and its creed was sharpened when the Lahore session of December 1929 declared Purna Swaraj, complete independence, as the party's goal. The linguistic provinces, district committees, primary registers and the fifteen-member Working Committee persisted through non-cooperation, civil disobedience, the provincial ministries of 1937 to 1939 and the Quit India movement, outlasting every strategy adopted under them.

### Q: What is the difference between the All India Congress Committee and the Working Committee?

The All India Congress Committee and the Working Committee are the two central organs created in their Nagpur form in December 1920, and they differ as a legislature differs from an executive. The All India Congress Committee, raised to 350 members drawn from the provincial committees, met between annual sessions to decide major questions of programme and to elect the party's leadership. The Working Committee of fifteen, drawn from the larger body, governed continuously: it issued circulars, fixed campaign targets, supervised the provincial and district committees, managed central funds and acted for the party in emergencies such as the Bardoli suspension of February 1922. The annual session stood above both as the party's sovereign gathering.
