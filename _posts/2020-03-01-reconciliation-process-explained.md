Most legislation in the Senate can be talked to death. A measure that cannot attract sixty votes to close debate cannot reach a final vote at all, and the arithmetic of extended debate has ended more major bills than any vote on passage. Budget reconciliation is the standing exception to that arithmetic. It is an optional procedure written into section 310 of the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974, and it carries a measure from an instruction inside a budget resolution to the President's desk under a statutory ceiling on debate, so that a simple majority of senators, voting after a fixed allowance of hours, decides the outcome. First used in 1980, the procedure has since carried deficit reduction acts, tax reductions and tax increases, a restructuring of federal cash welfare, a health coverage expansion and the largest tax revision of its decade, and the statutes that owe their existence to it rank among the most consequential fiscal laws of the modern Congress.

The exception is narrow by design, and its narrowness is the subject of much of what follows. Reconciliation opens only when an adopted budget resolution instructs committees to use it. It carries only changes to spending, revenues and the debt limit. A separate Senate rule strips out matter judged extraneous to those budgetary purposes, one entire program, Social Security, sits beyond its reach altogether, and the President's veto waits at the end of the road exactly as it does for any other bill. Within those boundaries, though, the procedure has become the principal instrument by which narrow Senate majorities enact their central fiscal measures, which is why a rule drafted to discipline deficits ended up shaping the substantive output of the modern Senate. The sections that follow walk the pipeline stage by stage, then test the design against four decades of use.

## Where Budget Reconciliation Comes From

Section 310 of the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974, Public Law 93-344, codified at 2 U.S.C. 641, created the procedure as an adjunct to the annual budget resolution process described by the Congressional Research Service. A budget resolution sets aggregate levels for spending and revenues and assigns amounts to committees, and reconciliation supplies a route for changing the laws that produce those levels. Without that route, a resolution could declare a fiscal target while the statutes governing taxes, entitlement programs, and the debt limit continued on a different course. With that route, Congress could direct committees to recommend amendments to existing law, assemble the recommendations in a single bill, and consider that bill under expedited rules, above all in the Senate, where ordinary debate has no fixed endpoint. The design joined discipline over totals, expressed in a resolution adopted by both chambers, to legislation, expressed in a bill presented to the President and capable, after signature or a veto override, of altering legal rights and obligations.

Before the Act of 1974, Congress made fiscal decisions through a structure that divided responsibility among many committees and offered no regular mechanism for adding the pieces together. Authorizing committees wrote the laws that created programs and, for a large share of federal spending, set the terms on which money flowed without annual action. The tax committees controlled the revenue laws. The Appropriations Committees produced annual bills for discretionary programs. No committee owned the aggregate. No regular vote forced the House and the Senate to state, in one document, total revenue, total spending, and the resulting deficit or surplus. The absence mattered more as permanent spending programs grew, because a rising portion of the budget rested on laws already enacted rather than on annual appropriations choices. A Congress that wished to reduce outlays under such a program, or to raise revenue under the tax code, had to amend substantive law committee by committee, bill by bill, under procedures that allowed delay.

The conflict over impoundment under President Richard Nixon gave institutional reform a sharper edge and a timetable. Nixon refused to spend funds that Congress had appropriated for programs that the administration opposed, and members of both parties treated that refusal as a challenge to the constitutional power of the purse. The response in [the Congressional Budget Act of 1974](/2020/01/01/congressional-budget-act-1974-guide/) addressed the executive branch and the legislative branch together. Title X imposed controls on impoundment, and the earlier titles built a process with House and Senate Budget Committees and a Congressional Budget Office able to supply estimates independent of the Office of Management and Budget. The new process required Congress to adopt a concurrent resolution on the budget, stating totals and functional priorities for the fiscal year that began on October 1 and for succeeding years. A framework alone, however, could not rewrite a benefit formula, close a tax provision, or raise the statutory debt limit. Section 310 addressed that gap inside the same statute that created the framework.

### Where does the reconciliation procedure come from?

Reconciliation comes from section 310 of the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974. Congress created the procedure to give budget resolutions practical effect by directing committees to propose changes in existing law and by protecting the resulting bill from a Senate filibuster.

A concurrent resolution expresses agreement between the House and the Senate, and adoption completes congressional action on the measure. Because a concurrent resolution on the budget was designed as an internal congressional instrument, a budget resolution never went to the President for signature and never became law. The resolution could establish fiscal policy for Congress and bind committees through points of order inside each chamber, but the resolution could not amend the Internal Revenue Code, alter eligibility for a federal benefit, change a payment rate, or revise the debt limit. Every such change required separate legislation that passed both chambers in identical form and survived presentation to the President. The resolution decided, and reconciliation executed a defined portion of the decision through statute.

Section 310 sat among the provisions that turned the budget resolution from a statement into an enforceable plan. Neighboring sections governed the contents of the resolution, the timetable for action, allocations to committees, and points of order against legislation that breached the adopted levels. Section 310 performed a different function from a point of order. A point of order could block a bill that violated the resolution, and enforcement of that kind worked in a negative direction, preventing movement away from the plan. Reconciliation worked in a positive direction. Instructions in a resolution could order committees to produce the changes in law that the plan required, and the resulting bill carried procedural protection during consideration. To reconcile, in the usage of the Act, meant to bring existing law into conformity with a budget resolution after Congress had compared the two and found a difference. The object was a body of statutes, measured against totals that Congress had adopted for spending, revenues, and debt.

The original conception placed reconciliation at the end of the annual process rather than at the beginning. The enacted text of section 310 bore a heading that joined the second required concurrent resolution and the reconciliation process, and that pairing reflected the sequence that the authors expected. A first budget resolution, adopted in the spring, would set targets for the coming fiscal year. Committees would then act on authorization, appropriations, revenue, and debt legislation during the intervening months, under allocations derived from the targets. A second budget resolution, adopted in September, would reaffirm or revise the first resolution after Congress had a record of action to examine. Where action during the interval departed from the final totals, or where laws in force produced inconsistent levels, instructions in the second resolution would direct committees to recommend the changes needed to close the gap. The Budget Committees would assemble the recommendations, and Congress would complete action on a reconciliation bill before the fiscal year began on October 1. In that conception, reconciliation served as a final alignment device.

Alignment of that kind carried a strong association with deficit reduction, and the association shaped the early understanding of the tool. A second resolution that found spending above the adopted level or revenues below that level called for legislation that reduced outlays, raised revenues, or combined the two to narrow the deficit. Instructions specified amounts of change rather than detailed policy, and a committee retained discretion over the provisions used to reach an assigned amount, subject to committee jurisdiction and to later votes in each chamber. The aggregate instruction, however, pointed toward conformity with a total, and conformity after a year of separate decisions usually meant restraint. Reconciliation began as a deficit tool, built to enforce a budget that Congress had already adopted against statutes that Congress had enacted earlier. The later transformation into a majority tool, prized because a governing majority could pass major fiscal legislation in the Senate by a simple majority, grew out of procedural features present from the start rather than out of a later amendment that rewrote section 310.

The late placement in the original timetable also exposed a practical weakness that experience soon made plain. By September, appropriations decisions for a fiscal year beginning on October 1 had largely been made, and committees had limited appetite for reopening settlements reached weeks earlier. The interval between adoption of a second resolution and the start of the fiscal year left little room for committees to draft recommendations, for Budget Committees to assemble an omnibus bill, for each chamber to pass a version, and for a conference to resolve differences. A procedure designed to correct the record at the end of a session therefore risked arriving after correction had become hardest to achieve. The weakness invited Congress to move instructions earlier, attach instructions to the first budget resolution, and use reconciliation to make fiscal policy during the session rather than to tidy the record after other decisions had closed. The first use in 1980 followed the earlier placement and demonstrated the consequences for the scale and the politics of the procedure.

A clear pass through the mechanism begins with the instruction. Reconciliation was optional under the Act, and a budget resolution without reconciliation instructions generated no reconciliation bill. When a resolution contained instructions, each instruction named a committee, specified the amount of change to achieve in spending, revenues, or the debt limit, and set a date for submission of recommendations. Instructions could reach one committee or many committees, and a single budget resolution could generate at most one reconciliation bill for each of three subjects, namely spending, revenues, and the debt limit. That ceiling, recognized in Congressional Research Service descriptions, prevented a majority from multiplying reconciliation bills under one resolution without limit, while the breadth of the three subjects allowed a single bill to carry extensive changes across jurisdictions. An instruction assigned a budgetary result and left the choice among provisions to the instructed committee, whose members held jurisdiction over the programs or revenue sources in question.

Submission formed the second stage, and packaging formed the hinge between committee action and floor action. An instructed committee considered changes within the jurisdiction assigned by the rules of the relevant chamber, voted on legislative language, and transmitted recommendations to the Budget Committee of that chamber by the date in the instruction. Compliance with an instruction was measured on the net budgetary effect of a committee submission, a standard that the Congressional Research Service emphasized in explanations of the process. A committee could therefore combine provisions that raised spending in one place with larger reductions elsewhere, provided that the net result satisfied the instruction, and a revenue committee could combine increases and reductions in a comparable fashion. Where several committees received instructions in one chamber, section 310(b)(2) required the Budget Committee to report an omnibus reconciliation bill without any substantive revision of the submissions received. The Budget Committee acted as an assembler rather than as an author, protecting the judgments made in the instructed committees. Where a single committee received an instruction, the Act allowed that committee to report reconciliation legislation directly to the chamber, bypassing assembly by the Budget Committee. In either route, the product that reached the floor was a bill to amend existing law, ready for passage and presentation after expedited consideration.

Privilege formed the third stage and supplied the feature that later gave reconciliation an importance beyond budget enforcement. In the House, a reconciliation bill moved under a special rule, typically from the Rules Committee, which set debate time and the amendments allowed. In the Senate, section 310 supplied protection directly. Section 310(e)(2), applying the procedures of section 305, limited total debate on a reconciliation bill to 20 hours, and the limit covered the bill and all amendments, motions, and appeals, with time divided equally between the majority and the minority. A conference report received 10 hours, a first degree amendment two hours, and a second degree amendment, motion, or appeal one hour, while time spent voting did not count against the 20 hours and a motion to proceed was not debatable. Amendments offered in the Senate had to be germane and had to conform to sections 310(d) and 313 of the Budget Act, and passage required a simple majority. The practical effect of a fixed debate limit was decisive. A filibuster depends on the absence of a fixed endpoint for debate, and a statute that fixed the endpoint at 20 hours removed the need for three fifths of the Senate for cloture before a final vote. Content limits accompanied the time limit, including the Byrd rule in section 313, codified at 2 U.S.C. 644, under which extraneous provisions faced a point of order that took three fifths of the Senate to waive, and section 310(g), which barred a reconciliation bill from containing recommendations on the Social Security old age, survivors, and disability insurance program. Privilege was a bounded fast track, built for budgetary legislation that met conditions stated in the Act.

Congress left the procedure unused during the first several budget cycles under the Act and then turned to the procedure in 1980, under conditions close to the original deficit purpose. The budget resolution for fiscal year 1981 contained reconciliation instructions, and committees in both chambers produced recommendations for savings in programs within the relevant jurisdictions and for increases in revenues. The resulting measure, the Omnibus Reconciliation Act of 1980, Public Law 96-499, was signed by President Jimmy Carter on December 5, 1980. The Congressional Research Service catalog of reconciliation measures enacted into law between 1980 and 2017 estimated a fiscal year 1981 deficit reduction of 8.276 billion dollars, including 4.631 billion dollars in outlay reductions and 3.645 billion dollars in revenue increases. Spending changes reached areas that included child nutrition subsidies, student loan interest terms, federal employee retirement cost of living provisions, highway obligation authority, veterans burial allowances, disaster loans, Medicare, Medicaid, and unemployment compensation. Revenue changes reached areas that included mortgage subsidy bonds, estimated corporate tax payments, the telephone excise tax, and the alcohol import duty. The opening title of the act stated a purpose tied to implementation of reconciliation requirements in the first concurrent resolution on the budget for fiscal year 1981, a detail that marked the departure from the second resolution sequence in the original design. The bill was assembled late in the session across a wide field of jurisdictions, and enactment came after the fiscal year had begun.

The experience of 1980 proved several propositions that later Congresses relied on between 1981 and 2019. Committees with separate jurisdictions could meet assigned savings targets on a common schedule and surrender the resulting language to a Budget Committee that assembled the pieces without rewriting the substance. A measure assembled in that fashion could hold together through debate in both chambers, through resolution of differences, and through presentation to a President whose signature was required for the changes to become law. Expedited consideration in the Senate could bring a large and contested fiscal bill to a final vote, and the germaneness and content conditions in the Act could operate alongside the debate limit without preventing passage. Reconciliation could gather many modest changes into a single enactment with a measurable effect on the deficit, and the gathering itself created a legislative vehicle of consequence. Once an omnibus reconciliation bill had moved from instruction to enactment in 1980, the procedure had a demonstrated capacity that statutory text alone could not convey.

A procedure built for alignment at the end of a budget process, and first used for deficit reduction in the Omnibus Reconciliation Act of 1980, became over succeeding decades a principal instrument through which a Senate majority pursued major fiscal priorities under protection from extended debate. Congressional Research Service summaries recorded the broadening in restrained language, observing that reconciliation served in most instances to reduce deficits through spending reductions or revenue increases and that in some years the process also encompassed revenue reduction and spending increases in selected areas. The broadening required no new statute, because the features that made reconciliation useful for deficit reduction also made reconciliation useful for other fiscal programs that a majority could express as instructions. An instruction could direct a revenue reduction as readily as a revenue increase, provided that the resolution adopted the corresponding totals, and the 20 hour limit protected the resulting bill regardless of the deficit effect. Between 1980 and the end of 2019, that symmetry turned a device for enforcing a previously adopted budget into a device for choosing and enacting a governing fiscal program. A deficit tool became a majority tool, and the transformation began with design choices that section 310 made at the creation of the modern congressional budget process.

## The Instruction and the Committee Submissions

Reconciliation began, in every instance between 1980 and 2019, before any bill existed. A majority in each chamber first agreed on budget totals and then, in a separate and optional step, directed named committees to rewrite the laws within their jurisdiction so that those totals could be met. The instruction fixed who had to act, how much change had to be produced, and by what date. The committee submissions that answered the instruction translated a numerical command into statutory language. Floor debate operated on material those two stages had already shaped.

The authority came from section 310 of the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974, Public Law 93-344, codified at 2 U.S.C. 641. That section permitted a budget resolution to direct committees to report changes in existing law relating to spending, revenues, or the statutory limit on the public debt. Without instructions, a budget resolution remained a statement of intent that committees could honor or disregard during ordinary legislation. With instructions, the resolution acquired a mechanism for compelling the committees with jurisdiction over direct spending and revenue law to produce conforming legislation on a timetable the resolution itself supplied.

### What is a reconciliation instruction?

A reconciliation instruction is a directive in a concurrent budget resolution that names a committee, specifies the amount by which that committee must change spending, revenues, or the debt limit over a stated period, and sets the date by which the committee must submit its legislative recommendations to the Budget Committee.

An instruction had three essential components, described in consistent terms by the Congressional Research Service. The first was the committee itself, identified by name. Only a named committee received an instruction, and only matter within the jurisdiction of a named committee could properly appear in the resulting bill. A committee that received no instruction had no obligation to report, and its programs stood outside the measure. The second component was the fiscal target, a specified amount of change over a stated period. The Budget Act permitted three types of target. A committee could be directed to change laws relating to spending, to change laws relating to revenues, or to change the amount of the statutory debt limit. An instruction framed in terms of the deficit was understood as a combination of a spending instruction and a revenue instruction, since the deficit changed only when one of those two quantities changed. The third component was time, expressed as the period over which the target was measured and as a reporting deadline.

The distribution of targets followed jurisdictional logic. Any committee with jurisdiction over a program of direct spending could receive a spending instruction, a category that reached committees overseeing agriculture, veterans programs, federal retirement, and health programs financed outside the annual appropriations process. Revenue and debt limit instructions, by contrast, ran to one committee in each chamber, the Committee on Ways and Means in the House of Representatives and the Committee on Finance in the Senate, because the revenue laws and the debt limit lay within the jurisdiction of those two committees alone. That concentration placed the tax writing committees at the center of nearly every exercise between 1980 and 2019 that touched revenues.

Fiscal targets were drafted with qualifiers that mattered as much as the numbers. Practice between 1980 and 2019 settled on formulations such as at least, at most, no more than, and no less than, which converted an exact figure into a floor or a ceiling. A committee instructed to reduce the deficit by at least a specified amount complied by producing that amount or more. A committee instructed to increase the deficit by no more than a specified amount complied by producing any figure beneath the ceiling, and gained room to trade components against one another during drafting. An exact amount left no margin against estimating error, while a floor or a ceiling absorbed revisions by the Congressional Budget Office and the Joint Committee on Taxation. The period of measurement carried the same weight, since an instruction measured over one fiscal year demanded a different order of change from an instruction measured over a five year or ten year span.

The instruction lived in a concurrent budget resolution, and that placement explained both the strength and the limits of the device. A concurrent resolution was the form Congress used for matters that required agreement of both chambers but operated only within Congress itself. Adoption required passage in identical form by the House of Representatives and by the Senate, ordinarily after a conference had reconciled differing versions, but a concurrent resolution was not presented to the President for signature or veto, and it was not a law. No president between 1980 and 2019 signed a budget resolution, and no budget resolution altered any entitlement, tax rate, or appropriation by its own force. The entire process therefore began with a document that had no legal force of its own. Its power came from the Budget Act and from the internal rules of each chamber, which enforced the resolution levels through points of order. Statutory change required a second, distinct act of legislation, presented to the President. Reconciliation existed to build that second act.

Because the resolution had no legal force, failure to agree had a direct consequence. In any year in which the two chambers failed to adopt the same budget resolution, no instruction took effect and reconciliation was not triggered. Measured against the Congressional Research Service's record, ten fiscal years, fiscal 1999, 2003, 2005, 2007, 2011, 2012, 2013, 2014, 2015 and 2019, closed without an adopted budget resolution at all, and in those years the expedited path stood unavailable. Adoption of a resolution without instructions, a common outcome, left reconciliation unused. Instructions were an optional element among the matters section 301 of the Budget Act permitted a resolution to contain, and majorities chose the option only when an objective required changes in direct spending or revenue law that ordinary procedure, with unlimited Senate debate and a sixty vote cloture threshold, placed at risk.

Instructions sat inside an allocation framework that gave the resolution internal discipline. Under section 301, the resolution stated aggregate levels for total budget authority, total outlays, total revenues, the surplus or deficit, and the public debt. Under section 302(a), those totals were translated, through a process often described as crosswalking, into an allocation of spending to each committee with jurisdiction over spending. The allocation fixed the amount a committee could report without breaching the resolution, and allocations were enforceable by points of order in both chambers. The instruction told a committee how much the laws in its jurisdiction had to change, while the allocation told the committee how much room the resolution allowed for spending under its control. A submission that met its instruction but collided with the aggregates or an allocation invited a point of order on the floor, so drafting proceeded with both sets of numbers in view.

The reports accompanying a budget resolution added guidance that bound no committee in law. Budget Committees explained assumed policy changes in report language, setting out the programs from which assumed savings were expected to come. The Congressional Research Service emphasized that instructed committees were not bound by those assumptions. A committee owed the resolution a number, not a particular policy. An agriculture committee instructed to produce a specified reduction could take the reduction from one program, spread the reduction across several programs, or pair reductions with increases in another area, provided the submission stayed within its jurisdiction and the arithmetic satisfied the instruction. The Budget Committees and floor majorities set the fiscal destination, while the committees of jurisdiction, which held the hearings and understood the statutory machinery of their programs, chose the route.

Work on a submission proceeded through ordinary committee process, under a discipline ordinary legislation lacked. An instructed committee held hearings at which department officials, analysts from the Congressional Budget Office, and representatives of affected interests described the programs under review. Staff, working with counsel and the Office of the Legislative Counsel, converted policy decisions into legislative text. The committee met in markup, voted on amendments, and ordered the result transmitted to the Budget Committee rather than reported to the full chamber. An ordinary committee worked against an open brief, free to define the scope of a bill and to report when deliberation concluded. An instructed committee worked against a numerical target fixed by vote of both chambers before drafting began, and against a reporting deadline stated in the resolution. Resolutions adopted between 1980 and 2019 set dates ranging from weeks after adoption to June 15, the date the Budget Act timetable contemplated for completing action on reconciliation legislation. A short deadline favored chairmen who had prepared legislation in anticipation of the instruction. A missed deadline weakened a committee in later negotiation, and compliance could then be achieved on the floor by amendment drafted without the committee of jurisdiction in the lead.

Compliance was measured on a net basis, and that rule shaped nearly every submission of consequence between 1980 and 2019. The Congressional Research Service stated the rule plainly. The budgetary effect of all of a committee submission was aggregated, and the committee complied if the net figure met the instruction, regardless of the direction of individual changes. A committee instructed to reduce spending could increase spending on one program alongside larger reductions on others, and the submission complied. A tax writing committee could pair rate reductions with provisions that raised revenue by narrowing a deduction, and compliance turned on the combined result. A deficit instruction was tested against the combined net effect on the deficit. Drafting under the net rule became an exercise in portfolio construction, with each provision scored, traded, resized, or dropped as the committee moved the aggregate toward the required figure.

Measurement belonged to the Budget Committees, acting on estimates from the two professional estimating offices Congress maintained. For spending provisions, the Congressional Budget Office prepared the cost estimates. For revenue provisions, the Joint Committee on Taxation prepared the revenue estimates. A submission containing both, as a Finance Committee submission routinely did, carried estimates from both offices that the Budget Committee combined for the compliance determination. The process by which those numbers were produced, from baseline construction through the treatment of behavioral response, is explained in [how the Congressional Budget Office scores legislation](/2020/05/15/how-cbo-scores-legislation/), and the authority of the figures rested on acceptance by the Budget Committee in each chamber, whose determination governed treatment of the submission on the floor. Prudent committees built margin into a submission, producing a net figure beyond a floor or beneath a ceiling, so that a late estimating revision would not convert compliance into noncompliance. Estimates were the submission, for compliance purposes, since a committee whose text met the target under one set of assumptions and missed the target under the official estimates had missed the instruction in the only sense the process recognized.

When a committee completed its work, the submission moved to packaging, and the statute confined the receiving committee within narrow limits. Section 310(b)(2) of the Budget Act directed the Budget Committee in each chamber to report an omnibus reconciliation bill containing the submissions, without any substantive revision. The Budget Committee could assemble, order, and number the material, secure cost estimates required to establish compliance, and report the assembled measure. The Budget Committee could not rewrite a program, strike a provision whose policy its members opposed, or substitute a different reduction for the reduction a committee of jurisdiction had chosen. Amendments were not in order during the Budget Committee meeting at which the bill was ordered reported, although members remained free to state support or objection for the record. The meeting functioned as an act of assembly rather than a markup, and the restriction protected the bargain embodied in each submission.

The bar on substantive revision applied even when a submission failed to comply. The Congressional Research Service recorded that Budget Committees incorporated recommendations into the omnibus bill even when the recommendations did not satisfy the directive, leaving noncompliance to be addressed during floor action, usually by an amendment offered to achieve compliance. The Budget Committee acting alone had no authority to manufacture compliance by editing another committee work. A defined exception relieved packaging of its formality in the simplest cases. When a resolution instructed only one committee in a chamber, section 310 permitted that committee to report its legislation directly to the full chamber, bypassing the Budget Committee entirely. With a single submission, no act of combination was required. Single committee instructions arose most naturally when a resolution pursued a revenue change alone, engaging Ways and Means or Finance, or when a debt limit change was carried by the tax writing committees without accompanying spending instructions.

The numerical limit on bills completed the architecture. Because the Budget Act recognized three subjects, a single budget resolution could generate at most one reconciliation bill for spending, at most one for revenues, and at most one for the debt limit, and therefore at most three bills in total. A majority could not return to the process within the same resolution after a bill had been enacted or vetoed, since a further bill required another budget resolution containing fresh instructions, as the Congressional Research Service explained. Practice between 1980 and 2019 used the capacity in two patterns. In the dominant pattern, submissions were combined into one omnibus bill, so each chamber voted once on a consolidated statement of majority budget policy. The Omnibus Reconciliation Act of 1980, Public Law 96-499, the first measure enacted through the process, followed that combined form. In the second pattern, subjects were split into two bills, a division chosen for coalition management, presidential strategy, or timing.

Instruction design was strategy performed before any committee met. One choice concerned scale, since an instruction demanding a large change committed the majority to programmatic decisions of corresponding size. A second choice concerned the window, since a target measured over ten years allowed changes to be assembled from provisions that phased in across the period, while a shorter span forced immediate effects. A third choice concerned the roster of committees, since naming many committees spread burden and political exposure, while naming few committees concentrated authority and left large areas of direct spending untouched. A fourth choice concerned the form of the target, exact, floor, or ceiling, which allocated estimating risk between the Budget Committee that wrote the instruction and the committees that answered the instruction. An instruction also determined what a bill could not contain, since matter outside the jurisdiction of every instructed committee had no place in the vehicle, and a provision with no budgetary effect faced exclusion under the Byrd rule, section 313 of the Budget Act, at the consideration stage.

The first use in 1980 illustrated the strategic possibilities. The concurrent resolution on the budget for fiscal year 1981, House Concurrent Resolution 448 in the Ninety Sixth Congress, gave authorizing committees instructions that amounted to approximately two thirds of the savings required under that resolution, a proportion recorded in findings the Senate adopted in later proceedings on the treatment of appropriations savings. Extension of instructions to authorizing committees had been contested ground, since changes in authorizing law for programs financed through later appropriations raised the question whether an instruction could produce genuine savings. The budget resolution for fiscal year 1982, adopted in 1981 under the Gramm Latta substitute supported by the administration of President Ronald Reagan, settled the contest in favor of breadth by covering authorizing legislation in the final instructions approved by both chambers. The resulting Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981, Public Law 97-35, enacted on August 13, 1981, demonstrated the reach of a broadly drawn roster in a single omnibus measure.

The instructions in the concurrent resolution on the budget for fiscal year 2018, House Concurrent Resolution 71 in the One Hundred Fifteenth Congress, illustrated the mature form. Title II directed the Senate Committee on Finance to report changes in laws within its jurisdiction that increased the deficit by no more than 1.5 trillion dollars over fiscal years 2018 through 2027, a ceiling that authorized a large revenue reduction while fixing an outer bound. The same title directed the Senate Committee on Energy and Natural Resources to report changes that reduced the deficit by at least 1 billion dollars over the same period, a floor applied to a far smaller target. Parallel House instructions ran to the Committee on Ways and Means, under the identical ceiling, and to the Committee on Natural Resources, under the identical floor. Each committee was required to submit recommendations to the Budget Committee of its chamber by November 13, 2017. A deadline within weeks of adoption of the resolution on October 26, 2017 compressed the drafting schedule for the tax legislation that became the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act.

By the end of 2019, the record supported a clear account of where power lay. The Budget Committees held initiation and measurement, drafting the instructions, determining compliance on estimates from the Congressional Budget Office and the Joint Committee on Taxation, and assembling the omnibus bill under a statutory bar on substantive revision. The committees of jurisdiction held composition, choosing the programs and tradeoffs through which a numerical target became statutory text. The full chambers held adoption, exercised first on the resolution and afterward on the bill. The instruction and the submission, a command stated in numbers and an answer stated in law, remained the foundation on which every reconciliation measure enacted between 1980 and 2019 was built.

## The Privilege on the Senate Floor

Floor consideration formed the third stage of reconciliation, and the stage at which the procedure diverged most sharply from the path of ordinary legislation. Committee action produced a bill, but floor consideration determined whether the targets in the budget resolution could become law over sustained opposition. In the House, the answer depended on majority control of the schedule and of the terms of debate. In the Senate, the answer depended on a statutory privilege that removed the ordinary power of a minority to prolong debate until a measure collapsed or a supermajority assembled to end discussion. The Congressional Research Service described Senate reconciliation as consideration under the procedures for a budget resolution, with a single decisive exception for the length of debate. That exception, in section 310 of the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974, governed everything that followed on the Senate floor, from the motion to take up the bill to the final vote on passage.

Consideration in the House came first in sequence and presented the simpler problem. The House acted by majority rule under standing rules that already allowed a determined majority to limit debate and to reach a vote, so a reconciliation bill required no statutory debate cap in that chamber. Instead, consideration proceeded under a special rule reported by the Rules Committee and adopted by the House for the particular measure. The Congressional Research Service reported that, in practice through the period ending in 2019, such special rules established the duration of general debate and specified which amendments, if any, could be offered on the floor. General debate under those rules commonly ran between one hour and three hours, with time equally divided and controlled by the majority and minority floor managers. The special rule functioned as both a clock and a gate. The rule fixed both the hours available and the amendments in order before debate began. The number of amendments made in order remained limited in every instance described by the Congressional Research Service, and in some instances no amendments were permitted.

The restrictive character of House practice under a special rule shaped the bill before the Senate received the measure. Reconciliation bills combined revenue provisions scored by the Joint Committee on Taxation with mandatory spending provisions scored by the Congressional Budget Office, and the combined effect had to conform to the instructions in the budget resolution. An open amendment process could disturb that arithmetic, and the special rule prevented the disturbance by screening amendments before consideration. The majority paid a price in deliberation, because members outside the committees of jurisdiction had little opportunity to reshape the measure, but obtained a bill whose fiscal effect remained predictable in the Senate.

Eligibility for expedited consideration in the Senate rested on the character of the measure rather than on a separate vote granting privilege. A bill that qualified as a reconciliation bill under section 310 of the Budget Act, codified at 2 U.S.C. 641, because a budget resolution had directed the committees to report legislation and the bill carried out those instructions, entered the Senate with the privilege attached. Section 310(e) directed that consideration proceed under the procedures of section 305, the provision governing budget resolutions, subject to the debate limit stated in section 310(e)(2). The privilege therefore traveled with the measure from the moment of reporting. No motion to suspend the rules was required, no unanimous consent was required to obtain protection from extended debate, and no preliminary supermajority vote stood between reporting and consideration. Floor managers thus inherited time limits that operated by force of statute rather than by agreement among senators.

The first mechanical consequence appeared at the threshold of consideration. The motion to proceed to a reconciliation bill was not debatable. Under ordinary practice, a motion to proceed could itself draw extended debate and force the majority to assemble sixty votes on cloture before consideration of the substance had begun. The Budget Act removed that preliminary contest for reconciliation bills. A senator could offer the motion to proceed, the Senate voted without debate on the motion, and adoption by a simple majority placed the bill before the Senate for amendment and passage. The Congressional Research Service identified the nondebatable motion to proceed as a direct result of the section 305 procedures incorporated by section 310(e). Delay at the threshold often proved as effective as delay on the merits, and the privilege eliminated that form of obstruction at the outset.

### Why can a reconciliation bill not be filibustered?

A reconciliation bill cannot be filibustered because the Budget Act limits total Senate debate to twenty hours by statute. Expiration of that time ends debate automatically, without a cloture motion or a sixty vote threshold. Passage then requires only a simple majority of senators voting, with a quorum present.

The answer rested on a distinction between a rule of the Senate and a command of a statute. The filibuster rested on Senate rules and precedents that permitted extended debate in the absence of a limit on discussion of a pending question. Cloture under Rule XXII supplied the internal remedy, and Rule XXII demanded a supermajority to invoke it. The Budget Act supplied a remedy enacted as law in 1974, in advance of any particular bill. Section 310(e)(2) fixed the total quantity of debate permitted on a reconciliation bill, and debate ended by operation of that provision when the quantity ran out. A senator who wished to continue speaking after expiration had no procedural basis for recognition for debate, because the statute had withdrawn debate time from the measure altogether. The presiding officer could then put the question and the Senate could proceed to passage. The privilege thus defeated extended debate by preemption rather than by persuasion, and the limit applied equally to measures favored by either party when that party held the majority.

The twenty hour cap defined the Senate privilege in quantitative terms. Section 310(e)(2), applying the section 305 procedures, limited debate on the bill and on all amendments, motions, and appeals to twenty hours in total. The figure measured debate on the entire consideration of the measure rather than debate on the bill in isolation, so every subsidiary question consumed part of the same finite stock. Every subsidiary question, from a lengthy amendment argument to an appeal from a ruling of the chair, drew from the common pool. A conference report on a reconciliation bill received a separate and smaller allocation of ten hours under the same framework. Time was equally divided between the majority and the minority and was controlled by the floor managers acting for each side, a structure that prevented the majority from monopolizing the clock and prevented the minority from exhausting the clock unilaterally. Each side controlled the use of its share and husbanded time for the questions that mattered most to its position.

Sub limits inside the overall cap apportioned debate among individual questions and preserved the ability of the Senate to reach many amendments within twenty hours. Debate on a first degree amendment, an amendment to the bill itself, was capped at two hours, equally divided between the mover of the amendment and the manager of the bill. Debate on a second degree amendment, an amendment to a pending amendment, and debate on a debatable motion or an appeal, was capped at one hour. The tiered structure reflected the hierarchy of questions before the Senate. A first degree amendment could rewrite a title of the bill and warranted the longer period, while a second degree amendment refined or displaced a proposal already under discussion and received the shorter period. Without sub limits, a handful of amendments could have consumed the entire twenty hours, and floor managers relied on the limits to plan a sequence that fit inside the aggregate allowance.

Accounting for the clock completed the mechanical picture. Time spent voting did not count against the twenty hours, a feature the Congressional Research Service noted in its description of the procedures. The statute charged discussion on the floor against the limit and left the mechanism of decision uncharged, so debate was the scarce resource subjected to rationing while voting remained available after that resource expired. That separation also explained the most consequential design choice in section 310, the choice to cap debate rather than consideration. A cap on consideration would have terminated the entire proceeding at a fixed hour, amendments pending and amendments unoffered alike. A cap on debate terminated only the speaking. After the twenty hours expired, senators could continue to offer amendments, make motions, and raise appeals, and the Senate could vote on each question in turn, but no further debate was in order on those questions beyond time yielded or provided by agreement. The Congressional Research Service described a period in which senators could continue to act after time had expired, without further debate, and that amendment session receives detailed treatment in the following section. Its origin belonged to the design of the privilege itself, because limiting words rather than actions preserved the right to propose changes and to force recorded votes while removing the ability to prevent a final vote through speech alone.

Germaneness imposed a second standing constraint that distinguished reconciliation from ordinary Senate consideration and reinforced the fiscal purpose of the procedure. Amendments to a reconciliation bill in the Senate had to be germane to the provisions of the bill and had to conform to other requirements of the Budget Act, including section 310(d) and the Byrd rule in section 313, codified at 2 U.S.C. 644. The Congressional Research Service stated the germaneness requirement without qualification in its summary of Senate procedures for reconciliation bills, and the requirement applied throughout consideration. In the Senate, germaneness formed an exception to general practice. Amendments to ordinary bills generally did not have to be germane, apart from specific situations such as consideration after cloture, and senators valued the freedom to attach unrelated proposals to a moving vehicle as leverage. Reconciliation withdrew that freedom. An amendment that ranged beyond the subject matter of the bill could be challenged by a point of order and excluded, however popular the unrelated proposal might have been on separate merits.

Unanimous consent agreements supplied the working arrangements that made the statutory structure administrable in practice. The Congressional Research Service reported that the Senate often relied on a series of unanimous consent agreements to structure consideration of amendments to a reconciliation bill. Such agreements typically specified which amendments would be offered, how much debate time would be allocated to each amendment, and whether a vote threshold different from a simple majority would apply to a particular question, a quorum being present. Agreements could also allocate the twenty hours across days of consideration. A single senator could block a proposed arrangement by objecting, but agreements emerged regularly because both sides gained predictability. The majority gained an orderly path to passage, and the minority gained guaranteed opportunities to offer designated amendments and obtain recorded votes. Consent agreements operated inside the statutory cap rather than in place of the cap, and no agreement could restore a right to filibuster the bill, because expiration of debate followed from the statute itself. If negotiations failed, the default rules of sections 305 and 310 governed and the bill moved toward disposition on the statutory schedule.

Passage required a simple majority, and the combination of a simple majority threshold with a guaranteed end to debate formed the complete privilege. A reconciliation bill that obtained the votes of a majority of senators voting, a quorum being present, passed the Senate. On a tie vote, the Vice President could cast the deciding vote under the Constitution, as in any other Senate proceeding. No separate vote to end debate preceded the vote on passage, because debate ended by expiration under the statute, and no supermajority was layered on top of the majority required for the question itself. The Congressional Research Service and the Senate Budget Committee treated the absence of a cloture requirement as the defining practical feature of the procedure, the feature that explained why majorities selected reconciliation as the vehicle for fiscal measures that commanded majority support but lacked the broader support that ordinary consideration demanded. The privilege did not guarantee passage, since a majority had to be assembled and held through every amendment vote that could alter the bill, but the privilege guaranteed a decision, and a guaranteed decision changed bargaining behavior throughout consideration.

The contrast with ordinary legislation turned entirely on mechanics. Under Rule XXII of the Standing Rules of the Senate, debate on an ordinary bill could continue until the Senate voted to invoke cloture, and invocation required the votes of three fifths of the senators duly chosen and sworn, sixty votes in a Senate of one hundred members with no vacancies. A measure supported by fifty five senators and opposed by forty five senators therefore failed to reach a final vote under ordinary procedures if the opponents sustained extended debate and the supporters fell short of sixty on cloture, even though a majority favored passage. A reconciliation bill supported by the same fifty five senators reached a final vote after twenty hours of debate and passed. [the filibuster and cloture rules](/2022/02/15/filibuster-and-cloture-explained/) supplied the background against which the value of the bypass could be measured, because the bypass had value only in relation to the sixty vote barrier that governed the ordinary path. Stated mechanically, reconciliation substituted a time limit set by statute for a vote threshold set by rule, and substituted a simple majority on the merits for a supermajority as the effective price of enactment in the Senate.

Dated experience demonstrated that the substitution decided outcomes rather than operating as an abstract advantage. The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1993, the deficit reduction measure of the Clinton administration, reached final Senate action on the conference report on August 6, 1993. The Senate adopted the conference report by a vote of 51 to 50, with Vice President Al Gore casting the tie breaking vote after the senators divided evenly at 50 to 50, according to the tie vote record maintained by the Senate. The House had adopted the same conference report on August 5, 1993, by a vote of 218 to 216. A measure carried only by a tie breaking vote could not have survived a sixty vote cloture requirement. The privilege supplied the path to enactment, President Clinton signed the measure on August 10, 1993, and the episode entered reconciliation practice as evidence that the procedure could carry a major fiscal program on the narrowest possible majority.

A second dated illustration closed the record available at the end of 2019 and confirmed that the privilege operated across administrations and across the direction of fiscal policy. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 proceeded in the Senate as a reconciliation measure under the budget resolution for fiscal year 2018, whose long title described a measure to provide for reconciliation pursuant to titles II and V of that resolution. The Senate passed its version of the bill on December 2, 2017, by a vote of 51 to 49, after debate conducted within the twenty hour framework and amendment consideration structured by consent agreements of the kind the Congressional Research Service described. Conference action followed, and the Senate agreed to the conference report on December 20, 2017, by a vote of 51 to 48. President Trump signed the measure on December 22, 2017, as Public Law 115-97. Revenue reduction formed the object in 2017 rather than deficit reduction through revenue increase, but the mechanical lesson matched the lesson of 1993. Fifty one votes sufficed for passage in the Senate because debate had a statutory end, and the same fifty one votes would have fallen nine short of cloture under the ordinary rule.

The record from the first use of reconciliation in 1980, in the Omnibus Reconciliation Act of 1980, Public Law 96-499, through the enactments of 1993 and 2017, showed a consistent pattern in Senate floor behavior under the privilege. Majorities treated the twenty hour allowance as a planning horizon, scheduling general debate, amendment sequences, and conference report consideration to fit inside allocations fixed by law. Minorities treated the equal division of time as a guaranteed platform for a public case against a measure the minority lacked the votes to defeat. The Senate Budget Committee acted as guardian of the privilege, advising on precedents and on conformity of amendments with sections 310 and 313. By the end of 2019, the mechanics had acquired a settled character: House action under a special rule that fixed debate and controlled amendments, and Senate action under a privilege that capped debate, required germane amendments, and ended with passage by a simple majority. Consideration ended not with exhaustion of the Senate but with expiration of a number in a statute, and that impersonal ending allowed the procedure to function for measures of opposed fiscal direction without regard to the party that held the majority in a given Congress.

## The Vote-a-Rama and the Road to Enactment

The strangest stretch of a reconciliation bill's life in the Senate begins when the clock runs out. Section 310 of the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974 capped debate on a reconciliation bill at twenty hours, and that cap made cloture unnecessary and passage a matter of a simple majority. The statute capped debate, however, and debate alone. The text placed no matching cap on the number of amendments a senator could offer, no cap on motions or appeals, and no limit on voting itself, and the Congressional Research Service recorded that time spent conducting votes did not count against the twenty hours. The vote-a-rama grew out of that gap between a closed debate and an open amendment right. The session simply continued past the point at which speeches ended, because the business that remained was voting.

### What happens after the twenty hours of debate run out?

After the twenty hours expire, consideration continues without debate. Senators may offer germane amendments, motions, and appeals, each disposed of in succession under unanimous consent agreements that allow brief explanations and votes of about ten minutes, until no further amendment is offered and passage follows.

The absence of debate did not mean the absence of rules. An amendment offered after time expired had to be germane, as every Senate amendment to a reconciliation bill had to be germane under the Budget Act. The amendment also had to conform to sections 310(d) and 313 of the Budget Act, the first governing the budgetary character of amendments and the second housing the Byrd rule, under which matter extraneous to the purpose of reconciliation could be struck on a point of order. Motions, appeals, and points of order remained in order as well, each disposed of without debate. The chamber worked through a queue that ended only when no senator sought recognition to offer another amendment. Final passage then followed as an ordinary question decided by a simple majority, the same majority that the twenty hour limit had been designed to make sufficient.

Order inside that queue came almost entirely from agreement rather than from the statute. The Congressional Research Service described a practice in which the Senate typically proceeded by unanimous consent to a nominal period for identifying and explaining each amendment, followed by votes of about ten minutes. A sponsor received a brief moment to state what an amendment did, an opponent received a brief moment in reply, and the vote followed. The ten minute vote was itself a discipline, since a roll call that drifted longer multiplied across dozens of questions into hours of lost time, and leaders on both sides had an interest in holding members close to the floor and ready to vote. Unanimous consent supplied that discipline because no statute imposed it, and a single objection could disturb an arrangement that rested on consent.

Persuasion had largely ended before the first post-debate vote was called, and that fact shaped the purpose of most amendments offered in the session. After time expired, an amendment received a minute or less of explanation and a ten minute vote, a format suited to recording a position rather than building a coalition. Many amendments were offered for the record the vote created. A roll call placed every senator on one side or the other of a defined question, and that record traveled beyond the chamber into campaign advertising, interest group scorecards, and later floor arguments over whether a senator had supported or opposed a proposition when a vote carried a price. An amendment could fail by a wide margin and succeed completely at the sponsor's actual purpose, which was to make colleagues answer a question in public.

The minority party held the strongest incentive to exploit that logic, and reconciliation handed the minority a rare instrument. A minority shut out of the bill's drafting, facing a measure that required only a simple majority and could not be filibustered, retained one asset after debate ended: an unlimited supply of germane amendments and the right to demand a disposition of each one. Wedge amendments followed directly from that asset. A well designed minority amendment forced majority senators to choose between voting for language that divided the majority coalition and voting against a proposition that polled well or sounded difficult to oppose, and either answer created material for the next campaign. Questions touching taxes, health care benefits, energy production, or the deficit could be framed to isolate majority senators from states where opinion ran against the party position. The majority countered with amendments of its own, designed to give vulnerable members a safer vote to cast or to dispose of a damaging proposition in a form the coalition could accept. Majority leaders also worked to hold the underlying bill intact, because an adopted amendment could disturb the budgetary arithmetic on which the bill rested or create a provision vulnerable to a Byrd rule challenge at a later stage.

Fatigue was a procedural fact of the vote-a-rama rather than a colorful detail. Senators, floor staff, clerks, parliamentarians, and legislative counsel worked through sessions that began after a full day of debate and extended past midnight toward dawn, reading amendment text, checking budgetary effects, ruling on germaneness and extraneous matter, and recording votes at ten minute intervals. Error risk rose with exhaustion in ways that touched the substance of law. An amendment drafted in haste could misstate a cross reference, alter a date, or change a formula in a manner the sponsor had not intended, and an amendment adopted in that condition carried its defect into the enrolled bill unless a later stage caught the defect. A manager's amendment offered late in the process presented the same risk at larger scale, since a single amendment could revise many provisions at once and members voted on the package after limited examination. The safeguards against such error were institutional: the parliamentarian's advice on points of order, the Budget Committee's checks against the reconciliation instructions, scoring information from the Congressional Budget Office and the Joint Committee on Taxation, and the discipline of germaneness and the Byrd rule. Those safeguards operated under strain during a vote-a-rama, which explained why leaders preferred negotiated unanimous consent agreements that fixed the amendment queue in advance.

The scale of a mature vote-a-rama appeared most clearly in the budget resolution session of March 2013, a proceeding that resembled the reconciliation vote-a-rama in form while differing in the measure under consideration. A budget resolution was a concurrent resolution that set fiscal levels and carried reconciliation instructions in some years, but a budget resolution was not a law, was never presented to the President, and was debated under a fifty hour limit rather than the twenty hours allowed for a reconciliation bill. The distinction mattered, and the 2013 session served as a cousin of the reconciliation vote-a-rama rather than an instance of the procedure itself. The Senate's own compilation of vote-a-rama sessions recorded forty-three roll call votes on the fiscal year 2014 budget resolution, S.Con.Res. 8, on March 22, 2013, and the chamber adjourned at 5:22 in the morning on March 23, 2013. Contemporary reporting counted more than five hundred amendments filed, and the resolution passed by a vote of 50 to 49. The governing lesson for reconciliation was structural: once debate time expired on a budget measure, the right to offer amendments converted exhaustion of the clock into a second proceeding, measured in roll calls rather than in hours, and ended by depletion of the queue rather than by any rule.

Reconciliation bills generated marathons of the same character on measures that carried the force of law. The Health Care and Education Reconciliation Act of 2010, H.R. 4872, reached the Senate in March 2010 as the companion measure to the health care statute signed on March 23, 2010, and the amendment session ran through the night of March 24, 2010. The Senate's vote-a-rama compilation recorded twenty-nine roll call votes on the measure on March 24, 2010, and the chamber adjourned at 2:55 in the morning on March 25, 2010. Contemporary reporting described a session of about twelve hours in which Republicans offered more than forty amendments and the Democratic majority turned back each amendment, a pattern that matched the vote-a-rama as a record making exercise for the minority and a coalition holding exercise for the majority. Two provisions of the bill dealing with student loans fell to Byrd rule points of order during the same consideration. The Senate passed the amended bill on March 25, 2010, by a vote of 56 to 43, and the struck provisions carried the measure back to the House, which agreed to the Senate amendments later on March 25, 2010, by a vote of 220 to 207. President Obama signed the measure on March 30, 2010, as Public Law 111-152.

The tax measure of 2017 supplied a second reconciliation illustration, with a major manager's amendment and a conference committee at the end of the road. The Senate took up H.R. 1, the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, under reconciliation instructions in late November 2017, and the decisive amendment session ran through December 1, 2017, into the early hours of December 2, 2017. Amendments from both parties were disposed of in rapid succession under the post-debate discipline, a manager's amendment carrying negotiated revisions was adopted, and individual questions ranged from education savings accounts to the child tax credit to the treatment of energy development. One amendment, offered by Senator Ted Cruz on the use of section 529 accounts for elementary and secondary education expenses, was adopted by a vote of 51 to 50 with the Vice President breaking the tie, an outcome that showed the post-debate session deciding real text and not merely recording positions. The Senate passed the bill on December 2, 2017, by a vote of 51 to 49, with Senator Bob Corker the only Republican voting against passage. Because the Senate text differed from the version the House had passed on November 16, 2017, the measure entered the stage at which the two chambers had to produce a single text.

Resolution of differences between the chambers proceeded by one of three routes, and reconciliation practice used each route across the decades after 1980. The first route was the conference committee, in which conferees appointed by each chamber negotiated a single compromise text and filed a conference report that returned to both chambers for approval without amendment. A conference report was privileged in both chambers, and the Budget Act limited Senate debate on a conference report on a reconciliation bill to ten hours, equally divided and controlled by the majority and minority leaders, a limit that applied equally to debate on a message between the chambers and on amendments in disagreement. The ten hour limit preserved the essential reconciliation bargain at the last stage: the minority could argue against the compromise, but the minority could not prevent a vote, and the compromise stood or fell by a simple majority in the Senate. The tax measure of 2017 followed the conference route after Senate passage, as conferees reconciled provisions on which the chambers had differed, including the timing of the corporate rate reduction and the treatment of the alternative minimum tax, and both chambers approved the resulting report in December 2017 before presentment later that month.

The second route was an exchange of amendments between the chambers, in which one chamber amended the text received from the other chamber and returned the measure, and the receiving chamber then concurred, concurred with a further amendment, or refused the change. The exchange route avoided the formal appointment of conferees, and the Senate's ten hour debate limit covered the messages and amendments in disagreement just as the limit covered a conference report. The Consolidated Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1985 reached enactment after the chambers used an exchange of amendments to resolve differences that remained after conferees had failed to produce an agreed conference report, as the Congressional Research Service recorded in its account of the stages of consideration. The health reconciliation measure of 2010 ended by a variant of the same logic, since the House disposed of the Senate's changes by agreeing to the Senate amendments rather than by requesting a conference, and the Restoring Americans' Healthcare Freedom Reconciliation Act of 2015 followed the pattern, with the House agreeing to the Senate amendment before the measure went to the President and met a veto. The third route was the simplest: one chamber took up the other chamber's text and passed the measure unchanged, which eliminated the need for conference or exchange entirely.

Whichever route produced agreement, the constitutional requirement at the end of the legislative road was identical text. Only after the House and Senate had approved the same words in the same bill could the measure be enrolled and presented to the President under Article I, Section 7, of the Constitution. Presentment converted a privileged internal procedure into an ordinary bill at the threshold of the executive branch, and every expedited feature of reconciliation fell away at that threshold. The President had ten days, Sundays excepted, after presentment in which to sign the bill into law. The President could instead return the bill with objections, which constituted a veto, and Congress could enact the measure over a veto only by a vote of two thirds in each chamber. If the President took no action while Congress remained in session, the bill became law without a signature at the end of the ten day period. If adjournment of Congress prevented return of the bill during that period, presidential inaction produced a pocket veto, and the measure failed without an opportunity for an override vote. Reconciliation altered none of those outcomes.

The veto therefore stood as the one ordinary checkpoint that reconciliation could not bypass, dilute, or schedule away, and the record through the end of 2019 contained presidential vetoes that ended reconciliation measures after complete congressional passage. President Clinton vetoed the Balanced Budget Act of 1995, H.R. 2491, on December 6, 1995, a measure that had carried spending reductions and tax provisions through both chambers under reconciliation and that failed to become law after the veto. President Obama vetoed the Restoring Americans' Healthcare Freedom Reconciliation Act of 2015, H.R. 3762, on January 8, 2016, after the House agreed to the Senate amendment and completed congressional action on a measure directed at the health care statute enacted in 2010. The Congressional Research Service counted four vetoes of reconciliation legislation across the full record and recorded that Congress had not successfully overridden any of those vetoes, a result consistent with the arithmetic of the procedure: a bill that passed the Senate by a simple majority under reconciliation rarely commanded the two thirds in both chambers that an override demanded.

Enactment, when presentment ended in a signature or in the ten day period expiring without one, closed a journey whose length surprised observers who associated reconciliation with speed. The expedited features of the process governed floor consideration in the Senate, and floor consideration occupied only a fraction of the calendar between a budget resolution and a statute. The interval between adoption of the resolution and enactment of the resulting bill varied widely with the measure, from a single season in the fastest cases to a span crossing two calendar years in the slowest, and the 2010 health measure and the 2005 deficit reduction act stood among the long journeys. That variation carried the central lesson of the endgame. The twenty hours of Senate debate, the ten hours on a conference report, and the ten minute votes of the vote-a-rama were islands of statutory speed inside a process whose total length depended on bargaining among institutions, and the marathon was only the close of Senate consideration, with conference, presentment, and a possible veto waiting beyond the chamber door.

## The Limits on What the Vehicle Can Carry

Reconciliation exchanged one kind of difficulty for another. The procedure created by section 310 of the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974, Public Law 93-344 and codified at 2 U.S.C. 641, freed a qualifying bill from extended Senate debate, and passage turned on a simple majority. The price of that privilege was a dense set of restrictions on content. A reconciliation bill between 1980 and 2019 was a budget instrument with a defined errand, namely the alteration of outlays, revenues, or the debt limit as instructed in a budget resolution.

The limits rested on three distinct foundations. One foundation was the instruction itself, because a committee that reported language outside the amounts or the subject matter assigned by the budget resolution exposed the product to a point of order. A second foundation was the set of points of order written into the Budget Act, most prominently section 313, the Byrd rule, codified at 2 U.S.C. 644, and section 310(d), which governed amendments. A third foundation was a small number of categorical exclusions that drafting could not cure, most prominently the exclusion of the Social Security old-age, survivors and disability insurance program. A categorical exclusion removed a subject from the vehicle altogether, regardless of drafting skill, budgetary effect, or support within the majority.

### What can a reconciliation bill not contain?

A reconciliation bill cannot contain extraneous matter under the Byrd rule, recommendations changing Social Security, provisions outside the reporting committee jurisdiction, amendments that breach instructed amounts, or matter that increases the deficit beyond the budget window without offset in the same title.

The center of the enforcement system was the Byrd rule, adopted in 1985 and made permanent in 1990 as section 313 of the Budget Act. In outline, the rule defined extraneous matter through six tests stated in a single statutory passage, and a provision failed the rule if the provision fell within any one of the six definitions, a structure examined at length in [the Byrd rule explained in full](/2020/03/15/byrd-rule-explained/). A provision was extraneous if the provision produced no change in outlays or revenues and no change in the terms and conditions under which outlays were made or revenues were collected, if the provision produced an outlay increase or a revenue decrease at a time when the committee that submitted the title containing the provision had failed to comply with the committee instruction, if the provision lay outside the jurisdiction of the committee that submitted the title or provision, if the provision produced a change in outlays or revenues that was merely incidental to the nonbudgetary components of the provision, if the provision would increase the deficit for a fiscal year beyond the years covered by the reconciliation measure, or if the provision recommended changes in Social Security. The Congressional Research Service summaries treated the six definitions as alternatives, so a provision with a large budgetary effect could fail on jurisdiction alone.

The remedy for a sustained Byrd rule point of order gave the rule a character unlike the character of most Senate points of order. A violation did not defeat the bill, did not return the bill to committee, and did not prevent further consideration. The point of order operated surgically. A senator raised the point of order against designated matter, identified by title, by section, by page and line, or in some instances by a single word, and the presiding officer ruled on each challenged item separately. If the presiding officer sustained the point of order as to an item, the Senate struck that item and the balance of the bill remained before the Senate. Stricken matter could not return through an amendment during the same consideration. A majority that lost a favored provision therefore lost the provision, not the enterprise, and the balance of the bill ordinarily proceeded to a passage vote.

The escape route from a Byrd rule ruling restored the supermajority problem that reconciliation had been designed to avoid, but the restored problem operated at the level of content rather than at the level of passage. A motion to waive the Byrd rule required the affirmative vote of three fifths of the senators duly chosen and sworn, sixty votes in a Senate without more than one vacancy, and an appeal of a ruling of the chair on a Byrd rule point of order required the same three fifths vote to succeed. A bare majority could pass the bill that remained after enforcement, yet a bare majority could not retain a provision that the rule condemned. A majority with fifty one votes possessed the votes to enact the compliant core of a program, and the same majority lacked the votes to protect a noncompliant provision.

The presiding officer issued the ruling, and Senate practice between 1985 and 2019 treated the advice of the Senate Parliamentarian as the operative guidance behind the ruling. The Parliamentarian, a nonpartisan officer, assessed challenged matter against the six definitions and prior rulings, and presiding officers followed that advice in the ordinary course. The Congressional Budget Office and the Joint Committee on Taxation supplied the estimates on which several definitions depended, and the chair of the Senate Budget Committee identified provisions for review. Much of the decisive work occurred before a bill reached the floor, in the informal scrub known as the Byrd bath. In a Byrd bath, staff presented draft text to the Parliamentarian, and sponsors redrafted, relocated, or abandoned matter identified as at risk before formal consideration began. Provisions removed at that stage acquired the label of Byrd droppings in Senate parlance, and the scrub explained the relative scarcity of formal floor precedents.

Among the six definitions, the prohibition on deficit increases beyond the budget window exerted the most visible influence on enacted law, because the definition compelled a choice between permanence and passage. Section 313 treated a title as extraneous if the title increased net outlays or decreased revenues in a fiscal year beyond the years covered by the reconciliation measure and the change lacked an offset within the same title in that year. The test applied year by year outside the window, and a provision that satisfied every instruction inside a ten year window failed if the provision produced a deficit increase in the eleventh year. Drafters possessed three compliant responses: a permanent cost paired with a permanent offset inside the same title, a smaller permanent change shown as deficit neutral or deficit reducing outside the window, or termination of the costly provision before the window closed. The third response, the sunset, became the signature drafting device of the tax reconciliation statutes of the period from 2001 to 2019.

The Economic Growth and Tax Relief Reconciliation Act of 2001, Public Law 107-16, enacted on June 7, 2001, supplied the standing illustration. The Congressional Research Service account of the act recorded that the tax cuts carried a sunset after ten years in order to comply with the Byrd rule, and section 901 of the act provided for reversion to prior law after December 31, 2010. Rate reductions, marriage penalty relief, the enlarged child tax credit, and estate tax changes therefore entered the statute book with a common expiration date that reflected the length of the budget window. The Jobs and Growth Tax Relief Reconciliation Act of 2003, Public Law 108-27, enacted on May 28, 2003, followed the same discipline. The 2003 act accelerated reductions that the 2001 act had phased in and cut the tax rates applied to dividends and capital gains, with the reduced rates on dividends and capital gains effective through the end of 2008 and the accelerated individual rate reductions tied to the 2010 expiration inherited from the 2001 act. Later statutes extended portions of the two acts, but permanence in 2001 and in 2003 would have produced deficit increases beyond the window without offsets in the same titles, at a price of sixty votes that the majorities of 2001 and 2003 did not possess.

The tax statute enacted on December 22, 2017, Public Law 115-97, under the budget resolution for fiscal year 2018, repeated the pattern with a deliberate asymmetry. Drafters made the corporate rate reduction permanent, paired with permanent base broadening inside the same titles so that the titles satisfied the test outside the window. The individual side received the opposite treatment. The revised individual rate schedules, the enlarged standard deduction, the suspension of personal exemptions, the enlarged child tax credit, the limitation on deductions for state and local taxes, the new deduction for qualified business income of pass through entities, and the enlarged estate tax exemption carried an expiration date of December 31, 2025. Analyses by the Congressional Budget Office, the Joint Committee on Taxation, the Brookings Institution, and the Penn Wharton Budget Model attributed the 2025 expiration to the Byrd rule prohibition on deficit increases beyond the ten year window. A late Byrd bath episode in December 2017 illustrated the merely incidental test as well, because the Parliamentarian advised that several provisions in the House passed text, among them the short title of the act itself, were extraneous, the Senate struck the provisions, and the House voted a second time on the amended text before enrollment.

The Social Security exclusion stood apart because the exclusion admitted no drafting cure and rested on two reinforcing provisions. Section 310(g) of the Budget Act barred a reconciliation bill from containing recommendations with respect to the old-age, survivors and disability insurance program established under Title II of the Social Security Act, and the sixth definition of extraneous matter in section 313 repeated the prohibition inside the Byrd rule. A provision that recommended changes to that program was extraneous by definition, and a bill that contained such recommendations violated section 310(g) before any question of budgetary effect arose. The bar was absolute in the sense that subject matter, rather than the size or the direction of a budgetary effect, triggered the exclusion, and a drafter could not rescue a Social Security provision by demonstrating a large deficit reduction or by drafting the change as a change in the terms and conditions under which revenues were collected. The exclusion covered the old-age, survivors and disability insurance program, and the Congressional Research Service consistently stated that the exclusion did not cover Medicare or other programs established under other titles of the Social Security Act. Medicare provisions therefore traveled through reconciliation repeatedly between 1980 and 2019, while old-age, survivors and disability insurance benefits and the eligibility rules of that program remained outside the vehicle.

Other points of order policed content around the edges of the Byrd rule. Section 310(d) barred amendments that would increase spending or reduce revenues beyond the amounts set in the instructions, and Senate practice under the Budget Act required that amendments to a reconciliation bill be germane to the bill in addition to conformity with sections 310(d) and 313. An amendment with a genuine budgetary effect therefore failed if the amendment ranged beyond the pending title or breached the instructed totals. The budget resolution itself generated further points of order that operated as content limits in practice, because enforcement provisions in Titles III and IV of the Budget Act protected committee allocations, aggregate levels of outlays and revenues, and the deficit or surplus totals stated in the resolution. The chair of the Budget Committee supplied the authoritative statements of allocation and aggregate levels for enforcement purposes, and the combined effect was cumulative. A provision had to fit the instruction, fit the allocation, satisfy the Byrd rule, respect the amendment rules, and avoid the categorical exclusions, and failure at any one gate excluded the provision.

Majorities that confronted the limits developed a repertoire of workarounds and counter-moves between 1980 and 2019, and the repertoire began with drafting. The merely incidental test invited the most sustained drafting effort, because the test asked whether a change in outlays or revenues was merely incidental to the nonbudgetary components of a provision, and drafters responded by constructing provisions with budgetary effects that were direct, substantial, and integral to the operation of the provision. A program administered through fees, refundable credits, or direct spending tied to eligibility conditions presented a stronger case than a regulatory command with a remote effect on federal collections. Staff consulted the Congressional Budget Office and the Joint Committee on Taxation during drafting, since a provision scored at no change in outlays or revenues faced exclusion as matter without a budgetary effect. Drafters also merged policy into budgetary text by expressing a policy choice as a condition of payment, a rate, a credit formula, or an eligibility term for a funded program. Relabeling a mandate as a tax penalty or a prohibition as a funding condition did not guarantee survival, because the Parliamentarian assessed the relationship between the budgetary and the nonbudgetary components as a matter of substance, and provisions whose primary character was regulatory repeatedly fell in Byrd bath review between 1985 and 2019.

Acceptance of sunsets ranked as the most consequential workaround because acceptance traded duration for enactment. A majority that lacked sixty votes and lacked offsets faced a choice among a permanent provision that the rule excluded, a temporary provision that the rule allowed, and abandonment of the provision. The majorities of 2001, 2003, and 2017 chose temporary enactment in the major tax statutes of the period, on the stated expectation within those coalitions that a later Congress would extend the provisions before expiration. The workaround embedded a future legislative fight into every affected statute and gave opponents a scheduled opportunity to relitigate the policy. Waiver offered a second route where a coalition of sixty existed for a particular item, and waiver motions between 1985 and 2019 occasionally protected discrete provisions. The Congressional Research Service tally of Byrd rule actions in the period found that waiver motions failed far more often than waiver motions succeeded.

The most dramatic counter-moves aimed at the referee rather than at the text, and the record through the end of 2019 showed a sharp difference between discussion and execution. A presiding officer ruling against Parliamentarian advice appeared in procedural debate as a theoretical route around an adverse interpretation, and section 313 required three fifths of the Senate to sustain an appeal of a ruling of the chair, so a simple majority could not accomplish through an appeal the result that the rule withheld. No majority between 1985 and 2019 carried through a floor repudiation of Parliamentarian advice on extraneous matter in a reconciliation measure. Replacement of the officeholder occurred once and demonstrated both the availability and the reputational price of the personnel route. In May 2001, Majority Leader Trent Lott removed Parliamentarian Robert Dove from the office, and Dove retired in May 2001 after service that Senate sources described as thirteen years in the top office across two periods of service. Senator Russ Feingold stated on the Senate floor in May 2001 that the decision followed frustration with advice on a number of budget related issues, during the consideration season that produced the tax reconciliation act of 2001, and Alan Frumin succeeded Dove as Parliamentarian. The episode entered Senate memory as a warning rather than as a precedent for routine use, and the 2001 statute as enacted bore a sunset that complied with the rule in any event.

A final counter-move operated on the supply of vehicles rather than on the content of any single vehicle. Congress adopted a budget resolution for fiscal year 2017 in January 2017, S.Con.Res. 3, agreed to on January 13, 2017, with reconciliation instructions directed toward health care legislation, and the resulting measure, the American Health Care Act of 2017, H.R. 1628, passed the House and failed to secure passage in the Senate during 2017. Congress then adopted a second budget resolution within the same calendar year, the budget resolution for fiscal year 2018, H.Con.Res. 71, agreed to on October 26, 2017, and the resulting measure became the tax statute enacted on December 22, 2017 as Public Law 115-97. The Congressional Research Service account of the stages of consideration identified calendar year 2017 as the example of the two resolution pattern, because no provision of the Budget Act barred adoption of a resolution for a second fiscal year in a calendar year in which a resolution for an earlier fiscal year had been adopted late. The tactic doubled the number of privileged vehicles available within a single year of unified government, without relaxing any content limit.

The structural consequence of the system was that the limits, rather than the privilege, shaped the final text of reconciliation statutes between 1980 and 2019. The privilege determined the vote threshold for passage and the allocation of debate time, matters of procedure that left no trace in the United States Code. The limits determined duration, through sunsets imposed by the beyond window test, scope, through the exclusion of Social Security and the deletion of provisions whose budgetary effects were incidental to regulatory purposes, and drafting form, through the preference for rates, credits, and conditions of payment over freestanding commands. The major reconciliation statutes of the period, from the Omnibus Reconciliation Act of 1980, Public Law 96-499, through the tax statute of 2017, recorded the discipline in omissions and expiration dates as faithfully as the operative provisions recorded policy judgments.

## The Record, the Irony and the Argument About Abuse

The record of reconciliation from 1980 to 2019 divides into distinct eras. Reconciliation was established by section 310 of the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974, Public Law 93-344, codified at 2 U.S.C. 641, as a means of aligning existing spending and revenue law with an adopted budget resolution. After 2000 the same machinery carried tax reductions scored to increase deficits.

The first use came in 1980 under a Democratic president, and the second came in 1981 under a Republican president, a pairing that set a pattern of ownership by both parties. The Omnibus Reconciliation Act of 1980, Public Law 96-499, signed by President Jimmy Carter on December 5, 1980, was the first reconciliation bill to pass the House and the Senate, and the Congressional Research Service described its estimated effect as a reduction in the fiscal year 1981 deficit through outlay reductions and revenue increases across programs that included child nutrition, student loans, Medicare, Medicaid, and unemployment compensation. The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981, Public Law 97-35, signed by President Ronald Reagan on August 13, 1981, operated at a far larger scale as part of the opening program of the Reagan administration, and the Congressional Research Service summary recorded an estimated deficit reduction over three fiscal years driven mainly by spending changes, including conversion of categorical programs into block grants, reductions in food stamp benefits and dairy price supports, changes to Medicaid, and elimination of the Social Security minimum benefit.

The middle years of the 1980s extended that pattern through a succession of omnibus measures. The Tax Equity and Fiscal Responsibility Act of 1982, Public Law 97-248, signed on September 3, 1982, raised revenue by narrowing tax preferences, tightening compliance rules, and reversing portions of the tax reductions enacted in 1981, while also reducing outlays in Medicare, Medicaid, and income support programs. The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1982, Public Law 97-253, signed on September 8, 1982, reduced outlays further in areas that included farm payments, food stamps, and federal retirement adjustments. The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1983, Public Law 98-270, signed on April 18, 1984, delayed cost of living adjustments for federal retirees. The Consolidated Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1985, Public Law 99-272, signed on April 7, 1986, combined spending reductions in student loans, Medicare, and Medicaid with revenue provisions that included an extension of the cigarette tax. The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1986, Public Law 99-509, signed on October 21, 1986, and the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Public Law 100-203, signed on December 22, 1987, continued the Reagan sequence, and the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1989, Public Law 101-239, signed on December 19, 1989, continued the pattern under President George H. W. Bush.

The two largest deficit reduction enactments of the era followed in 1990 and 1993, by different political routes. The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1990, Public Law 101-508, signed by President George H. W. Bush on November 5, 1990, gave statutory form to the budget summit agreement of that year, combining revenue increases with spending restraint and establishing, in its Budget Enforcement Act title, discretionary spending caps and a PAYGO discipline that governed congressional budgeting through much of the following decade. The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1993, Public Law 103-66, signed by President Bill Clinton on August 10, 1993, passed Congress without Republican support, and the Senate Budget Committee figures cited by the Congressional Research Service put its five year deficit reduction at a level built from revenue increases, including higher top rates for individuals and corporations and an increase in transportation fuel taxes, and spending reductions concentrated in Medicare and other mandatory programs. The 1990 act ratified a negotiated settlement between a Republican president and a Democratic Congress, while the 1993 act carried a Democratic program through a Democratic Congress on party line votes.

A different use appeared in 1996, when reconciliation carried a restructuring of federal welfare policy that reached well beyond budgetary adjustments, even though the budgetary effects supplied the procedural justification. The Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996, Public Law 104-193, signed on August 22, 1996, replaced Aid to Families with Dependent Children, an open ended federal entitlement for eligible families, with the Temporary Assistance for Needy Families block grant, which fixed federal funding, imposed work requirements and time limits, and transferred substantial discretion to the states.

The budget cycle for fiscal year 1998 produced two enacted reconciliation measures from a single budget resolution. The Balanced Budget Act of 1997, Public Law 105-33, signed on August 5, 1997, carried the spending side of the balanced budget agreement between President Clinton and the Republican Congress, restraining projected Medicare spending and creating the State Children's Health Insurance Program. The Taxpayer Relief Act of 1997, Public Law 105-34, signed on the same day, carried the revenue side of the same agreement, creating the child tax credit, reducing capital gains rates, and establishing new savings incentives for education and retirement.

### Has reconciliation been used to increase the deficit?

Yes. The Joint Committee on Taxation estimated a revenue loss of about 1.35 trillion dollars for the 2001 act and about 350 billion dollars for the 2003 act, and a revenue loss of about 1.5 trillion dollars for the 2017 act, each measured over a ten year window at enactment.

The turn arrived with the tax legislation of 2001 and 2003, which used instructions written to permit revenue reduction rather than to require revenue increase or spending restraint. The Economic Growth and Tax Relief Reconciliation Act of 2001, Public Law 107-16, signed by President George W. Bush on June 7, 2001, lowered individual income tax rates, created a new lowest bracket, expanded the child tax credit, and reduced estate taxes on a schedule that reached repeal for a single year. The Joint Committee on Taxation estimate prepared as Congress cleared the measure in May 2001 put the budgetary cost at about 1.35 trillion dollars over the period from fiscal year 2001 to fiscal year 2011, Because the Byrd rule barred provisions that would increase the deficit beyond the budget window covered by the resolution, the act carried sunset provisions under which its tax reductions expired at the end of 2010. The Jobs and Growth Tax Relief Reconciliation Act of 2003, Public Law 108-27, signed on May 28, 2003, accelerated rate reductions scheduled under the 2001 act, lowered the rates applied to dividends and capital gains, and expanded expensing for business investment, and contemporary Congressional Budget Office descriptions placed its ten year cost at about 350 billion dollars, a ceiling that Senate moderates had demanded during negotiation.

Two measures from the fiscal year 2006 budget cycle showed both directions operating in the same year under the same resolution. The Deficit Reduction Act of 2005, Public Law 109-171, signed on February 8, 2006, reduced projected spending in programs that included Medicaid, student loans, and agricultural supports, and its passage in the Senate turned on a tie breaking vote by the Vice President after the chamber divided evenly. The Tax Increase Prevention and Reconciliation Act of 2005, Public Law 109-222, signed on May 17, 2006, extended reduced rates on dividends and capital gains and provided relief from the alternative minimum tax, increasing projected deficits on the revenue side while the companion act reduced projected spending. Budget scholar Allen Schick, in writing on the congressional budget process, treated such combinations as evidence that reconciliation had become a general purpose vehicle for the majority's fiscal program rather than a discipline imposed on that program. The College Cost Reduction and Access Act of 2007, Public Law 110-84, signed on September 27, 2007, under a Democratic Congress and a Republican president, reduced subsidies to student loan providers and used the savings to increase Pell Grant funding and reduce interest rates on subsidized student loans, demonstrating that a change in party control changed the direction of reconciliation policy without changing the procedure itself.

The health coverage legislation of 2010 presented the most procedurally intricate use in the record, because reconciliation served as a repair mechanism for a bill that had already passed the Senate under regular order. The Senate passed the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act in December 2009 with sixty votes. In January 2010 a special election in Massachusetts filled the Senate seat left vacant by the death of Senator Edward Kennedy with a Republican, Scott Brown, and the Democratic caucus fell to fifty nine votes, which meant that an amended version of the health bill sent back to the Senate under regular order faced a filibuster that the majority could no longer close. The House therefore passed the Senate bill without change, and Congress carried the agreed amendments in a separate measure, the Health Care and Education Reconciliation Act of 2010, Public Law 111-152, signed on March 30, 2010, which adjusted subsidy levels, tax provisions, and Medicare payment policies in the main act and restructured the federal student loan program by ending bank based guaranteed lending. The Congressional Budget Office scored the reconciliation measure together with the main act as reducing projected deficits over the following decade. Reconciliation could not have carried the entire health program on its own, because provisions regulating insurance markets lacked the direct budgetary effect the procedure requires, but reconciliation could carry the budgetary amendments that made the enacted program acceptable to both chambers.

The final enactment in the period closed the arc that the 2001 act had opened. The measure commonly known as [the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017](/2013/06/15/tax-cuts-and-jobs-act-2017-guide/), Public Law 115-97, signed on December 22, 2017, reduced the corporate income tax rate to a flat rate, lowered individual rates on a temporary schedule, limited the deduction for state and local taxes, expanded the child tax credit,. The Joint Committee on Taxation projected a revenue loss of approximately 1.5 trillion dollars over the ten year budget window at enactment, and the Congressional Budget Office estimate published in April 2018, which incorporated debt service and macroeconomic feedback, placed the increase in deficits over the period from 2018 to 2028 at a higher figure. The budget resolution for fiscal year 2018 had authorized the deficit increase in advance through instructions permitting the tax committees to report legislation that reduced revenues by a stated amount, so the 2017 act complied with its instructions even as it enlarged projected deficits, and the individual provisions carried expiration dates after 2025 because the Byrd rule prohibited deficit increases beyond the budget window.

The failures and vetoes that punctuate the record define the outer boundary of the vehicle as clearly as the enactments define its reach. The Balanced Budget Act of 1995, which passed Congress as H.R. 2491, reached President Clinton in December 1995 and met a veto on December 6, 1995, during the confrontation over spending that also produced two federal government shutdowns, and Congress did not override the veto. The Taxpayer Refund and Relief Act of 1999, H.R. 2488, carried a large tax reduction to the same president, who vetoed the measure on September 23, 1999. The Marriage Tax Relief Reconciliation Act of 2000, H.R. 4810, followed in the next cycle and met a veto on August 5, 2000, after which an override attempt failed in the House. The Restoring Americans' Healthcare Freedom Reconciliation Act of 2015, H.R. 3762, which would have repealed central provisions of the 2010 health act and restricted funding for Planned Parenthood for a period, passed both chambers and met a veto from President Barack Obama on January 8, 2016, and the House override attempt fell short of the required two thirds majority. The sequence from 2017 supplied the remaining category of failure, a defeat inside the Senate itself. The American Health Care Act passed the House in May 2017 as a reconciliation bill, and the Senate considered its own versions under reconciliation procedures in July 2017, but the final attempt, a narrow measure directed at repealing selected mandates and taxes, failed on a vote of 49 to 51 in the early hours of July 28, 2017, after which the effort ended.

Counting the enactments gives the record its precise shape. The Congressional Research Service report titled Budget Reconciliation Measures Enacted into Law Since 1980, report number R40480, catalogues each enacted measure individually in its appendix and lists each measure sent to a president in its table of budget resolutions and resultant reconciliation acts. Counting only the entries in that catalogue bearing a public law number and an enactment date on or before December 31, 2019, and excluding the four vetoed measures that appear in the same table without a public law number, yields twenty one enacted reconciliation measures for the period from 1980 to 2019. The catalogue also supports a negative finding of equal importance. No measure in the Congressional Research Service catalogue through the end of 2019 enacted a climate program, and no enacted reconciliation statute in the period took climate policy as its subject, since the energy provisions that appeared in various measures operated as tax credits, fees, or spending items inside larger fiscal packages rather than as a climate statute.

The irony at the center of the record follows directly from the statutory design. Section 310 authorizes a budget resolution to direct committees to report changes in laws within their jurisdiction so that spending, revenue, and debt limit levels conform to the levels set in the resolution. The text directs conformity, not reduction. A resolution that sets a revenue level lower than the level projected under existing law generates instructions for tax reduction just as lawfully as a resolution that sets a revenue level higher than the projection generates instructions for tax increase, and a reconciliation bill satisfies its instructions when the reported changes match the instructed amounts. Nothing in section 310 requires a reconciliation bill to reduce the deficit, and nothing in the statute limits the procedure to measures whose net effect improves the fiscal position. The Byrd rule, adopted in the Senate in 1985 and later codified, restricts the content of reconciliation bills by barring extraneous matter, and one of its tests treats a provision as extraneous when the provision increases the deficit beyond the years covered by the resolution, which explains the recurring architecture of sunsets in the tax acts of 2001, 2003, and 2017. A procedure designed for deficit reduction therefore became available for deficit enlargement through a series of lawful choices, each taken in a budget resolution adopted by majority vote, and the transformation required no amendment to the statute that created the procedure.

The argument about abuse draws its force from that gap between original purpose and later practice. The charge, pressed by senators and budget process scholars at several points in the record and reflected in floor debates over the tax acts of 2001 and 2017 and the health act of 2010, holds that a deficit tool became a majority tool, a route around the Senate's ordinary requirement of sixty votes to close debate, available for whichever large fiscal measure the majority of the moment wished to enact. On that reading, the substantive output of the modern Senate bears the imprint of a rule written for a different purpose, since the measures that define eras of policy, from welfare restructuring to the tax revisions of 2001 and 2017 to the financing structure of the 2010 health act, reached the statute book through a procedure whose debate limits, amendment restrictions, and content screens were designed for budget enforcement legislation.

The counter reading answers the charge on the same record and reaches a different characterization of the same facts. Reconciliation rests on express statutory authorization enacted in 1974, and every use between 1980 and 2019 traced to a budget resolution adopted by both chambers under that statute, so no use in the period involved a procedure invented for the occasion or a rule suspended to permit the vote. Both parties used the vehicle whenever each held the combination of a narrow Senate majority and a large fiscal program, including deficit reduction under Presidents Carter, Reagan, George H. W. Bush, and Clinton, welfare restructuring and tax relief signed by President Clinton in 1996 and 1997, tax reduction under President George W. Bush, student loan and health legislation under President Obama, and tax revision under President Trump, which suggests a settled institutional practice rather than a series of evasions. The scope of the vehicle remained policed throughout by the Byrd rule, a Senate rule adopted in 1985 precisely to prevent the expansion of reconciliation beyond budgetary matter, under which parliamentarians advised and presiding officers ruled provisions out of measures from both parties, forcing majorities to strip policy language, accept sunsets, or abandon provisions entirely. The presidential veto survived intact as a check, and the four vetoes in the record, three by President Clinton and one by President Obama, each ended a reconciliation effort that had cleared Congress, while the Senate defeat of July 2017 showed that a majority fractured on substance gained no protection from expedited procedure.

Several common misunderstandings about the vehicle persist in public discussion, and the record corrects each of them. The first holds that reconciliation can carry any legislation a majority wishes to enact, since a reconciliation bill may contain only changes to spending, revenue, or debt limit law that comply with instructions, the Byrd rule exposes every provision to a point of order for extraneous matter in the Senate, and the Congressional Budget Act excludes Social Security from reconciliation altogether, providing that a reconciliation measure may not contain recommendations with respect to the old age, survivors, and disability insurance program. The second misunderstanding treats the budget resolution as a stage in lawmaking, when a budget resolution is a concurrent resolution adopted by both chambers for internal congressional use, never presented to the President, incapable of becoming law, and binding only as a set of instructions and allocations that later legislation must satisfy. The third misunderstanding dates the procedure to the polarized politics of the twenty first century, when the statute creating reconciliation dates from 1974 and the first use dates from 1980, and the heavy deficit reduction traffic of the 1980s preceded every later controversy. The fourth misunderstanding assumes that a majority frustrated by an adverse parliamentary ruling can set the Parliamentarian aside at no cost, since no majority in that period overruled the presiding officer on a Byrd rule point of order to retain extraneous matter in a reconciliation bill, and the single personnel episode carried a visible institutional price. In May 2001, Majority Leader Trent Lott dismissed Parliamentarian Robert Dove after reconciliation rulings that included advice limiting the number of tax reduction measures the fiscal year 2002 resolution could generate and a determination that a disaster assistance provision was extraneous, and the dismissal drew sustained criticism as a threat to the nonpartisan character of the office, with Dove having previously been dismissed in 1987 by a Democratic majority leader and restored in 1995.

A reader equipped with the record can test any proposal against the procedure without waiting for official characterizations, because three questions, asked in order, predict whether a proposal can travel by reconciliation. The first question asks whether an adopted budget resolution instructs a committee with jurisdiction over the proposal to report changes producing a stated change in spending, revenues, or the debt limit, since without instructions in a resolution adopted by both chambers no reconciliation vehicle exists and the proposal must move under regular order regardless of its content. The second question asks whether the provision changes spending, revenues, or the debt limit as its primary effect, measured in the scoring of the Congressional Budget Office and the Joint Committee on Taxation, since a provision whose budgetary effect is merely incidental to a regulatory or policy purpose fails the statutory test for inclusion, and a proposal directed at Social Security fails at the threshold because the statute excludes that program from the procedure. The third question asks whether the provision would survive the extraneous matter screen of the Byrd rule in the Senate, which examines whether the provision produces a change in outlays or revenues, whether that change is more than incidental to the nonbudgetary components of the provision, whether the provision complies with the instructions of its committee, whether the provision increases the deficit beyond the budget window, and whether the provision lies outside the jurisdiction of the reporting committee. A proposal that draws an affirmative answer on the instruction question, a primary budgetary effect on the scoring question, and a clean passage on the extraneous matter question can travel by reconciliation and requires only a simple majority in the Senate, while a proposal that fails any one of the three questions must find sixty votes, a different vehicle, or a different design.

**The reconciliation pipeline**

| Stage | Actor | Vote threshold | Time limit | Constraint that applies |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Instruction in a budget resolution | Both chambers | Simple majorities to adopt the resolution | The reporting date is set by the resolution itself | The resolution is not a law and changes no statute |
| Committee submissions | The instructed committees | Committee majorities | The reporting date named in the instruction | The net budgetary target each committee must meet |
| Packaging | The Budget Committees | Committee majority | None in the statute | Submissions are reported without any substantive revision |
| House consideration | The House, under a special rule | Simple majority | Set by the special rule | Only amendments the rule makes in order may be offered |
| Senate debate | The Senate | Simple majority for passage | Twenty hours for all debate, ten hours on a conference report | Amendments must be germane, and the motion to proceed cannot be debated |
| The vote-a-rama | The Senate | Simple majority on each amendment | No debate remains once time has expired | Amendments are unlimited in number but each must be germane and budget conforming |
| Reconciling the two chambers | A conference committee, or the two chambers exchanging amendments | Simple majorities in both chambers | Ten hours of Senate debate on a conference report | The conference report cannot be amended |
| Enactment | The President | Signature, or two thirds of both chambers to override a veto | Ten days for presidential action, Sundays excepted | The veto is the one checkpoint the procedure cannot bypass |

A reader who wants to predict whether a proposal can travel by reconciliation can apply the diagnostic that the record keeps confirming. Three questions decide almost every case. An adopted budget resolution must instruct the change, because without an instruction the expedited procedures never attach to a measure at all. The provision must change spending, revenues or the debt limit as its primary effect, because the vehicle carries budgetary law and nothing else. And the provision must survive the extraneous matter screen in the Senate, where the Parliamentarian's advice and the sixty vote waiver threshold together decide what the majority's fifty first votes are permitted to reach. A proposal that answers all three questions in its favor is a candidate for the fast track. A proposal that fails any one of them must find sixty votes, a different vehicle, or another Congress.

That diagnostic explains the shape of the statutes the procedure has produced. Deadlines cluster at the edge of the budget window because the deficit tests reach beyond it. Programs are redesigned to manufacture a budgetary effect because incidental effects fail the screen. Majorities accept half measures and expiring provisions as the price of enactment, and the resulting statutes carry the fingerprints of the rule that carried them: sunsets nobody chose on policy grounds, titles struck for want of a budgetary consequence, and omnibus texts assembled by committees working to numbers set in a document that was never a law. A deficit tool became a majority tool because the majority's problem changed. The obstacle facing a modern Senate majority is rarely the drafting of a program; it is the sixty votes standing between a program and a vote. Reconciliation removes exactly that obstacle and no other, and everything the procedure has done since 1980 follows from the precision of that removal.

For a reader preparing to work with this material, the pipeline repays structured study. A student who can reconstruct the stages from memory, name the constraint that binds at each one, and apply the three question diagnostic to a new proposal holds the core of the subject, and readers working through the record stage by stage can [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) as the stages accumulate. The examination side of the subject rewards the same discipline, since questions on the process turn on the exact points where the fast track differs from ordinary legislation, and candidates can [practice and revise US government and civics material on ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/us-government-civics-study.html) with the pipeline as a worked example before testing whether the distinctions hold under time pressure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does budget reconciliation actually work?

Reconciliation begins when both chambers adopt a budget resolution containing instructions that direct named committees to report changes in spending, revenues or the debt limit by set amounts and a set date. Each instructed committee drafts its changes and sends them to its Budget Committee, which packages the submissions into a single omnibus bill without substantive revision. The House considers the bill under a special rule, and the Senate considers it under expedited procedures: debate is capped at twenty hours, amendments must be germane, and passage takes a simple majority. After debate time expires, senators may keep offering amendments in a vote-a-rama until the chamber votes on passage. The two chambers then resolve their differences, and the President signs or vetoes the result.

### Q: Why can reconciliation avoid a filibuster?

The filibuster works by extending debate, and debate on a reconciliation bill cannot be extended. Section 310(e) of the Budget Act caps total Senate debate on the measure, including all amendments, motions and appeals, at twenty hours, equally divided between the majority and the minority. When the hours expire, debate ends by operation of the statute rather than by a cloture vote, so the sixty votes ordinarily needed to close debate are never required. The motion to proceed to the bill is likewise not debatable. A determined minority can force hundreds of amendment votes even after time expires, but it cannot prevent the final vote, and passage then requires only a simple majority of senators voting.

### Q: How many hours of debate does reconciliation allow?

Total debate on a reconciliation bill in the Senate is limited to twenty hours. The cap covers everything debatable: the bill itself, every amendment, every motion and every appeal of the chair's rulings. Inside that total, debate on a first degree amendment is limited to two hours and debate on a second degree amendment, a debatable motion or an appeal is limited to one hour, each divided equally between the sides. A conference report on a reconciliation bill receives ten hours. Time spent actually voting does not count against the allowance; the clock measures debate, not the mechanics of voting. Once the twenty hours are consumed, no further debate is in order, although amendment votes continue.

### Q: What is a vote-a-rama in reconciliation?

A vote-a-rama is the stretch of a reconciliation bill's Senate consideration that begins when the twenty hours of debate have expired. The statute limits debate but does not limit the number of amendments, so senators may continue offering amendments one after another, each disposed of by a roll call vote with no debate beyond a nominal explanation allowed by unanimous consent. Votes are typically compressed to about ten minutes and stacked back to back, sometimes running through the night. Most amendments offered in a vote-a-rama are expected to fail; their purpose is to force the other side to vote on politically difficult questions and to build a record. The session ends when no senator seeks recognition to offer another amendment, and the bill then proceeds to a passage vote.

### Q: How many reconciliation bills can pass per year?

The Budget Act sets no annual quota. The practical limit comes from the budget resolution, because reconciliation procedures attach only to instructions an adopted resolution contains, and Congress adopts at most one budget resolution for each fiscal year. In most years that means a single round of reconciliation at most, and in years when the chambers fail to agree on a resolution it means none. A single resolution can generate up to three bills, one for each subject of spending, revenues and the debt limit, although committees usually combine subjects into one or two measures. Calendar 2017 showed the outer edge of the practice: the fiscal 2017 resolution adopted in January and the fiscal 2018 resolution adopted in October each produced a reconciliation vehicle in the same calendar year.

### Q: Was reconciliation designed to cut deficits?

In conception, yes. Section 310 was drafted as the closing mechanism of the original budget process, in which a second budget resolution late in the session would set final totals and reconciliation would conform the law to them, which in the deficit conscious design of 1974 meant bringing spending down and revenues up to meet the resolution. The first uses fit the design: the acts of 1980 and 1981 harvested committee savings, and the largest reconciliation statutes of 1990 and 1993 were deficit reduction packages. The statutory text, however, requires only that committees change spending and revenue law by instructed amounts; it does not require the change to shrink the deficit. That gap between conception and text is what later majorities used when the same vehicle carried the tax reductions of 2001, 2003 and 2017.

### Q: Which major laws used reconciliation?

By the Congressional Research Service's catalogue, twenty-one reconciliation measures were enacted between 1980 and the end of 2019. The measures that reshaped the largest areas of policy include the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981, which carried the Reagan administration's spending reductions; the deficit reduction acts of 1990 and 1993; the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996, which replaced the federal cash welfare entitlement with block grants; the Balanced Budget Act and the Taxpayer Relief Act of 1997; the tax acts of 2001 and 2003; the Deficit Reduction Act of 2005; the Health Care and Education Reconciliation Act of 2010, which completed the health coverage legislation of that year; and the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017.

### Q: Who decides what qualifies for reconciliation?

Different questions have different deciders. Whether a committee's submission satisfies its instruction is judged by the Budget Committees, working from estimates by the Congressional Budget Office and the Joint Committee on Taxation. Whether material in the Senate's bill is extraneous under the Byrd rule is decided formally by the presiding officer, who rules on the advice of the Senate Parliamentarian, the chamber's nonpartisan procedural counsel; before floor consideration, the majority's Budget Committee chair submits the bill's provisions for the Parliamentarian's review in the scrub known as the Byrd bath. The Senate itself has the last word, since sixty votes can waive a point of order or sustain an appeal, but through 2019 no majority overruled the chair to rescue matter struck as extraneous.

### Q: Is a budget resolution a law?

No. A budget resolution is a concurrent resolution, adopted by the House and Senate in identical form but never presented to the President, never signed, and carrying no force of law. It binds Congress internally: its totals, allocations and points of order discipline what committees may report and what the chambers may consider, and its reconciliation instructions trigger the expedited procedures described in this article. It cannot by itself change a tax rate, a benefit formula or the debt limit, because only a bill or joint resolution presented to the President can change statute. That is precisely why reconciliation exists: the resolution states the fiscal policy, and the reconciliation bill is the separate legal instrument that carries the policy into law.

### Q: What does the Senate Parliamentarian do in reconciliation?

The Parliamentarian is the Senate's nonpartisan procedural adviser, and in reconciliation the office carries unusual weight. Before a reconciliation bill reaches the floor, the Parliamentarian reviews its provisions, and those of amendments, against the Byrd rule's tests for extraneous matter, advising which provisions a point of order would strike. During consideration, the presiding officer rules on points of order on the basis of that advice, and rulings follow the advice in the ordinary course. The Parliamentarian cannot block anything personally; the formal authority belongs to the chair, and the Senate can waive a rule or overturn a ruling with sixty votes. In practice the advice has operated as the effective boundary of the vehicle, which is why majorities consult the office during drafting, long before any vote.

### Q: What makes a provision extraneous in a reconciliation bill?

A provision is extraneous when it fails one of the Byrd rule's six tests in section 313 of the Budget Act. The tests strike matter that produces no change in spending or revenues, matter outside the instructed committee's jurisdiction, matter whose budgetary effect is merely incidental to its policy purpose, matter that raises deficits beyond the budget window, and matter recommending changes to Social Security. The test that does most of the work is the merely incidental one: a provision whose policy ambitions dwarf its fiscal effect, such as a regulatory mandate attached to a trivial spending change, is extraneous even though money moves. A sustained point of order removes the provision and leaves the rest of the bill intact, so the screen edits the text rather than killing the vehicle.

### Q: Can Social Security be changed through reconciliation?

No. Section 310(g) of the Budget Act provides that a reconciliation bill shall not contain recommendations with respect to the old age, survivors and disability insurance program, which is Social Security's core. The Byrd rule reinforces the exclusion by defining any recommendation on the program as extraneous matter subject to a point of order in the Senate. The bar is categorical rather than a drafting problem to be engineered around: benefit formulas, eligibility rules and the payroll tax structure of Social Security all sit outside the vehicle. Other retirement and health programs are not covered by the bar; Medicare, for example, has been changed repeatedly in reconciliation bills. Changes to Social Security itself must travel by ordinary legislation, exposed to the filibuster like any other bill.

### Q: What happens if a committee misses its reconciliation target?

A committee that misses its date or its number does not stop the process, but its subject suffers. The Budget Committees assemble the omnibus bill from the submissions actually received, and a committee that reports nothing leaves its part of the instruction unexecuted, so the savings or revenue it was assigned usually fall out of the measure. A committee that reports changes missing its instructed amount creates a compliance question measured on the net effect of its submission, assessed by the Budget Committee on Congressional Budget Office and Joint Committee on Taxation estimates, and a bill that breaches the resolution's totals is exposed to points of order on the floor. In practice the dates in instructions have often slipped, because the instruction's force is political before it is legal: the majority that wrote the target decides how much failure to tolerate.

### Q: Why do reconciliation bills have expiration dates?

The Byrd rule is the usual author of the dates. One of its tests treats a provision as extraneous if it increases the deficit, or reduces revenues, in years beyond the budget window the resolution covers, and waiving that point of order takes sixty votes the majority typically does not have. Drafters who want a provision to survive the screen therefore arrange for it to expire inside the window, so the years beyond it show no deficit effect. The device shaped the modern tax code's calendar: the individual rate cuts of 2001 and 2003 were written to sunset, the 2003 investment rate reductions ran on their own schedule, and the individual provisions of the 2017 tax act were set to expire at the end of 2025 while its corporate rate reduction, drafted to fit the instruction's terms, was made permanent.

### Q: What is the Byrd bath?

The Byrd bath is the informal name for the review a reconciliation bill undergoes in the Senate before floor consideration begins. Staff of the Budget Committees and the Senate Parliamentarian examine the bill provision by provision against the Byrd rule's six tests, identifying language likely to draw a point of order and be struck as extraneous. The majority's Budget Committee chair formally submits the text for the review, and minority staff press the case against provisions they want flagged. Provisions identified as vulnerable are often rewritten or dropped before the bill is ever called up, which is why the bath matters: it edits the bill in private, at drafting depth, rather than on the floor. The name plays on Senator Robert Byrd's authorship of the rule and on the scrubbing the text receives.

### Q: How many reconciliation bills can one budget resolution produce?

Up to three. Section 310 organizes instructions by subject, and a budget resolution may direct changes in spending, in revenues and in the debt limit. Senate practice treats each subject as capable of supporting its own reconciliation bill, so a single resolution can produce a spending bill, a revenue bill and a debt limit bill. Majorities usually prefer fewer vehicles: combining spending and revenue instructions into one omnibus bill concentrates the coalition's votes in a single passage fight, and the tax and spending halves of the 1997 settlement traveled as two bills only because the resolution's architects chose to separate them. The limit that binds is therefore political arithmetic, not the statute: every additional bill is another twenty hour debate and another vote-a-rama the majority must survive.

### Q: Can the President veto a reconciliation bill?

Yes. Reconciliation changes how a bill moves through Congress, not what happens when it leaves. The finished measure is presented to the President like any other bill, to be signed or vetoed within ten days, Sundays excepted, and Congress can override only by two thirds of both chambers. Four reconciliation bills completed the congressional journey and died at that checkpoint: the Balanced Budget Act of 1995 and the tax reconciliation measures of 1999 and 2000, each vetoed by President Clinton, and the health repeal act passed in 2015 and vetoed by President Obama in January 2016. No override succeeded. The list is a standing reminder that the procedure solves the Senate's internal arithmetic and nothing beyond it.

### Q: Can reconciliation be used to raise the debt limit?

Yes. The debt limit is one of the three subjects section 310 names, alongside spending and revenues, so a budget resolution may instruct the committees with jurisdiction over the statutory limit to report a change in it, and that change travels under the same expedited procedures as any other reconciliation content. In practice majorities have preferred other vehicles for the limit, which has usually been raised in freestanding bills or folded into broader budget settlements rather than carried through a reconciliation instruction. The distinction that matters is mechanical: reconciliation can carry a debt limit change because the statute says so, not because the limit is spending or revenue. Where an instruction does not reach the limit, a reconciliation bill cannot touch it at all.

### Q: Does the House use reconciliation too?

The House considers reconciliation bills under expedited arrangements too, but it does not need them in the Senate's sense. House rules already let a majority structure debate: a reconciliation bill is typically taken up under a special rule from the Rules Committee that fixes debate time, often one to three hours, and specifies which amendments, if any, may be offered, and the majority that controls the Rules Committee controls the terms. The Senate's problem is different, because its standing rules place no general limit on debate, so the statutory ceiling in section 310 supplies the limit the chamber's own rules lack. The privilege reconciliation confers is therefore decisive in the Senate and largely administrative in the House, which is why accounts of the process concentrate on the Senate floor.

### Q: How is reconciliation different from regular legislation?

Four differences decide most cases. A reconciliation bill exists only because a budget resolution instructed it, while an ordinary bill needs no one's permission to be introduced. Its content is confined to changes in spending, revenues and the debt limit, screened in the Senate by the Byrd rule, while an ordinary bill may carry any subject in Congress's power. Its Senate debate is capped at twenty hours with germane amendments only and passage by simple majority, while an ordinary bill faces unlimited debate, nongermane amendments and the sixty vote cloture threshold. And its life is tied to the fiscal calendar of the resolution that created it, while an ordinary bill may wait years for a vehicle. The trade is reach for speed: the ordinary route can carry anything and pass nothing over a determined minority; reconciliation passes quickly and carries only what fits the budget.
