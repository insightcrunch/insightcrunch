## The Verdict First: An Inherited Plot, an Invented Play

The sources of Romeo and Juliet form a family tree, and the tree settles the oldest argument about the play. Shakespeare invented almost none of the story. The secret marriage, the sleeping draught, the failed letter, the tomb, and the double death all reached him ready made, descending through Italian, French, and English versions across more than a century of retelling. What he invented was nearly everything that makes the inherited plot matter: the compression of months into days, the inwardness of two teenagers who think in poetry, a Mercutio expanded from a name in a guest list into the play's most electric voice, and a language of light, haste, and sacrament that no earlier version contains. The verdict defended across this article is therefore double, and both halves are needed. As narrative, the play is an inheritance, and pretending otherwise falsifies its history. As drama and as poetry, the play is an original work of the first order, and the tree, properly read, proves that too, because the tree shows exactly which layer of the work arrived from elsewhere and which layer could only have come from the man who wrote the play.

The distinction between the two halves is the whole discipline of source study, and it begins with vocabulary. A source is a text the playwright demonstrably used, established by verbal parallels, shared details, and shared errors that cannot be coincidence. An analogue is a text that resembles the play without proof of contact, evidence of the story's climate rather than of the playwright's reading. The family tree drawn in this article keeps the two apart at every node. Ovid stands at the root as an ancestor and analogue of genius, known to the playwright and echoed in the play, yet not the channel through which the plot arrived. Arthur Brooke stands at the top of the trunk as the proven source, the version whose lines can be matched against the play scene by scene. Between those poles the line runs through Masuccio Salernitano, who first told the tale in recognisable form, through Luigi da Porto, who gave it Verona and the lovers' names, through Matteo Bandello, who amplified it, through Pierre Boaistuau, who carried it into French and darkened its moral, and through William Painter, whose prose rendering stood on English shelves beside Brooke's poem. Each stage added something the next stage kept. The tree is not a decoration on the play's scholarship. It is the play's biography before the play existed.

The contrary idea, that the play sprang entire from one imagination, is itself a historical product with a date on it. It belongs to the centuries of bardolatry, when the plays were increasingly read in isolation from the story collections, translations, and source poems that the first audiences and the first editors took for granted, and when originality had hardened into the modern sense of creation from nothing. School and popular editions long printed the play without its ancestors, and a work read without ancestors looks unbegotten. The scholarly recovery of the sources, completed in outline by the middle of the twentieth century, did not demote the play. It restored the conditions under which the play's actual distinction can be seen, since distinction is a comparative property and comparison needs the company. The notion of total originality and the fact of deep inheritance are not two sides of an argument. They are two states of information, and the tree is what the fuller information looks like when drawn.

Quotations from the play in this article come from René Weis's Arden Third Series edition, cited by act, scene, and line, and the source texts are cited in the standard scholarly collection, Geoffrey Bullough's Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare, whose first volume prints the Italian, French, and English versions in full or in translation. Dates follow the consensus of that scholarship. Where scholars disagree, the disagreement is named and adjudicated, because a family tree is an argument about descent, and descent is proved, not asserted.

## The Sources of Romeo and Juliet as a Family Tree

A family tree is the right model for this material because the versions behave like generations. Each retelling inherits the plot's skeleton from a parent text, modifies it under the pressure of a new language, a new form, and a new audience, and passes the modified inheritance onward. The modifications are datable, attributable, and cumulative, which is what makes the metaphor more than a metaphor. When the heroine's age falls from eighteen in da Porto to sixteen in Brooke to not yet fourteen in the play, the tree records a direction of travel, a steady intensification that reaches its limit in Shakespeare's hands. When the feuding surnames appear in Dante two centuries before the lovers do, the tree records that the feud is older than the love story it eventually frames. When Brooke's title page credits Bandello while the poem's lines follow Boaistuau, the tree records a concealed parentage that only textual comparison can expose. Read as a tree, the tradition stops being a list of books and becomes a single long act of storytelling, carried out by many hands in four languages, at the end of which stands a stage play that outgrew every ancestor.

A working tree of this kind rewards keeping, because source study is cumulative: each edition, facsimile, and parallel passage found later needs its place in the descent. The sections that follow take the tree node by node, from the ancient root to the crown, and each section asks the same two questions of its stage. What did this version receive, and what did it add that the finished play still carries?

The table repays a particular way of reading. Read down the final column and the tradition's creative history stands in one list: a pattern, a pair of names, an engine, a city, a set of voices, a doctrine, a poem, a prose twin, a play. No column of the table is headed invention, because invention, in the modern sense of making from nothing, is the one entry no node can claim, not even the crown. Read across any row and the economics of transmission appear: each maker paid for the inheritance by altering it, and the alterations cluster where the new maker's own world pressed hardest, in da Porto's Verona, in Boaistuau's doctrine, in Brooke's preface, in the play's calendar. The tree is finally an argument about where creativity lives in a tradition. It lives at the joints, in the small set of decisions each reteller made about matter that arrived already shaped, and the play's distinction is that its joint decisions were the boldest and its execution the most complete.

## The Root: Ovid's Pyramus and Thisbe and the Shape of the Ending

Ovid's tale of Pyramus and Thisbe, told in the fourth book of the Metamorphoses, is the oldest surviving telling of the pattern the play inherits: two young lovers in Babylon, forbidden by their parents to marry, whispering through a crack in the wall between their houses, arranging a night meeting at the tomb of Ninus outside the city, and dying there in sequence through a misunderstanding. Thisbe arrives first and flees a lioness, dropping her veil. Pyramus finds the veil bloodied by the lioness's jaws, concludes that Thisbe is dead, and kills himself with his sword beneath the mulberry tree. Thisbe returns, finds him dying, and takes the same sword. The mulberry's fruit, white before, turns dark with the blood, and the gods preserve the colour as a memorial. Every structural element of the pattern is present in embryo: parental prohibition, a secret channel of communication, a trysting place among tombs, an error about a death, and a double suicide in which the second lover dies by the first lover's weapon.

The kinship with the play is unmistakable, and the play itself acknowledges the ancestor. Shakespeare dramatised the same tale, in the same years, as the rude mechanicals' interlude in A Midsummer Night's Dream, where Pyramus and Thisbe are played for comedy with the wall, the lion, and the double death all present, drawn from Arthur Golding's English Metamorphoses of 1567. A playwright who could stage the Ovidian pattern as farce in one play and as tragedy in another understood exactly what the pattern was and how much of its power lay in the timing of the error. In Romeo and Juliet the error is rebuilt with machinery the Italian versions supplied, a sleeping draught rather than a lioness, a detained letter rather than a dropped veil, but the geometry of the ending is Ovid's: a lover finds a beloved apparently dead in a place of tombs, acts on the appearance, and is then joined in real death by the beloved, who wakes or returns too late. The full study of [Ovid's Pyramus and Thisbe as Ancestor](/2025/11/07/ovid-pyramus-and-thisbe-source/) traces that geometry scene by scene; within the tree, the point is classification. Ovid is the pattern's author, not the plot's courier.

### What exactly did Shakespeare take from Pyramus and Thisbe?

The shape of the ending, and little of the story that leads to it. From Ovid came the forbidden lovers, the secret meeting among tombs, the fatal misreading of a sign, and the double death by one weapon. The feud, the marriage, the draught, and the letter all entered far later, in Italy.

The distinction matters because it disciplines every claim made further down the tree. If Ovid were treated as the play's source, the Italian centuries would shrink to a footnote, and the genuinely interesting question, how a Babylonian myth became a Veronese novella and then an English stage play, would dissolve. Treated as the root, Ovid explains the pattern's durability without stealing the credit for its transmission. The tale type was available across Europe, in classical texts, in sermon exempla, and in the common stock of stories about lovers divided by family hatred. What Europe did not yet have, before the Italian fifteenth century, was this story: two named lovers, in a named city, married in secret by a friar, divided by exile, and reunited only in a tomb. The root gave the tree its shape. The trunk, when it came, would be Italian.

The root's channel into England deserves its sentence in the record, because it shows the playwright drinking from the classical line directly while the plot arrived by the Italian road. Arthur Golding's translation of the Metamorphoses, completed in 1567, was the period's Ovid, the version in which Shakespeare and his school generation read the poem, and its Pyramus and Thisbe is brisk, moralised, and vivid. The communication device at the tale's centre, the crack in the wall through which the Babylonian lovers whisper, is the ancestor of every mediated wooing in the later tradition: the orchard wall Romeo scales, the Nurse who carries words between the houses, the letter that must cross a quarantined road. The play keeps rebuilding Ovid's wall in new materials, a barrier with a narrow channel through it, and the channel's failure is always the tragedy. That deep pattern, barrier, channel, failure, is the root's true bequest, older than any plot the novelle assembled, and it is one of the few inheritances the play took from the classical line at first hand.

## The Names Before the Lovers: Dante's Montecchi and Cappelletti

Two centuries before any lover bore them, the play's family names were already famous, and already ruined. In the sixth canto of the Purgatorio, Dante turns from the souls crowding round him to rebuke an Italy torn by faction, and his catalogue of feuding houses reaches Verona: "Vieni a veder Montecchi e Cappelletti, / Monaldi e Filippeschi, uom sanza cura" (Purgatorio 6.106-107), come and see the Montecchi and the Cappelletti, the Monaldi and the Filippeschi, careless man. The lines go on to sort the houses by their fortunes, some already fallen and some fearing the fall, and the pairing of Montecchi with Cappelletti does specific work: these are the exemplary names for a city consuming itself. Dante knew the families as powers in the Guelph and Ghibelline struggles of the previous century. The Montecchi were a Veronese family of the Ghibelline party; the Cappelletti were based at Cremona and aligned with the Guelphs; later chroniclers and commentators fused them into a single Veronese feud, and Dante's couplet, memorised wherever Italian was read, did more than any chronicle to weld the two names together as a proverb for hereditary hatred.

The tree records this node with care because its contribution is unlike any other. Dante handed on no plot, no characters, and no lovers. He handed on a pair of surnames carrying a charge of historical doom, available to any later writer who needed a feud with a pedigree. When da Porto came to set his tale in Verona, he reached for the two most resonant names the city offered and gave one to each household. The play's Prologue therefore opens onto ground Dante prepared: "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene, / From ancient grudge break to new mutiny, / Where civil blood makes civil hands unclean" (Prologue, 1-4). Ancient is the operative word. The grudge felt ancient to an audience because, in the names themselves, it was: the households had been shorthand for civil blood for nearly three centuries before the lovers were joined to them. The separate study of [Dante's Montecchi and Cappelletti](/2025/11/28/dante-montecchi-cappelletti-feud/) follows the names through the chronicles; in the tree they stand as proof that a story can inherit its materials piecemeal, a pattern from Ovid, a proverb from Dante, and wait centuries for the teller who assembles them.

There is a further lesson in the node. Because the names predate the lovers, the feud in the finished play has a weight that no single telling invented and no single telling can explain. Later writers did not need to motivate the hatred at length, because the surnames arrived pre-loaded; da Porto spends little time justifying the enmity, Brooke still less, and the play, most economically of all, never explains the grudge's origin. Critics who fault the play for an unmotivated feud are measuring a gap the tradition never treated as a gap. The tree shows why: the feud was not a plot device in search of a cause. It was a historical commonplace in search of a story, and the story, once found, inherited the commonplace's authority entire.

The chronology of the node tightens the tree in a way worth marking. Da Porto placed his lovers in the Verona of Bartolomeo della Scala, who ruled the city in the first years of the fourteenth century, the very decades in which Dante wrote and in which the Montecchi and Cappelletti of the chronicles had their historical being. The setting is not decorative antiquarianism. By anchoring the tale in Dante's Verona, da Porto claimed for his fiction the documentary dignity of the age that had made the surnames proverbial, and the claim held: later tellers repeat the early fourteenth century colouring without examining it, and the play, which names no date, nonetheless breathes the air of a feud already old when its oldest speakers were young. The tree's second node thus does double duty. It supplies the names, and it supplies the century in which the names make sense.

## The First True Telling: Masuccio's Mariotto and Giannozza

The tale itself, as a tale, begins in Siena in the 1470s. Masuccio Salernitano, a Neapolitan courtier and man of letters, included in his collection Il Novellino, printed at Naples in 1476, a story of Mariotto Mignanelli and Giannozza Saraceni, two young Sienese lovers whose secret marriage sets the machinery of the whole tradition running. Mariotto, crossed in his love, is married to Giannozza in secret by a friar of the Augustinian order, the first appearance in the line of the two elements that define the plot's middle: a clandestine wedding performed by a churchman, and the churchman himself as the lovers' active ally. When Mariotto kills a citizen in a street brawl and flees into exile at Alexandria, the marriage is trapped between a husband who cannot return and a family pressing Giannozza toward a second match. The friar supplies the escape that every later version keeps: a draught that will make Giannozza sleep as if dead for three days, so that she can be taken from the tomb and sent to her husband. The plan fails at the point where every later plan fails. Word of her death travels; word of her survival does not. Mariotto, hearing in Alexandria that Giannozza is dead, returns to Siena and is seized at the tomb he came to open, and is beheaded as a criminal. Giannozza, restored and learning what her rescue cost, dies of grief in a convent.

Set against the tree, Masuccio's contribution is precise and large. He invented, or first recorded, the plot's signature machinery: the friar as marriage broker and pharmacist, the marriage kept secret from the families, the exile that follows a killing, the potion that counterfeits death, and the message that miscarries. Remove those elements and no Romeo and Juliet exists in any version. Yet the tale is not the play's story in Italian dress, and the differences mark the distance the tradition still had to travel. Siena is not Verona. The lovers' families are not feuding houses; the obstacle is Giannozza's family and circumstance, not a hereditary war between surnames. There is no ball, no balcony, no duel with a kinsman of the bride, no second suitor pressing a rival claim in quite the later form, and the deaths are divided between a scaffold and a convent rather than gathered into one tomb and one hour. Masuccio built the engine. The engine still needed a city, a feud, and names.

### Why is the first true telling set in Siena and not Verona?

Because Masuccio wrote out of his own world, the courtly and civic Italy he knew, and set his tale among Sienese families of his own region and class. Verona enters the story only two generations later, when da Porto, a soldier of the Venetian terra firma, claimed the tale for the city whose feuding names Dante had made famous.

Masuccio also set the tradition's tone in one respect that later tellers reversed. His friar is a convenience of the plot, his lovers are figures in an exemplum about desire and fortune, and the telling keeps the brisk, external manner of the novella at its most economical. Nothing in the Siena tale lingers inside a lover's mind. The long speeches, the contested interpretations, the sense that the heroine is the most intelligent person in her city: those lie far ahead, through da Porto's deepening, Bandello's rhetoric, and the English versions' expansions. The tree's value is that it lets each quality be dated. Interiority is not a property of the story. It is a deposit laid down at particular nodes, and the largest deposit of all was laid down at the last one.

Masuccio's manner of telling also entered the inheritance, in a form later stages would soften. Il Novellino is a book with a temper: its fifty tales are framed by their author's addresses and closed by his own moralising codas, in which vice is named and scourged in the teller's person. The Mariotto tale sits inside that temper. Its ending, an execution and a death of grief in a convent, is offered as instruction, and the lovers' sufferings carry the chill of an example rather than the warmth of a celebration. The tree records the temper because the tradition's long argument about the lovers, whether they are warnings or heroes, begins here and not with Boaistuau: the French moralist and Brooke after him inherited a posture the first teller had already struck. The play's gentleness toward its lovers, its refusal to scourge them even in the Chorus's summary, is the end of a century long softening, and the softening is legible only because the first node's hardness survives to measure it.

## The Trunk: Luigi da Porto Sets the Story in Verona

Luigi da Porto of Vicenza (1485-1529), a soldier in the service of Venice, gave the story the form in which the world knows it. His Historia novellamente ritrovata di due nobili amanti, the newly recovered history of two noble lovers, was composed about 1524 and printed at Venice in 1530, after his death. Da Porto had been grievously wounded in the Friuli campaigns of 1511 and left partly paralysed, and the tale carries a soldier's frame: it is told to him, he writes, by Pellegrino, an archer from Verona serving in his company, during a march, to shorten the road. Whether the frame is fact or the conventional found-history gesture of the period, da Porto insists on the tale's truth and on its Veronese setting, placing it in the days when Bartolomeo della Scala ruled the city, the Verona of Dante's generation. Onto Masuccio's Sienese engine he grafted the elements that turned a novella about a secret marriage into Romeo and Juliet.

The grafts can be enumerated, and the tree exists to enumerate them. Da Porto set the tale in Verona. He named the lovers Romeo Montecchio and Giulietta Cappelletti, taking the surnames from Dante's couplet and thereby importing the proverb of the ancient feud as the story's ground bass. He made the feud itself the obstacle: the Montecchi and Cappelletti of his Verona are divided houses, and the lovers' secret is dangerous because of who they are, not merely because their parents have other plans. He kept Masuccio's friar, now the Franciscan Fra Lorenzo, and kept the draught and the miscarried letter, sharpening the failure into the tradition's cruellest mechanism: the friar sent to Mantua with word that Giulietta lives is detained because a house he has entered is suspected of plague, and the letter never moves. He gave Romeo exile at Mantua after the killing of Giulietta's cousin Tebaldo, and he ended the tale in the Cappelletti tomb, where Romeo, having taken poison, lives long enough for Giulietta to wake and speak with him before he dies, after which she dies by holding her breath, refusing a world emptied of him. He supplied minor figures the later tradition would seize on: a courtier named Marcuccio, noted for hands cold as ice, standing in the far ancestry of Mercutio, and a Count of Lodrone as the husband chosen for Giulietta, the first form of Paris. Even the heroine's age is da Porto's datum: his Giulietta is eighteen, and the descent of her age down the tree, eighteen to sixteen to thirteen, is one of the tradition's clearest directional lines.

### Why does da Porto insist that his story is true?

Because a found history carried an authority an admitted fiction did not. By crediting the tale to a Veronese archer in his own company, and by anchoring it in the Verona of Bartolomeo della Scala, da Porto gave his novella the status of recovered fact, and the claim of truth became part of the inheritance every later teller repeated.

The insistence mattered to the tree's growth. A true story invites retelling as chronicle rather than invention, and each successor could present the work as transmission, polishing a record rather than fabricating a fable. The claim was almost certainly a literary device, since no Veronese record holds these lovers, yet it fixed the tale's register: from da Porto onward, the story travels under the colours of history, and the play inherits the colouring in its own way, naming its city, its ruling house, and its families with a chronicler's confidence while inventing freely inside the frame. The separate study of [Luigi da Porto and the Birth of 'Giulietta'](/2025/10/03/luigi-da-porto-giulietta-origin/) examines the soldier, his wound, and his own thwarted love as the tale's hidden springs; in the tree, da Porto's station is structural. Before him, the tradition holds a pattern and an engine in separate hands. After him, it holds Romeo and Juliet.

One more of da Porto's choices deserves its place in the record, because the play reverses it and the reversal is famous. In the Italian trunk, the heroine wakes before the hero dies. The lovers speak in the tomb; Romeo's death happens in her arms and with her knowledge, and her own death follows as a vow kept rather than a shock received. The scene is pathetic, but its pathos is shared between two conscious people dividing a farewell. Every English version down to the play inherits some form of the shared farewell, and the play's decision to withhold it, to let Juliet wake minutes too late and find the farewell already spent, is the single most consequential alteration at the tree's crown. It belongs to the crown section below; here it is enough to mark that the trunk's ending and the play's ending differ at precisely the joint where the play's originality bites hardest, and that the difference can only be seen against the tree.

The man behind the trunk invites one paragraph of the map's attention, because his circumstances are the tradition's first piece of biography. Da Porto was a gentleman soldier of Vicenza whose career ended in the wars of the League of Cambrai, wounded in 1511 and left with a body that never wholly recovered; his surviving letters, many addressed to Lucina Savorgnan, record a love pursued across the obstacles of family and fortune and never fulfilled. Readers of the letters have long set them beside the novella, and the parallel is suggestive rather than probative: a maimed soldier, a divided world of great houses, a love conducted under prohibition. Source study files the biography as context, not as evidence, since the tale's descent is proved by its text and not by its author's heart. Yet the context explains a quality in the telling that the map can register. Da Porto's version is the first to linger, to give the lovers' interviews room and the feud a civic density, and the lingering has the feel of a man writing from inside the material. The trunk is where the story stops being an exemplum and starts being a place.

## The Great Expansion: Bandello Amplifies the Tale

Matteo Bandello (1485-1562), friar turned courtier and the most prolific novelliere of his century, retold the tale in the second part of his Novelle, printed at Lucca in 1554, where it stands as the ninth novella of that part. Bandello knew da Porto's version and said, in the manner of his collection, that he had the story from a living teller; the scholarly verdict, resting on close parallel, is that his page descends from da Porto's, enlarged by a professional storyteller's instincts. The plot he passes on is da Porto's plot, beat for beat: Verona, the feud, the secret marriage to Fra Lorenzo, the killing of Tebaldo, the exile, the draught, the quarantined messenger, the tomb. What Bandello changes is the tale's scale and temperature. His novella is far longer than da Porto's, and the length is spent on the things the stage would later need most: speeches. Where da Porto summarises an interview, Bandello writes it out, giving the lovers, the friar, and the parents extended rhetorical performances, laments, reproaches, and pleas, so that the story moves from chronicle toward drama while still wearing prose.

The expansion has one consequence the tree must single out, because the play's comic and emotional architecture depends on it. Bandello raises the Nurse from the household furniture of the earlier tellings to a major figure: Giulietta's confidante, go-between, and counsellor, a woman with a voice, a history, and a function in nearly every turn of the intrigue. In da Porto she is present but slight; in Bandello she becomes the heroine's second interlocutor, and the line from Bandello's nurse to the play's Nurse, with her reminiscence, her bawdry, her errands, and her catastrophic counsel in Act 3, is one of the straightest descents in the whole tree. Bandello likewise thickens the household around the lovers, servants, kinsmen, the Count Paris of Lodrone given fuller courtship, so that the tale's Verona gains the social density a stage play requires. The tree's lesson at this node is about the economics of transmission: Bandello is the least original teller in the line and among the most consequential, because amplification is itself a bequest. Shakespeare never read Bandello's page directly, on the standard verdict, yet a share of what feels Shakespearean in the play's peopled world entered the tradition at Bandello's desk.

### What did Bandello most enlarge in the tale?

The speaking parts. Bandello stretched da Porto's narrative with full-scale speeches for the lovers, the friar, and the parents, and he built the Nurse into a principal figure, the heroine's confidante and messenger. The plot he inherited he left nearly untouched; the voices he amplified are his bequest to the stage.

Bandello also bequeathed the tale its European currency. The Novelle were the Italian fiction of the age most read abroad, quarried by translators in France and England for ready-made plots, and it was Bandello's version, not da Porto's, that crossed the Alps. The tree therefore passes through Bandello by necessity of transmission as much as by content: he is the node at which an Italian novella becomes a European property. A family tree that skipped him would misrepresent the descent, since the French and English versions both descend from his page. His novella is the clearest proof in the whole line that descent is a matter of documented contact, not of merit. The best version is not always the parent. The read version is.

The form of Bandello's book helped make it the read version. Each novella in the Novelle carries a dedicatory letter to a named patron or patroness, framing the tale as a gift within a courtly exchange and claiming, in the period's convention, an occasion on which the story was told aloud in company. The Romeo and Juliet novella travels inside that machinery of dedication and occasion, which lent the tale a social passport: it arrived in France and England not as raw Italian matter but as a finished article of European court culture, prefaced, presented, and vouched for. Boaistuau kept the presenting habit while changing its moral; Brooke kept it in his address to the reader; and the habit's last descendant is the play's Chorus, a presenter standing before the audience with the tale already in hand. The tree's lines run through paratext as well as plot, and the dedicatory frame is one of its quietest continuities.

## The French Graft: Boaistuau Moralizes the Story

Pierre Boaistuau published his Histoires tragiques at Paris in 1559, six tales Englished from no one and drawn, as his title declares, from the Italian of Bandello. The Romeo and Juliet story stands third in the collection, and Boaistuau's retelling is the hinge of the whole tree, the point at which the tale changes language, nation, and moral temperature together. His plot follows Bandello closely, since he is translating, but translation in his hands is editorial work. He frames the tale as a warning. His lovers are exhibited as examples of disobedience and ungoverned desire, their deaths a judgement the reader is meant to carry away, and the narrative voice interrupts, comments, and moralises where Bandello's had been content to amplify. Details darken in his keeping: the friar's conduct is handled with a censorious edge that fits a France entering its wars of religion, and the ending gathers a sterner apparatus of punishment and example around the tomb. The story that Italy told as a pathetic history of two noble lovers becomes, in French, a tragic history with a doctrine attached.

The graft matters to the play for two reasons, one negative and one decisive. The negative reason is that Shakespeare's play descends from the moralised French line, not from the warmer Italian one, so the play's refusal of Boaistuau's doctrine is a deliberate unmooring from its nearest ancestors, visible only against the tree. The decisive reason is documentary. Brooke's English poem, the play's direct parent, was made from Boaistuau's French, not from Bandello's Italian, and the proof is the classic proof of descent in textual scholarship: shared innovations and shared errors. Where Boaistuau departs from Bandello, adding a detail, shifting a name, or bending a passage, Brooke departs with him; where Boaistuau's French misleads or compresses, the English poem inherits the bend. Two texts that merely resemble a common original diverge independently. Two texts that share the same departures descend one from the other. On that evidence the tree's line runs Bandello to Boaistuau to Brooke, whatever title pages claim.

### Why does Brooke's title page credit Bandello when the poem follows Boaistuau?

Because the Italian name carried the prestige. Brooke's title announces the tale as written first in Italian by Bandell and Englished by the translator, naming the famous origin rather than the French intermediary actually open on the desk. The credit is marketing; the descent, proved by shared departures, runs through Boaistuau.

Boaistuau's node carries the tree's most useful warning about itself. A family tree drawn from title pages, prefaces, and authors' claims would place Brooke under Bandello, da Porto under a Veronese archer's oral tale, and the play under no one, since its author never named a source at all. A tree drawn from the texts, from the distribution of details and errors, gives a different and defensible shape. Source study trusts the second kind of evidence, and the sections on the English versions show what the second kind yields when the comparison is made line by line.

The moralising graft shows in small judicial details that travel with it down the line. In the French telling and in Brooke's poem after it, the machinery of punishment extends beyond the lovers: the apothecary who sold Romeo the poison is seized and hanged for the sale, an act of public tidying that closes the account the law had opened. The play drops the hanging entire. Its apothecary walks out of Act 5 unpunished by any court, remembered only by Romeo's gold and his own poverty, and the Prince's closing sentences fall on the fathers, not on the instruments. A detail so small is a perfect tracer for the tree's argument. Where the moral frame is strong, the story punishes down the chain of agents; where the play has struck the frame away, blame gathers at the top, on the houses whose hatred stocked the apothecary's market. The graft's presence in two versions and its absence at the crown measure, in miniature, what the crown section argues at large.

## The English Poem: Arthur Brooke's Romeus and Juliet

The tale reached England in verse. Arthur Brooke's The Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet was printed by Richard Tottel at London in 1562, a poem of 3,020 lines in poulter's measure, the alternating twelve and fourteen syllable lines that were the period's workhorse for long narrative. Brooke, who drowned in 1563 on the expedition to Newhaven, was a young translator working, as the textual evidence settles, from Boaistuau's French while crediting Bandello on the title page. His poem is the play's direct parent, the one text in the tree whose descent into Shakespeare's work can be demonstrated at the level of phrasing, sequence, and detail across the whole length of both works. Where the play and the poem are laid side by side, the correspondence is not a matter of a shared story. It is a matter of the same story told in the same order with the same furniture: the feast at the Capulets', the meeting, the exchange of vows, the marriage, the duel, the exile, the forced match, the draught, the tomb. The separate study of [Arthur Brooke's 'Romeus and Juliet' (1562)](/2025/09/19/arthur-brooke-romeus-and-juliet-1562/) reads the poem entire; the tree needs its parentage, its additions, and its frame.

Brooke's additions are the tradition's last major deposit before the play. He expands the Nurse further, giving her the garrulous, reminiscent manner that Bandello had sketched and the play would immortalise. He keeps the heroine at sixteen, midway down the age line from da Porto's eighteen toward the play's thirteen. He stretches the action across roughly nine months, where the Italian tellings had allowed a slower calendar still, and the play's answering compression into less than a week is measurable only against his expanse. He names, in a list of guests at the feast, a courtier Mercutio whose hands are cold as ice, Brooke's rendering of da Porto's Marcuccio, a name and a physical trait and nothing more: the play's Mercutio, with the Queen Mab speech, the puns, the duel, and the curse, is what Shakespeare built on that full stop of a mention. And Brooke frames the whole with a prose address to the reader that reads the tale as Boaistuau taught England to read it, as a warning. His lovers, in his own phrase, enthralled themselves to "unhonest desire", neglecting parents and friends, and their end is exhibited so that readers will govern their own affections better. The poem delights in the story and condemns it in the same breath, and the condemnation is not hypocrisy but the period's licence: a tragic history was permitted its pleasures because its preface paid the moral tax.

The tree must also record a ghost at this node. In his address to the reader, Brooke remarked that he had seen the same argument set forth on stage. The sentence has nourished a century of speculation about a lost English Romeo and Juliet play predating the poem, a phantom branch between Boaistuau and the English tradition. The evidence is a single remark, capable of innocent explanations, a school performance, a court entertainment, a mistranslation of his French, and no text, record, or second witness has ever surfaced. The scholarly handling is a model of the discipline this article follows: the remark is noted, the possibility is kept open at the level of a dotted line, and no weight is hung on it. Trees are drawn from witnesses. A branch attested by one ambiguous sentence is drawn in pencil, and the play's documented descent needs no phantom, since Brooke's poem alone accounts for the play's matter.

The poem's texture deserves a word, because texture explains what the playwright quarried and what he refused. Poulter's measure, the jog trot of twelve and fourteen syllable lines, is a carrier's metre: it hauls narrative steadily and unglamorously, and Brooke's 3,020 lines move at its pace, dilating where the novella dilates, on the Nurse's talk, on Romeus's long deliberations, on the slow calendar of courtship and exile. The play's relation to that texture is one of systematic refusal. Where the poem ambles, the play gallops; where the poem explains, the play enacts; where the poem's lovers narrate their feelings to the reader, the play's lovers think aloud in soliloquy and duet. Yet the quarrying is real beneath the refusal. Scene order, the placing of the feast, the sequence of the tomb, the very distribution of emphasis across the five acts, follow the poem's map. Shakespeare read Brooke the way a dramatist reads, for the load bearing structure, and then rebuilt every room. The tree's Brooke node is thus the crown's true workshop: the place where the difference between inheriting a story and writing a play can be measured line by line, and measured nowhere else so exactly.

## The Prose Twin: William Painter and the Question of a Second Parent

Five years after Brooke's poem, the tale appeared in English again, in prose. William Painter's Palace of Pleasure, the bestselling anthology of classical and Italian stories that supplied the Elizabethan stage with plots for a generation, printed The Goodly History of the True and Constant Love of Rhomeo and Julietta in its second tome of 1567. Painter, like Brooke, worked from Boaistuau's French, and his version is the statelier twin: a full prose rendering, moral frame intact, addressed to readers of the anthology rather than to the buyers of a single poem. The question the node poses is the liveliest open question in the play's source study. Did Shakespeare, whose main source was plainly Brooke, also consult Painter? The tree's shape depends on the answer: a single trunk with Brooke alone, or a trunk with a graft, two English renderings feeding one play.

Geoffrey Bullough, whose Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare remains the foundation collection, treats the matter with characteristic discipline. Brooke's poem, he shows, accounts for the play entire: its sequence, its details, its very phrasing recur in the play with a density no other version matches. The parallels with Painter, where they have been alleged, are few, and each can be explained by the common descent of both English versions from Boaistuau: two translators of the same French text will sometimes light on the same English solution without either having seen the other's page. On Bullough's reading, Painter is a side branch, flourishing in its own right, feeding the reading public but not the play. Jill Levenson's Oxford edition, by contrast, leaves the door wider open, allowing that a dramatist ransacking the tale in the mid 1590s, with the Palace of Pleasure a standard quarry for playwrights, may well have had both English versions in view, and that a scatter of details in the play sits at least as comfortably with the prose as with the poem.

### What does Painter give the story that Brooke does not?

A prose life in the period's most influential story collection. Painter's rendering carried the tale to the common reader and the professional playwright in a form built for plundering, and its presence in the Palace of Pleasure made the story part of the standard furniture of the English imagination.

The adjudication this article defends follows the evidence's weight rather than its possibilities. Brooke is the trunk: the density of verbal and structural parallel is of a different order from anything Painter offers, and no reading of the play requires Painter to explain it. Painter is best entered on the tree as a consulted possibility, a graft drawn in lighter ink, probable as background reading and unprovable as a working source. That verdict is not a hedge. It is what the distinction between source and analogue was invented to say, applied within a single language and a single decade. The tree loses nothing by the lighter ink, because its main line, French graft to English poem to stage play, stands on parallels that no scholar of any party disputes.

Painter himself is worth a sentence of the map's attention, because his book's design explains its influence. He was a clerk of the ordnance, a civil servant translating in the margins of an official life, and the Palace of Pleasure was built as a storehouse: brief moral prefaces, tales sorted for the reader's profit and delight, and a prose style of deliberate elevation that made Italian matter feel at home in English. His Rhomeo and Julietta keeps Boaistuau's moralising frame and delivers the tale entire, and for thirty years it was the version a prose reader, a schoolmaster, or a plot hunting dramatist was likeliest to meet. The tree records him as the tradition's great distributor in England. Distribution is not authorship, and the distinction holds in his case as firmly as in Bandello's.

## The Crown: What Shakespeare Did With the Inheritance

The play was written about 1595, printed first in the short quarto of 1597 and then, in the fuller and more authoritative text, in 1599. By the tree's arithmetic its author stood at the end of a line of at least eight tellings across sixteen centuries, and he worked, as the parallels prove, with Brooke's poem open before him. What he did with the inheritance can be stated at map depth here, since the detailed audit of his changes belongs to the study of the poem and the play side by side. He compressed. Brooke's nine months become a span of days, Sunday to Thursday, with marriages, duels, exiles, and deaths crowded into a single breathless week, and the compression converts a story about fortune's slow grinding into a story about speed itself. He lowered the heroine's age to the edge of fourteen: "She hath not seen the change of fourteen years" (1.2.9), a change that sharpens every pressure on her, from her father's bargaining to the friar's risk. He enlarged Mercutio from a name and a pair of cold hands into the play's presiding wit, and invented for him the Queen Mab speech, which has no counterpart anywhere in the tree. He built the lovers' first exchange as a shared sonnet, a form folded into dialogue that no source attempts. And he struck off the moral frame his line had carried since Boaistuau: where Brooke's preface condemns the lovers, the play's Chorus merely foretells them, "A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life" (Prologue, 6), and leaves judgement to the event.

The crown's sharpest alteration stands at the tomb, and the tree is needed to see it. In da Porto, and in every telling after his, the heroine wakes while Romeo still lives, and the two divide a farewell between them. The play refuses the farewell. Romeo dies believing Juliet dead, in a tomb where the waking is timed minutes behind the poison, and Juliet wakes to a husband already cold, in silence, with the watch approaching. Her death then takes the form no earlier heroine's took: she finds his dagger and uses it, "O happy dagger! / This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" (5.3.169-170), where da Porto's Giulietta had died by a held breath after long speech. The alteration costs the audience the tradition's most comfortable scene and buys, in exchange, the ending the world remembers, in which the catastrophe is not shared but sequential, each lover dying of the same error at a different minute. The Prince's closing sentence has become the tradition's epitaph, and it is spoken over a stage the tree filled: "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309-310). A story of more woe: the line is almost a source note, since the woe had been accumulating, telling by telling, for a hundred and twenty years.

The crown's alterations include one addition of a whole death, and it shows the dramatist working as the tradition never had. In da Porto, in Bandello, and in Brooke, the chosen husband, the Count of Lodrone who becomes Paris, survives the catastrophe; he is a pressure on the heroine, not a casualty. The play sends Paris to the tomb with flowers on the wedding night that became a funeral, sets him against Romeo in the dark, and kills him there, so that the tomb gathers three young men dead of the feud's logic and Paris dies asking to be laid beside Juliet. The addition is economical and pitiless: it completes the pattern by which every suitor the feud produced is destroyed by the feud's own instruments, and it gives the final scene a crescendo of discovery, Paris, then Romeo, then Juliet, that no source attempts. Against the tree, the invention is unmistakable, because the tree holds the version in which Paris lives. That is the general service the descent performs for criticism. It certifies the additions.

Stated so, the crown's work looks like a list of improvements, and the tree forbids that complacency in one direction as much as it forbids the myth of total invention in the other. Every improvement is an alteration of received material, visible only because the material survives for comparison. The compression is legible against Brooke's nine months. The age is legible against da Porto's eighteen. Mercutio is legible against a guest list. Even the language, the play's most unborrowable glory, works by transforming inherited occasions: the balcony scene exists in the sources as a window interview, and the play keeps the window, the night, and the danger, and fills them with the imagery of sun, moon, stars, and saints that no source contains. Inheritance and invention are not rivals in this work. They are the warp and the weft, and the tree's business is to keep them distinguishable.

## The Scholars Who Drew the Tree

The tree itself is a scholarly construction, and its principal architect deserves his station in it. Geoffrey Bullough's Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare, whose first volume appeared in 1957, gathered the whole line between covers: da Porto and Bandello in translation, Boaistuau, Brooke entire, Painter, and the analogues, each with an introduction weighing its claim on the play. Before Bullough, the sources were a connoisseur's knowledge, scattered in editions and journals. After Bullough, they were a documented descent that any reader could test, and the testing has refined rather than overturned his shape: Brooke as the main source, Painter as a possibility, the Italian versions as ancestors known through intermediaries. Kenneth Muir's Shakespeare's Sources, published in the same year, reached a matching configuration by an independent route, and the convergence of the two books fixed the tree's outline for the scholarship that followed. The editors of the play, Jill Levenson in the Oxford edition of 2000 and René Weis in the Arden Third Series edition of 2012, carry the same outline into their introductions, adjusting the weight given to Painter and to the analogues at the margins.

One older branch of speculation shows the discipline at work by its pruning. Nineteenth and early twentieth century scholarship occasionally proposed that Shakespeare read Bandello, or even da Porto, in the Italian, giving the tree a direct line from Italy to London that bypassed France and the English translators. The proposal dies on the evidence of the parallels. Where the play matches the tradition at the level of wording and detail, it matches the French and English versions, including their departures from the Italian; where the Italian versions stand alone, the play does not follow them. A reader of Bandello would have inherited Bandello's readings. The play inherits Boaistuau's, at one remove through Brooke. The verdict of the modern scholarship, Bullough's, Muir's, and the editors', is therefore firm: the tree has no Italian shortcut, and its long way round, through Paris and through Tottel's printing shop, is the documented road.

The working method behind that verdict deserves a plain description, because the tree is only as good as the labour it summarises. A source scholar lays the play and a candidate version side by side and hunts for the unglamorous evidence: a shared phrase where the tradition offered many, a detail present in two texts and absent from the line between them, an error faithfully copied. The hunt is slow, and its results are cumulative in the manner of a legal case rather than a lightning insight. Bullough's volume is great not for a theory but for the completeness of its exhibits, hundreds of pages of the versions themselves, so arranged that the reader can audit the parallels. That auditability is the tree's real foundation. Every branch drawn in this article can be checked against printed texts that Bullough put into general circulation, and a claim that cannot survive the checking, like the Italian shortcut, is pruned in the open. The family tree is a summary of verdicts that the evidence was invited to overturn and did not.

## Originality After the Tree: Language and Character, Not Plot

The complication the tree poses is real, and it should be faced without flinching. If the plot, the machinery, the city, the names, the feud, and even the heroine's death device were all inherited, what is left that can be called original? The answer begins with a recovery of the period's own vocabulary, because the modern sense of originality, creation from nothing, would have puzzled the age that produced the play.

### What did the word invention mean in Shakespeare's century?

In the rhetoric every grammar school taught, invention, inventio, was the finding of material, the first stage of composition, in which a writer searched the storehouse of known matter for what the subject needed. A poet's invention was praised for the quality of the finding and the fitting, not for manufacturing a story no one had told.

On that understanding, working from sources was not a concession but the method itself, and the play's originality must be located where the period located excellence: in disposition, in character, and above all in language, elocutio, the clothing of found matter in words. Harold Bloom's Shakespeare: The Invention of the Human (1998) makes the strongest modern case on that ground, arguing that the playwright's true invention is personality itself, that his major figures possess an inwardness, a self hearing and self revision, that no earlier literature had rendered. Set against the tree, Bloom's claim needs the tree's correction and receives its confirmation in the same act. The correction: the lovers Bloom praises arrive with their situations, their speeches' occasions, and much of their story ready made, and any account of the play that ignores the descent mistakes the nature of the achievement. The confirmation: walk the tree node by node and the inwardness Bloom names is nowhere in it. Da Porto's lovers declare; Bandello's declaim; Brooke's moralise their own story in retrospect. The play's Juliet, thinking her way through the potion speech's terrors, and its Romeo, revising himself between one line and the next, are doing something none of the ancestors' figures do. The tree shows the deposit is Shakespeare's because the tree shows every earlier stratum without it.

Between the found matter and the finished character stands the middle term the rhetoricians called disposition, the ordering of the material, and the play's disposition is where its language and character are manufactured. The ancestors tell the story in the story's own order at the story's own pace, and the effect is chronicle. The play reorders the inheritance for the stage: Mercutio, the tradition's marginal name, is built up across the first half and killed at the exact centre, so that the comedy dies with him and the tragedy's machinery takes the second half at his speed; the balcony is placed early, before any blood, so that the love is established at full lyric height while the feud is still a street nuisance; the tomb sequence is stripped of its inherited farewell and reordered into a sequence of solitary arrivals. None of those decisions is a plot invention, and all of them are originality of the highest practical kind, the kind an audience feels as inevitability. Disposition is the bridge on which found matter crosses into character, because a figure is, in the theatre, the sum of the moments the ordering grants and withholds.

The verdict, then, stands where the evidence puts it. The story of Romeo and Juliet is a European inheritance, assembled across sixteen centuries from an Ovidian pattern, a Dantean proverb, a Sienese engine, a Veronese trunk, and a French moral graft, Englished in verse by Brooke and in prose by Painter. The play of Romeo and Juliet is Shakespeare's, in the only sense of possession the evidence supports and the only sense his century would have asked for: he found the matter, and in the finding made it unrecognisable as anyone else's. Plot descends. Language and character originate. A family tree that is read carefully does not diminish the crown. It shows what the crown is made of, and which part of it grew there.

## The Names of the Lovers: A Small History Inside the Tree

The names themselves have a descent, and it can be traced as exactly as the plot's. Masuccio's pair are Mariotto and Giannozza, good Sienese names with saints behind them, carrying no charge beyond their bearers. Da Porto replaced both, and the replacements are among his most consequential gifts to the tradition. Romeo, in Italian usage, was a byname for a pilgrim bound for Rome, a word with devotion and wandering inside it; scholars have often heard the resonance the play would later activate when its Romeo enters the Capulet feast in the language of pilgrimage, a pilgrim at a shrine, in the shared sonnet of the first meeting. Whether da Porto chose the name for its resonance or for its Veronese currency, the tree preserves the happy accident: the hero arrives at the crown already named for the role the sonnet gives him. Giulietta, the diminutive of Giulia, fixed the heroine's name in the form the world keeps, and the diminutive itself does work down the line, shrinking in felt scale as her age shrinks in the tellings, from da Porto's eighteen year old Giulietta to the play's child of thirteen, still called by the little name.

The surnames travel a plainer road with the same lesson. Montecchi becomes, in French and English mouths, Montague; Cappelletti becomes Capulet; and the naturalisations are the tradition in miniature, Italian matter made pronounceable in each new language while keeping its outline recognisable. Brooke's Romeus and Painter's Rhomeo record the hero mid journey, his name not yet settled into the spelling the play would fix. Even the friar's name wanders and settles: Fra Lorenzo in the Italian, Frère Laurent in the French orbit, Friar Laurence on the English stage. A family tree usually tracks persons. This one can also track phonemes, and the tracking confirms the descent at the smallest scale the evidence offers. Names, like plots, descend with modification.

Two supporting names complete the small history. Tebaldo, da Porto's name for the heroine's cousin, hardens down the line into Tybalt, a name English ears could hear against Tibert, the cat of the beast epic, and the play cashes the association openly when Mercutio hails him as a prince of cats. The cousin's name thus acquires, in transit, an animal edge the Italian never gave it, one more case of a translation quietly recharacterising what it carries. Marcuccio makes the opposite journey, from almost nothing to almost everything. Da Porto's cold handed courtier, kept by Brooke as a Mercutio named once at the feast, carries a name descended from Mercury, the god of eloquence, roads, and theft, and readers have often found the descent apt for the part the play builds on the mention: the quickest tongue in Verona, a thief of scenes, a messenger who delivers only a curse. The tree cannot prove the playwright heard the god inside the name. It can show that the name, like the plot, arrived with more inside it than its last Italian bearer had used.

## A Thread Through the Tree: The Friar's Descent

One figure lets the whole method be watched in miniature, because he appears at every node and changes at every telling: the friar. Masuccio invents him as a device, an Augustinian who marries the lovers and compounds the draught, present because the plot needs a churchman with a pharmacy and absent as a personality. Da Porto gives the device a name, Fra Lorenzo, a cell, a knowledge of herbs and of the city, and the first hint of a conscience, since his Lorenzo acts from affection for Romeo and pays for the failure in the tale's accounting. Bandello enlarges him with the collection's general rhetoric, longer speeches of counsel and excuse. Then the line darkens. Boaistuau, writing for a France arming itself over religion, handles the friar with a censor's suspicion, and Brooke's preface completes the turn, exhibiting the friar among the tale's warnings, a churchman abused by the lovers' disobedience in the Protestant translator's framing and an agent of it in the tale's.

The play receives the figure at the end of that long darkening and does the most characteristic thing in its whole handling of the inheritance: it declines to decide him. Shakespeare's Friar Laurence keeps every inherited function, the marriage, the draught, the letters, the final narration, and is granted the play's most sententious wisdom and its most human panic in a single part, fleeing the tomb at the watch's approach with the admission that he dares not stay. The ancestors each settle the friar, as device, as friend, as warning. The play unsettles him, and the unsettling is visible only down the thread: a reader who meets the friar first in Shakespeare cannot tell how much of the figure's ambiguity is design, since ambiguity might be the residue of a confused tradition. The tree settles the question. The tradition was never confused about the friar; it was successively certain in opposite directions. The ambiguity is the crown's own work, the product of an author who inherited two settled friars, the ally and the warning, and fused them into a man. A single thread, followed honestly, thus teaches the tree's whole lesson: descent is documented, transformation is datable, and the play's originality stands exactly where the thread changes character.

## The Tree's English Century: From Brooke's Poem to the Playhouse

Between Brooke's poem in 1562 and the play in about 1595 the tale lived an English life that the tree should register, because it explains the audience the play was written for. The story arrived as part of a flood. George Pettie's Petite Palace of Pettie His Pleasure (1576) Englished Italianate tales for the same readership; George Whetstone's Heptameron of Civil Discourses (1582) naturalised the novella frame itself, with tales told in company after the Italian manner; Fenton's Tragical Discourses had already shown Bandello marching into English through French. By the time the professional theatres were hungry for plots, the Veronese lovers were not an exotic import requiring introduction. They were part of the furniture of literate England, known from a bestselling poem and a bestselling anthology, the sort of story a playwright could assume and an audience could follow into its variations.

That familiarity shaped the play's boldest choices, because variation is only legible against a known norm. An audience meeting the tale for the first time needs the farewell in the tomb explained and savoured; an audience that knows the story can be denied the farewell and feel the denial as a blow. The play's compression works the same way: five days land as violence only against the memory of a slower telling. Shakespeare's originality, as the crown section argues, is an art of transformation, and transformation presupposes a public that holds the untransformed version in mind. The English century of the tale, the decades of Pettie, Whetstone, Fenton, Painter, and Brooke in reprint, built that public. The tree's last invisible branch is therefore not a text at all but a readership, and its existence is documented by the market that kept the versions in print. Source study usually stops at the playwright's desk. The desk stood in a room the tradition had furnished.

## The Cousins Around the Tree: Analogues and the Wider Family

A family tree drawn honestly has cousins as well as a line of descent, and the cousins explain why this story, of all stories, was available to be inherited. The pattern of the counterfeit death and the tomb was old Mediterranean property long before Masuccio. The Greek romance of Xenophon of Ephesus, the Ephesiaca, written in the early centuries of the Christian era, gives its heroine Anthia a physician's sleeping draught, taken to escape a forced marriage; she is entombed as dead, wakes in the tomb among robbers, and is carried off into further separation from her husband Habrocomes. No line of descent runs from Xenophon to the Italian novelle that scholarship can document, and the romance is therefore classed as an analogue. Yet its existence matters to the tree, because it shows that the draught and the waking in the tomb were not Masuccio's freak inventions. They were devices the story culture of the Mediterranean had kept in store for a thousand years, waiting for a teller to join them to a feud, a friar, and a city.

The analogues cluster thickest around the pattern's root. Ovid's Pyramus and Thisbe, already placed at the tree's head, is the ancestor Shakespeare demonstrably knew, through Arthur Golding's Englishing of the Metamorphoses in 1567 and through the tale's general currency. The playwright's own handling of the cousin material is the best commentary on the distinction this article maintains. In A Midsummer Night's Dream the Ovidian pattern is staged outright, as the mechanicals' tragedy of Pyramus and Thisbe, with its wall, its lion, and its double death played to laughter. Shakespeare could do that because the pattern, as pattern, was common property, a piece of the shared European furniture. What he could not have taken from Ovid, or from any analogue, is the play Romeo and Juliet actually is: the Veronese feud, the secret marriage, the draught, the letter, and the tomb arrive only down the documented line of the novelle. Analogues explain a story's climate. Sources explain its text. The tree keeps the two explanations separate, and the separation is what makes the descent, where it is claimed, worth believing.

The cousins also discipline the tree's pride. A story with this many relatives was never going to belong to one teller, and each age's version is partly a selection from a common stock: the divided lovers from Ovid, the apparent death from the romance tradition, the friar and the letter from the novella's own development. Shakespeare's contemporaries understood the stock as a commons. Playwrights drew plots from Italian collections as a matter of professional routine, and the audience's pleasure lay in the handling, not in the novelty of the fable. The tree, with its root in myth and its cousins in romance, restores that commons to view. The play stands at the crown of a family, and families are not diminished by having ancestors. They are explained by them.

The genre the tale travelled in is itself a cousin worth naming, because genre was the vehicle of transmission. Boaistuau's title, Histoires tragiques, announced a European form: the tragic history, a true or reputedly true tale of catastrophe, offered for profit and example, which Belleforest continued in French, Fenton and Whetstone naturalised in English prose, and Brooke naturalised in English verse. Romeo and Juliet reached Shakespeare as an eminent specimen of that genre, and the play keeps the genre's bones, its foreknowing frame, its exemplary catastrophe, its closing sentences of public judgement, while dissolving its moral into event. Readers who feel the play's strange double allegiance, to a story it treats as fable and to persons it treats as real, are feeling the genre and the drama in one work. The tree explains the doubleness. The crown grew on a tragic history's stock.

## How the Story Travelled: Books, Translators, and the Roads Between

The descent was carried by physical books along traceable roads, and the roads have their own evidence to give. The tale's first century is Italian and manuscript fed: Masuccio's Novellino printed at Naples in 1476, da Porto's history printed at Venice in 1530, Bandello's Novelle printed at Lucca in 1554 by the Busdraghi press, the first three parts of a collection whose scale made it the fiction warehouse of the century. Bandello's volumes were quarried across Europe because they offered what translators and playwrights needed, hundreds of plotted tales with speeches ready to lift. In France the quarry trade was organised by Boaistuau's Histoires tragiques of 1559 and continued after his death by François de Belleforest, whose continuations swelled the series and kept Bandello's matter in French circulation for decades. The relay through French was the rule for Italian fiction entering England, not the exception, and the Romeo and Juliet line obeys it exactly: England received the tale in French dress, twice over, within five years.

The English end of the road shows the same system at work. Brooke's poem came from Richard Tottel, the printer whose Miscellany of 1557 had taught English readers the sonnet, and a long narrative poem in poulter's measure was a commercial form with a known market. Painter's Palace of Pleasure, issued by the bookseller trade in 1566 and 1567, was a still bigger machine, an anthology built for the plot hungry, and its success created the shelf on which the tale sat waiting for a playwright. The parallel case of Geoffrey Fenton's Certain Tragical Discourses, also of 1567 and also drawn from Bandello by way of Belleforest's French, proves that Brooke and Painter were not eccentric. England in the 1560s was importing Italian fiction through French at industrial scale, and the tale of the Veronese lovers arrived as one consignment in a general trade. When the play followed a generation later, its author worked in a theatre that had been raised on such consignments. The tree's roads explain the play's opportunity: the story was there, in English, in two versions, in the common stock of the profession, and the wonder is not that a playwright used it but that one playwright's use of it eclipsed the road itself.

The road was built by a translation culture whose rules differ from the ones a modern reader assumes, and the difference matters for reading the tree. Elizabethan translators did not understand their task as the transparent transfer of a fixed text. To English a book was to naturalise it: to abridge, expand, moralise, and re-voice it for the readers at hand, and the translator's prefaces of the period defend those liberties as the office itself. Boaistuau's moral frame, Brooke's condemnatory address, and Painter's stately smoothing are therefore not corruptions of the line. They are the line working as its century intended, each translator acting as a licensed co-author. That is why the tree can be traced through translations at all, since a transparent translation would leave no fingerprints, while a naturalising one signs every page. The play stands at the end of the same practice raised to genius. Shakespeare Englished Brooke into the theatre with the same licence his predecessors had used on Bandello, and incomparably greater force. Translation, adaptation, and authorship were one spectrum in the period, and the family tree of this story is the spectrum's best map.

## What Each Retelling Kept: The Skeleton and Its Variations

Comparison across the whole line is the tree's working method, and five beats of the story show the method at its clearest, because each beat is constant enough to track and variable enough to date a telling by. The meeting first. Masuccio's lovers are joined by report and negotiation within Siena's civic world; da Porto fixes the tradition's choice, a feast at the Cappelletti house where Romeo, come with friends in the season's licence, is seen by Giulietta and speaks with her across the feud's line, and every later version keeps the feast, the house, and the instant mutual choice. The marriage second. From Masuccio onward the wedding is secret, sacramental, and performed by the friar, and the constancy is the point: four languages and a hundred and twenty years do not dislodge it, because the secret marriage is the engine's flywheel, the act that makes every later event at once a sin, a sacrament, and a fact the families cannot be told.

The killing third. Masuccio's Mariotto kills a fellow citizen in a brawl, an accident of temper; da Porto sharpens the victim into the heroine's cousin Tebaldo and the brawl into the feud's own logic, so that the exile that follows is the feud collecting its debt; Brooke hands the sharpened version to the play, which refines it once more into the duel sequence of Act 3, where the killing is two killings and the exile is pronounced over a corpse. The draught fourth. The device never changes, a potion that counterfeits death for a term of days, and only its term and its pharmacist's speech grow: the play's friar specifies "two and forty hours" (4.1.105) with a clinician's precision that the novelle, content with their three days, never needed. The tomb fifth, and here the variation becomes the tradition's signature. Every telling brings the husband to the entombed wife with death in his hand, and every telling before the play lets the wife wake in time to speak with him. The skeleton holds to the last joint, and at the last joint the play breaks it, as the crown section of this article sets out. A family tree earns its keep by making that break visible: an alteration is only an alteration against a documented norm, and the norm, beat by beat, is what the tree preserves.

A sixth beat, the settlement after the tomb, shows the tradition's moral temperature cooling as it descends. The Italian tellings close the record with reconciliation under authority, the houses composed by the spectacle of the tomb and the story filed as history. Brooke, carrying Boaistuau's doctrine into English, adds a distribution of punishments: the apothecary hanged, the Nurse banished for her part in the concealment, the friar withdrawing to a hermitage to end his days in penance, the families reconciled over the bodies under the Prince's hand. The play refuses the whole apparatus. No one is hanged, banished, or sent to a hermitage in its closing movement; the friar is met by the Prince's verdict, "We still have known thee for a holy man" (5.3.270), the Nurse vanishes unpunished, and the settlement takes the form of grief made monumental, two golden statues and a shared sentence of woe. The beat's descent is the tradition in one gradient: from record, to judgement, to mourning. Each teller kept the reconciliation. What the reconciliation meant is what each age rewrote.

## Reading the Play With the Tree in Hand

The tree is not an antiquarian exercise, and its final use is in the reading of the play itself. Four features of the text change their aspect when the descent is held in mind. The Chorus first. The play's Prologue speaks with a foreknowledge that can look like a flaw, spoiling the ending in the first fourteen lines, until the tree shows foreknowledge as the tradition's native posture: da Porto's history announces a recovered fact, Boaistuau's frame exhibits a completed judgement, Brooke's preface argues the moral before the poem begins. Every ancestor tells the tale as something already over. The Chorus's certainty is an inherited voice, the novella's retrospect surviving inside a form, drama, that normally hides its ending. What looks like a spoiler is a genealogy.

The friar's last speech second. When Friar Laurence, in the tomb's aftermath, offers the Prince "a brief history" of the night and the marriage, the play briefly becomes its own source: a connected prose minded narrative of the whole action, delivered by the one character who was present at every joint of it. Generations of readers have felt the speech as a falling off, a summary after the poetry. Against the tree it reads differently, as the tale's original form surfacing at the end of its longest journey, the novella's voice reasserting itself inside the play that had transmuted it. Shakespeare gives the story back to a narrator at the moment his drama is done with it, and the gesture closes the tree's circle: the play ends by becoming a telling, one more version for the line to carry.

The calendar third. The play's time is famously double, at once a few days by the action's count and elastic by the references, and editors have worried the inconsistency for centuries. The tree supplies the proportion. Brooke allowed the story some nine months, in which affections could ripen and cool and ripen again; the play takes the same beats and refuses them the months, and some of the text's temporal strain is the sound of a nine month story being held inside a five day frame. The strain is not carelessness but compression made audible, and knowing the source's calendar turns an apparent blemish into evidence of the crown's boldest decision.

The Prince fourth. The play's Verona is a governed city, watched by a Prince whose edicts open and close the action and whose final sentences distribute the blame, and that civic frame can feel like Shakespearean scene painting. Against the tree it is an inheritance with the volume raised. Da Porto set his tale under a named ruler, Bartolomeo della Scala, and the tradition keeps a magistrate at the story's edge, receiving the catastrophe and pronouncing on it, because a tale told as true history requires an authority to close the record. The play takes the magistrate from the margin to the structure: Escalus frames the feud's public cost in the first scene and totals it in the last, and the lovers' private story is held, as the ancestors held it, inside a civic account that the feud's survivors must settle. The frame is inherited. Its prominence is the dramatist's, since a stage can show a city as a novella can only report one.

In each case the tree does what source study exists to do: it converts a puzzle about the play into knowledge about the play, by restoring the company the play keeps.

## Why the Tree Is Not a Straight Line: Confluence and the Common Stock

The metaphor of a tree implies a single line of descent, and the honest source scholar qualifies the metaphor at exactly the points where the evidence refuses to be linear. Three qualifications belong in any account that aims to be believed. The first is confluence. A dramatist worked with what lay on the desk and in the memory together, and the play shows the marks of more than one stratum of the tradition: Brooke's poem as the working text, the common knowledge of the tale that Painter's anthology and a generation of retelling had made ambient, and behind both the Ovidian pattern carried in the playwright's own classical reading. Descent in literature is rarely the clean branching of biology. It is a river system, with a main channel, side channels that rejoin, and groundwater everywhere. The tree in this article draws the main channel in full ink and the rest in lighter strokes, and the variation in the ink is itself information.

The second qualification concerns the common stock. Some elements of the play that feel source like, the feud's unexplained antiquity, the lovers' instant mutual election, the catastrophe by mistimed message, belong to no single version because they belonged to the tale type as Europe held it in common. A feature found in the play and in Brooke may descend from Brooke, or it may have been drawn independently from the stock both men shared, and only the distinctive features, the arbitrary details, the shared errors, decide between the two histories. This is why the proofs in this article lean so heavily on departures and mistakes. Resemblance is cheap in a tradition built on resemblance. Error is the fingerprint.

The third qualification is humility about the lost. The sixteenth century's stages, pamphlets, and manuscripts survive in fragments, and Brooke's remark about a stage version stands as the tree's reminder that whole branches may have existed and vanished without record. The discipline's answer is not to invent the missing branches but to mark the possibility and to build nothing on it. The tree as drawn is therefore a minimum: the descent that the surviving witnesses prove. The actual thicket of tellings, oral, theatrical, and printed, was certainly denser. A map that shows only the proven roads is not a failure of imagination. It is what evidence looks like when it is drawn.

## The Tree in the Editions: How the Editors Present the Descent

A reader who wants to test this article's tree against the play will find the descent documented, in varying shapes, in the apparatus of the standard editions, and the history of the editing is the history of the tree's refinement. The Arden Second Series edition by Brian Gibbons (1980) gave the play a full source discussion and printed Brooke's poem in relation to the text passage by passage, setting the pattern for classroom study. Jill Levenson's Oxford edition (2000) widened the account to the whole European line and kept the Painter question deliberately open. The New Cambridge edition by G. Blakemore Evans (1984) weighed the analogues with particular care. René Weis's Arden Third Series edition (2012), the text quoted throughout this article, summarises the descent in its introduction with the economy of a settled question: Brooke the main source, the Italian line behind him through Boaistuau, Painter in the margin, Ovid at the root. The convergence across forty years of editing is itself a datum. Editors who disagree about much else reproduce the same tree, because the parallels compel it.

The play's own texts add a final segment to the tree, and editors treat it with the same genealogical tools. The quarto of 1597 is a short, rough text, evidently derived from performance and memory; the quarto of 1599 is longer, cleaner, and announces itself as newly corrected, augmented, and amended, and modern editions rest on it. The play that crowns the family tree therefore descends to readers through two witnesses of unequal authority, a last reminder that every stage of this story, including the crown, reaches the present through the accidents of transmission. The tale that began in Ovid's epic survives by the same means at every level: copying, printing, error, and correction. Source study and textual study are one discipline wearing two names, and Romeo and Juliet, inherited at every joint, is their joint masterpiece of evidence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch source tree, and what does each of its branches represent?

The InsightCrunch source tree is the dated family tree of the Romeo and Juliet story set out in this article, running from Ovid's Pyramus and Thisbe through Dante's feuding names, Masuccio, da Porto, Bandello, and Boaistuau to Brooke, Painter, and the play. Each branch represents one documented act of transmission: a version that demonstrably received the tale from a parent text and handed it on with additions. Branches are drawn from evidence, from verbal parallels, shared details, and shared errors, not from resemblance alone, which is why analogues such as Ovid stand at the root as ancestors while proven intermediaries form the trunk.

### Q: Which version of the story did Shakespeare follow most closely?

Arthur Brooke's English poem, The Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet of 1562, is the version Shakespeare followed most closely. The correspondence runs at every level: the order of scenes, the cast of the story, the furniture of the feast, marriage, duel, exile, draught, and tomb, and phrasing that recurs in the play's lines. No other version in the tree matches the play with anything like that density. Brooke is therefore classed as the play's main source, the trunk's final segment, while every earlier version stands as an ancestor whose matter reached the playwright through Brooke's page and, at most, through Painter's prose beside it.

### Q: Did Shakespeare read the story in Italian, or only in translation?

The evidence points to translation only. Every detailed parallel between the play and the tradition runs through the French and English versions, including their departures from the Italian originals, and no parallel requires a reading of da Porto or Bandello in Italian. Scholars who once proposed direct Italian reading could not produce a passage where the play follows the Italian against the intermediaries. The consensus represented by Bullough and the modern editors is that Shakespeare's Italy in this play is Boaistuau's France and Brooke's England at one and two removes, a conclusion that the shared errors make difficult to escape.

### Q: Why is Ovid's Pyramus and Thisbe an ancestor of the play rather than a source of it?

Because descent cannot be documented from Ovid to the play, only resemblance and general knowledge. A source is a text the writer demonstrably used for this work, proved by parallels too specific for chance. Pyramus and Thisbe supplies the pattern, forbidden lovers, a secret meeting, a fatal misreading, a double death, and Shakespeare plainly knew the tale, staging it as comedy in A Midsummer Night's Dream. Yet the play's plot, its machinery of friar, marriage, draught, and letter, descends through the Italian novella line, not from the Metamorphoses. Ovid is therefore the root and analogue: the pattern's author, not the plot's courier.

### Q: What enters the story for the first time in Masuccio's tale of Mariotto and Giannozza?

The plot's signature machinery enters with Masuccio. His Siena tale of 1476 contains the first secret marriage performed by a friar, the first friar acting as the lovers' ally and pharmacist, the first sleeping draught used to counterfeit a heroine's death, and the first failure of communication between the separated lovers that turns rescue into catastrophe. Mariotto's exile after a killing and his death on his return also belong to Masuccio's design. What his tale lacks is everything Veronese: the feud, the surnames, the ball, the tomb shared at one hour. The engine is his; the city and the names waited for da Porto.

### Q: Why do scholars treat Luigi da Porto's novella as the true start of the Romeo and Juliet story?

Because da Porto's telling, written about 1524 and printed in 1530, is the first in which the story is recognisably itself. Earlier stages hold pieces: Ovid the pattern, Dante the names, Masuccio the machinery. Da Porto assembles them in Verona, names the lovers Romeo Montecchio and Giulietta Cappelletti, grounds their danger in the feud between the houses, and carries the action through marriage, exile at Mantua, failed letter, and tomb. Every later version, Bandello, Boaistuau, Brooke, Painter, and the play, descends from his configuration of the parts. A start, in source study, is where the configuration begins, and the configuration begins with da Porto.

### Q: How did Dante's Purgatorio give the tale its two family names?

By proverb, two centuries before the lovers existed. In the sixth canto of the Purgatorio, Dante rebukes divided Italy with a catalogue of feuding houses and names the Montecchi and the Cappelletti together as the type of ruinous faction. The couplet lodged the surnames in the memory of every Italian reader as a matched pair signifying hereditary hatred. When da Porto needed feuding Veronese houses for his tale, he took the pair Dante had welded, one surname for each household. The play's Prologue inherits the charge: its ancient grudge feels ancient because, in the names, it was.

### Q: Why does the family tree pass through Bandello when his plot follows da Porto so closely?

Because transmission follows reading, not originality. Bandello's amplified retelling of 1554, with its long speeches and its enlarged Nurse, was the version Europe actually read: the Novelle circulated everywhere, and both Boaistuau in French and the English line after him descend from Bandello's page rather than from da Porto's. A version that changes little can still be the indispensable parent if it is the one the next generation opened. The tree records contact, and the contact at this node is certain. Skipping Bandello would give a tidier tree and a false one.

### Q: How can scholars tell that Brooke worked from Boaistuau's French rather than Bandello's Italian?

By shared departures from the Italian. Where Boaistuau alters Bandello, adding a detail, shifting an emphasis, or bending a passage in translation, Brooke's English bends with him, and where Boaistuau compresses or errs, the poem inherits the compression. Independent translators of the same original diverge in different places; a translator and his exemplar depart together. That pattern, repeated across the poem, is the standard proof of descent in textual scholarship, and it outweighs Brooke's own title page, which credits Bandello. The lines testify against the title page, and source study believes the lines.

### Q: Did William Painter's prose version feed the play, or is it a side branch of the tree?

The weight of evidence makes Painter a side branch with a possible graft. Brooke's poem accounts for the play entire, at a density of parallel that Painter never approaches, so no feature of the play requires Painter as its explanation. Geoffrey Bullough accordingly treats the prose Palace of Pleasure version of 1567 as a parallel English rendering whose few resemblances to the play descend from the Boaistuau text both translators shared. Jill Levenson's Oxford edition leaves open a wider consultation. The tree drawn here follows Bullough's weight of proof: Brooke the trunk, Painter a lighter line, probable as background, unprovable as a working source.

### Q: Does Brooke's preface point to a lost stage version, and would it add a hidden branch to the tree?

Brooke's address to the reader remarks that the same argument had been set forth on stage, and the sentence has long suggested a lost English play of Romeo and Juliet older than the poem. No text, performance record, or second witness has ever confirmed it, and the remark admits innocent explanations, from a school or court entertainment to a loose memory of his French. The tree therefore draws the branch in pencil: noted, possible, and carrying no weight. The documented descent through Brooke's poem explains the play without the phantom, and source study builds on witnesses, not on single ambiguous sentences.

### Q: How do scholars prove that one version of the story descends from another?

By the distribution of details, above all by shared errors and shared innovations. Resemblance proves little, since two tellers of one tale will resemble each other. Proof begins where a later text reproduces a parent text's distinctive additions, its peculiar mistakes, its mistranslations, and its arbitrary choices, features no independent retelling would duplicate. Brooke's poem shares Boaistuau's departures from Bandello, so Brooke descends from Boaistuau. The play shares Brooke's configuration in scene order and detail, so the play descends from Brooke. The method is the same one used to establish the descent of manuscripts: descent is where the errors agree.

### Q: Which plot elements survive almost unchanged from the Italian versions all the way to the play?

The skeleton survives: a secret marriage performed by a friar, a killing that exiles the husband, a forced second match, a sleeping draught that counterfeits the heroine's death, a message that fails to reach the exile, and a tomb in which the husband dies by his own hand beside the wife who then dies as well. Masuccio assembled most of the skeleton, da Porto set it in the feud and the city, and Brooke carried it into English nearly intact. Around that skeleton everything else in the play, the speed, the ages, the wit, the imagery, the withheld farewell, is transformation. The bones are inherited; the life in them is not.

### Q: Is each translation in the chain also a rewriting of the story?

Yes, and the tree depends on the fact. Boaistuau translating Bandello adds a moral frame and darkens judgements; Brooke translating Boaistuau adds a condemnatory preface, expands the Nurse, and stretches the calendar; Painter translating the same French polishes the tale into stately prose for an anthology. No stage in the chain is a transparent window onto its parent, which is why source study compares texts rather than trusting any version's claim to fidelity, and why the descent can be traced at all: each rewriting leaves fingerprints, additions and errors, by which its place in the line is proved.

### Q: Why is Geoffrey Bullough's collection of the sources the map that source study still starts from?

Because Bullough's Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare, first volume 1957, put the whole descent in one place with the texts themselves: da Porto and Bandello in translation, Boaistuau, Brooke entire, Painter, and the analogues, each introduced and weighed. Before that collection the sources were scattered through editions and journals, and claims about them were hard to test. After it, any reader could lay version against play and check the parallels on which the tree rests. Later scholarship has refined the weight given to Painter and to the analogues, but the outline Bullough documented, Brooke as main source within a long Italian and French descent, still stands.

### Q: If Shakespeare invented almost none of the plot, where does his originality lie?

In language and character, the two layers the tree shows arriving only at the crown. The language: imagery systems of light and darkness, sacrament, haste, and disease that no source contains, a first meeting built as a shared sonnet, a Queen Mab speech grown from a name in a guest list. The character: an inwardness in both lovers, thought revising itself line by line, that the ancestors' declaring and declaiming figures never approach. Harold Bloom located the invention in personality itself; the tree corrects the claim's scope and confirms its substance. The story is Europe's. The experience of the story is Shakespeare's.

### Q: How many languages, and how many years, separate Ovid's lovers from Shakespeare's?

Four languages and nearly sixteen centuries. The pattern begins in Ovid's Latin, in a poem completed about AD 8; the tale proper forms in Italian, from Masuccio in 1476 through da Porto and Bandello; it crosses into French with Boaistuau in 1559; and it reaches English twice, in Brooke's verse of 1562 and Painter's prose of 1567, before the play of about 1595. The novella chain itself, from Siena to the London stage, spans roughly a hundred and twenty years. The tree's length is the point: the play is the last heir of the longest inheritance in its author's work.

### Q: Why did the tale keep attracting retellers for well over a century before it reached the stage?

Because each age found its own use for it. Masuccio made it an exemplum of desire and fortune; da Porto, a true history of his region's famous feud; Bandello, a showpiece of rhetorical amplification; Boaistuau, a warning against disobedience dressed as a tragic history; Brooke, a moral poem for English readers; Painter, a jewel for a bestselling anthology. The story's machinery, secret love, counterfeit death, failed message, proved adaptable to every purpose while losing none of its pathos. A tale that can serve exemplum, chronicle, rhetoric, doctrine, and anthology in turn is a tale built to survive transmission, and survival is what a family tree records.

### Q: What did the French stage of the journey add to the story's moral frame?

A doctrine, stated openly. Boaistuau's Histoires tragiques of 1559 retells Bandello under a frame that exhibits the lovers as a warning: disobedience to parents and ungoverned desire are named as the tale's lesson, the narration comments and censures, and the friar's part is handled with new severity. Italy had told a pathetic history; France attached a judgement to it. The addition mattered because the English line descends through the French: Brooke's condemnatory preface translates Boaistuau's posture into English, and the play's refusal to condemn its lovers is a refusal of the frame its own nearest ancestors carried.

### Q: How does knowing the family tree change the way the play should be read?

It changes the questions. Read without the tree, the play's unexplained feud, unexplained omniscience of its Chorus, and headlong calendar look like loose ends; read with the tree, each is an inheritance with a date on it, the feud a Dantean proverb, the foreknowing Chorus the descendant of a line of moralising frames, the calendar a compression of Brooke's nine months. The tree also disciplines praise. What the ancestors already contain should not be credited to the playwright, and what they lack, the language, the inwardness, the withheld farewell at the tomb, stands out in its true size. Source study ends in criticism, better aimed.
