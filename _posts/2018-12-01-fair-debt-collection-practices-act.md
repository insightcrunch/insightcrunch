The operative question behind the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act was narrow, and Congress framed the question around a specific actor rather than around debt itself. When a consumer fell behind on a bill, the creditor often handed the account to an outside collection agency, and a stranger with no prior relationship to the household began demanding payment. Federal law before 1977 regulated what a creditor had to disclose when credit was extended, and federal law regulated how credit information was reported, but no federal statute set conduct rules for the independent collector who pursued the debt after default. The statute answered a gap question: what may a third party collector say and do, to the consumer and to everyone around the consumer, in the effort to collect money owed to somebody else? Every operative provision in the enacted text traces back to that framing, because Congress legislated against a defined set of collection behaviors rather than against indebtedness, default, or the underlying obligation to pay.

The behaviors in the congressional record were concrete. Senate Report No. 95-382, the report of the Senate Committee on Banking, Housing, and Urban Affairs that accompanied the bill in 1977, cataloged collection abuse as a pattern of pressure tactics rather than as isolated rudeness. Collectors telephoned consumers late at night and in the early morning, rang telephones repeatedly with the intent to annoy, and used obscene and profane language on calls and in letters. Collectors threatened violence, threatened arrest on a civil debt, and threatened seizure of property or wages without legal authority to carry out the threat. Collectors impersonated attorneys, impersonated government officials, and sent documents designed to look like court process or official government forms. Collectors also carried the pressure beyond the household, disclosing debts to employers, telephoning neighbors and relatives, and using those contacts to shame a consumer into payment. The Senate report treated third person contacts as a distinct harm, stating that, apart from efforts to obtain location information, contact with friends, neighbors, relatives, or an employer served no legitimate collection purpose and produced serious invasions of privacy and the loss of jobs.

The distinction between a creditor collecting its own account and an agency collecting for someone else drove the statutory design, for reasons of incentive and information treated in full in the scope discussion. A creditor collecting in its own name dealt with a customer the creditor might serve again, while an agency commonly collected on a contingency basis, held no ongoing relationship with the household, and often received only a name, an address, a telephone number, and a balance, with limited information about disputes, payments already made, or errors in the amount claimed. Congress therefore wrote conduct rules for the intermediary, and the enacted definition of the regulated actor, the debt collector, became the hinge on which coverage turned.

Congress also legislated on a stated theory of harm that extended past embarrassment or annoyance. The findings enacted in the statute connected abusive collection to personal bankruptcies, connecting pressure and misrepresentation to filings by consumers who might otherwise have negotiated or paid over time. The findings connected abusive collection to marital instability and to job loss, reflecting testimony that collectors telephoned workplaces, disclosed debts to supervisors, and pressed consumers at hours and in places that disrupted employment and family life. The findings connected abusive collection to invasions of individual privacy, reflecting the practice of broadcasting a private financial difficulty to neighbors, relatives, and coworkers who had no role in the transaction. That theory mattered for the constitutional and structural choices in the bill, because a statute aimed at deception and coercion in interstate commerce rested on a different footing than a statute aimed at the terms of credit itself, and Congress drafted findings that tied each category of prohibited conduct to a documented consequence.

## Why Congress Acted and What the Statute Is

The statute has a single formal identity, and precision about that identity prevents a common confusion between the 1977 enactment and the older act the 1977 law amended. The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act is Public Law 95-109, published at 91 Statutes at Large 874, signed by President Jimmy Carter on September 20, 1977, and effective on March 20, 1978. Congress enacted the law as Title VIII of the Consumer Credit Protection Act, adding a new title to an existing consumer credit code rather than enacting a free standing code. The codified text sits in Title 15 of the United States Code at sections 1692 to 1692p, where section 1692 carries the congressional findings and declaration of purpose and the following sections carry definitions, communication rules, prohibitions, validation procedures, liability rules, and enforcement assignments. The six month interval between signature and effectiveness was written into the statute itself, which provided that the title took effect upon the expiration of six months after the date of enactment, giving agencies, creditors, and enforcement officials a defined period to conform scripts, forms, and procedures to the new federal rules.

The Consumer Credit Protection Act supplied the legislative lineage into which the 1977 title fit. Congress enacted that parent act as Public Law 90-321 on May 29, 1968, and Title I of the 1968 act, the Truth in Lending Act, required disclosure of finance charges and annual percentage rates so that consumers could compare the cost of credit before borrowing; a reader tracing the disclosure side of the lineage can consult [the Truth in Lending Act framework that began the federal consumer credit code](/2018/10/15/truth-in-lending-act-1968-guide/) for the structure Congress later extended. The 1968 act also restricted wage garnishment in Title III and created the National Commission on Consumer Finance in Title IV, charging the commission with studying the consumer finance industry and recommending further legislation. Congress spent the following decade adding titles to the same code as specific problems drew legislative attention, addressing credit reporting, credit billing, and equal access to credit through separate enactments that shared definitions, enforcement agencies, and a common placement in Title 15. The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act followed that established pattern in 1977, occupying the next open title in the parent act and borrowing the parent act architecture of findings, definitions, substantive duties, civil liability, and administrative enforcement.

### What Problem Did the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act Solve?

Congress enacted the statute to stop abusive tactics by independent collection agencies hired to pursue consumer debts owed to someone else. Collectors had used threats, obscenity, deception, and pressure on employers and neighbors, and state law left many consumers without a remedy, so federal law set uniform conduct rules.

The collection agency industry that Congress confronted in the 1970s had grown alongside the expansion of consumer credit in the decades after the Second World War. Department stores, oil companies, banks, and finance companies extended revolving credit and installment credit to a broad consumer market, and defaulted accounts became a predictable byproduct of that lending volume. Creditors faced a practical choice between maintaining internal collection departments and assigning delinquent accounts to outside specialists, and many creditors chose assignment because an agency could pursue accounts across a wide geography and absorb the cost of repeated calls and letters. Agencies competed for creditor business on recovery rates, and recovery rates rewarded persistence and pressure. A collector paid a percentage of amounts recovered had a direct financial stake in each payment extracted, and a collector handling accounts for distant creditors had limited accountability to the consumer at the other end of the telephone line. By the middle of the 1970s, congressional committees treated the independent agency sector as a distinct industry with distinct practices, separate from the lending industry that originated the debts, and that separation explains why the legislative response regulated collection conduct rather than credit terms.

State law supplied only a partial answer before 1977, and the uneven quality of state protection formed a central premise of the federal bill. Some states licensed collection agencies and prescribed conduct rules, and Wisconsin, for example, enacted collection restrictions within the Wisconsin Consumer Act in 1973. Many states, however, had no dedicated collection practices statute at all, leaving consumers to general fraud law, general harassment law, or common law tort claims that required a consumer to hire counsel and litigate after the harm had occurred. Even among states with collection statutes, definitions, prohibited practices, and enforcement mechanisms varied, so an agency operating by mail and telephone across state lines faced a patchwork of inconsistent obligations, and a consumer in one state received materially weaker protection than a consumer in a neighboring state on an identical account. Federal findings addressed that patchwork directly by declaring the existing body of law inadequate, and the design that followed set a federal floor of conduct while preserving stronger state protections rather than displacing state law wholesale.

Congress placed the justification for federal action inside the statute, in section 1692, subsections (a) through (e), and the findings repay close reading because later interpreters have treated the findings as a guide to the scope of the operative sections. Subsection (a) states the evidentiary premise: abundant evidence documented abusive, deceptive, and unfair debt collection practices by many debt collectors, and abusive practices contributed to personal bankruptcies, marital instability, the loss of jobs, and invasions of individual privacy. Subsection (b) states the legal premise: existing laws and procedures for redressing the injuries were inadequate to protect consumers. The findings name harms rather than sentiments, and each named harm corresponds to testimony in the committee record about bankruptcy filings made under collection pressure, about families strained by repeated calls and workplace disclosures, and about employment lost after a collector contacted a supervisor.

Subsections (c), (d), and (e) complete the argument by addressing feasibility, federal jurisdiction, and purpose. Subsection (c) finds that means other than misrepresentation or other abusive practices were available for the effective collection of debts, a finding that answered the industry position that strict tactics were a practical necessity; Congress asserted, as a legislative fact, that collectors could recover debts without deception or coercion. Subsection (d) supplies the commerce foundation, finding that abusive collection practices were carried on to a substantial extent in interstate commerce and through the means and instrumentalities of interstate commerce, and that even purely intrastate practices directly affected interstate commerce, language that tracked the structure of commerce findings Congress used in other consumer legislation of the period. Subsection (e) states three purposes in a single sentence: eliminating abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors, insuring that debt collectors who refrained from abusive practices were not competitively disadvantaged, and promoting consistent state action to protect consumers against collection abuses. The competitive disadvantage clause deserves emphasis, because Congress framed abusive tactics as an unfair competitive method within the collection industry itself. An agency that observed voluntary limits on calling hours, honesty, and third party contact competed for creditor accounts against an agency that ignored those limits, and Congress designed the federal floor in part to remove the market reward for abuse.

The evidentiary path to those findings ran through a decade of consumer credit investigation. The National Commission on Consumer Finance, created by the Consumer Credit Protection Act in 1968, studied the consumer finance industry and reported to Congress in the early 1970s, and the legislative history of the 1977 act drew on the commission conclusion that the vast majority of consumers who obtained credit fully intended to pay their debts. That conclusion undercut the premise that default ordinarily reflected dishonesty, and committee materials treated most default as the product of job loss, illness, divorce, or overextension rather than fraud. Consumer witnesses described specific episodes in hearings: calls placed before dawn and after midnight, threats of arrest delivered to elderly consumers, collectors posing as sheriff deputies or attorneys, notices printed to imitate court summonses, and disclosures made to employers that placed jobs at risk. Industry witnesses defended collection as a necessary service and warned against restrictions that would reduce recoveries and raise the cost of credit, and the enacted text reflects a legislative judgment between those positions, regulating the manner of collection in detailed terms while leaving the right to pursue lawful payment, including through the courts, intact.

Movement through the 95th Congress proceeded on parallel House and Senate tracks that converged in the late summer of 1977. Representative Frank Annunzio of Illinois introduced the principal House bill, H.R. 5294, on March 22, 1977, and the House Committee on Banking, Finance, and Urban Affairs reported the measure in House Report No. 95-131. The House considered and passed H.R. 5294 on April 4, 1977, thirteen days after introduction, a pace that reflected the groundwork laid in earlier Congresses, where comparable collection bills had received hearings without reaching enactment. On the Senate side, a companion measure, S. 918, went to the Senate Committee on Banking, Housing, and Urban Affairs on March 4, 1977, and that committee took control of the legislation when the House bill arrived. The Senate committee reported H.R. 5294 with an amendment on August 2, 1977, in Senate Report No. 95-382, substituting a revised text that became the operative basis of the enacted law and supplying the section by section explanation that courts and agencies later consulted when construing ambiguous provisions. The Senate passed the amended bill on August 5, 1977, and the House agreed to the Senate amendment on September 8, 1977, clearing the measure for the President without a conference committee.

Senate Report No. 95-382 shaped the statute in substance as well as in explanation. The committee stated that the legislation addressed a documented pattern rather than a handful of complaints, and the report explained the decision to cover independent collectors while generally excluding creditors collecting their own debts, resting that line on the relationship rationale described in the findings debate. The report construed the third party communication limits narrowly and deliberately, explaining that contacts with friends, neighbors, relatives, and employers, other than contacts genuinely aimed at obtaining location information, were not legitimate collection practices and produced serious invasions of privacy and job losses. The report also recorded the committee view that mortgage servicing companies and others who serviced outstanding debts for others fell outside the intended definition of debt collector so long as the debts were not in default when taken for servicing, an explanation that later figured in disputes over the boundary between servicing and collection. Because the Senate amendment supplied the final text, the Senate report acquired an interpretive weight in later litigation that the earlier House report did not match, and litigants on both sides of collection disputes mined the report for statements about coverage, communications, and enforcement.

The closing steps of enactment fixed the dates that define the statute in citation and in practice. President Jimmy Carter signed the enrolled bill on September 20, 1977, and the law entered the Statutes at Large as Public Law 95-109 at 91 Statutes at Large 874. Effectiveness, however, waited on the delayed date Congress had written into the text. The effective date provision stated that the title took effect upon the expiration of six months after enactment, which produced the March 20, 1978 effective date that agency guidance from the Federal Reserve System, the Comptroller of the Currency, and the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation used when instructing supervised institutions in 1978. The delay gave collection agencies an interval to revise telephone scripts, form letters, and training materials before the prohibitions attached. A transition rule accompanied the delay, providing that the debt validation provision applied only to debts for which the initial attempt to collect occurred after the effective date, so the new notice and dispute machinery attached to collection campaigns that began under the new regime rather than to campaigns already under way in the autumn of 1977.

Equally instructive is the set of problems Congress chose not to address in the 1977 title, because the omissions define the statute as precisely as the prohibitions do. The act did not forgive, reduce, or reschedule any debt, and the text contains no mechanism for adjusting the amount a consumer owed. The act did not impose its conduct code on creditors collecting their own debts in their own names as a general matter, a deliberate exclusion the Senate report explained and later Congresses debated without reversing in the original framework. The act did not federalize the whole field: the relation to state laws provision preserved state collection law except to the extent of an inconsistency, and the statute expressly treated a state law as consistent when the state law afforded greater protection than the federal title. The act also created an exemption route under which a state could obtain relief from federal coverage for a class of practices when state requirements were substantially similar and adequately enforced, reinforcing the stated purpose of promoting consistent state action rather than supplanting state legislatures. The result was a conduct statute layered over an existing state system, aimed at a defined intermediary, and bounded by findings that named the harms Congress intended the operative sections to prevent.

Read as a whole, the opening record of the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act explains why a statute about telephone calls, letters, and workplace contacts occupies a permanent place in the federal consumer credit code. Consumer credit had expanded faster than the law governing the recovery of unpaid accounts, and the recovery function had migrated to independent firms whose compensation rewarded pressure and whose operations crossed state lines beyond the effective reach of uneven state statutes. Congress responded in 1977 with findings that documented the abuse and its consequences, a definition that isolated the third party collector, and a delayed effective date that gave the industry a fixed interval to conform. The sections that follow in the codified text, addressing definitions, communications, harassment, false representations, unfair practices, validation, liability, and enforcement, all execute the judgment Congress recorded at the outset: collection of legitimate debts could proceed, and collection by threats, deception, and exposure could not.

## The Scope of the Statute: Who Counts as a Debt Collector and Who Falls Outside

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act regulates conduct only when two conditions align: the person engaged in collection qualifies as a debt collector, and the obligation pursued qualifies as a debt. Both conditions come from section 803, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 1692a. Congress in 1977 did not write a general code of collection ethics for every person who seeks payment. Congress wrote a code for a defined class of collectors pursuing a defined class of obligations, and then carved exclusions out of that class. A court never reaches the conduct rules in sections 1692d, 1692e, or 1692f if the collector falls outside section 1692a(6) or the obligation falls outside section 1692a(5).

### The Two Prongs of the Debt Collector Definition

Section 1692a(6) defines a debt collector through two alternative routes, and either route suffices. The first reaches any person who uses an instrumentality of interstate commerce or the mails in a business whose principal purpose is the collection of any debts. The second reaches any person who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another. The first asks what kind of business the person conducts. The second asks for whom the person collects, and how often. The Supreme Court in Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc. in 2017 resolved a major dispute under the second route while leaving the first route undecided on the facts before the Court.

The principal purpose prong looks to the character of the business as a whole. A collection agency whose operations center on recovering debts for clients satisfies the prong on the face of its business model. The statutory phrase is the collection of any debts, without the qualifying phrase owed to another in the second prong. That difference mattered in Henson, because a business organized to buy defaulted accounts for its own account faces a harder textual question under the first prong if collection is the principal purpose of the enterprise. Courts weigh the share of revenue and employees devoted to collection, and whether collection is an occasional incident of a larger enterprise or the enterprise itself. A bank that lends and takes deposits presents a different profile from a firm that exists only to pursue delinquent accounts.

The regularly collects prong looks instead to repetition and to the identity of the person to whom the debt is owed. The word regularly excludes isolated or occasional efforts. A firm that pursues accounts week after week enters the class, provided the debts pursued are owed to another. The classic case is the independent collection agency: the creditor owns the claim, the agency owns nothing, and the agency pursues the consumer as agent of the owner. The phrase also reaches indirect collection, so a firm cannot escape the definition by routing demands through subcontractors or letter services. Section 1692a(4) reinforces the point by providing that the term creditor does not include a person who receives an assignment of a debt in default solely to facilitate collection of that debt for another. A paper transfer arranged only to move a defaulted account into collection hands does not convert the collector into the creditor.

### The Definitions of Debt, Consumer, and Creditor

The definition of debt in section 1692a(5) limits the Act to consumer obligations. A debt is an obligation or alleged obligation of a consumer to pay money arising out of a transaction in which the money, property, insurance, or services that are the subject of the transaction are primarily for personal, family, or household purposes, whether or not the obligation has been reduced to judgment. The transaction element ties coverage to a consensual exchange such as a credit purchase, a loan, or a contract for services, rather than to an obligation imposed by law. The judgment phrase confirms that entry of a court judgment on a consumer transaction does not strip the obligation of its character as a debt.

The personal, family, or household purpose test excludes commercial debt. A loan taken to stock a business or equipment purchased for a trade falls outside section 1692a(5), even when the same agency pursues a commercial account in the morning and a household account in the afternoon. Coverage attaches obligation by obligation rather than firm by firm. Mixed purpose transactions require a judgment about primary purpose, resolved on the facts of the purchase. Tax obligations arise from operation of law rather than from a transaction, and courts have therefore treated tax liabilities as outside the definition. Tort obligations likewise arise from injury and legal duty rather than from a transaction, so a damages claim against a person who caused an accident is not a debt under the Act. The boundary reflects the 1977 choice to regulate the consumer credit marketplace, where a creditor extended value in advance and a collector later demanded repayment.

Section 1692a(3) defines consumer to mean any natural person obligated or allegedly obligated to pay any debt. The restriction to natural persons excludes corporations and other entities. The word allegedly does significant work: a person pursued for a debt the person does not in fact owe, including a person pursued because of mistaken identity, stands in the position of a consumer. Coverage does not depend on proof that the underlying obligation was valid. Section 1692a(4) defines creditor to mean any person who offers or extends credit creating a debt or to whom a debt is owed, subject to the assignment qualification described in the discussion of the second prong. The definitions are relational: for a given account, the inquiry asks who extended the credit, who owns the claim, and who pursues payment.

### Is the original creditor a debt collector under the FDCPA?

Generally, no. An original creditor collecting a debt owed to that creditor, in the creditor's own name and through the creditor's own employees, falls outside the statutory definition of debt collector in section 1692a(6). Coverage changes if the creditor collects for another person or collects under a different name suggesting outside collection.

The general rule flows from both the text and the exclusions. Under the regularly collects prong, the original creditor collects a debt owed to the creditor, not a debt owed to another. Under clause (A), an officer or employee of a creditor, while collecting debts for the creditor in the name of the creditor, is expressly outside the term. The retail chain that sold furniture on its own credit plan and the bank that issued a credit card each occupies the creditor position when pursuing its own accounts in its own name. The in-house collection department, staffed by employees who work under the creditor's name and letterhead, is an extension of the creditor. The independent agency hired to pursue the same account, working for a contingency fee and holding itself out as a collector, is a debt collector. The consumer experience of the two campaigns can be similar in tone and pressure. The statutory treatment differs because the identity and incentives of the pursuer differ. That distinction is the consequential limit of the statute: the party most consumers deal with first, and often longest, is the original creditor, and that party is generally not covered.

Congress drew the line at the creditor boundary for reasons stated in the legislative record of the 95th Congress. Senate Report No. 95-382 from 1977 described the target of the legislation as the independent collector, and the findings in section 1692(a) speak of abusive practices by debt collectors in a market where collectors worked for creditors rather than for themselves. The reasoning in the Senate Report and in House Report No. 95-131 from 1977 rested on a distinction in incentives. A creditor that sold goods or lent money depended on reputation and repeat business, and risked losing future customers by abusing delinquent customers. The independent collector had no comparable stake, no ongoing relationship to protect, and often worked on a contingency basis in which compensation rose with the amount extracted, so restraint carried a direct financial cost. The consumer had never chosen the collector and could not discipline the collector by withholding future business, because the consumer was never the collector's customer. The collector was the creditor's vendor, unknown to the consumer until the first demand arrived.

A second strand of the 1977 reasoning involved federalism. Creditors were already subject to state regulation of credit practices and state unfair practices law. The stated purposes in section 1692(e) include promoting consistent state action, and the Act operates alongside state law rather than displacing state law except where state law is inconsistent, under section 1692n. California, for example, enacted the Rosenthal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, which applied collection standards to creditors collecting their own debts as well as to third party collectors. Creditor conduct also remained subject to other federal law, including the prohibition on unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts or practices in the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010. Whether the 1977 incentive distinction described the market accurately in later decades is a question legislators and litigants pressed repeatedly after 1977. The text retained the creditor boundary, and courts applied the boundary as written.

### The Exclusions in Clauses A Through F

Section 1692a(6) closes with six categories that the term debt collector does not include, in clauses (A) through (F). Several exclusions are conditional, and several apply only to the extent of described activity. Clause (A) covers any officer or employee of a creditor while, in the name of the creditor, collecting debts for such creditor. The conditions are cumulative: the person must be an officer or employee of the creditor, the collection must be for that creditor, and the collection must be conducted in the creditor's name. Clause (B) covers a person acting as a debt collector for another person where both are related by common ownership or affiliated by corporate control, provided the person acting does so only for related or affiliated persons, and provided the principal business of the person acting is not the collection of debts. The clause accommodates a shared service affiliate that handles billing and delinquency work for affiliated lenders as an incident of a larger administrative business. A collection subsidiary formed to pursue accounts for the group as its main business cannot qualify, because the principal business condition fails.

Clause (C) covers any officer or employee of the United States or of any state to the extent that collecting a debt is in the performance of official duties. A tax agency employee pursuing a liability under official authority acts under clause (C). The exclusion is functional rather than personal: the same individual acting privately receives no shelter from clause (C). Clause (D) covers any person while serving or attempting to serve legal process in connection with the judicial enforcement of a debt. The process server who delivers a summons and complaint performs a function the legal system requires for notice. A process server who goes beyond service and demands payment operates outside the narrow activity clause (D) describes. Clause (E) covers a nonprofit organization that, at the request of consumers, performs bona fide consumer credit counseling and assists consumers in the liquidation of debts by receiving payments from consumers and distributing the amounts to creditors. Because money passes through the organization's hands, a definition without clause (E) risked sweeping a counseling service into the collector class. The exclusion is limited to nonprofit status, consumer request, and genuine counseling and distribution activity, and an operation that uses a counseling label as a front for fee extraction does not match the clause (E) description.

Clause (F) is the broadest exclusion, and it operates to the extent of described activity rather than as a status exemption. The first subclause covers activity incidental to a bona fide fiduciary obligation or a bona fide escrow arrangement. Trustees, receivers, guardians, and escrow agents hold and disburse money under duties that exist independently of collection, and payment of a debt out of funds held in that capacity is incidental to the fiduciary or escrow function. A collection agency cannot manufacture the exclusion by labeling a client contract a fiduciary arrangement, because the agency's duty in such a contract is collection and nothing else. The second subclause covers activity concerning a debt that was originated by the person collecting, aligning with the principle that the originator stands in the creditor position. The third subclause covers activity concerning a debt that was not in default at the time the person obtained the debt, and is the statutory home of the mortgage servicer and the purchaser of performing accounts. A servicer that obtains a portfolio of current mortgage loans, and that sends statements, processes payments, and manages escrow accounts for taxes and insurance, performs work Congress treated as servicing rather than collection, so long as the loans were not in default when the servicer obtained them. Timing controls: a servicer that obtains an account after default occupies a different position from a servicer that held the account before any default occurred, even if both send identical letters after the default. The prevailing reading in federal appellate decisions treats status as fixed by the condition of the debt at acquisition. The fourth subclause covers a debt obtained as a secured party in a commercial credit transaction involving the creditor, as when a lender takes a security interest in accounts receivable as collateral for a business loan and later collects the receivables after the borrower's default on that loan.

### The False Name Exception

The definition contains an express counterweight to the creditor exclusion, introduced by the word notwithstanding. The term debt collector includes any creditor that, in collecting its own debts, uses a name other than its own that would indicate that a third person is collecting the debts. The inclusion applies notwithstanding the exclusion provided by clause (F), which forecloses an argument that origination or acquisition timing shelters a creditor that collects under a false front. The exception targets a specific deception: the creditor that conceals its identity behind the name of an apparent collection agency, so that the consumer believes an outside firm has taken over the account.

A demand on the creditor's own letterhead signals a dispute within an existing relationship. A demand on the letterhead of an unfamiliar agency signals transfer and professional pursuit, and can extract payment from fear of the unknown party. A creditor that obtains the pressure of the second signal while retaining the shelter of the first position engages in misrepresentation of the kind the Act exists to eliminate, and Congress responded by reclassifying the creditor as a debt collector for collection under the false name. The reclassification is conduct specific: a creditor that uses a pseudonym for one portfolio and its own name for another is a debt collector as to the pseudonym portfolio. When the exception applies, the conduct rules in sections 1692b through 1692o govern the creditor's collection of its own account.

### Debt Buyers and the Holding in Henson v. Santander

The growth of debt buying placed pressure on the second prong that Congress in 1977 had no occasion to anticipate at scale. In the debt buying model, a creditor that has charged off a defaulted account sells the account, often in a portfolio and at a fraction of face value, to a buyer that takes ownership and pursues the consumer for profit. The buyer did not extend the credit and did not act as an agent. The buyer resembles a creditor in ownership, and resembles a collector in acquiring the account precisely because the account was in default. Consumers sued debt buyers under the Act, and buyers answered that the debts, after purchase, were owed to the buyer rather than owed to another. The question reached the Supreme Court in Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc.

The Supreme Court decided Henson on June 12, 2017, affirming the Fourth Circuit, in a decision reported at 582 U.S. 79. The decision was unanimous, and Justice Neil Gorsuch wrote the opinion, the first of Justice Gorsuch's tenure after joining the Court in April 2017. Ricky Henson and the other petitioners had defaulted on automobile loans from CitiFinancial Auto. Santander purchased the defaulted loans and sought to collect. The petitioners sued under the Act, and Santander answered that Santander was not a debt collector because Santander collected debts Santander owned. The district court dismissed the case, and the Fourth Circuit affirmed in a decision reported at 817 F.3d 131 in 2016.

The opinion reasoned from the text in its present tense. After a purchase, the debt is owed to the purchaser, who therefore collects for the purchaser's own account rather than for another person. The petitioners argued that the word owed carried a past orientation, so that a debt once owed to the originator remained a debt owed to another in the hands of any later purchaser. The Court rejected that reading, treating owed as describing the relationship at the time of collection, and pointing to other provisions that distinguish between creditors, who are owed debts, and debt collectors, who pursue debts for creditors. The Court observed that Santander fit awkwardly between the two categories Congress had described in 1977, and held to the text Congress enacted, stating that any decision to extend the definition to debt buyers belonged to Congress.

The reservation in Henson matters as much as the holding. The petitioners had not preserved, and the Court therefore did not decide, an argument under the principal purpose prong: that Santander qualified as a debt collector because Santander engaged in a business whose principal purpose is the collection of any debts. The first prong contains no owed to another qualifier. Plaintiffs in later cases pressed principal purpose claims against debt buyers whose business consisted overwhelmingly of purchasing and collecting defaulted accounts. Henson resolved the ownership question under the second prong, and conferred no general exemption on debt buyers.

Invitations to Congress followed the decision in the years after 2017, in introduced bills proposing to amend section 1692a(6) to reach persons collecting debts owned by the collector when the debts were in default at acquisition. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau issued Regulation F, codified at 12 C.F.R. part 1006, effective November 30, 2021. Regulation F restated the statutory definition rather than expanding it, because the Bureau's rulemaking authority runs to the statute Congress wrote. The federal boundary after Henson therefore remained where the Court had placed it, pending congressional action, while regulators used other authorities, including the Dodd-Frank Act prohibition on unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts or practices, to address debt buyer conduct outside the FDCPA definition.

State law supplied a second response, on two tracks. The first track was state statutes broader than the federal definition. The California Rosenthal Act applied conduct standards to creditors and debt buyers without the federal owed to another limitation, and the California Fair Debt Buying Practices Act, enacted in 2013 and effective for debts sold or resold on or after January 1, 2014, required a debt buyer suing on a purchased account to possess and plead specified records about the account and the chain of title. New York acted through regulation when the Department of Financial Services adopted rules at 23 NYCRR part 1 in 2014, with provisions effective in 2015, imposing substantiation and disclosure duties on third party collectors and debt buyers. The second track was licensing. California adopted the Debt Collection Licensing Act in Senate Bill 908, signed on September 25, 2020, requiring persons engaged in the business of debt collection in California, expressly including debt buyers, to obtain a license from the Department of Financial Protection and Innovation, effective January 1, 2022. Licensing gives a regulator examination, bonding, and revocation authority, rather than replicating the private claim structure of the FDCPA. The scope of the statute is a set of nested boundaries: consumer purpose for the obligation, natural persons for the consumer, principal purpose and owed to another for the collector, six exclusions, a false name provision, and a Supreme Court holding that places the debt buyer who collects for the buyer's own account outside the second prong while leaving the first prong open.

## Communication Rules and Conduct Prohibitions Governing Contact

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act regulates contact through three related provisions. Section 1692c governs communication in connection with debt collection, defining when and where a debt collector may communicate with a consumer, when communication with other persons is barred, and when a consumer may end communication by written notice. Section 1692b governs the acquisition of location information from persons other than the consumer and imposes conditions on every such contact. Section 1692d governs the manner of contact, prohibiting conduct whose natural consequence is to harass, oppress, or abuse any person in connection with the collection of a debt. Precision about which subsection applies matters, because the conditions, the persons protected, and the exceptions differ from one subsection to the next.

### What hours may a debt collector call a consumer?

A debt collector may communicate between 8 a.m. and 9 p.m. local time at the consumer location, unless the collector knows that another time is inconvenient. Section 1692c(a)(1) treats that interval as the assumed convenient time in the absence of knowledge of circumstances to the contrary, measured at the consumer location.

Section 1692c(a) applies without the prior consent of the consumer given directly to the debt collector or the express permission of a court of competent jurisdiction. Within that frame, section 1692c(a)(1) prohibits communication at any unusual time or place, or at a time or place known or which should be known to be inconvenient to the consumer. The knowledge component reaches circumstances that a collector has reason to recognize from information in the collector possession. A consumer statement about work schedules, sleep schedules tied to night work, or medical care gives the collector knowledge that displaces the statutory assumption, because the presumption in the second sentence of section 1692c(a)(1) operates only in the absence of knowledge of circumstances to the contrary.

The time standard is local time at the consumer location rather than local time at the collector office. A call placed at 8:30 a.m. from a collector office may fall before 8 a.m. at the consumer location when the consumer lives in a time zone west of the collector, and a call placed at 8:30 p.m. by collector time may fall after 9 p.m. at the consumer location when the consumer lives east of the collector. Communication at an unusual place, or at a place known or which should be known to be inconvenient, falls within section 1692c(a)(1) independently of the hour, and the statute compiles no list of inconvenient places. For purposes of section 1692c, the term consumer carries the expanded meaning in section 1692c(d), which includes the consumer spouse, a parent if the consumer is a minor, and the consumer guardian, executor, or administrator.

### Representation by an attorney and communication at a place of employment

Section 1692c(a)(2) prohibits direct communication with a consumer in connection with the collection of a debt when the debt collector knows the consumer is represented by an attorney with respect to that debt and has knowledge of, or can readily ascertain, the attorney name and address. The representation must concern the debt at issue, and the knowledge element has two parts, knowledge of the representation and knowledge of, or ready ability to ascertain, the identity and address of the attorney. Direct communication remains permissible when the attorney fails to respond within a reasonable period of time to a communication from the debt collector, and when the attorney consents to direct communication with the consumer. Outside those qualifications, the channel prescribed by the statute runs through the attorney.

Section 1692b(6) imposes a parallel restriction for location information contacts. After a debt collector knows the consumer is represented by an attorney with regard to the subject debt and has knowledge of, or can readily ascertain, the attorney name and address, the collector may not communicate with any person other than that attorney for the purpose of acquiring location information, unless the attorney fails to respond within a reasonable period of time to communication from the debt collector. Attorney representation therefore reorganizes both direct collection communication and third party location inquiries.

Section 1692c(a)(3) prohibits communication at the consumer place of employment if the debt collector knows or has reason to know that the employer prohibits the consumer from receiving such communication. The trigger is employer prohibition, and the knowledge standard reaches both actual knowledge and reason to know. Information that an employer bars personal collection calls at a workplace, whether communicated by the consumer or by the employer, engages the prohibition as the statute phrases the standard. Section 1692c(a)(3) operates alongside section 1692c(a)(1), under which a workplace contact may also occur at a place known to be inconvenient.

### Acquiring location information from other persons

Section 1692b permits third party contact for one defined objective, acquiring location information about the consumer, and surrounds that contact with six conditions. The term location information has the meaning given in section 1692a(7), namely a consumer place of abode and the telephone number at that place, or the consumer place of employment. A communication that moves beyond confirming or correcting that information leaves the frame that section 1692b establishes, and each condition applies to any debt collector communicating with any person other than the consumer for that purpose.

Under section 1692b(1), the collector must identify himself, state that he is confirming or correcting location information concerning the consumer, and identify his employer only if expressly requested. Self identification and a statement of the location purpose are required at the outset, while employer identification waits for an express request. Under section 1692b(2), the collector may not state that the consumer owes any debt. A location inquiry that discloses the existence of a debt, the amount of a debt, or the identity of a creditor as a means of explaining the inquiry exceeds the permission that section 1692b grants, because the statute confines the stated purpose to confirming or correcting location information.

Section 1692b(3) limits repetition. A collector may not communicate with any such person more than once unless the person requests a further communication or unless the debt collector reasonably believes that the earlier response of that person is erroneous or incomplete and that the person has correct or complete location information at the time of the further communication. Both qualifications look to the response already given. A general desire to try again, without a request and without a reasonable belief tied to error or incompleteness in the earlier response, meets neither condition.

Section 1692b(4) prohibits communication by postcard. Section 1692b(5) prohibits use of any language or symbol on any envelope, or in the contents of any communication effected by the mails or telegram, that indicates that the debt collector is in the debt collection business or that the communication relates to the collection of a debt. The postcard rule removes a format whose contents are visible in handling, and the envelope and contents rule removes markings that would disclose the collection purpose to persons who handle or see the mailing or telegram. Together with section 1692b(2), the two provisions keep a location inquiry from announcing a debt to household members, coworkers, or mail handlers. The sixth condition, in section 1692b(6), on communication after knowledge of attorney representation, is addressed with section 1692c(a)(2), because the two provisions impose the same channeling rule in their respective settings.

In Kuntz v. Rodenburg LLP, decided by the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit on September 22, 2016, a collection law firm sought location information about a consumer from the consumer father and, after twelve autodialed attempts without a conversation between December 18, 2013 and January 20, 2014, held a conversation with the father on January 20, 2014. The Eighth Circuit held that two later calls did not violate section 1692b(3) because the firm reasonably believed that the January 20, 2014 response was incomplete and that the father had or could obtain location information. The decision turned on the content of the one completed conversation and on an objective assessment of the belief formed from that conversation.

### Communication about a debt with third parties

Section 1692c(b) states the general bar that section 1692b qualifies for location inquiries. Except as provided in section 1692b, and without the prior consent of the consumer given directly to the debt collector, or the express permission of a court of competent jurisdiction, or communication as reasonably necessary to effectuate a postjudgment judicial remedy, a debt collector may not communicate, in connection with the collection of any debt, with any person other than the consumer, the consumer attorney, a consumer reporting agency if otherwise permitted by law, the creditor, the attorney of the creditor, or the attorney of the debt collector. The list of permitted recipients is closed in structure, and a relative, a neighbor, a friend, or an employer falls outside the list unless a separate basis in the statute applies. The consent exception has a defined form, since consent must be prior consent of the consumer given directly to the debt collector, and consent given to a creditor or to another collector does not, on the statutory wording, substitute for consent given directly to the debt collector that makes the third party communication.

The term communication, as defined in section 1692a(2), means the conveying of information regarding a debt directly or indirectly to any person through any medium. The definition reaches indirect conveyance and contains no limitation to a particular medium, so the analysis under section 1692c(b) turns on whether information regarding a debt was conveyed to a person outside the permitted list rather than on the device or format used. A voicemail message heard by a household member or a statement made to a coworker may present a section 1692c(b) question when information regarding the debt reaches a third party through that medium. Conversely, a contact that conveys no information regarding a debt may fail the definition of communication, a distinction that formed part of the analysis in Kuntz v. Rodenburg LLP for autodialed attempts that conveyed no information about a debt.

The reference to a consumer reporting agency in section 1692c(b) carries the qualifier if otherwise permitted by law, and the provision therefore preserves reporting to a consumer reporting agency within the limits imposed by other law, including the Fair Credit Reporting Act provisions that govern furnishing and use of consumer report information. Credit reporting occupies a distinct channel from direct communication with persons who know the consumer, and the statute bars communication about the debt with persons in the consumer household, workplace, and social circle who hold no listed status.

### Ending communication by written notice

Section 1692c(c) gives a consumer a right to end communication by notice in writing. If a consumer notifies a debt collector in writing that the consumer refuses to pay a debt or that the consumer wishes the debt collector to cease further communication with the consumer, the debt collector shall not communicate further with the consumer with respect to that debt, except within the three exceptions that follow in the statute. The statute specifies that if such notice is made by mail, notification is complete upon receipt. The right therefore attaches upon receipt of a written notice that states either a refusal to pay or a wish that the collector cease further communication. An oral statement during a telephone call does not trigger section 1692c(c) on the statutory text, although oral statements may matter under the inconvenient time and place rule in section 1692c(a)(1) and under the harassment rule in section 1692d.

The three exceptions are narrow, and each describes a single category of closing communication. The first exception permits a communication to advise the consumer that the debt collector further efforts are being terminated. The second exception permits a communication to notify the consumer that the debt collector or creditor may invoke specified remedies which are ordinarily invoked by such debt collector or creditor. The third exception permits a communication, where applicable, to notify the consumer that the debt collector or creditor intends to invoke a specified remedy. The second and third exceptions differ in the commitment conveyed, since a notice under the second exception reports that specified remedies ordinarily invoked may be invoked, while a notice under the third exception reports an intent to invoke a specified remedy. Neither exception authorizes a resumption of collection appeals or repeated contact.

A cease communication notice under section 1692c(c) ends communication about the debt, and the notice does not extinguish the debt itself. Section 1692c(c) regulates communication with respect to a debt, and the section contains no language cancelling the obligation, discharging the balance, barring a creditor from assigning the account, or preventing a lawful action to enforce the obligation through a court. The exceptions for notice about specified remedies presuppose that remedies outside communication remain available after a cease notice takes effect. A consumer that sends a cease notice stops the channel of direct collection communication covered by the section, subject to the three exceptions, while the underlying obligation remains governed by the contract, by state law on enforcement and limitations, and by other provisions of federal law. Treating a cease notice as a discharge misstates the provision and obscures the distinction between silencing collection contact and resolving the debt.

### Harassment, oppression, and abuse in the manner of contact

Section 1692d opens with a general prohibition and then lists conduct that violates the section, with wording that makes the list illustrative rather than exhaustive. A debt collector may not engage in any conduct the natural consequence of which is to harass, oppress, or abuse any person in connection with the collection of a debt, and the enumerated conduct that follows applies without limiting the general application of the opening prohibition. The protected class in section 1692d is any person, a broader formulation than the consumer focused language of section 1692c, so a family member or coworker subjected to harassing conduct in connection with collection may fall within section 1692d even though that person owes no debt.

The first enumerated practice, in section 1692d(1), is the use or threat of use of violence or other criminal means to harm the physical person, reputation, or property of any person. The prohibition reaches threats as well as completed acts, and harm to reputation and property as well as physical harm. The second enumerated practice, in section 1692d(2), is the use of obscene or profane language, or language the natural consequence of which is to abuse the hearer or reader. The third enumerated practice, in section 1692d(3), is the publication of a list of consumers who allegedly refuse to pay debts, except to a consumer reporting agency or to persons meeting the requirements of section 1681a(f) or section 1681b(a)(3) of Title 15. The exception preserves the consumer reporting channel, while publication to employers or other public audiences falls outside the exception. The fourth enumerated practice, in section 1692d(4), is the advertisement for sale of any debt to coerce payment of the debt, which targets use of an advertisement of the debt itself as pressure for payment.

The fifth enumerated practice, in section 1692d(5), is causing a telephone to ring or engaging any person in telephone conversation repeatedly or continuously with intent to annoy, abuse, or harass any person at the called number. The sixth enumerated practice, in section 1692d(6), is, except as provided in section 1692b, the placement of telephone calls without meaningful disclosure of the caller identity. The exception in the sixth paragraph connects back to the location information setting, where section 1692b(1) prescribes a staged identification sequence that withholds employer identification unless expressly requested. Outside that setting, a call placed without meaningful disclosure of the caller identity violates section 1692d(6), and the prohibition covers anonymous calling apart from any question about call frequency.

Section 1692d(5) litigation has turned on intent and on the pattern of calls considered as a whole, rather than on a fixed numerical threshold, because the statutory text states no number. Courts examining volume, frequency, pattern, and substance have treated intent to annoy, abuse, or harass as an element that may be inferred from the circumstances of a calling campaign, including calls placed after a request to stop, calls placed at times known to be inconvenient, and calls placed in rapid succession. In Kuntz v. Rodenburg LLP, the Eighth Circuit addressed section 1692d(5) together with section 1692b(3) and affirmed summary judgment for the collector on a record of twelve autodialed attempts without conversation over about one month, followed by a brief completed conversation and two later calls. The court described the harassment question as fact intensive and held that no reasonable jury could find the requisite level of harassment on that record.

In Lundstedt v. I.C. System, Inc., in a ruling filed on September 27, 2017, the United States District Court for the District of Connecticut considered allegations that a collector called a home telephone twenty nine times over the course of twenty four days in an effort to collect a sum owed to a telephone company. The court concluded that the plaintiff had alleged facts that plausibly showed intent to annoy, harass, and abuse through the number and concentration of calls, and allowed the claim under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act to proceed past judgment on the pleadings while dismissing other claims in the same action. The contrast between the two decisions rests in part on procedural posture, since one decision assessed a summary judgment record and the other assessed allegations, and in part on the density of the calls described in each record. Neither decision converts a call count into a statutory rule.

Regulation F later supplied call frequency presumptions under section 1006.14(b) for the prohibition in section 1692d(5), and the rulemaking discussion in the closing sections of this article treats those presumptions in full.

## Deceptive Representations, Unfair Practices, and the Validation Machinery

Three sets of provisions in the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act control the content of collection, the methods of collection, and the opening exchange between a debt collector and a consumer. Section 1692e governs truthfulness, and a collector that describes a debt, a consequence, a document, or the collector identity inaccurately violates federal law even when the underlying debt exists. Section 1692f governs fairness in method, and a collector that adds an unauthorized charge, mishandles a postdated check, or exposes a debt on a postcard violates federal law even when every statement was accurate. Section 1692g governs the start of the relationship through the validation notice and the dispute procedure. Sections 1692h and 1692i close the sequence with two narrow operational rules, one for payments when a consumer owes multiple debts and one for the place where a collector may file suit. Read together, the provisions place the burden of accuracy on the party with the records.

### False, Deceptive, or Misleading Representations

Section 1692e states a general rule in a single sentence, and the remainder of the section illustrates the rule without limiting the rule. A debt collector may not use any false, deceptive, or misleading representation or means in connection with the collection of any debt. The language reaches words, omissions, documents, letterhead, envelope design, and telephone statements, because Congress wrote the prohibition around representation and means rather than around a single medium. The enumerated paragraphs identify applications that Congress had documented, and conduct outside the enumerated paragraphs can violate the general prohibition.

The provision applied most often in litigation concerns the debt itself. Section 1692e(2)(A) prohibits the false representation of the character, amount, or legal status of any debt. Character concerns what the obligation is, and a representation that an unsecured obligation is secured, or that a discharged obligation remains enforceable as a personal liability, misstates character. Amount concerns how much the collector claims, and a demand that folds in a fee the contract does not authorize, interest at a rate the governing agreement does not support, or a balance that omits payments already credited misstates amount. Legal status concerns enforceability and posture, and a representation that a judgment exists when no judgment was entered, that a lien attaches when no lien was perfected, or that a time barred obligation remains subject to suit in the same manner as an obligation inside the limitations period misstates legal status.

Section 1692e(2)(B) addresses the collector side of the transaction, and the paragraph prohibits the false representation of any services rendered or compensation that may be lawfully received by a debt collector for the collection of a debt. A statement that a collection fee was earned under the agreement when the agreement authorizes no such fee, or that a charge represents a cost actually incurred when no corresponding cost was incurred, falls inside the paragraph. The paragraph pairs with section 1692f(1), which prohibits collection of an unauthorized amount as an unfair practice. A demand for an unauthorized fee is a false representation about compensation under section 1692e(2)(B) and an attempt to collect an amount outside the agreement or governing law under section 1692f(1), because a fee demand injures through deception and through extraction.

Misrepresentation of professional and governmental identity receives separate treatment because identity changes how a consumer evaluates a communication. Section 1692e(3) prohibits the false representation or implication that any individual is an attorney or that any communication is from an attorney. A letter drafted to resemble law firm correspondence, a signature block that suggests bar membership for a person without a license, or a telephone statement that legal counsel prepared a demand when counsel played no role all implicate the paragraph. A consumer who believes a lawyer has reviewed the file may assign the claim a proximity to litigation that a collector letter, accurately labeled, would not carry. Section 1692e(1) covers the parallel governmental claim, and the paragraph prohibits the false representation or implication that the debt collector is vouched for, bonded by, or affiliated with the United States or any state, including use of a badge, uniform, or facsimile of a badge or uniform. Section 1692e(9) extends the governmental concern to paper, and the paragraph prohibits use or distribution of a written communication that simulates or is falsely represented to be a document authorized, issued, or approved by a court, official, or agency of the United States or any state, or that creates a false impression as to source, authorization, or approval. A form styled as an official summons or a seal placed to suggest agency action can violate the paragraph even when the body text contains no express false sentence, because design and format communicate source.

Documents receive two further paragraphs that operate in opposite directions. Section 1692e(13) prohibits the false representation or implication that documents are legal process. A demand package formatted as a summons or a notice headed as a court order, when no court issued process, misleads the recipient about compulsion and the consequences of inaction. Section 1692e(15) prohibits the converse representation, the false representation or implication that documents are not legal process forms or do not require action by the consumer. A collector cannot soften the character of a document that does require a response in order to induce inattention. Read together, the paragraphs require accurate labeling of legal significance in both directions, inflation of a private demand into apparent process and deflation of genuine process into apparent routine correspondence. Section 1692e(14) imposes the same identity discipline for the firm itself, and the paragraph prohibits use of any business, company, or organization name other than the true name of the debt collector business, company, or organization.

Threats form a distinct cluster, and Congress regulated threats through two paragraphs with different tests. Section 1692e(5) prohibits the threat to take any action that cannot legally be taken or that is not intended to be taken. The paragraph has a legality prong and an intent prong, and a threat fails when either prong fails. A threat to garnish wages in a circumstance where garnishment is unavailable for the claim cannot legally be taken. A threat to file suit within a short period, made as a scripted statement on accounts the collector has no intention of suing on, is not intended to be taken. Section 1692e(4) addresses a narrower set of threatened consequences, and the paragraph prohibits the representation or implication that nonpayment will result in arrest or imprisonment of any person or in seizure, garnishment, attachment, or sale of property or wages, unless the action is lawful and the debt collector or creditor intends to take the action. Arrest and imprisonment language carries a particular coercive weight because nonpayment of a private consumer debt, standing alone, is a civil matter. The paragraph conditions even a lawful enforcement consequence, such as garnishment after judgment, on actual intent, so a collector cannot recite the full catalog of postjudgment remedies as a routine warning on accounts where no judgment exists and no enforcement step is planned.

Two paragraphs govern disclosures and information flow. Section 1692e(11) contains the disclosure often called the mini Miranda warning in practitioner usage, a label that refers to a scripted identification duty and not to any constitutional right. In the initial written communication, and in the initial oral communication when the initial communication is oral, the collector must disclose that the debt collector is attempting to collect a debt and that any information obtained will be used for that purpose. In subsequent communications, the collector must disclose that the communication is from a debt collector. The paragraph contains an exception for a formal pleading made in connection with a legal action. The function is notice of role, because a consumer speaking with a collector may volunteer account details or payment information under a mistaken belief about the listener. Section 1692e(8) governs information moving toward consumer reporting agencies and other recipients of credit information. The paragraph prohibits communicating or threatening to communicate credit information that is known or should be known to be false, including the failure to communicate that a disputed debt is disputed. A collector that reports a debt after the consumer disputed the debt, and that reports the debt without the disputed status, violates the paragraph through omission. The dispute notation matters because a report shapes decisions by readers who lack access to the underlying correspondence.

The section reaches deceptive devices through a residual paragraph and a separate forms provision. Section 1692e(10) prohibits the use of any false representation or deceptive means to collect or attempt to collect any debt or to obtain information concerning a consumer, and courts apply the paragraph to pretext devices and documents engineered to extract information under a false premise. Section 1692j reaches a person who never contacts the consumer at all. The section makes unlawful the design, compilation, and furnishing of any form knowing that the form would be used to create the false belief in a consumer that a person other than the creditor is participating in the collection of or in an attempt to collect a debt the consumer allegedly owes the creditor, when in fact the person is not so participating. The classic application involves forms supplied by a vendor and mailed by a creditor, where the forms suggest that an outside collection agency or attorney has taken over the account and the outside participant has no actual role. Congress imposed liability on the furnisher to the same extent and in the same manner as a debt collector under section 1692k, and the extension prevents evasion through outsourcing of deceptive paper.


### Unfair or Unconscionable Means

Section 1692f states a general prohibition on unfair or unconscionable means to collect or attempt to collect any debt, and the section then enumerates applications in the same nonlimiting structure that section 1692e uses. Section 1692f(1) carries the greatest financial consequence among the enumerated paragraphs, and the paragraph prohibits the collection of any amount, including any interest, fee, charge, or expense incidental to the principal obligation, unless the amount is expressly authorized by the agreement creating the debt or permitted by law. The test looks to two sources and to no other source. A contract clause that authorizes a specific charge supports collection of that charge, and governing law that permits a charge supports collection even when the agreement is silent. Collector custom, internal fee schedules, and amounts a predecessor demanded supply no authorization.

Payment instruments receive three paragraphs because a postdated check in collector hands creates a risk of pressure through timing and through criminal exposure. Section 1692f(2) regulates acceptance of a check or other payment instrument postdated by more than five days, and a collector may accept such an instrument only when the person who gave the instrument receives written notice of the collector intent to deposit, given not more than ten nor less than three business days before deposit. Section 1692f(3) prohibits solicitation of a postdated check or other postdated payment instrument for the purpose of threatening or instituting criminal prosecution, a practice that converts a payment device into a threat device. Section 1692f(4) prohibits depositing or threatening to deposit a postdated check or other postdated payment instrument before the date on the check or instrument. The three paragraphs allocate distinct duties, a notice duty on acceptance, a purpose prohibition on solicitation, and a timing prohibition on deposit, and a collector can comply with one paragraph and violate another on the same instrument.

The remaining paragraphs of section 1692f address charges imposed through concealment, property dispossession outside court process, and exposure of the debt through the communication channel itself. Section 1692f(5) prohibits causing charges to be made to any person for communications by concealment of the true purpose of the communication, and the statute gives collect telephone calls and telegram fees as examples. A collector that arranges contact so that the consumer pays for the call, without revealing that collection is the purpose, shifts the cost of collection through concealment of purpose. Section 1692f(6) prohibits taking or threatening to take nonjudicial action to effect dispossession or disablement of property in three circumstances, where no present right to possession of property claimed as collateral exists through an enforceable security interest, where no present intention to take possession exists, or where the property is exempt by law from dispossession or disablement. The paragraph leaves judicial process intact and regulates self help. Section 1692f(7) prohibits communicating with a consumer regarding a debt by postcard, a medium that exposes the message to postal workers, household members, and any other person who handles the card. Section 1692f(8) applies the same exposure principle to envelopes, and the paragraph prohibits using any language or symbol, other than the debt collector address, on any envelope when communicating with a consumer through the mails or by telegram, with an allowance for use of a business name when the name does not indicate that the business is debt collection.

### What Is a Validation Notice and How Does Debt Validation Work?

A validation notice is a written notice a debt collector must send within five days after the initial communication, stating the amount owed, the creditor name, and the consumer right to dispute in writing within thirty days and obtain verification before collection resumes on the disputed portion.

Section 1692g(a) specifies five contents for the notice, and omission of a required content is a defect in the notice rather than a defect in style. The notice must contain the amount of the debt and the name of the creditor to whom the debt is owed. The notice must contain a statement that unless the consumer, within thirty days after receipt of the notice, disputes the validity of the debt or any portion of the debt, the debt will be assumed to be valid by the debt collector. The notice must contain a statement that if the consumer notifies the debt collector in writing within the thirty day period that the debt or any portion of the debt is disputed, the debt collector will obtain verification of the debt or a copy of a judgment against the consumer, and a copy of the verification or judgment will be mailed to the consumer by the debt collector. The notice must contain a statement that, upon the consumer written request within the thirty day period, the debt collector will provide the consumer with the name and address of the original creditor, if different from the creditor named as the creditor to whom the debt is owed. The five day duty has two qualifications stated in the statute. No separate notice is required when the initial communication contained the required information, and no notice is required when the consumer paid the debt before the notice was due. A communication in the form of a formal pleading in a civil action is not treated as an initial communication for purposes of subsection (a) under section 1692g(d).

Section 1692g(b) gives the written dispute legal effect. If the consumer notifies the debt collector in writing within the thirty day period that the debt or any portion of the debt is disputed, or that the consumer requests the name and address of the original creditor, the debt collector must cease collection of the debt or the disputed portion until the debt collector obtains verification of the debt or a copy of a judgment, or the name and address of the original creditor, and mails a copy of the verification or judgment, or the name and address of the original creditor, to the consumer. The pause attaches to the disputed portion, so a dispute addressed to a fee component or an interest calculation pauses collection on that portion under the statutory text. Collection activities and communications that do not otherwise violate the subchapter may continue during the thirty day period unless the consumer gave the written dispute or the written request for original creditor information, and the statute imposes no deadline on the collector for producing verification. The consequence of delay falls on collection, because collection on the disputed portion remains paused for as long as verification remains unmailed.

Two clarifications prevent the most persistent misreading of the validation period, and both clarifications appear in the statute. The thirty day period is a dispute window that triggers verification rights, and the period is not a payment deadline. No sentence in section 1692g requires payment within thirty days, sets a due date thirty days after receipt, or imposes a penalty for disputing rather than paying. The assumption language in section 1692g(a)(3) describes an assumption by the debt collector for collection purposes, and section 1692g(c) provides that the failure of a consumer to dispute the validity of a debt under the section may not be construed by any court as an admission of liability by the consumer. A consumer who misses the written dispute window loses the automatic pause and the statutory verification mailing that a timely written dispute triggers, and the missed window does not establish liability, validate an inaccurate amount, or extinguish defenses to the underlying obligation in court.

The overshadowing doctrine protects the notice against dilution by the rest of a collection letter or by collection activity during the dispute window. A validation notice that is printed in the required words but contradicted by a demand for payment in a shorter period, or paired with a statement that dispute requires a telephone call rather than writing, fails to convey the right in usable form. Courts developed the doctrine before Congress codified the governing sentence, and in Swanson v. Southern Oregon Credit Service, Inc., 869 F.2d 1222 (9th Cir. 1988), the Ninth Circuit addressed a collection notice that contradicted the validation disclosure through a demand framed on a shorter timetable. Congress amended section 1692g(b) in 2006 to state the rule in statutory text, providing that any collection activities and communication during the thirty day period may not overshadow or be inconsistent with the disclosure of the consumer right to dispute the debt or request the name and address of the original creditor. Overshadowing is a test of the communication as received.

### Payment Application on Multiple Debts and Venue for Collection Suits

Section 1692h governs a narrow accounting decision that carries direct consequences for balances, interest, and credit reporting. If a consumer owes multiple debts and makes a single payment to a debt collector with respect to the debts, the debt collector may not apply the payment to any debt disputed by the consumer and, where applicable, must apply the payment in accordance with the consumer directions. Direction controls allocation, and the disputed debt exclusion controls even when allocation would otherwise follow a collector default order based on age, balance size, or fee generation. A collector that applies a directed payment to a different account, or that directs funds toward a disputed balance, violates the section through bookkeeping rather than through a communication.

Section 1692i governs forum. A debt collector that brings a legal action on a debt against a consumer must bring an action to enforce an interest in real property securing the consumer obligation only in the judicial district or similar legal entity where the real property is located. For any other action, the collector must bring the action only in the judicial district or similar legal entity where the consumer signed the contract sued upon or where the consumer resides at the commencement of the action. The venue rule prevents a collector from filing in a distant forum that raises the cost of defense above the amount in dispute and produces default judgments through distance rather than through adjudication. Section 1692i(b) adds a limiting sentence, and the sentence provides that nothing in the subchapter shall be construed to authorize the bringing of legal actions by debt collectors. The section restricts forum for suits a collector is otherwise authorized to bring, and the section grants no suit authority of its own.

## How Courts Judge Deception, Who Counts as a Collector, and What a Violation Costs

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act states prohibitions in general terms. Section 1692e forbids a false, deceptive, or misleading representation or means in connection with the collection of a debt, section 1692d forbids conduct the natural consequence of which is to harass, oppress, or abuse a person, and section 1692f forbids an unfair or unconscionable means to collect a debt. None of those provisions defines deception from the perspective of a particular reader. The courts of appeals supplied that standard, and the Supreme Court supplied boundary decisions on who counts as a debt collector, which defenses remain available, and when the period for filing suit begins. Sections 1692k and 1692l then determine what a violation costs and which public officials may act apart from a private lawsuit.

### What is the least sophisticated consumer standard?

Courts judge a collection letter or call by asking how the least sophisticated consumer would understand the communication, rather than how a lawyer or careful reader would parse the wording. The test is objective. It protects naive consumers, while presuming that the communication receives a basic reading and a willingness to read with care.

The leading exposition came from the Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit in Clomon v. Jackson, 988 F.2d 1314 (2d Cir. 1993). Christ Clomon received collection letters on the letterhead of Philip D. Jackson, an attorney who served part time as general counsel to a collection agency. The letters bore Jackson's signature and used language suggesting that Jackson had reviewed the individual file and directed the next measure. Jackson had approved the form of the letters, but the evidence showed that Jackson played virtually no day to day role in the process, reviewed no individual files, and decided neither which letters were sent nor which recipients received a letter. The Second Circuit held that the letters violated section 1692e, including the prohibition on a false representation that a communication is from an attorney. The court reasoned that the Act protects the gullible as well as the shrewd, and that a court therefore measures a communication against the understanding of the least sophisticated consumer rather than a typical or average reader.

Three features of the Clomon formulation shaped litigation elsewhere. The test is objective: the question is not whether the named plaintiff felt misled, because the court asks whether a hypothetical consumer at the low end of sophistication would draw the false inference the communication invites. The test has a floor: the least sophisticated consumer is presumed to possess rudimentary knowledge about the financial world and a willingness to read a collection notice with some care, so a court does not credit a bizarre or idiosyncratic reading. The test turns on net impression: an accurate sentence can contribute to a violation when the surrounding language, letterhead, and signature block combine to imply a false fact, such as meaningful attorney review of an individual account.

The standard differs from the reasonable person test of tort and contract law in the direction of protection and in the knowledge attributed to the reader. A reasonable person test asks how a person of ordinary prudence and ordinary knowledge would understand a statement, and a defendant can defeat a claim by showing that an ordinary reader would have noticed a qualification or reconciled an inconsistency. The consumer standard asks how a consumer with little experience of financial documents, little familiarity with legal process, and a tendency to take a collector's statements at face value would understand the same statement. A qualification in smaller type, a disclosure that contradicts the dominant message of a letter, or a threat phrased to imply a lawsuit the collector has no authority to file can fail under the consumer standard even when a court might excuse the same language under a reasonable person test. Congress directed the prohibitions at communications that reach consumers across the full range of literacy and financial experience, and the circuit courts read the prohibitions to protect the consumers most likely to be misled.

The circuits phrase the standard in two principal forms. Most circuits refer to the least sophisticated consumer or, in some opinions, the least sophisticated debtor. The Third Circuit applied the least sophisticated debtor formulation in Wilson v. Quadramed Corp., 225 F.3d 350 (3d Cir. 2000), in a dispute over whether collection language overshadowed the validation notice required by section 1692g. The Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit reformulated the test in Gammon v. GC Services Ltd. Partnership, 27 F.3d 1254 (7th Cir. 1994), as the perspective of the unsophisticated consumer, with an express element of reasonableness. The Seventh Circuit reasoned that the word least, read at its extreme, could describe a single inattentive person at the bottom of a distribution and impose liability for a reading no other consumer would adopt. Under Gammon, the hypothetical consumer is unsophisticated but reasonable, and a collector is protected against an unrealistic or peculiar interpretation. The Seventh Circuit treated the change as a clarification, and held in Gammon that an unsophisticated consumer could reasonably read a collector's reference to systems supplied to government tax agencies as an implication of government affiliation or endorsement prohibited by section 1692e.

Courts apply the same perspective to claims that a letter overshadowed the validation rights in section 1692g, to claims of a threatened action the collector did not intend or had no legal right to take, and to format and envelope claims under section 1692f. The inquiry remains anchored to the hypothetical consumer rather than to the collector's intent, and a collector's honest belief that a letter was clear carries little weight when the text conveys a false impression from the protected perspective. The Act treats most violations as matters of strict liability, and intent goes to the size of an award and to a single, narrow defense.

### Attorneys, Foreclosure Firms, and Mistakes of Law in the Supreme Court

The Court decided Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291 (1995), on April 18, 1995. Attorney George Heintz, representing a bank that had sued Darlene Jenkins over a defaulted car loan, sent a letter including in the amount due the cost of insurance the bank had purchased after Jenkins failed to maintain insurance. Jenkins sued, and the district court dismissed on the ground that the Act did not apply to attorneys engaged in litigation. The Supreme Court reversed in an opinion by Justice Breyer and held that the Act applies to attorneys who regularly engage in consumer debt collection activity, even when that activity consists of litigation. The definition in section 1692a(6) reaches a person who regularly collects or attempts to collect debts owed to another, and contains no exception for attorneys or litigation conduct. An earlier version had exempted an attorney collecting a debt on behalf of and in the name of a client, and Congress repealed that exemption in 1986 without substituting a narrower litigation exemption. A firm that regularly files collection suits or sends demand letters must therefore comply with the communication, harassment, and unfair practices provisions in the same manner as a nonlawyer agency.

The Court narrowed the principal defense in Jerman v. Carlisle, McNellie, Rini, Kramer and Ulrich LPA, 559 U.S. 573 (2010), decided on April 21, 2010. A law firm filed a foreclosure complaint against Karen Jerman, and the attached validation notice stated that the debt would be assumed valid unless Jerman disputed the debt in writing. Jerman disputed the debt, the creditor acknowledged payment, and the firm withdrew the foreclosure. Jerman sued, contending that section 1692g permits an oral dispute and that the notice violated the Act by imposing a writing requirement. The district court found a violation and then granted summary judgment to the firm under the bona fide error defense in section 1692k(c). The Supreme Court held that the defense does not apply to a violation resulting from a mistaken interpretation of the Act itself. Justice Sotomayor's opinion reasoned that bona fide error, read with the requirement of procedures reasonably adapted to avoid the error, addresses clerical, factual, and procedural mistakes, such as a miscalculated balance or a letter mailed to a wrong address from a faulty record. Legal interpretation does not lend itself to that kind of procedural safeguard. Justice Kennedy, joined by Justice Alito, dissented and would have allowed the defense to reach a good faith mistake of law. After Jerman, advice of counsel remains relevant to intent and to the amount of additional damages, but advice of counsel does not supply a defense to liability.

The Court addressed foreclosure firms in Obduskey v. McCarthy and Holthus LLP, decided on March 20, 2019. Wells Fargo Bank hired the firm to carry out a nonjudicial foreclosure on a Colorado home owned by Dennis Obduskey, a process in which a public trustee conducts a sale outside a court proceeding. Obduskey invoked section 1692g(b), which obliges a debt collector receiving a timely written dispute to cease collection until verification of the debt is obtained and mailed to the consumer. The firm proceeded without supplying verification, and Obduskey sued. The Supreme Court affirmed dismissal in a unanimous opinion by Justice Breyer, holding that a business engaged in no more than nonjudicial foreclosure proceedings is not a debt collector under the primary definition in section 1692a(6), except for the limited purpose of section 1692f(6). The reasoning turned on a sentence in section 1692a(6) stating, for the purpose of section 1692f(6), that debt collector also includes a person whose business has as its principal purpose the enforcement of security interests. The word also, confined to one subsection, indicated that a security interest enforcer falls outside the primary definition. The Court also cited a federalism concern over conflicts between federal validation rules and state foreclosure procedure. The holding is bounded: a firm that seeks payment from the borrower or conducts ordinary collection activity apart from foreclosure can fall within the primary definition, and the Court reserved the question of judicial foreclosure.

The Court fixed the start of the limitations period in Rotkiske v. Klemm, 589 U.S. 8 (2019), decided on December 10, 2019. A collection firm sued Kevin Rotkiske over credit card debt, and process was served at an address where Rotkiske no longer lived and accepted by a different person. Rotkiske did not respond, the firm obtained a default judgment in 2009, and Rotkiske stated that he first learned of the judgment in 2014, when a mortgage application was denied. Rotkiske sued under the Act in 2015. Section 1692k(d) permits an action within one year from the date on which the violation occurs. The Supreme Court affirmed dismissal in an opinion by Justice Thomas, holding that, absent an equitable doctrine, the period begins on the date the violation occurs and not on the date the consumer discovers the violation. The word occurs has a plain meaning tied to the event, and Congress wrote discovery based triggers in other statutes when Congress intended one. The decision resolved a division in which the Fourth and Ninth Circuits had applied a discovery rule. The Court distinguished a general discovery rule from equitable doctrines tied to fraudulent concealment, and did not decide a fraud specific rule on the facts because Rotkiske had not preserved that theory on appeal. Justice Ginsburg dissented in part and would have applied an equitable, fraud based rule where the alleged misconduct prevented discovery. A consumer who learns of a violation after the first anniversary of the act therefore holds no federal claim for that act, absent tolling on particular facts.

### Damages, Attorney Fees, and the Bona Fide Error Defense

Section 1692k(a) makes a noncomplying debt collector liable for three components. The first is any actual damage sustained as a result of the failure, covering out of pocket loss such as a payment on a debt that was not owed, and, on supporting evidence, emotional distress. The second is additional damages, commonly called statutory damages because no quantified loss need be proved. In an individual action, section 1692k(a)(2)(A) permits additional damages as the court allows, up to $1,000 for the action. The ceiling applies to the action rather than to each violation, so a plaintiff who proves five violations faces the same ceiling as a plaintiff who proves one. In a class action, section 1692k(a)(2)(B) permits an amount for each named plaintiff as could be recovered in an individual action, plus an amount for all other class members, without regard to a minimum individual recovery, up to the lesser of $500,000 or 1 percent of the net worth of the debt collector. The third component, in a successful action, is costs and a reasonable attorney fee as determined by the court under section 1692k(a)(3).

Section 1692k(b) directs the court in setting additional damages. In an individual action the court considers, among other relevant factors, the frequency and persistence of noncompliance, the nature of the noncompliance, and the extent to which the noncompliance was intentional. In a class action the court considers those factors plus the resources of the debt collector and the number of persons adversely affected. A single inadvertent misstatement, corrected promptly, supports an award at the low end. Repeated calls after a written instruction to stop, or a form letter sent to thousands of consumers misstating the amount due, supports an award at or near the individual ceiling and a substantial award within the class ceiling, which also limits a small agency's exposure for a technical violation that reached a large mailing list.

Fee shifting carries more weight than the ceilings suggest. An individual claim often involves a modest loss: a fee that should not have been charged, wages lost answering repeated calls, or distress from a threat that produced no transaction. Litigation over such a loss, compensated only by the loss and at most $1,000, would cost more in attorney time than the maximum recovery could repay, and a consumer paying counsel by the hour would lose money by winning. Section 1692k(a)(3) reverses that arithmetic. A successful plaintiff recovers a reasonable fee, calculated in most districts as a reasonable hourly rate multiplied by hours reasonably expended, in addition to damages and costs. Fee awards allow consumer attorneys to accept individual cases on a contingent expectation of a statutory fee and give a collector reason to settle a meritorious claim early. The structure is plaintiff sided by design. A prevailing defendant obtains fees only under the second sentence of section 1692k(a)(3), on a finding that the action was brought in bad faith and for the purpose of harassment. A defendant that wins on the merits, without that finding, bears its own fees.

Section 1692k(c) contains the bona fide error defense construed in Jerman. A collector escapes liability by showing by a preponderance of the evidence that the violation was not intentional, resulted from a bona fide error, and occurred notwithstanding the maintenance of procedures reasonably adapted to avoid any such error. The collector must identify a genuine error, such as a clerical transposition or a record that carried a paid balance forward as open, and must prove procedures in place at the time of the violation, ordinarily through written policies, training records, and audit practices. A procedure adopted after suit does not satisfy the defense for the violation that prompted the suit. Section 1692k(e) separately protects an act done or omitted in good faith in conformity with an advisory opinion of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, even when the opinion is later amended, rescinded, or held invalid. The defense covers factual and procedural mistakes inside a documented compliance system; the advisory opinion provision covers conduct that followed formal agency guidance; neither protects a reading of the statute that a court later rejects.

### Public Enforcement by Federal Agencies and the States

Section 1692l, captioned administrative enforcement, assigns public enforcement across the federal government, and sections 1692n and 1692o govern the relation to state law. The original allocation reflected the regulatory structure of 1977: no single agency supervised the collection industry as a whole, so Congress routed enforcement through agencies that already supervised institutions close to each class of collector.

Section 1692l(a) authorizes the Federal Trade Commission to enforce compliance, except where enforcement is specifically committed to another agency under subsection (b). For the Commission's functions under the Federal Trade Commission Act, a violation of the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act is deemed an unfair or deceptive act or practice in violation of that Act, and the Commission may use all functions and powers under that Act, irrespective of whether the person is engaged in commerce or meets other jurisdictional tests in that Act, including enforcement in the same manner as if the violation had been a violation of a Federal Trade Commission trade regulation rule. Subsection (b) assigns enforcement for regulated institutions to the agencies that supervise them: the appropriate federal banking agency and the National Credit Union Administration act under section 8 of the Federal Deposit Insurance Act and parallel provisions for depository institutions and credit unions, and other paragraphs assign sectors such as motor carriers, air carriers, and persons subject to the Packers and Stockyards Act to the agencies with authority over those sectors. The Commission served as general enforcer for independent agencies and collection law firms outside the supervised banking system. State attorneys general enforced parallel state collection statutes, many covering original creditors and conduct the federal statute did not reach.

The original statute withheld general rulemaking power from every agency in that roster. Former section 1692l(d) provided that neither the Commission nor any other agency referred to in subsection (b) could promulgate trade regulation rules or other regulations with respect to the collection of debts by debt collectors. The Commission could issue advisory opinions and administered the exemption process for state regulated classes of practices, but no agency could write substantive rules defining how a collector must validate a debt or disclose the status of a time barred debt. Interpretive development occurred through case law and enforcement actions, while the statutory text remained the sole source of substantive obligations from 1977 until the amendments of 2010 took effect.

The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act, enacted on July 21, 2010, changed that allocation. Section 1089 of the Dodd-Frank Act amended section 1692l(d) to replace the prohibition with a grant: except as provided in section 1029(a) of the Consumer Financial Protection Act of 2010, the Bureau of Consumer Financial Protection may prescribe rules with respect to the collection of debts by debt collectors. The transfer of rulemaking authority took effect on July 21, 2011. The Bureau restated the inherited exemption procedures as Regulation F in 2011 and later exercised substantive rulemaking authority under the amended provision. Conforming changes substituted the Bureau for the Commission in the annual reporting function in section 1692m and in the advisory opinion provision. The Commission retained enforcement authority under section 1692l(a). After the 2011 transfer, the Bureau writes rules and reports to Congress, the Bureau and the Commission both enforce the statute and its rules, and the sectoral agencies in subsection (b) retain enforcement for supervised institutions.

State law interacts with that structure through preservation and exemption, mechanisms that point in opposite directions. Section 1692n provides that the federal statute does not annul, alter, or affect state debt collection laws except to the extent of an inconsistency, and that a state law is not inconsistent when the protection it affords a consumer is greater than federal protection. A state may impose stricter limits, license collectors, extend coverage to original creditors, or provide higher damages, and a collector must comply with both regimes. Section 1692o supplies the exemption: the Bureau shall by regulation exempt a class of debt collection practices within a state when state law subjects that class to requirements substantially similar to the federal requirements and adequate provision for enforcement exists. Before the Dodd-Frank amendments the Commission held the same function, under procedures the Bureau carried into Regulation F. An exemption removes the specified class from federal requirements because state law already replicates them; preservation leaves both regimes in force, and the ordinary position for a collector is cumulative compliance.

## The Rulemaking Gap and the Arrival of Regulation F

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act spent most of its life as a statute without rules. Congress enacted the act on September 20, 1977, and the act took effect in March 1978, but the text contained no grant of general rulemaking authority to any agency under the act itself. The statute specified prohibited conduct, validation duties, and remedies in detail, and left its general phrases, such as harassment and unfair practice, to be worked out case by case. The one rulemaking function the original text did assign concerned state exemptions. The Federal Trade Commission could determine that a class of debt collection practices within a state was subject to requirements substantially similar to the federal requirements, with adequate provision for enforcement, and exempt that class from the federal act. That was a narrow determination, not authority to write substantive rules governing collectors across the country.

Enforcement sat with the commission, which approached a violation of the collection statute through the framework it used for unfair and deceptive practices under the Federal Trade Commission Act. The commission brought actions, reported to Congress, and answered interpretive questions, but it could not convert its answers into binding legislative rules. The Government Accountability Office later identified the missing authority as a practical constraint on addressing the adequacy of account information supplied to collectors and the use of new communication technologies. In a February 2009 report, the commission recommended that Congress give it rulemaking authority under the act, so legal requirements could keep pace with changes in the marketplace rather than waiting for litigation to settle each new practice.

What guidance existed took a deliberately nonbinding form. In 1988 the commission's staff published the Staff Commentary on the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, cited at volume 53 of the Federal Register, page 50097. The commentary worked through the statute section by section and stated the staff's reading of disputed questions. The document stated that it was not binding on the commission or on the public, and courts treated it accordingly. In Heintz v. Jenkins, decided in 1995, the Supreme Court declined to give conclusive weight to a commentary passage, noting the disclaimer and the absence of any indication that Congress had authorized the commission to create the exception described. The practical result was a federal collection law whose operative detail accumulated in court decisions, consent orders, and staff opinion letters.

### Why did it take more than forty years for debt collection rules to be written?

The statute gave no agency power to write binding rules under the act itself, so interpretation came from courts and nonbinding staff guidance. That arrangement changed when the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 transferred rulemaking authority to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, which issued the first comprehensive rules in 2020.

The delay had a structural cause. Rulemaking requires a rulemaker, and for more than three decades the collection statute had none with general authority. The commission asked for that authority in the 2009 report, evidence that the gap was recognized inside the enforcing agency well before Congress acted. When Congress did act, it acted as part of a larger reorganization of consumer financial law rather than through a collection specific bill. The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act, Public Law 111-203, enacted on July 21, 2010, created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and listed the collection act among the enumerated consumer laws transferred to the new bureau. Section 1089 of the Dodd-Frank Act amended the collection statute itself, and section 1061 transferred the commission's functions under the enumerated laws, including the state exemption determination, to the bureau. Readers tracing how that transfer reshaped federal oversight can follow [the account of the bureau's creation and powers](/2013/12/01/cfpb-creation-and-powers/), which sets out the institutional design that made a collection rulemaking possible for the first time.

The transfer took legal effect on the designated transfer date of July 21, 2011. From that date, the bureau held authority to prescribe rules with respect to the collection of debts by debt collectors, and the bureau republished the inherited exemption procedures as part of a new Regulation F at title 12 of the Code of Federal Regulations, part 1006. The bureau also acquired a broader instrument. The Dodd-Frank Act empowered the bureau to identify and prevent unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts or practices in connection with consumer financial products and services, a category reaching collection conduct by entities the collection statute's definitions do not cover, including many original creditors. The commission retained enforcement authority, so the post 2011 arrangement divided labor rather than relocating it.

### What Regulation F settled about communication, validation, and time-barred debt

The bureau issued the first final rule on October 30, 2020, addressing communications and the prohibitions on harassment, false or misleading representations, and unfair practices, and issued the second final rule on December 18, 2020, addressing validation information, disclosures, and time-barred debt. Both rules amended Regulation F, 12 C.F.R. part 1006, and both took effect on November 30, 2021, after the bureau announced on July 30, 2021 that a proposed postponement would not be adopted. The two rules together were the first comprehensive rules under a statute enacted in 1977, more than four decades earlier.

The October 2020 rule converted the harassment prohibition into telephone call presumptions. A collector who places more than seven telephone calls within seven consecutive days in connection with a particular debt is presumed to have violated the prohibition on conduct the natural consequence of which is to harass, oppress, or abuse, as is a collector who calls within seven consecutive days after a telephone conversation with the consumer about that debt. A collector who places seven or fewer calls within seven consecutive days, and who observes the interval after a conversation, is presumed to comply. The presumptions are rebuttable and apply per debt rather than per consumer. The rule replaced case law in which courts assessed call volume after the fact and substituted a number a collection operation could program into its dialing systems in advance.

Electronic communication received a framework of the same practical character. Regulation F permits collectors to use email and text messages under specified procedures, requires a reasonable and simple method for the consumer to opt out of communications sent to a particular address or number, and supplies procedures intended to prevent disclosure of the debt to third parties through a misdirected message. The rules also restrict social media messages viewable by the general public or by the consumer's contacts. A limited-content message, defined by regulation, gives a collector a way to request a reply without revealing the debt to a third party who hears or reads the message. Such a message may contain only specified items, such as the consumer's name, a request that the consumer reply, the name of the collector, and a telephone number. A message meeting the definition is not treated as a communication in connection with the collection of a debt for purposes of the act's third party disclosure restrictions, which resolved a recurring voicemail problem under the pre rule case law.

The December 2020 rule rebuilt validation around a standard set of validation information. A collector must provide an itemization of the debt, including the itemization date, the amount owed on that date, and the charges and payments since that date, together with the name of the creditor, account number information, and information about consumer protections and how the consumer may dispute the debt. The bureau published a model validation notice, designated Model Form B-1, and a collector that uses the model form, or a form substantially similar to it, receives a safe harbor for compliance with the validation information requirements. The same rule prohibits a collector from bringing or threatening to bring a legal action against a consumer to collect a time-barred debt, meaning debt for which the applicable statute of limitations has expired. The December rule also prohibited furnishing information about a debt to a consumer reporting agency before the collector had spoken with the consumer about the debt or had mailed a notice and allowed a reasonable period for delivery.

### Amendments across the life of the statute

Between enactment and Regulation F, Congress amended the act infrequently and narrowly. The most substantial pre 2010 revision came in the Financial Services Regulatory Relief Act of 2006, Public Law 109-351, enacted on October 13, 2006. Section 802 of that law added a provision, codified at section 809(d) of the collection act, stating that a communication in the form of a formal pleading in a civil action is not treated as an initial communication for validation purposes. The same section added a related provision for certain notices a collector is required to send under other federal or state laws, so a legally required notice would not by itself trigger the validation machinery. The 2006 law also wrote into the disputed debt provision a statement that collection activities during the thirty day validation period may continue, provided they do not overshadow or contradict the disclosure of the consumer's right to dispute the debt. That sentence codified a doctrine courts had developed under the name of overshadowing. Section 801 of the 2006 law excluded from the definition of debt collector certain private entities that operate bad check enforcement programs for a state or district attorney, under conditions the new provision specified.

The Dodd-Frank amendments of 2010 were institutional rather than substantive in most respects. Section 1089 amended the administrative enforcement provisions of the act, adjusted the state exemption provision to substitute the bureau for the commission as the deciding authority, and supplied the rulemaking grant the original text lacked. The amendments also required the bureau to report annually to Congress on compliance with the act. One casualty of the transfer was the interpretive arrangement built around the commission's staff. The 1988 Staff Commentary had been written for a statute administered by the commission, and once rulemaking authority and the official interpretation function moved to the bureau, the commentary ceased to be the operative federal gloss on the text. Regulation F carries its own official interpretations in a supplement to part 1006, issued by the agency that wrote the rules. The enforcement structure that resulted is shared, between the bureau, the commission, state attorneys general, and private plaintiffs.

## The State Layer, the Measured Record, and the Statute's Limits

### Why the creditor boundary is not a lawless zone

The federal act's best known boundary is the debt collector definition, treated in full in the scope discussion, which generally excludes a creditor collecting a debt owed to itself in its own name. A reader who stops at that definition can come away with the impression that abusive collection by an original creditor sits outside the law. That impression is wrong in degree. The accurate statement is that the federal collection statute has a definitional boundary, not that no law applies beyond it.

State law supplies much of what sits beyond it. Every state has an unfair and deceptive acts and practices statute of general application, and deceptive conduct by an original creditor in collecting a consumer debt can fall within those statutes whether or not the creditor qualifies as a debt collector under federal law. Many states also have debt collection statutes of their own, and a number define the persons covered more broadly than the federal definition. California's Rosenthal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act is the standard example. The Rosenthal Act, at sections 1788 and following of the California Civil Code, incorporates the substantive standards of specified sections of the federal act by reference, so a covered person must comply with federal collection standards as a matter of California law, and its definition of debt collector reaches a person who, in the ordinary course of business, regularly engages in debt collection on behalf of that person or of others. Original creditors collecting their own accounts in California are therefore subject to the incorporated federal standards through state law, even though the federal act itself does not reach them. The comparison between [the federal framework and the state statutes that supplement it](/2019/02/01/federal-vs-state-consumer-protection/) shows a layered system in which the federal act functions as a floor for third party collection and state law determines how much of a creditor's own collection activity is regulated, and by whom.

Federal law beyond the collection act supplies a further layer. The bureau's authority under the Dodd-Frank Act to prohibit unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts or practices applies to covered persons offering or providing consumer financial products and services, a category that includes creditors collecting their own debts in many settings. The Federal Trade Commission Act's prohibition on unfair or deceptive acts or practices performs a similar function for entities within the commission's jurisdiction. A creditor that misstates the amount owed or threatens action it cannot take may face liability under one of these general prohibitions, or under state law, even where a claim under the federal collection act would fail at the definitional threshold. The boundary matters for which damages provisions and which regulator apply. It does not create an unregulated space.

### What the complaint record and the collection function show

Measured effects begin with the complaint record, because complaints are the evidence both regulators publish in the greatest volume. The two principal federal sources count differently and should not be merged. The bureau's annual report on the act for calendar year 2023, released on September 5, 2024, reported roughly 68,000 debt collection complaints received in that year, and that medical debt complaints made up 11 percent of that total, with a recurring complaint concerning debts already paid. The Federal Trade Commission's Consumer Sentinel Network, which aggregates reports from consumers and contributing agencies, recorded 124,450 reports in the debt collection category in calendar year 2023, according to the commission's Consumer Sentinel Network Data Book for 2023. The gap reflects differences in intake and sources, and neither figure is a count of proven violations. The broader pattern in [the record of consumer credit law's effects on households and markets](/2019/01/01/consumer-credit-law-impact/) is that complaint volume about collection has remained substantial across decades of complete change in collection technology.

The findings behind the complaints identify a recurring mechanism. The bureau has reported that collectors frequently receive accounts with limited or inaccurate information about the amount owed, the identity of the consumer, or payments already made, and that collection then proceeds against the wrong person, for the wrong amount, or on a debt the collector has no right to collect. The Government Accountability Office reached a related conclusion for credit card debt, reporting that agencies and debt buyers often lacked documentation needed to verify a debt, with verification growing harder as accounts were sold and resold.

The collection function has a legitimate side that the same record should not obscure. Debts owed and unpaid represent losses to the creditor that extended credit, supplied goods, or provided services, and a system in which valid debts could not be collected at all would price that loss into credit decisions. ACA International, the trade association for the collection industry, states the industry position in those terms, arguing that unrecovered consumer debt increases the price of goods and services to cover creditor losses and makes credit more expensive and less available to consumers. A study released by ACA International and authored by PricewaterhouseCoopers estimated that third party collection returned 39 billion dollars to businesses in 2005. The consumer side of the ledger appears in the bureau findings described above, in which the objection is not to collection of valid debts but to collection of debts not owed, in amounts not owed, and by methods the statute prohibits. Industry figures measure amounts recovered. Bureau findings measure the incidence of error and prohibited conduct during recovery. A market can return large sums to creditors and impose concentrated harm on a minority of consumers at the same time.

Collection also reaches the consumer through credit reporting, where a collection account can outlast the calls that produced it. Collectors that furnish information to consumer reporting agencies place tradelines on credit reports, and those tradelines affect credit scores and rental screening. A disputed debt furnished without an indication of the dispute converts a collection dispute into a reporting problem governed principally by the Fair Credit Reporting Act, with its own duties for furnishers to investigate disputes forwarded by reporting agencies. The December 2020 rule's sequencing requirement was designed so that the reporting consequence did not precede the consumer's first notice of the debt. The interaction between [collection activity and the credit reporting system that records it](/2018/11/01/fair-credit-reporting-act-1970-guide/) explains why the practical stakes of a collection account often lie less in the calls than in the report a prospective landlord or lender later reads.

### What the statute leaves unresolved

The honest limits of the act are visible in its text and in its litigation history. The creditor exclusion is a limit Congress wrote and has maintained through every amendment cycle, and its consequence is a federal standard that governs the agent more completely than the principal whose debt is being collected. The debt buyer question narrowed coverage further through Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., treated in full in the scope discussion, which sets out the holding on debts owed to another and the open principal purpose question.

The damages provision supplies a second limit. Section 1692k permits a court to award additional damages of up to 1,000 dollars in an individual action, beyond any actual damages proved. That figure has not changed since the act was enacted in 1977, and the act contains no mechanism indexing it to inflation, so the sum available as a statutory deterrent has declined in real terms across the life of the statute. The commission identified the point in its February 2009 report, recommending that Congress increase the damage amounts to reflect inflation since 1977 and adjust them periodically. Congress did not adopt that recommendation. Fee shifting carries much of the practical enforcement weight the fixed figure cannot, and class action damages are capped at the lesser of 500,000 dollars or one percent of the collector's net worth.

A third limit is chronological. The statute that took effect in 1978 described a communications environment of postal mail, landline telephones, and in person visits, and it said nothing about email, text messaging, mobile telephones, voicemail systems, or social media platforms, because those channels had not become collection media. Courts filled the silence with analogies, dividing over questions the text could not answer. Regulation F addressed the silence in 2021, but a regulation can only interpret the statute Congress wrote. Questions about later channels, and about automated systems conducting collection conversations, will be answered under general prohibitions drafted in 1977, unless Congress revisits the text.

## Reading the Act in Its Full Frame

The collection act reads differently once its rulemaking history, its boundaries, and its neighboring statutes are kept in view together. As a text, the act is a detailed conduct code for third party collectors that operated for forty four years without a rulebook, acquiring meaning through litigation and a nonbinding commentary. As an institution, the act changed character in 2011, when the bureau acquired the authority the original Congress had withheld, and changed again in 2021, when Regulation F converted the most litigated questions about calls, messages, validation, and time-barred debt into administrable standards with presumptions and safe harbors. As a boundary, the act excludes the original creditor and, after Henson, a class of debt buyers, routing a large share of collection conduct into state law and the general federal prohibitions.

A reader following the subject outward has four natural directions. The first runs to the parent consumer credit framework and the reporting statute governing what collectors furnish. The second runs to the bureau itself, its creation, its transfer of functions in 2011, and its unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts authority. The third runs downward to the states, where statutes such as California's Rosenthal Act extend collection standards to original creditors and where the state of residence can change the answer to a coverage question. The fourth runs to the measured record, the complaint databases maintained by the bureau and the commission, where claims made for and against the collection system can be tested against figures carrying a source, a period, and a stated method. The statute that emerges is narrower than its reputation, a 1977 conduct code whose reach is set by its definitions, whose detail was supplied four decades late by regulation, and whose gaps are filled, unevenly and state by state, by the law around it. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who counts as a debt collector under Fair Debt Collection Practices?

Section 1692a(6) defines a debt collector through two alternative routes, and either route brings a person inside the act. The first covers a person who uses the mails or an instrumentality of interstate commerce in a business whose principal purpose is the collection of debts. The second covers a person who regularly collects, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due to another, which reaches the independent collection agency working as the creditor's agent. Six exclusions in clauses A through F remove officers and employees collecting in the creditor's own name, qualifying affiliates, government employees acting officially, process servers performing service, genuine nonprofit credit counselors, and fiduciaries, originators, and persons who obtained the debt before default. Attorneys who regularly collect consumer debts are covered, and a creditor collecting under a name other than its own is treated as a debt collector.

### Q: Does Fair Debt Collection Practices apply to the original creditor?

Generally, the act does not reach an original creditor collecting a debt owed to that creditor, in the creditor's own name, through the creditor's own employees. Clause A of the definition states the exclusion, and the regularly collects prong points the same way because the debt is owed to the creditor rather than to another. The exclusion disappears when the creditor collects for another person or uses a name other than its own that suggests an outside collector is handling the account, because the false name provision treats that creditor as a debt collector. The boundary is definitional rather than a statement that creditor conduct is unregulated. State statutes such as the California Rosenthal Act apply collection standards to creditors collecting their own debts, and general federal and state unfair practices laws can reach deceptive creditor conduct that the federal collection statute does not cover.

### Q: When can a collector call under Fair Debt Collection Practices?

Section 1692c(a)(1) prohibits communication at an unusual time or place, or at a time or place the collector knows or should know is inconvenient to the consumer, and treats 8 a.m. to 9 p.m. local time at the consumer's location as the presumed convenient window. A consumer's statement about shift work, sleep schedules, or medical care can displace that presumption. Section 1692c(a)(3) separately bars contact at the place of employment when the collector knows or has reason to know the employer prohibits such communication. Section 1692c(a)(2) bars direct communication once the consumer is represented by an attorney on the debt and the attorney's name and address are known or readily ascertainable. Regulation F adds call frequency presumptions for the harassment prohibition, set out in the body of this article.

### Q: What is a validation notice under Fair Debt Collection Practices?

The validation notice is a written statement required by section 1692g(a) within five days after the initial communication with the consumer, unless the initial communication already contained the required information or the consumer paid before the notice was due. It must state the amount of the debt, the name of the creditor, that the debt collector will assume the debt valid unless the consumer disputes it in writing within thirty days after receipt, that a timely written dispute triggers verification or a judgment copy mailed to the consumer, and that a timely written request brings the name and address of the original creditor. Under section 1692g(b), a timely written dispute pauses collection of the disputed portion until verification is mailed. The thirty-day period is a dispute window, not a payment deadline, and missing it is not an admission of liability under section 1692g(c).

### Q: Can you tell a collector to stop contacting you under Fair Debt Collection Practices?

Yes. Section 1692c(c) provides that when a consumer notifies a debt collector in writing that the consumer refuses to pay a debt, or that the consumer wishes the debt collector to cease further communication, the collector must stop communicating with respect to that debt. Notice sent by mail is complete upon receipt. After the notice, the collector may communicate only to advise that further efforts are being terminated, to notify the consumer that specified remedies ordinarily invoked may be invoked, or to notify the consumer that the collector or creditor intends to invoke a specified remedy. An oral request during a call does not trigger this provision. The notice ends the communication channel; it does not cancel the balance, discharge the obligation, or bar a lawful court action to enforce the debt.

### Q: What did Henson decide about Fair Debt Collection Practices?

In Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., decided June 12, 2017, the Supreme Court held unanimously that a company that purchases defaulted debts and then collects those debts for its own account is not a debt collector under the statutory prong covering a person who regularly collects debts owed or due to another. After a purchase, the debt is owed to the purchaser, so collection is for the purchaser's own account rather than for another person. The Court read the word owed in the present tense and declined to treat the purchaser as the creditor's agent. The decision left the principal purpose prong undecided, because that argument was not preserved, and invited Congress to change the text. Later bills proposed extending the definition, while Regulation F restated the statutory definition rather than expanding it.

### Q: What is the least sophisticated consumer standard in Fair Debt Collection Practices?

Courts evaluate a collection communication from the perspective of the least sophisticated consumer rather than from the perspective of a lawyer or a careful reader. The leading exposition is Clomon v. Jackson, decided by the Second Circuit in 1993, where attorney letterhead implying individual review by counsel was held to violate section 1692e even though the attorney had approved only the form letters. The test is objective and protects consumers who are naive or inexperienced, while presuming a willingness to read a notice with some care, so bizarre or idiosyncratic readings do not count. The Seventh Circuit restated the test as the unsophisticated consumer with a reasonableness element in Gammon v. GC Services in 1994. The perspective applies to deception claims, overshadowing of validation rights, and threatened actions.

### Q: Why did it take decades to write Fair Debt Collection Practices rules?

The original statute contained no general rulemaking grant, so no agency could write binding rules under the act itself. The Federal Trade Commission enforced the statute and published a 1988 staff commentary, but that commentary stated it was nonbinding, and the Supreme Court declined to give it conclusive weight. Interpretive detail therefore accumulated through court decisions and enforcement actions. The commission asked Congress for rulemaking authority in a 2009 report. Congress supplied it through section 1089 of the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010, which created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and authorized the Bureau to prescribe rules for debt collection, effective on the transfer date of July 21, 2011. The Bureau issued two final rules in 2020, which together became Regulation F, effective November 30, 2021.

### Q: How much can a consumer recover in statutory damages under the act?

In an individual action, section 1692k(a)(2)(A) permits a court to award additional damages, often called statutory damages because no quantified loss need be proved, of up to 1,000 dollars for the action. The ceiling applies to the action as a whole rather than to each violation, so proving several violations does not multiply the ceiling. Actual damages, costs, and a reasonable attorney's fee sit on top of that figure. In setting the additional award, section 1692k(b) directs the court to consider the frequency and persistence of noncompliance, the nature of the noncompliance, and the extent to which it was intentional. A single inadvertent error supports a small award, while repeated violations after a written instruction to stop support an award at or near the ceiling.

### Q: What is the time limit for bringing a claim under the act?

Section 1692k(d) requires a private action to be brought within one year from the date on which the violation occurs. The Supreme Court construed that language in Rotkiske v. Klemm, decided in 2019, holding that the period begins when the violation occurs rather than when the consumer discovers it, because Congress wrote discovery-based triggers in other statutes when that result was intended. A consumer who first learns of a violation after the first anniversary therefore holds no federal claim for that act, absent a preserved equitable doctrine such as fraudulent concealment, which the Court did not decide in that case. Because each violation carries its own date, a continuing campaign of calls or letters can contain multiple acts with separate periods.

### Q: Does the act cover business or commercial debts?

No. Section 1692a(5) defines a debt as an obligation of a consumer to pay money arising out of a transaction in which the money, property, insurance, or services are primarily for personal, family, or household purposes. A loan taken to stock a business or equipment purchased for a trade falls outside the definition, even when the same agency pursues the account. Coverage attaches obligation by obligation rather than firm by firm, and mixed-purpose transactions are resolved on their primary purpose. Tax liabilities and tort damages are also outside the definition because they arise from operation of law rather than from a transaction. Business collection is governed by other law, including state unfair practices statutes and contract law, rather than by this act.

### Q: What did Regulation F change about calls, email, and text messages?

Regulation F, the Bureau's debt collection rule at 12 C.F.R. part 1006, supplied the first comprehensive standards under the act. For telephone calls, section 1006.14(b) creates rebuttable presumptions: a collector is presumed to violate the harassment prohibition by calling about a particular debt more than seven times within seven consecutive days, or by calling within seven days after a telephone conversation about that debt, and is presumed to comply when neither measure is exceeded. For electronic communication, the rule permits collectors to use email and text messages under specified procedures, requires a reasonable and simple opt-out method for a particular address or number, and defines a limited-content message that requests a reply without revealing the debt to third parties. The rule also standardized validation information with a model form, barred suit or threatened suit on time-barred debt, and restricted furnishing information to a consumer reporting agency before the consumer received notice.

### Q: Can a collector sue a consumer in any court it chooses?

No. Section 1692i restricts where a debt collector may bring a legal action on a debt against a consumer. An action to enforce an interest in real property securing the obligation must be brought in the judicial district or similar legal entity where the property is located. Any other action must be brought where the consumer signed the contract sued upon or where the consumer resides at the commencement of the action. The rule was written to stop collectors from filing in distant forums where the cost of defending exceeds the amount in dispute and default judgments follow from distance rather than adjudication. The section limits forum only for suits the collector is otherwise authorized to bring; it grants no authority to sue on its own.

### Q: What is the bona fide error defense under the act?

Section 1692k(c) provides that a debt collector is not liable if the collector shows by a preponderance of the evidence that the violation was not intentional, resulted from a bona fide error, and occurred despite the maintenance of procedures reasonably adapted to avoid such an error. The defense covers factual and clerical mistakes inside a documented compliance system, such as a transposed balance or a letter mailed to the wrong address from a faulty record, proved through written policies, training, and audit practices. In Jerman v. Carlisle, decided in 2010, the Supreme Court held that the defense does not cover a mistaken interpretation of the act itself, because legal readings do not lend themselves to procedural safeguards. Section 1692k(e) separately protects acts done in good faith conformity with a Bureau advisory opinion.

### Q: Can a collector charge interest or fees on top of the amount owed?

A collector may charge interest, a fee, or another expense incidental to the principal obligation only when the amount is expressly authorized by the agreement creating the debt or permitted by law. Section 1692f(1) makes collection of any other amount an unfair practice, and section 1692e(2)(B) separately treats a demand for unauthorized compensation as a false representation about services or compensation. Collector custom, internal fee schedules, and amounts a predecessor demanded supply no authorization. A contract clause authorizing a specific charge supports collection of that charge, and governing law can support a charge even when the agreement is silent. A fee demand that fails both sources violates the act through deception and through extraction.

### Q: Does a cease communication letter erase the debt or stop a lawsuit?

No. A cease communication notice under section 1692c(c) ends the collector's communication with the consumer about the debt, subject to the three narrow exceptions for closing notices about specified remedies. The section contains no language canceling the obligation, discharging the balance, barring assignment of the account, or preventing a lawful court action. The exceptions themselves presuppose that remedies outside communication remain available, because they permit notice that specified remedies may be invoked or that the collector or creditor intends to invoke one. The underlying obligation remains governed by the contract, by state law on enforcement and limitations periods, and by other federal provisions. Treating the notice as a discharge confuses silencing contact with resolving the debt.

### Q: What happens when a collector reports a disputed debt to a credit bureau without noting the dispute?

Section 1692e(8) prohibits communicating, or threatening to communicate, credit information that is known or should be known to be false, and the paragraph expressly includes the failure to communicate that a disputed debt is disputed. A collector that reports a debt to a consumer reporting agency after the consumer disputed the debt, and reports it without the disputed status, violates the paragraph through omission. The notation matters because readers of a credit report, such as lenders and landlords, lack access to the underlying correspondence. The furnishing of disputed information also interacts with the Fair Credit Reporting Act, which imposes separate duties on furnishers to investigate disputes. Under Regulation F, a collector may not furnish information about a debt to a consumer reporting agency before speaking with the consumer about the debt or mailing a notice and allowing a reasonable period for delivery.

### Q: How does the act treat a creditor that uses a fake collection agency name?

Section 1692a(6) provides that a creditor collecting its own debts is treated as a debt collector when it uses a name other than its own that would indicate a third person is collecting the debts. A demand on the creditor's own letterhead signals a dispute within an existing relationship, while a demand on unfamiliar agency letterhead signals transfer and professional pursuit, and Congress treated the use of that false front as the kind of misrepresentation the act exists to eliminate. The reclassification applies notwithstanding the creditor exclusions, and it is conduct-specific: a creditor using a pseudonym for one portfolio and its own name for another is a debt collector only as to the pseudonym portfolio. When the provision applies, the conduct rules in sections 1692b through 1692o govern the creditor's collection of its own account. Section 1692j separately reaches a person that furnishes deceptive forms for a creditor to use.

### Q: What is a class action worth under the act?

In a class action, section 1692k(a)(2)(B) permits each named plaintiff to recover additional damages up to the amount available in an individual action, plus an amount for all other class members, without regard to a minimum individual recovery, up to the lesser of 500,000 dollars or 1 percent of the debt collector's net worth. Section 1692k(b) directs the court to consider the frequency and persistence of noncompliance, its nature, the extent to which it was intentional, the resources of the debt collector, and the number of persons adversely affected. Successful plaintiffs also recover costs and a reasonable attorney's fee. The cap protects a small agency from ruin over a technical violation that reached a large mailing list, while the fee provision keeps meritorious cases economically viable for consumer counsel.

### Q: How does a consumer with several debts direct how payments are applied?

Section 1692h governs the decision. When a consumer owes multiple debts and makes a single payment to a debt collector with respect to those debts, the collector may not apply the payment to any debt the consumer has disputed, and must apply the payment in accordance with the consumer's directions where applicable. The consumer's direction controls allocation, and the disputed-debt exclusion controls even when a collector's default order, based on age, balance size, or fee generation, would point elsewhere. A collector that applies a directed payment to a different account, or that steers funds toward a disputed balance, violates the section through bookkeeping rather than through a communication. The rule matters because allocation changes balances, interest accrual, and what later appears in credit reporting.
