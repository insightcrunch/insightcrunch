The statute for federal crop insurance requires a producer who seeks subsidized coverage to purchase a policy through an approved insurance provider, on federal policy forms and at federal rates, and it requires that provider to accept every eligible applicant. The largest federal farm support program sits in the insurance title rather than the commodity title, and its size follows from that legal design. The Federal Crop Insurance Corporation stands behind the policies, the Risk Management Agency administers the rates and rules, and private companies carry the contract to the farm.

The same statute requires the federal government to pay a large share of the price of that coverage and to pay the companies that deliver it. Premium subsidies under section 1508(e) of title 7 averaged about 62 percent of total premiums across the periods measured by the Government Accountability Office and the Congressional Budget Office, and a separate reimbursement stream paid companies for selling, servicing, and adjusting the policies. A producer therefore bought a contract whose price, delivery, and risk had each been divided between the farm and the federal government before a loss occurred.

The sections that follow trace that division through the text of the statute, from its definitions and subsidy schedule to its delivery structure, its products, and its place beside the limited payment programs of the commodity title.

## The Statutory Identity and the Definitions That Control the Text

Federal crop insurance begins as a question of location, in the statute books and inside the government, before it becomes a question of money on any particular farm. The program has a statutory home, a corporate instrument through which the government acts, a fund through which its obligations are financed, and a vocabulary that the rest of the text obeys. The Congressional Research Service, in its primer on federal crop insurance, treats those elements as the foundation for everything that follows, and the order matters. 

The statutory home is the Federal Crop Insurance Act, which is Title V of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938, Public Law 75-430, enacted on February 16, 1938, and codified at sections 1501 and following of title 7 of the United States Code. That placement carries meaning of its own. Crop insurance did not arrive as a detached commercial code written for a private market, and it did not arrive as a temporary emergency measure tucked into an appropriations rider. It arrived as one title of a larger agricultural adjustment statute, part of the durable legal architecture through which the federal government organized its relationship with farm production.

The choice of a corporation as the operating instrument sharpens that signal. The act created the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation as an agency within the Department of Agriculture, under section 1503 of title 7, and section 1504 gives the corporation capital stock of 500 million dollars subscribed by the United States. The corporate form does several kinds of work at once. It gives the program a distinct legal personality capable of entering agreements, holding funds, and bearing insurance risk in an organized way. It also keeps the corporation inside the Department of Agriculture, under public direction, rather than leaving it as an independent commercial venture.

Governance follows from that hybrid character. The corporation acts through its Board, and the Board occupies a central place in the statutory scheme because so much of the program requires judgment applied to text. Products must be approved before they can be sold with federal support, and the process under section 508(h), created by the Agricultural Risk Protection Act of 2000, allows private parties to develop new insurance products for approval by the Board of the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation. The Board therefore functions as a gatekeeper between invention and entitlement.

Funding completes the statutory identity, and here the text draws a contrast that shapes the entire program. The Federal Crop Insurance Act carries permanent authorization, and its obligations are financed through the Federal Crop Insurance Fund, the standing fund through which the corporation meets the costs the statute imposes, including premium support, delivery costs, and the federal share of losses under its reinsurance arrangements. A program financed in that manner does not wait each year for an annual appropriations act to supply the money for benefit payments already promised under policies in force. The distinction is fundamental rather than clerical. Insurance is a promise made before the loss, at planting and through the growing season, and the credibility of that promise depends on the assured availability of funds when a drought, flood, or price collapse turns the promise into a claim.

That funding identity also explains why arguments about program cost take the form they do. Because the authority is permanent, debate concentrates on the design of the statute itself, on subsidy rates written into the text, on the terms of reinsurance, and on the definitions that determine how much liability the system creates, rather than on a single annual funding figure. The Government Accountability Office reported that federal costs averaged 3.8 billion dollars per year for fiscal years 2004 through 2008 and 8.5 billion dollars per year for fiscal years 2009 through 2014, a scale that reflects the accumulated effect of statutory choices about coverage, subsidy, and delivery. They arise from text: from who may insure, for how much, at what subsidized price, through which companies, and on what unit of account.

Administration adds one more layer to the identity. The Federal Agriculture Improvement and Reform Act of 1996 allowed the creation of the Risk Management Agency, which has administered the program since 1996, and that act required the Department of Agriculture to cease selling policies directly where coverage through an approved insurance provider was available. The modern structure therefore separates functions that a casual description often merges. The corporation supplies the legal and financial foundation. The Risk Management Agency administers. Private companies, operating under federal approval and federal reinsurance, sell and service the policies that producers actually hold. That separation also makes the definitional discipline visible in practice, because the agency administering the program, the corporation bearing the federal risk, and the company adjusting the claim must all apply the same coverage level, the same production history, and the same unit structure to reach the same result.

A definition in this statute is never a glossary entry written for convenience. It is operative text, and it decides outcomes before any adjuster visits a field. When the text defines a term, it fixes the set of persons, events, acres, and dollars to which later provisions apply, and a party outside the defined set receives nothing under the provision that uses the term, regardless of how sympathetic the underlying loss may be. The definitions therefore deserve reading as the load bearing walls of the program.

The first of those definitions identifies the seller. An approved insurance provider is a private insurance company that has been approved to participate in the federal program and that delivers crop insurance under the terms the statute and the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation impose. Approval is the threshold fact. The approved provider operates under the Standard Reinsurance Agreement, the negotiated agreement between the corporation and each provider that sets risk sharing and compensation, and that agreement disciplines how the company earns, what risk the company retains, and what risk passes to the federal government. The company also operates under a rule that expresses the public character of the program in commercial form: companies selling federal crop insurance must accept every eligible applicant, and an approved insurance provider cannot refuse an eligible producer. Approval thus converts a private seller into a regulated conduit for a public program, with the obligations of the program attached.

The second foundational definition is catastrophic risk protection, known throughout the program by its abbreviation, CAT. The Federal Crop Insurance Reform Act of 1994, Title I of the Federal Crop Insurance Reform and Department of Agriculture Reorganization Act of 1994, created catastrophic coverage as the base layer of the system, and the definition of that layer is deliberately narrow. Catastrophic coverage indemnifies yield losses beyond 50 percent of expected production, and it pays at 55 percent of the expected price. The payment rate had been 60 percent when the coverage was created and was later reduced to 55 percent. The federal government subsidizes the catastrophic premium at 100 percent, and the producer pays only an administrative fee for each crop in each county. Every element of that definition does work. The 50 percent production threshold means that catastrophic coverage responds only after half of expected production has been lost, a loss so deep that the coverage functions as protection against ruin rather than as compensation for an ordinary shortfall. The full premium subsidy means that the base layer costs the producer only the fee, which explains the historical pattern the record shows: in 1995, with a linkage requirement in force, participation rose above 80 percent of the eligible area, and over half the insured area sat at the catastrophic level.

Additional coverage is the defined space above that base layer, and the phrase earns its meaning by contrast. Additional coverage is the buy up protection a producer elects beyond catastrophic coverage, at higher coverage levels, for a premium that the producer shares with the government under the subsidy schedule the statute prescribes. Where catastrophic coverage is uniform, additional coverage is elective and graduated, and that difference in legal character produces a difference in economic character. The statute encourages the purchase without making it free. Section 1508(e) of title 7 sets the subsidy shares, and for basic and optional units the government share falls as coverage rises: 100 percent for catastrophic coverage, then 67 percent at the 50 percent coverage level, 64 percent at 55 percent, 64 percent at 60 percent, 59 percent at 65 percent, 59 percent at 70 percent, 55 percent at 75 percent, 48 percent at 80 percent, and 38 percent at 85 percent. The schedule is the definition of additional coverage translated into price. Each step upward buys more protection and receives, proportionally, less public help in paying for it.

Actual production history supplies the factual base on which both layers rest. Neither catastrophic coverage nor additional coverage can operate without an answer to a prior question: how much production should a given acre, crop, and producer be expected to yield in a normal year? Actual production history, known as APH, answers that question from the record of the farm itself rather than from a county average or an official guess. A definition that looks backward in that fashion quietly rewards documentation. The producer with complete, verifiable records holds a benchmark grounded in demonstrated performance, while gaps or weaknesses in the record translate directly into a weaker guarantee, because the guarantee can only be built from the history the text recognizes.

### What does actual production history mean in federal crop insurance law?

Actual production history means the record of yields actually produced on insured acreage over a sequence of prior crop years, as documented and approved under the policy. The statute and policy terms use that record to establish expected production for the insured crop, and the guarantee builds from that expected figure.

The consequences of that definition run deeper than record keeping. Actual production history decides the guarantee, and the guarantee decides nearly everything else, because liability, premium, and indemnity all scale from the expected production that the history establishes. Two neighboring operations planting the same crop in the same county can hold guarantees of materially different sizes for no reason other than the difference in their documented histories, and the statute accepts that difference as a feature rather than a defect. Insurance priced and promised on demonstrated yield is insurance tied to the productive reality of the specific farm, and the tie disciplines both sides of the contract. A history that overstates normal production would inflate the guarantee and invite payment for losses that merely reflect ordinary performance for that land, while a history that understates normal production would shrink the guarantee and leave real losses uncompensated. The definition therefore carries an accuracy burden that the rest of the text assumes has been met. Every later calculation inherits whatever truth, or whatever error, the production history contains.


The coverage level is the elected percentage of expected production, or of expected revenue under a revenue policy, that the policy guarantees, and its complement is the deductible that the producer absorbs before any indemnity can arise. A guarantee at 75 percent of expected production leaves the first 25 percent of loss with the producer, exactly as a deductible in any other line of insurance leaves the first layer of loss with the insured. The relationship is arithmetic, but its effect is behavioral and financial at once. A higher coverage level shrinks the deductible, brings smaller and more frequent losses inside the policy, and raises both liability and premium. A lower coverage level widens the deductible, confines the policy to deeper losses, and lowers cost while leaving more risk on the farm balance sheet. The coverage level therefore decides the deductible, and the deductible decides which losses are private misfortunes and which losses become claims under federal law.

Liability is the dollar expression of the guarantee: the maximum amount the policy will pay for the insured crop on the insured acreage, built from expected production, the elected coverage level, the applicable price, and the acres insured. The term sets the ceiling of the promise. It also sets the base for pricing, because premium is calculated against liability rather than against acreage alone or against the value the producer hopes to harvest. A change in any input to liability, a stronger production history, a higher coverage level, or a higher price, enlarges the promise and the price of the promise in the same motion.

Premium is the price of that promise, expressed as a rate applied to liability and collected for the coverage the policy provides. The statutory subsidy then divides that price between the producer and the federal government according to the schedule in section 1508(e), and the division explains much of the program politics recorded by oversight bodies. The Government Accountability Office reported that the federal government paid an average of about 62 percent of total premiums in 2014, and the Congressional Budget Office likewise reported in 2017 that premium subsidies had averaged 62 percent of total premiums over the preceding five years. Premium also funds delivery, because administrative and operating reimbursement to companies is calculated as a percentage of premium and supports agent commissions, loss adjustment, and other delivery costs. The definition of premium therefore reaches in three directions: it measures what coverage costs, it determines what the subsidy is worth in dollars, and it supplies the base from which the companies that sell and service policies are compensated.

An indemnity is the payment that becomes due when an insured loss carries production, or revenue, below the guarantee the policy established. The concept completes the chain that the earlier definitions built. Production history set expected production, the coverage level set the guaranteed share of that expectation, liability converted the guarantee into dollars, and premium priced the resulting promise. The indemnity pays the shortfall the policy covers, no more and no less, and its size is therefore determined long before the loss occurs, at the moment the definitional inputs were fixed. A loss that falls inside the deductible produces no indemnity however real the damage, and a loss that pierces the guarantee produces an indemnity measured by the terms rather than by the full economic harm the farm suffered. That gap between harm and payment is not an accident of administration. It is the deductible and the price rate operating exactly as the definitions require.

Unit structure supplies the text with its unit of account, the grouping of acres across which production is measured, the guarantee is applied, and loss is determined. A basic unit is the foundational grouping recognized by the policy. An optional unit divides acreage into smaller groupings where the policy terms allow subdivision, so that production and loss are measured on a narrower base. An enterprise unit combines all of the insured crop in the county into a single unit, and multicounty enterprise units, introduced by the farm bill enacted in 2018, extend that combination across county lines. The definition quietly decides diversification credit. Under an optional unit, a localized loss on one grouping can trigger an indemnity even when other groupings performed well, because the text measures the loss in isolation. Under an enterprise unit, strong production on part of the acreage offsets weak production elsewhere before any shortfall is found, so the same physical loss may produce no payment at all. The statute prices that difference openly: for enterprise units the government share of premium is 80 percent at every coverage level from 50 percent through 70 percent, then 77 percent at 75 percent, 68 percent at 80 percent, and 53 percent at 85 percent, a richer subsidy than basic and optional units receive at most levels, reflecting the lower risk of insuring all of a crop in a county as one unit. Enterprise units were authorized by the farm bill enacted in 2008, and the Supplemental Coverage Option, authorized in 2014 and subsidized at 65 percent, covers part of the deductible band of an underlying policy, up to 86 percent of expected revenue or yield at the county level. Unit choice is thus a choice about how much of the farm diversification the producer keeps as self insurance and how much the policy is allowed to ignore.

Prevented planting coverage addresses the loss that consists of a crop never planted at all. The reform legislation of 1994 authorized prevented planting coverage, recognizing that an insured cause of loss can intervene before planting and leave acreage idle through no failure of the producer. The definition matters because the ordinary machinery of the program, built on production history and harvested yield, has nothing to measure when no crop enters the ground. Prevented planting terms supply a separate basis of payment for that situation, bounded by the policy conditions, so that the absence of a crop becomes a compensable event in its own right rather than an awkward gap between yield based definitions. Its place in the text confirms the general lesson of the section. In federal crop insurance law, money follows definitions: the statutory home gives the promise its permanence, the corporation and its fund give the promise its backing, and each defined term, from approved provider to prevented planting, fixes one more point at which a farm loss either enters the federal promise or remains outside it.

## Three Rebuilds: How Three Statutes Solved a Participation Problem

The participation problem was present at the creation. When Congress enacted the Federal Crop Insurance Act as Title V of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938, Public Law 75-430, 52 Stat. 72, on February 16, 1938, the statute did not merely authorize a new form of farm assistance. It created a seller. The Federal Crop Insurance Corporation, established as an agency within the Department of Agriculture under 7 U.S.C. 1503, with capital stock of $500 million subscribed by the United States under 7 U.S.C. 1504, was the instrument through which the federal government itself would write crop insurance, collect premiums, adjust losses, and pay indemnities. The act, codified at 7 U.S.C. 1501 and following, placed the government in a role that private insurers had been unwilling or unable to sustain on a broad scale: bearing concentrated agricultural risk that could strike many policyholders in the same county, in the same season, and for the same weather reason at once.

That design answered an institutional problem before it answered a marketing problem. Crop risk differs from the risk behind an ordinary property policy because losses are correlated. A drought or a flood does not select one farm at random while sparing the neighbors whose premiums might offset the claim. The grower most eager to buy is often the grower whose land, drainage, or production history makes a loss most likely, a pattern of adverse selection that pushes premiums upward. Higher premiums then drive away lower risk producers, the pool becomes more concentrated in risk, and the price required to sustain the pool rises again. A government corporation with subscribed public capital offered an escape from that spiral, at least in theory, because the corporation could begin operations without first persuading a private market that the business could pay.

For decades after 1938, too few producers reached that conclusion. Uptake stayed low, and low uptake was not a harmless disappointment for a voluntary insurance program. A thin pool weakened the very logic of insurance, since premiums came from a narrower and often riskier base and the political claim that crop insurance could stand in place of emergency assistance remained unproven in practice. A program that insured only a modest share of planted area could not plausibly be described as the main line of defense against farm losses, however sound its statutory architecture appeared on paper. When widespread crop failure arrived, the uninsured did not simply disappear from congressional concern. Their losses returned to Congress in another form, as pressure for ad hoc disaster payments enacted after the damage had already occurred.

That disaster payment cycle was the trap Congress kept trying to escape. Ad hoc assistance had an obvious political attraction in a crisis, because it responded to visible losses in particular places and required no premium from the producer before the event. Its defects accumulated across repetitions, though. Relief decided after a loss could not give a producer certainty at planting time, could not be integrated cleanly into a farm plan built months earlier, and arrived under rules written for a particular emergency rather than under a contract purchased in advance. More corrosively, the expectation of after the fact assistance weakened the case for paying premiums at all. If a producer believed that a sufficiently broad disaster would bring a payment regardless of insurance status, the rational comparison was between a certain premium in the spring and a possible public payment after the harvest, with no premium due if the feared loss never came.

The 1938 design thus contained a tension that administrative effort alone could not resolve. Congress wanted insurance to displace improvisation, but insurance was voluntary, charged a price, and had to compete against the memory of relief granted without a prior premium. That gap between availability and participation framed the work of the later statutes. Each rebuild attacked the gap from a different direction. The statute of 1980 widened the offer and subsidized its price while bringing private companies into delivery. The statute of 1994 deepened the subsidy, built a low cost floor of coverage, and for a brief period made that floor a condition of other farm benefits. The statutes that followed then removed the condition and completed the turn toward price, raising the public share of the premium until voluntary purchase at scale became the normal outcome rather than the hoped for exception.

The first rebuild came with the Federal Crop Insurance Act of 1980, Public Law 96-365, and its changes were structural in three respects identified by the Congressional Research Service in its primer on the federal crop insurance program. First, the act expanded the commodities and geographic scope covered, so that the federal offer reached beyond a narrower initial footprint. Second, it introduced premium subsidies, an acknowledgment that the full actuarial price of the policy was itself a barrier to the participation Congress wanted. Third, it allowed private sector companies to sell and service policies, loosening the original model in which the government corporation stood as the direct seller to the farm. Taken together, the three changes redefined the federal role without abandoning it. The government remained the architect, the subsidizer, and the bearer of the statutory framework, while private delivery was invited to supply local sales capacity and servicing that a public agency had struggled to turn into broad enrollment.

### Why did participation remain low after premium subsidies began?

Participation remained low because a partial subsidy left a substantial premium for coverage that many producers judged too narrow, too unfamiliar, or too weak against the losses that mattered most on their farms, while the expectation of ad hoc disaster assistance after a bad year reduced the cost of staying uninsured.

The record of the decade after 1980, as summarized by the Congressional Research Service, was plain: participation stayed low through the 1980s, loss ratios ran high, and ad hoc disaster payments continued. Those three facts belonged together. Low participation kept the pool narrower than the design required. A narrow pool, weighted toward producers who expected the coverage to pay, made high loss ratios more likely, because the premiums collected were measured against claims from a book of business that did not reflect the full spread of agricultural risk. High loss ratios in turn confirmed official doubts about whether the program could operate as disciplined insurance rather than as another channel of farm support, and the continuation of disaster payments confirmed producer doubts about whether buying coverage was necessary at all, since disaster payments had continued alongside the program. The 1980 reforms, for all their structural intelligence, had not broken the circle.

Price was part of the explanation, but not the whole of it. A first subsidy is not the same thing as a sufficient subsidy. Where the remaining producer paid premium still represented a conspicuous outlay at planting, a season when cash is committed to seed, fuel, fertilizer, rent, and credit, the policy competed against expenses whose return felt more immediate and more certain. Only repeated experience, stronger coverage, or a lower net price could overcome that framing, and the subsidy introduced in 1980 had not yet been carried far enough to change the arithmetic for enough farms.

Distribution reform also had limits. Bringing private companies into selling and servicing changed who carried the policy to the farm gate, but it did not by itself change what the policy cost or what rival expectation it faced. A private agent could explain a contract and service a claim more effectively than a distant public office, yet the agent could not repeal the producer calculation about disaster relief. Nor could expanded scope alone cure a high loss ratio. Adding commodities and territory enlarged the potential market, but potential is not enrollment, and enrollment concentrated among higher risk producers reproduces the original actuarial difficulty inside a larger statutory frame. The lesson Congress drew from the 1980s was therefore not that the 1980 direction had been wrong. The lesson was that the dosage had been inadequate and the incentives incomplete. If price was to do the work, the subsidy had to grow. If universality mattered, a basic tier had to be cheap enough, or compulsory enough, to bring nearly every producer into at least a minimal relationship with the program.

That reasoning produced the second and most aggressive rebuild, the Federal Crop Insurance Reform and Department of Agriculture Reorganization Act of 1994, Public Law 103-354, signed on October 13, 1994. Title I of that act was the Federal Crop Insurance Reform Act of 1994, and the Congressional Research Service identifies five changes that together amounted to a new participation strategy. The act increased premium subsidies. It created catastrophic coverage, usually shortened to CAT. It authorized prevented planting coverage. It created the Noninsured Crop Disaster Assistance Program, known as NAP. And it required producers to obtain at least CAT coverage as a condition of receiving commodity program benefits, a linkage requirement codified at the time at 7 U.S.C. 1433f. Where the act of 1980 had coaxed, the act of 1994 pushed, and the difference in tone reflected more than a decade of frustration with voluntary enrollment that refused to reach the level the insurance model needed.

Catastrophic coverage was the centerpiece because it redefined the entry point. CAT indemnifies yield losses beyond 50 percent of expected production at 55 percent of the expected price, a payment rate that stood at 60 percent when the tier was created and was later reduced to 55 percent. The premium for CAT is subsidized at 100 percent, and the producer pays only an administrative fee for each crop in each county. In practical design terms, CAT was not rich insurance. It responded only after a producer had lost more than half of expected production, and it valued that qualifying loss at a little more than half of the expected price. Its purpose was different from the purpose of a high coverage policy purchased to protect a production plan in detail. CAT established a floor, a basic public layer beneath the farm, cheap enough at the premium line that refusal became difficult to justify and simple enough that enrollment at that level could be made a condition of participation in other programs.

The rest of the 1994 package filled gaps around that floor. Authorizing prevented planting coverage recognized that a crop loss does not always take the form of a failed harvest after planting. Creating NAP addressed a different hole, at the edge of the insurable world. The Congressional Research Service records that NAP covers producers of crops for which no CAT coverage is available, that it is administered by the Farm Service Agency rather than through insurance companies, and that it later received permanent authority under section 196 of the act of 1996, at 7 U.S.C. 7333. NAP was therefore not a rival to crop insurance in the ordinary market sense. It was a recognition that a system built around insurance availability needed a companion mechanism for producers whose crops stood outside that availability, if the broader promise of a standing disaster framework was to be credible.

Linkage was the enforcement device that turned the new floor into near universal contact with the program. By conditioning commodity program benefits on holding at least CAT coverage, Congress attached crop insurance to a stream of support that eligible producers already valued and already administered through the farm program system. The condition changed the default. A producer no longer had to be persuaded, in a stand alone sales conversation, that some coverage was worth acquiring. The producer who wanted commodity benefits had to cross the insurance threshold first, and because the CAT premium itself was fully subsidized, the financial obstacle to crossing that threshold had been reduced to the administrative fee and the effort of enrollment. For one brief period, it was treated as an obligation incident to receiving other public benefits, an obligation Congress imposed because generations of voluntary effort had failed to build the pool.

The response in 1995 showed what compulsion attached to a fully subsidized floor could do. With linkage in force, participation rose above 80 percent of the eligible area, a level that earlier statutes had sought and missed. Yet the composition of that participation revealed the limits of the achievement. Over half of the insured area sat at the CAT level, which carried the full premium subsidy. In other words, linkage succeeded spectacularly at bringing area into the program and far less completely at persuading producers to buy up to deeper coverage at their own additional expense. The distinction matters for understanding every later choice. Congress had proved that a condition could manufacture breadth. It had also learned that breadth at the minimum tier was not the same as a mature insurance market in which large numbers of producers voluntarily paid a meaningful share of the premium for substantial protection. The 1995 figures captured both the breakthrough and the reservation in a single result.

There was also a fiscal pattern underneath the participation gains, and the Economic Research Service gave it a precise shape. Writing in Amber Waves in 2005, the Economic Research Service estimated the added subsidy cost of each additional acre insured at $3.31 in constant 2000 dollars for the period from 1981 to 1994, at $10.51 after the act of 1994, and at about $26 after the act of 2000. Those figures describe marginal cost, the public price of persuading or requiring the next acre into the system, and they rise steeply across the statutory sequence. The earliest period bought additional insured acres relatively cheaply. The settlement of 1994 more than tripled that marginal figure, and the settlement after 2000 more than doubled it again. Participation was not becoming easier to buy as the program matured. Each successive statute reached producers who had been harder to bring in, or bought deeper commitment from producers already in, and the Treasury paid more for each increment of progress than it had paid for the increment before.

Linkage, however, lasted only a single year. The Federal Agriculture Improvement and Reform Act of 1996, Public Law 104-127, repealed the linkage requirement, and 7 U.S.C. 1433f was repealed on April 4, 1996. The repeal formed part of the settlement associated with [the Freedom to Farm Act of 1996](/2020/12/15/freedom-to-farm-act-1996-guide/), and its brevity carries the central irony of the participation story. The one device that had driven participation above 80 percent of eligible area was abandoned almost immediately after demonstrating its power. Congress had discovered an effective instrument and then declined to keep it. The reasons lie in the character of the instrument itself: a condition attached to commodity benefits coerced enrollment from producers who had not chosen insurance on its merits, concentrated much of that enrollment at the minimum tier, and sat uneasily within a statute otherwise associated with loosening the connection between federal direction and farm decisions. Compulsion had solved the numerical problem while creating a political and philosophical one, and the repeal announced that the numerical problem would have to be solved another way.

The act of 1996 did more than repeal, though. The Congressional Research Service records that it allowed the creation of the Risk Management Agency, which has administered the program since 1996, and that it required the Department of Agriculture to cease selling policies directly where approved insurance provider coverage was available. Those two provisions completed a long institutional migration begun in 1980. The government corporation created in 1938 remained the statutory foundation, but the public role had shifted decisively toward administration, standard setting, subsidy, and oversight, while the transaction with the producer belonged to approved private providers where such coverage was available. What remained unresolved after linkage fell was the producer facing a voluntary choice again, and whether price alone could accomplish what price plus compulsion had accomplished in 1995.

The Agricultural Risk Protection Act of 2000, Public Law 106-224, supplied the answer by raising premium subsidies for basic and optional units to their lasting statutory levels. The schedule set at 7 U.S.C. 1508(e), as tabulated by the Congressional Research Service, shows how completely price had become the chosen instrument. For basic and optional units, the government share of premium is 100 percent for CAT, then 67 percent at the 50 percent coverage level, 64 at 55, 64 at 60, 59 at 65, 59 at 70, 55 at 75, 48 at 80, and 38 at 85. The percentages decline as coverage rises, preserving a larger producer contribution at the deepest levels of protection, but the public share at the middle levels where many buy up decisions are made is substantial enough to transform the comparison that had defeated the lighter subsidy of 1980. The state was no longer offering a modest discount on a product producers were free to refuse. It was absorbing well over half the premium across much of the schedule and inviting producers to buy protection whose private cost had been deliberately separated from its full actuarial cost.

The act of 2000 also changed who could imagine new products for the system. It created the section 508(h) process, under which private parties develop new insurance products for approval by the board of the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation. That channel mattered because participation depends on fit as well as on price. Section 508(h) institutionalized a route by which innovation could come from outside the government while remaining subject to approval inside the statutory system. The Congressional Research Service notes that revenue coverage became the largest share of policies sold within a decade of the act, a development consistent with that design opening, although the record establishes the sequence rather than assigning the whole change to a single provision. By the end of the rebuild sequence, the federal offer was broader, cheaper to the producer, privately delivered, and more capable of renewal from within.

The three rebuilds therefore solved different parts of a single problem, and their order is as instructive as their content. The act of 1980 recognized that availability had to mean more than a government corporation with authority to sell: scope widened, premiums were subsidized for the first time, and private companies entered delivery. The act of 1994 recognized that the first subsidy had been too small to overcome the disaster payment expectation and the planting season calculation against insurance: subsidies rose, CAT supplied a fully subsidized floor, prevented planting and NAP filled recognized gaps, and linkage forced the issue for a year with dramatic numerical results. The act of 1996 then withdrew the force while preserving the administrative modernization, and the act of 2000 recognized that a voluntary system at scale required the government to carry a far larger share of the price on a permanent basis, while section 508(h) supplied a standing channel for products better fitted to the risks producers actually bore. Participation was the dependent variable throughout, responding first weakly, then abruptly under compulsion, and finally durably when the subsidy schedule made purchase the economical choice. The whole sequence ran through [the farm bill's history and structure](/2020/11/01/farm-bill-history-and-structure/) as a recurring contest between standing rules known in advance and emergency measures written after losses had occurred, and after 2000 the participation problem stood solved by price rather than by compulsion.

## The Delivery Structure: A Public Program Sold by Private Companies

Federal crop insurance reaches a farm through a structure that separates the authority to create coverage from the work of selling and servicing it. A federal corporation holds the statutory authority and stands behind the money, a federal agency administers the rates and rules that give each policy its price and its terms, and private insurance companies deliver every contract through local agents. Each function sits where the design places it, and the system works only because the boundaries between those functions are drawn in statute, in program rules, and in a negotiated agreement that binds the public and private sides together.

The foundation layer is the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation. The Congressional Research Service, in its report titled Federal Crop Insurance: A Primer, records that the Federal Crop Insurance Act forms Title V of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 and that the act created the Corporation as an agency within the Department of Agriculture, a status carried in the statute at 7 U.S.C. 1503. The same framework gives the Corporation capital stock of 500 million dollars subscribed by the United States, as stated at 7 U.S.C. 1504. The Corporation is a federally owned insurer and reinsurer, capitalized with public money, and charged with carrying a program that private insurance on purely private terms had supplied only in fragments.

Because the Corporation holds the authority, the financial promises inside a policy trace back to a public balance sheet even though a private company name appears on the documents a producer signs. The federal government pays a large share of the premium under the subsidy schedule set in statute, and the Government Accountability Office reported that the federal government paid an average of about 62 percent of total premiums in 2014. The company collects the producer share of premium and receives federal payments connected with delivery, yet the premium support that makes coverage affordable, and the reinsurance that makes the risk bearable, both flow from the public side of the structure under authority centered in the Corporation.

The second layer is administration. The Federal Agriculture Improvement and Reform Act of 1996 allowed the creation of the Risk Management Agency, and the Congressional Research Service records that the agency has administered the program since 1996. Administration in this setting is a heavy technical function rather than a clerical one. The Risk Management Agency sets the premium rates that attach to crops, counties, coverage levels, and unit structures. The agency establishes the yield measures against which production is judged, including the production history that anchors a producer guarantee, and the agency writes the program rules that determine eligibility, reporting duties, policy procedures, and the handling of claims.

The separation between the Corporation and the agency reflects a separation between legal authority and daily management. The Corporation is the entity the statute created in 1938 and the entity that enters the reinsurance relationship with each company, while the Risk Management Agency supplies the administrative machinery that keeps county level rates, policy provisions, and procedural instructions consistent across a national program. A producer in one state buys a policy whose basic provisions match the provisions sold in another state, subject to the crop and county terms that apply to the insured acreage. Private delivery gives the program reach and local presence, but private delivery operates inside forms and prices that the public administrator has already fixed.

The third layer is the approved insurance provider, the private company approved to sell and service federal crop insurance policies. Private delivery entered the modern program through the Federal Crop Insurance Act of 1980, which, as the Congressional Research Service recounts, expanded the commodities and geographic scope covered, introduced premium subsidies, and allowed private sector companies to sell and service policies. The 1980 act therefore moved the program away from direct federal provision and toward a partnership in which companies carry the producer relationship. That relationship includes explaining coverage options, enrolling the producer, collecting the required reports and the producer share of premium, arranging loss adjustment when damage occurs, and paying the indemnity when a claim is established under the policy.

Between the company and the producer stands the commissioned local agent, the most visible part of the entire structure. Agents are paid through commissions that companies fund from the administrative and operating reimbursement the federal government provides. The Congressional Budget Office reported in 2017 that total administrative and operating reimbursements ranged from 11 percent to 16 percent of total premiums from 2000 through 2016, and the Government Accountability Office described companies using that reimbursement to pay agent commissions, loss adjustment, and other delivery costs. The commission structure explains why the agent layer exists at the scale the program requires. Crop insurance is a seasonal, deadline driven, locally specific product, covering particular crops in particular counties under elections made before planting.

One obligation defines the company layer as clearly as any other. The Congressional Research Service records the standard term that companies selling federal crop insurance must accept every eligible applicant, and an approved insurance provider cannot refuse an eligible producer. That duty changes the meaning of approval. Approval is not a license to select the safest farms and decline the rest, which is the ordinary freedom an insurer exercises when underwriting for private profit. Approval is an undertaking to serve the eligible population of a program designed for broad participation. A company may compete for a producer business, and a producer may choose among companies and agents, but the company may not close its door to an eligible applicant because the farm presents an unattractive risk.

The structure is not a public insurer in the ordinary sense, even though a federal corporation stands behind it. A public insurer in the ordinary sense would employ the sales force, issue policies through its own offices, adjust losses with its own staff, and carry the whole operation inside a government agency. Federal crop insurance after the reforms of the 1990s works differently. The public side retains the authority, the policy forms, the rates, the subsidy money, and the reinsurance, while the transaction with the producer belongs to a private company and its agents. The program is publicly financed and publicly governed, and privately delivered at every point where the producer actually touches it.

Neither is the structure a regulated private market in the ordinary sense, even though private companies earn revenue from it and compete for customers within it. In an ordinary regulated market, an insurer drafts policy forms subject to regulatory approval, files rates calculated from its own assessment of risk, and competes in part on price, offering a lower premium to win business from a rival. Federal crop insurance removes each of those freedoms. The government writes the policy forms, so coverage terms do not vary from company to company. The government sets the premium rates, so a company cannot discount a policy to attract a producer or raise a rate to discourage one. Price competition is forbidden by the design itself, because the premium for a given policy, crop, county, and coverage election is a federal figure rather than a company figure.

What remains for competition is service, and service competition shapes company behavior throughout the delivery system. Companies compete on the quality and reach of agent networks, on the speed and accuracy of paperwork, on the training given to the people who explain coverage and adjust losses, on the technology that moves acreage reports and claims through company systems, and on the reputation a company builds among producers for fairness when a loss is disputed. Service is not a soft supplement to price in this market. Service is the only competitive dimension the structure leaves open, and producers choosing among providers for identical coverage at an identical premium have reason to judge companies almost entirely on how well the companies and their agents perform the work of delivery.


### Who sells federal crop insurance and who bears the risk?

Federal crop insurance is sold by approved insurance providers through local agents, while the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation supplies the authority, the policy forms, and reinsurance behind each contract. The Risk Management Agency administers rates and rules, and the government retains most risk on policies placed in the assigned risk fund.

The contract that holds the public and private layers together is the Standard Reinsurance Agreement. The Congressional Research Service describes the agreement as the negotiated agreement between the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation and each approved insurance provider setting risk sharing and compensation. That compact description can mislead a reader into treating the agreement as an administrative formality. The agreement is closer to the operating contract of the delivery system. Statute establishes the program and the Corporation, policy documents establish the coverage, and the Standard Reinsurance Agreement establishes the terms on which a private company participates in selling that coverage, shares in the financial result of the business the company writes, and receives federal support for the cost of delivering the program.

Because the agreement is negotiated between the Corporation and each company, the delivery structure rests on contract as well as on statute. Congress can amend the act, and the Risk Management Agency can revise program rules, but the financial relationship between the federal reinsurer and the delivering company is spelled out in an agreement that the parties periodically renegotiate within limits that statute prescribes. The statutory rhythm of renegotiation and the budget conditions attached to later rounds form part of the compensation history of the program, and the architecture of the agreement stands apart from that history. The architecture answers three questions: which policies a company keeps largely for its own account, which policies the company shares with the federal reinsurer, and which policies rest mostly on the government.

The agreement answers those questions through funds. Under the risk sharing structure that the Congressional Research Service summarizes, the Standard Reinsurance Agreement sorts the business a company writes into a commercial fund, a developmental fund, and an assigned risk fund, and the company retains a share of premium and a share of underwriting gains or losses that varies with the fund in which a policy sits. A company that retains premium keeps a portion of the money collected for the risk, and retention carries the corresponding share of the result when claims are tallied against that premium. The funds therefore translate an abstract commitment to share risk into a graded set of positions, running from the business a company is most willing to hold for its own account to the business the federal government is most willing to carry for the program as a whole.

The commercial fund occupies the end of that scale where private retention runs highest. Policies placed in the commercial fund are the business a company treats as its core book, the coverage the company expects to produce a result the company is prepared to stand behind with the largest retained share the agreement allows. Retention at that level serves a disciplinary purpose. A company that keeps a substantial share of premium and result has a direct financial interest in accurate enrollment, careful paperwork, and sound loss adjustment, because mistakes in servicing business the company largely owns return to the company as underwriting losses rather than passing entirely to the federal account. The developmental fund sits between the commercial fund and the assigned risk fund, and its intermediate position reflects an intermediate judgment. A company retains a share of premium and result on developmental fund policies, but the federal partner carries a larger portion of the exposure than the federal partner carries on commercial fund business.

The assigned risk fund occupies the opposite end of the scale from the commercial fund, and the Congressional Research Service states the essential fact about the fund directly: under the Standard Reinsurance Agreement, the federal government bears most of the risk in the assigned risk fund. Policies rest in that fund because the risk presented is the kind of risk that the acceptance obligation makes unavoidable and that ordinary underwriting would decline or heavily penalize. A company that must accept every eligible applicant will inevitably write some coverage with a poor expected result, whether because of the loss history attached to the insured acreage, the hazard of the county, or the characteristics of the risk itself. If the company had to retain that business on the same terms as its commercial book, the acceptance duty would become a penalty for participation, and companies would face a standing incentive to discourage the very producers the program obliges them to serve. The assigned risk fund removes that incentive by leaving most of the premium result, favorable or unfavorable, with the government that imposed the duty to accept.

Retention across the three funds also determines how a company experiences a bad crop year. In a year of widespread drought or flood, indemnities rise across entire regions at once, because crop losses correlate in a way that losses in many other insurance lines do not. A failed rainy season or a regional freeze strikes thousands of insured farms in the same production cycle, and the claims arrive together. The fund structure and the reinsurance behind it spread the correlated shock across the federal account, which can absorb a catastrophic year as a fiscal event rather than as a threat to the solvency of the delivering companies. Companies still feel a severe year through the shares retained, and that retained exposure is deliberate, since a company insulated from every loss would service claims with less care. The balance the agreement strikes is exposure without ruin: participation in the result without responsibility for the full weight of a national crop failure.

The delivery structure took its settled form in 1996. The Congressional Research Service records that the Federal Agriculture Improvement and Reform Act of 1996 required the Department of Agriculture to cease selling policies directly where approved insurance provider coverage was available. That requirement closed a long transitional period. Private companies had sold and serviced policies since the 1980 reforms, yet the government had remained a competing direct seller alongside the companies the government was building into a delivery network. The 1996 act ended the duplication in any area where a company channel existed. The federal role narrowed to authority, subsidy financing, uniform rates and forms, and reinsurance. The selling function passed entirely to the approved providers and their agents, and a producer seeking coverage in an area served by an approved provider dealt with the company system while the department stepped out of the storefront.

In practice, the agent layer carries the program through a calendar of deadlines and documents that define the insured year. The first fixed point is the sales closing date, the last date by which a producer must apply for coverage or change an existing policy for a given crop. Sales closing dates fall before planting for the crop concerned, and the timing enforces a basic insurance principle through procedure. Coverage must be elected before the outcome of the season is known, because insurance purchased after a loss has become predictable is no longer insurance against uncertainty. The agent guides the producer through the elections that closing requires: the crop and county to be insured, the coverage level, the unit structure, and the price and yield terms that the federal program attaches to the choice.

The second fixed point is the acreage report, the producer statement of what was actually planted, where the acreage sits, and how the planted acreage corresponds to the coverage elected at sales closing. The acreage report converts an intention to insure into an insured fact. Premium, liability, and any eventual indemnity all depend on the acreage and production information the report establishes, which is why the report carries a deadline of its own and why accuracy in the report matters as much as the coverage election itself. The agent collects the report, checks the report against the policy record, and transmits the information into the company system, where the data also feeds the federal records that the Risk Management Agency uses to oversee the program.

Loss adjustment is the stage at which the delivery structure faces its hardest test. When a producer files a notice of loss, the company sends a loss adjuster to examine the insured crop, measure or appraise the production that remains, gather the production records the policy requires, and apply the policy provisions to the facts of the field. The adjuster works for the company side of the structure, yet the adjuster applies federal policy language, federal procedures, and federal standards for documenting production, because the indemnity under calculation is an indemnity under a federal policy rather than under a contract the company drafted. The producer experiences loss adjustment as a company visit and a company decision. The money and the rules behind the decision are federal, and a share of the financial result returns to the company through the retention position of the fund in which the policy sits.

Claims complete the circuit that the structure creates. When loss adjustment establishes that production or revenue fell below the guarantee the policy provides, the indemnity flows to the producer through the company. The company pays under the policy the company sold and serviced, and the reinsurance relationship with the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation settles the federal share behind the payment according to the fund allocation and retention terms of the Standard Reinsurance Agreement. The flow of money therefore mirrors the flow of responsibility throughout the design. Premium support moves from the government into the policy at the front of the season, delivery reimbursement moves from the government to the company for the work of selling and servicing, and indemnity money moves from the company to the producer at the end of a failed season, with the Corporation absorbing the reinsured portion of the loss behind the company payment.

The delivery structure, taken as a whole, allocates each element of crop insurance to the institution positioned to carry it. The Federal Crop Insurance Corporation holds the authority that Congress created in 1938 and the capital that the United States subscribed, and the Corporation stands as insurer and reinsurer behind the program. The Risk Management Agency administers the rates, yields, and rules that make a national set of policies uniform and calculable. Approved insurance providers sell and service every policy, accept every eligible applicant, and retain shares of premium and result that the Standard Reinsurance Agreement grades across the commercial, developmental, and assigned risk funds, with the government bearing most of the risk where the insured business is hardest to carry. The result is a program public in its promises, its money, and its rules, and private in its handshake, its paperwork, and its presence in the field, with each side dependent on the other for the coverage that reaches the farm.

## The Premium Subsidy: Rates Written Into the Statute

The premium subsidy is not an agency decision. Congress wrote the government share of the crop insurance premium into the Federal Crop Insurance Act itself, at section 1508(e) of title 7 of the United States Code, and the Congressional Research Service, in its primer on federal crop insurance, reproduced that schedule as a table because the table, in substance, is the policy. The Risk Management Agency, the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation, and the private companies that sell and service the policies all operate inside boundaries the schedule fixes. Changing the generosity of the program therefore requires changing numbers Congress enacted. That structure followed the introduction of premium subsidies in the Federal Crop Insurance Act of 1980 and their later increases under the reform legislation of 1994.

The Agricultural Risk Protection Act of 2000 raised premium subsidies for basic and optional units to the statutory levels set out in the schedule, as the Congressional Research Service recorded. The reform act of 1994 had already increased subsidies and created catastrophic coverage, and the 2000 act pushed the buy up shares higher still. The percentages in section 1508(e) are thus the product of repeated legislative decisions, each moving the government share upward in the belief that participation would follow price. The final shape of the table records both that belief and the limits Congress placed on it at higher coverage levels. Every percentage describes the portion of the total premium the government pays, with the producer paying the remainder.

For catastrophic coverage, the first line of the schedule, the government share is 100 percent of the premium, and the producer pays only an administrative fee for each crop in each county. Below that floor sits buy up coverage, protection purchased above the catastrophic tier. For basic and optional units, the government share is 67 percent at the 50 percent coverage level, 64 percent at 55 percent, and 64 percent at 60 percent. The opening steps are gentle, retaining a public contribution of roughly two thirds of the premium. Congress thereby made entry into buy up coverage cheap before the schedule began to tighten. A producer at the lowest buy up level pays only 33 percent of the premium.

The middle of the basic and optional schedule continues the descent in measured steps. The government share is 59 percent at the 65 percent coverage level, 59 percent at 70 percent, and 55 percent at 75 percent. Through this range the government remains the majority contributor to the premium, even though protection at the 75 percent level reaches well beyond the catastrophic floor. Coverage in this range matches the risk of ordinary commercial farming, deep enough to matter in a bad season and short of near complete protection. Congress kept the subsidy above half the premium through the whole range. The share drops below half only at the next step.

Above 75 percent, the basic and optional schedule changes character. The government share falls to 48 percent at the 80 percent coverage level and to 38 percent at the 85 percent coverage level, the highest level in the table. Those drops, from 55 percent to 48 percent and then to 38 percent, are steeper than any movement lower in the schedule. At the top, the positions of government and producer have reversed from the bottom of the buy up range, where the government pays 67 percent and the producer pays 33 percent. At 85 percent the government pays 38 percent and the producer pays 62 percent. The statute reserves its least generous treatment for the most complete protection, with no administrative discretion to soften the final steps.

The enterprise unit schedule follows a different path through the same levels. For enterprise units and multicounty enterprise units, the government share is 80 percent at every coverage level from 50 percent through 70 percent. The share then falls to 77 percent at 75 percent, 68 percent at 80 percent, and 53 percent at 85 percent. Where the basic and optional schedule declines from the first buy up step, the enterprise schedule holds a high flat rate across the middle and declines only at the upper levels. Even at the highest level, the enterprise share remains above half the premium. The Supplemental Coverage Option, authorized by the farm bill of 2014 and described by the Congressional Research Service as covering part of the deductible band of an underlying policy, carries a separate government share of 65 percent.

The reason for the enterprise premium lies in the structure of the unit itself. An enterprise unit treats all of a crop in a county as one unit, rather than dividing that crop into smaller basic or optional units. A poor result on one part of the insured acreage can then be offset by a better result on another part of the same crop in the same county, and an indemnity arises only when the combined result falls short by the required amount. The Congressional Research Service explained the enterprise discount as a reflection of the lower risk of insuring all of a crop in a county as one unit. A loss that would trigger payment on a small unit may trigger no payment on an enterprise unit covering the same ground. The government can therefore pay a larger share of a premium that prices a smaller expected loss.

The gap between the schedules widens as coverage rises, sharpening the incentive. At the 50 percent level, enterprise units receive 80 percent against 67 percent for basic and optional units. At 75 percent, the figures are 77 percent against 55 percent, and at 85 percent, 53 percent against 38 percent. The cheaper route in out of pocket premium terms pools production across the county, while the more expensive route preserves smaller units and the more frequent indemnities those units can generate. Congress did not mandate the enterprise structure. Congress priced the alternatives so that the structure carrying lower risk for the insurer and the treasury also costs the producer the least to buy. The enterprise rate, in effect, buys the unit structure the government prefers.

### How much of a crop insurance premium does the government pay at each coverage level?

On average, the federal government paid about sixty-two percent of total premiums, according to the Government Accountability Office for a single year and the Congressional Budget Office for a five year average. The actual share for any policy depended on the coverage level and unit type the producer chose.

No line in section 1508(e) contains that average. The table contains a full subsidy for catastrophic coverage, basic and optional rates stepping down from 67 percent to 38 percent, higher enterprise rates, and a separate rate for the Supplemental Coverage Option. The average emerges only after thousands of individual choices are combined and weighted by the premium volume attached to each choice. Producers selecting catastrophic coverage contribute premiums subsidized in full, producers selecting the highest basic and optional coverage contribute premiums subsidized at only 38 percent, and enterprise producers contribute premiums subsidized at the elevated enterprise rates. A shift in that blend moves the average even when the statute has not changed. The average is an outcome of producer choices, not a rate anyone sets.

The Government Accountability Office reported that the federal government paid an average of about 62 percent of total premiums in 2014, in report GAO-15-356, published in March 2015. The Congressional Budget Office, reporting in 2017, likewise stated that premium subsidies had averaged 62 percent of total premiums over the preceding five years, with statutory rates ranging from 38 to 100 percent. The two offices approached the figure from different vantage points, one describing a single year and the other an average across five years. Both calculations landing at about 62 percent indicates that the blend of choices behind the average was reasonably stable across that period. Neither office set the rate. Each office measured the outcome of the enacted schedule as producers had actually used it.

Separating the subsidy rate from the subsidy cost clarifies the average further, a distinction that discussions of [how the Congressional Budget Office scores legislation](/2020/05/15/how-cbo-scores-legislation/) make central whenever a provision changes a percentage in a table like section 1508(e). A one point change in a heavily used rate moves federal costs far more than a one point change in a lightly used rate, because cost depends on the premium volume to which a rate applies. High coverage policies carry large premiums, so the lower percentages at the top of the basic and optional schedule apply to larger sums, pulling the average down from the simple midpoint of the table. Enterprise policies pull in the opposite direction. The resulting average reflects the market Congress created, policy by policy. Treating 62 percent as a chosen rate misdescribes both the statute and the reporting behind the figure.

The declining basic and optional schedule rests on the character of higher coverage. Every policy leaves an uninsured band at the bottom, the portion of expected production below the coverage level that the producer absorbs without an indemnity, often described as the deductible band. As coverage rises from 50 percent toward 85 percent, that band shrinks and the policy responds to smaller, more frequent shortfalls. Shallow losses occur far more often than catastrophic losses, so a policy paying on shallow losses generates indemnities in many more seasons and commands a higher premium. A government paying the same share at 85 percent as at 50 percent would fund, at the highest rate, the most frequently triggered layer of protection. The declining share restrains the cost of insuring the deductible band, subsidizing the foundation generously and withdrawing support as the producer buys protection against ordinary shortfalls rather than disasters.

The slope also shapes behavior at the margin. A producer weighing a move from 70 percent coverage to 75 percent, or from 80 percent to 85 percent, compares added protection against added premium, and the schedule ensures that the producer share of that added premium is substantial. At the top of the table the producer pays 62 percent of the premium, making the final increment of coverage predominantly a private decision about income stability. Lower in the table the calculation tilts the other way, deliberately. The first buy up increments carry government shares of 67 percent, 64 percent, and 59 percent, cheap enough to lift a producer from the catastrophic floor toward protection capable of sustaining an operation through a bad year. The enterprise schedule works on a second margin, governing how coverage is organized rather than how much is bought, and its flat 80 percent share removes price as an obstacle to the preferred structure through the middle range.

Percentages acquire full meaning only at program scale, and the scale in the period the Government Accountability Office examined was measured in billions of dollars a year. In 2011, a record premium year, total premiums reached 11.8 billion dollars and premium subsidies were about 7.4 billion dollars, as the Government Accountability Office reported. A single year at that volume converts the abstract average of about 62 percent into a concrete claim on the treasury. Each percentage point in the heavily used parts of the table, applied to a premium base of that size, represents a sum larger than the budget of many smaller agricultural programs. Legislators setting the enterprise share at 80 percent, or letting the basic and optional share fall to 38 percent at the top, made decisions whose fiscal consequences accumulated acre by acre into those totals.

The longer record places the 2011 peak inside a broader expansion. According to the Government Accountability Office in report GAO-15-356, total federal costs averaged 3.8 billion dollars a year for fiscal years 2004 through 2008 and 8.5 billion dollars a year for fiscal years 2009 through 2014. The later average is more than double the earlier average, across a period in which the statutory schedule, following the increases enacted in 2000, was not the moving part. What moved was the scale of the program to which the schedule applied, the premium volume generated by the crops, prices, coverage levels, and participation producers brought to the table. Congress controls the percentages directly and the resulting cost only indirectly. A subsidy written as a share of premium, rather than as a fixed payment per acre, produces exactly that relationship.

Projections extend the same arithmetic forward, and a projected cost deserves the same care as a measured average. The Government Accountability Office report relayed a Congressional Budget Office projection that federal costs would average 8.9 billion dollars a year for fiscal years 2015 through 2024. That figure was a projection made in 2015, an estimate of a decade that had not yet run, to be read as an expectation formed under the conditions visible at the time rather than as a record of money later spent. In sequence, costs had averaged 3.8 billion dollars a year in the earlier period, risen to 8.5 billion dollars a year in the period ending in 2014, and were projected in 2015 to continue at 8.9 billion dollars a year. The projection assumed the new scale would persist. Underneath all three figures sat the schedule in section 1508(e), translating insured production into federal expense.

The interaction between the fixed rates and the growing premium base also explains why debate over the schedule so often takes the form of debate over single percentage points. A point added to a heavily used enterprise or middle range rate applies to a large share of the premium volume in the program, and the resulting cost follows automatically from enrollment and coverage decisions producers make afterward. A point removed from a lightly used rate, by contrast, changes federal expense only modestly, however large the change appears in the table itself. The schedule therefore distributes fiscal weight unevenly across its own lines, and the lines that look similar as percentages can differ greatly as commitments of public money. That unevenness is a direct consequence of writing the subsidy as rates in a statute rather than as appropriated sums for each tier. Congress decides the terms on which expense will be incurred, producers decide how much coverage to place on those terms, and the totals reported by the Government Accountability Office and projected by the Congressional Budget Office record the meeting of those two sets of decisions.

Against that scale, the catastrophic tier presents a paradox that explains the floor of the system. Catastrophic coverage carries the highest subsidy in the table, 100 percent of the premium, yet the promise purchased is the thinnest in the program. As the Congressional Research Service described the tier, catastrophic coverage indemnifies yield losses beyond 50 percent of expected production at 55 percent of the expected price. A producer holding only that coverage absorbs the first half of any production loss entirely and, for a loss beyond that point, is paid on the excess at the reduced price rate. The payment rate had stood at 60 percent when the tier was created under the reform act of 1994, before later legislation reduced the rate to 55 percent. Full subsidization and thin protection coexist because the tier is a floor, not complete insurance.

The economics of that floor follow from the rarity of the loss addressed. A production loss beyond half the expected crop is a rare event for most operations in most seasons, and a rare event can be insured at a modest premium before any subsidy is applied. Paying the whole of that modest premium costs the government far less per insured acre than paying two thirds of the premium for richer buy up coverage, while the producer price of entry falls to an administrative fee for each crop in each county. A fee removes the weighing that even a heavily subsidized premium requires, since a producer might still judge a premium too high for a promise that pays only in disaster. The legislation of 1994 reinforced the logic by requiring at least catastrophic coverage as a condition of commodity program benefits, a linkage the farm bill of 1996 repealed after participation rose above 80 percent of the eligible area in 1995, with over half the insured area at the catastrophic level.

Congress built a floor on those terms for reasons beyond the individual policy. A widely held catastrophic tier gives the government a standing structure for disaster, with producers enrolled and crops and counties recorded before any particular drought, flood, or freeze occurs, reducing pressure for improvised relief after a loss. The earlier history points the same way, since the expansion of 1980 introduced subsidies and private delivery yet participation stayed low through the 1980s, loss ratios ran high, and ad hoc disaster payments continued, as the Congressional Research Service noted. A free catastrophic floor attacks that participation problem at its cheapest point. Each producer drawn in at the floor has a relationship to the program and a reason to consider the buy up levels above it. The most generous percentage in the statute thus supports the less generous percentages below it.

Read as a whole, the schedule embodies distinct judgments sharing one subsection. At the floor, disaster protection costs the producer nothing in premium, though the protection is thin, paying only on losses beyond half the expected crop and then at 55 percent of price. In the lower and middle buy up range, the government remains the majority partner, paying shares stepping down from 67 percent through 64 percent and 59 percent to 55 percent. At the top of the basic and optional range, near complete protection is substantially a private purchase, at shares of 48 percent and 38 percent. In the enterprise column, unit structure matters enough to pay for, at 80 percent across the middle range. The measured result, an average public share of about 62 percent and annual costs rising from 3.8 billion dollars to 8.5 billion dollars between the periods the Government Accountability Office compared, is a consequence of those enacted rates, not a number any single provision declares.

## Paying the Companies: Delivery Allowances and Underwriting Gains

Accounts of federal crop insurance support tend to follow the money that reaches the farm. The premium subsidy draws the attention, because the subsidy is visible on the policy, because the subsidy share is set in statute, and because the subsidy can be described as a direct reduction in the price a producer pays for coverage. A second payment stream runs alongside the subsidy, in a different direction, through a different mechanism, and toward a different recipient. That stream pays the private insurance companies that sell and service the policies, and the stream rarely appears in figures that purport to describe support to farmers. The omission distorts the picture in both directions. A total that counts only premium subsidies understates what the federal government spends to keep the delivery system operating, and a debate that treats every program dollar as a dollar of producer support misunderstands where a substantial share of the money goes and what work that share is meant to purchase.

The stream in question is the administrative and operating reimbursement, described by the Congressional Budget Office and the Government Accountability Office as a payment calculated as a percentage of premium. The reimbursement compensates an approved insurance provider for delivering the policy, and companies use the payment to pay agent commissions, loss adjustment, and other delivery costs, according to the Congressional Budget Office. The agent who works through coverage levels, unit structures, and reporting deadlines with a producer is paid from that stream. The adjuster who travels to a damaged field, measures production, and documents a loss is paid from that stream. The clerical and technical labor of issuing policies, receiving acreage reports, checking records, transmitting data to the Risk Management Agency, and processing claims is paid from that stream. Private delivery, the architecture Congress chose for the modern program, has a price, and the administrative and operating reimbursement is that price, expressed as a share of the premium moving through the system.

The scale of the payment is large enough to matter in any honest accounting of program cost. The Congressional Budget Office reported in 2017 that total administrative and operating reimbursements ranged from 11 to 16 percent of total premiums from 2000 through 2016. A range of that width, attached to a premium base measured in billions of dollars, produces annual payments that rival the budgets of entire agricultural programs. The Government Accountability Office reported that in 2011, a record premium year in which total premiums reached 11.8 billion dollars, premium subsidies were about 7.4 billion dollars and administrative and operating payments to companies were about 1.3 billion dollars. The 2011 figures place the two streams in proportion. For roughly every six dollars the federal government paid toward the cost of premiums in that year, another dollar went to companies for delivering the policies that the premiums purchased, and that second dollar bought services without which the first six dollars could not have reached a producer in usable form.

The percentage basis of the reimbursement looks, at first glance, like the most natural way to pay for delivery. A percentage scales automatically. A year with more policies, more acres, and more premium generates more delivery payment, and a year with less business generates less, without any need for Congress or the Department of Agriculture to renegotiate a fee schedule line by line. The percentage also spares administrators the task of pricing each act of delivery separately, a task that would require a regulator to state, in advance and for every county in the country, the exact cost of selling a policy, adjusting a loss, and maintaining records for operations of very different sizes. The Congressional Budget Office figures show the arrangement persisting across very different premium environments, from 2000 through 2016, at rates within the band from 11 to 16 percent. Persistence of that kind reflects administrative convenience and a measure of acceptance on all sides, even as the same percentage basis generated the central defect that later reform efforts set out to correct.

The defect lies in the relationship, or the lack of a relationship, between the value of a premium and the cost of the work the reimbursement pays for. A premium reflects the value of the crop insured, the coverage level chosen, and the risk that the crop will fail, and when commodity prices rise, the value insured rises with them, even though the number of acres, the number of policies, and the number of farm visits required to service those policies remain much the same. An agent does not drive twice as far to reach a field because the corn in that field is worth twice as much. A loss adjuster does not measure production twice because the price applied to a measured shortfall has doubled. The paperwork attached to a policy does not double in thickness when markets move. Yet a reimbursement calculated as a fixed percentage of premium rises in step with the premium itself, because the formula knows only the premium figure and responds to nothing else. The payment therefore tracks the fortunes of commodity markets rather than the quantity or the difficulty of the delivery work performed.

That mismatch behaves badly in both market directions, and each direction produces a distinct complaint. When prices rise sharply, as prices did in the record premium year of 2011 described by the Government Accountability Office, the delivery allowance rises sharply as well, and companies receive a windfall measured against the actual cost of selling and servicing policies that changed little in number or complexity. The windfall is not a reward for efficiency, for expanded service, or for reaching underserved producers. The windfall is an artifact of arithmetic, paid because a percentage was applied to a larger number. When prices fall, the same arithmetic runs in reverse. The allowance contracts even though producers plant, report acreage, and file claims in poor yield years, and even though agents and adjusters remain required in the same counties. A delivery network built for a national program carries fixed costs in offices, trained staff, and local presence, and a payment formula that swings with commodity prices leaves that network alternately overpaid and squeezed for reasons unrelated to performance. The formula, in short, pays for movements in the markets rather than for work on the ground.

That agreement is the Standard Reinsurance Agreement, the negotiated contract between the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation and each approved insurance provider that sets risk sharing and compensation for the program, according to the Congressional Research Service. Because compensation terms sit inside the agreement, any serious attempt to restrain the growth of delivery payments had to pass through a renegotiation of the agreement itself. The Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008 directed a renegotiation for the 2011 reinsurance year, and the agreement that emerged took direct aim at the open ended character of the percentage based allowance. Rather than abolishing the percentage calculation altogether, the renegotiated agreement surrounded total delivery payments with boundaries, so that the amount companies collectively received could move within a defined corridor instead of expanding without limit whenever premiums expanded. The corridor had a lower boundary designed to protect the delivery network against a collapse in premium volume and an upper boundary designed to protect the federal government against windfall payments in high price years. The design acknowledged both sides of the mismatch problem at once.

### How did the renegotiated agreement limit delivery payments to insurance companies?

The renegotiated Standard Reinsurance Agreement introduced a floor and a ceiling on total administrative and operating payments to approved insurance providers under the federal crop insurance program. For 2011, the floor was $971 million and the ceiling was $1,221 million, according to the Congressional Research Service.

The floor, called the cup in program usage, and the ceiling, called the cap, converted an automatic formula into a bounded one. Within the corridor between cup and cap, the percentage based calculation continued to operate, and total payments could respond to changes in the scale of the program. Beyond the corridor, the calculation stopped controlling the result. If the percentage formula would have produced total payments below the cup, the floor held payments up, preserving a minimum level of support for the delivery system in a low premium environment. If the percentage formula would have produced total payments above the cap, the ceiling held payments down, preventing a price driven surge in premiums from passing straight through into delivery income. The Congressional Research Service reported that for reinsurance year 2011 the cup stood at 971 million dollars and the cap stood at 1,221 million dollars. For reinsurance year 2015, the figures had risen to a cup of 1,020 million dollars and a cap of 1,283 million dollars, and the figures were fixed at those levels after that point, according to the Congressional Research Service.

The fiscal stakes claimed for the renegotiation were substantial. The Department of Agriculture estimated that the 2011 renegotiation saved about 6 billion dollars across fiscal years 2011 through 2020 against the 2010 baseline, according to the Congressional Research Service. An estimate of that kind rests on a comparison between two paths. One path is the spending that followed under the renegotiated agreement, with its cup and cap on delivery payments and its revised risk sharing terms. The other path is a constructed baseline, representing what spending would have been had the terms in place in 2010 continued unchanged through the decade. The difference between the paths, accumulated across ten fiscal years, produced the figure of about 6 billion dollars. A savings figure measured against a baseline is not a sum of money removed from a vault, and a different baseline would have produced a different figure. The estimate nevertheless translated a technical change in a reinsurance agreement into a fiscal claim that legislators and budget officials could weigh against other demands on agricultural spending.

Renegotiation itself was placed on a leash by the same legislative sequence that produced the 2011 agreement. The Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008 limited renegotiation of the Standard Reinsurance Agreement to no more often than once every five reinsurance years beginning in 2011, according to the Congressional Research Service. The limit cut in two directions. For companies, the limit offered protection against a government tempted to reopen the agreement whenever a budget cycle created pressure for savings, because frequent reopening would make long term investment in delivery networks, staff, and systems difficult to justify. For the government, the limit imposed discipline of a different kind, because negotiators who know that an agreement must last at least five reinsurance years have reason to examine terms carefully before signing, rather than treating errors as problems that a quick reopening could fix.

A further constraint arrived with the next farm bill and narrowed the space within which any later renegotiation could move. Section 11012 of the Agricultural Act of 2014, Public Law 113-79, requires later renegotiations of the Standard Reinsurance Agreement to be budget neutral and to maintain or increase delivery subsidies relative to existing levels, according to the Congressional Research Service. Budget neutrality, in that setting, means that a renegotiation may rearrange compensation and risk sharing among the parties but may not be used as an instrument for increasing or decreasing the overall cost of the arrangement relative to the existing baseline. The delivery protection clause adds a directional rule inside the neutrality requirement, because delivery subsidies must be maintained or increased, and negotiators searching for offsets must therefore look elsewhere in the structure. Read together, the five year limit on reopening and the 2014 rules transformed the agreement into a settlement designed to endure, changeable only at intervals and only within boundaries that protect both the federal budget position and the delivery payment stream.

Delivery allowances, however, are only one of the two ways the agreement compensates companies, and an account that stopped with the cup and cap would miss the component that generated the sharpest analytical dispute of the period. Companies also participate in the underwriting results of the policies delivered. Under the risk sharing arrangements of the Standard Reinsurance Agreement, companies retain a share of premium and, with that share, a share of underwriting gains or losses, according to the Congressional Research Service. An underwriting gain arises when the premiums retained exceed the indemnities charged against them for a given book of business and period, while an underwriting loss arises when indemnities exceed the retained premium. The mechanism gives companies a financial stake in the quality of the business written and serviced. A company that writes carefully, verifies records accurately, and adjusts losses honestly shares in favorable results, and a company associated with poor underwriting outcomes bears part of the cost, subject always to the risk sharing structure that divides results between the companies and the federal government.

That division is organized through funds with distinct purposes and distinct allocations of risk. The Congressional Research Service describes risk sharing funds designated as commercial, developmental, and assigned risk funds, under which companies retain a share of premium and of underwriting gains or losses, with the federal government bearing most of the risk in the assigned risk fund. The assigned risk arrangement addresses a foundational obligation of the program. Companies selling federal crop insurance must accept every eligible applicant, and an approved insurance provider cannot refuse an eligible producer. A system that compels acceptance of all eligible business, including business in areas and commodities where loss experience is poor, cannot fairly place the full underwriting consequences of that compelled business on the company required to accept the business. The assigned risk fund answers the problem by shifting most of the risk for the riskiest placements to the federal government, while the commercial fund leaves a larger share of results with companies on business that resembles ordinary commercial insurance, and the developmental fund occupies ground between the two.

Against that structure, the 2011 agreement set a benchmark for judging what companies ought to earn from underwriting participation. The target rate of return on retained premium under the 2011 Standard Reinsurance Agreement was 14.5 percent, according to the Congressional Research Service. A target of that kind is not a payment and not a promise. The target is a calibration point used in designing the risk sharing terms, expressing the return that the structure is intended to make available to companies on the premium companies retain when experience unfolds as expected across the system. Comparison arrived in formal form from the Government Accountability Office. A 2017 study by the Government Accountability Office concluded that the average rate of return earned by the companies from the program exceeded the target rate set by the Department of Agriculture and exceeded comparable market rates of return, according to the Congressional Research Service summary of that study. Measured against the stated benchmark of the program itself, companies earned more than the design intended, and measured against returns available in comparable market settings, companies earned more than the alternatives that might have disciplined the negotiation.

The Government Accountability Office finding, stated at full strength, carries an implication that reaches beyond a single percentage. If the risk sharing structure was calibrated to deliver a target return of 14.5 percent on retained premium, and if experience under the structure delivered average returns above that target and above comparable market rates, then the calibration transferred more underwriting value to companies than the stated design required. Taxpayer funds support the program at every level, through premium subsidies, through delivery reimbursements bounded by the cup and cap, and through the federal share of underwriting risk, and an above target return on the retained share suggests that the division of underwriting results favored the companies more generously than the benchmark contemplated. The finding gains additional weight from the assigned risk fund, because the federal government bears most of the risk on the riskiest business, the downside exposure of companies is cushioned by design, and returns earned on a cushioned risk base that exceed market comparisons present a question about whether the compensation matches the risk actually borne.

The industry answer, also stated at full strength, begins with the character of agricultural risk itself. Crop insurance results swing with the weather, and weather at a national scale produces years that no calibration exercise can smooth away. A drought year produces underwriting losses, because indemnities surge across entire regions at once, claims arrive together rather than in the scattered pattern of ordinary insurable events, and the retained share of premium that generates gains in favorable years is overwhelmed by obligations in a catastrophic year. An average return calculated across a run of years that happen to contain favorable weather therefore describes a period, not a permanent condition, and the same companies that record above target returns across one span of years remain exposed to a season in which a widespread drought, an early freeze, or a harvest time flood reverses the arithmetic at a scale that no single year of gains may offset. Correlated risk distinguishes crop insurance from lines of insurance in which losses arrive independently and averages settle quickly toward expectations. In crop insurance, the average itself is hostage to the sequence of seasons included in the measurement, and a study window shapes the conclusion drawn from the window.

The industry answer also addresses the meaning of the target directly. The target rate of return of 14.5 percent on retained premium is, on that account, an average across years rather than a guarantee for any year, and a structure designed to average 14.5 percent across good years and bad years must necessarily deliver returns well above 14.5 percent in good years if the average is to survive contact with bad years. Judging the structure by returns in favorable periods alone would mistake the design for a defect. A company does not receive the average. A company receives the actual sequence, year by year, committing capital, staff, and reputation in advance and learning the result only after weather has decided the outcome, and the compensation for bearing that sequence must be assessed across the whole sequence. Capital committed to crop insurance delivery also cannot be withdrawn at the first sign of a deteriorating season, since policies are sold before planting and obligations run through harvest and adjustment, which means the return must compensate for capital locked into a risk whose outcome is correlated, seasonal, and largely beyond managerial control.

## The Products: From Yield Cover to Revenue Cover

The federal crop insurance program reached the producer not as a single contract but as a catalog of products, and the shape of that catalog explained much of the program itself. Two farms could plant the same crop in the same county, face the same weather, and hold policies that responded in entirely different ways, because one contract measured bushels and the other measured dollars. That difference was not a technical detail tucked into policy language. It determined whether insurance protected a harvest as a physical event, protected the income that the harvest was expected to generate, or protected some combination of the two. The history of the products was therefore a history of a widening definition of agricultural risk, moving from production alone toward revenue and toward layered coverage that filled different bands of loss in different ways.

At the center of that history stood a simple distinction. A policy could protect production, meaning the quantity of a crop that a producer harvested from insured acres. A policy could also protect revenue, meaning the value that production earned when quantity was combined with a market price. Yield protection answered the first concern and revenue protection answered the second, and the distance between the two answers revealed why revenue products became so central to the program. A drought, a flood, a hailstorm, or a disease outbreak reduced quantity in a direct and visible way. A producer sold a quantity at a price, and income depended on both terms at once.

The limitation of yield only coverage appeared most clearly when a short crop lifted prices. When production fell across a broad area, the reduced supply could support a higher price for the quantity that remained. A producer whose harvest was cut in half did not necessarily lose half of expected income, because each remaining bushel, pound, or hundredweight could sell for more than the price expected before planting. The market supplied a partial offset, and that offset complicated the meaning of a yield loss. A guarantee written only in physical terms treated a given percentage loss of production as the insured event, regardless of whether prices had risen enough to soften the income effect.

That exposure explained the direction of product development. A revenue guarantee joined the two halves of the equation in a single calculation, so that the indemnity reflected what the harvested crop was worth rather than merely how much of the crop survived. When yield fell and price rose, the revenue calculation credited the higher price and measured the remaining loss against the guarantee. When yield held and price fell, the revenue calculation registered a loss that a yield policy would have missed. When both yield and price fell together, the revenue calculation captured a compounded loss more severe than either measure would have shown on its own. The appeal was practical rather than abstract. Farm expenses, land costs, loan obligations, and household income all depended on dollars received, not on bushels in isolation. A lender evaluating repayment capacity, a landlord evaluating rent, and a producer deciding whether a farming operation could continue for another season all confronted revenue, and a product that insured revenue spoke directly to that shared concern.

Before the consolidation of 2011, the farm level market had been divided among several named plans whose boundaries a producer had to learn separately. For the 2011 crop year, the Common Crop Insurance Policy combined the earlier farm level plans known as Actual Production History, Income Protection, Revenue Assurance, and Crop Revenue Coverage into three options, Yield Protection, Revenue Protection, and Revenue Protection with the Harvest Price Exclusion, as the Risk Management Agency Common Crop Insurance Policy Basic Provisions, 11-BR, released in April 2010, established and as farmdoc daily at the University of Illinois described in March 2011. The consolidation did not erase the underlying distinction between yield and revenue. Instead, the consolidation placed that distinction inside one policy framework, so that a producer chose among three related forms of protection rather than among a set of historically separate products with overlapping purposes.

Yield Protection was the production form within the consolidated policy. Yield Protection covered production losses only, in the formulation given by farmdoc daily at the University of Illinois in March 2011, resting on the production history and guarantee structure associated with the older Actual Production History approach. The insured event remained a shortfall of quantity relative to the guaranteed quantity, and price movement after the guarantee was set did not enlarge or reduce the basic character of that promise. A strong price rally could leave a producer better off than the yield loss alone suggested, while a price collapse could leave a producer with a harvested crop that met the quantity guarantee and still failed to produce adequate income.

Revenue Protection stood on the other side of the consolidated choice. Revenue Protection guaranteed revenue using a projected price set before planting and a harvest price set after harvest, according to farmdoc daily at the University of Illinois in March 2011, and the guarantee rose when the harvest price exceeded the projected price. Each element of that description carried weight. The projected price belonged to the period before planting, when expectations were formed, input decisions were made, and the guarantee had to be established early enough to guide the season. The harvest price belonged to the period after harvest, when the market value of the crop could be measured against the earlier expectation. The guarantee began with the projected price and therefore began as a statement of expected value rather than a promise fixed in physical terms alone. The harvest price then tested that expectation against the market outcome of the season. When the harvest price rose above the projected price, Revenue Protection raised the guarantee itself, so the producer was protected at the higher value rather than at the value anticipated before planting.

### How does revenue protection differ from yield protection?

Yield Protection insures production alone, while Revenue Protection insures the value of production by combining yield with market prices. Revenue Protection uses a projected price set before planting and a harvest price set after harvest, and its guarantee rises when the harvest price exceeds the projected price.

The third option, Revenue Protection with the Harvest Price Exclusion, preserved the revenue structure and removed the rising guarantee. The harvest price exclusion variant removed the rise in the guarantee when the harvest price exceeded the projected price, in the terms given by farmdoc daily at the University of Illinois in March 2011. A producer who selected the exclusion accepted a guarantee anchored to the projected price for that purpose, even if prices climbed during the season. The harvest price still mattered to the valuation of the crop that was actually produced, but the target against which revenue was measured did not climb with the market. In exchange for giving up that feature, the producer obtained the product at a lower premium than the corresponding Revenue Protection policy, because the insurer had surrendered a defined element of exposure. The trade was intelligible in ordinary budget terms. The rising guarantee was valuable in precisely the circumstance of a short crop and high prices, and removing that guarantee left the producer more exposed in that circumstance. A producer who placed a higher priority on reducing premium expense could rationally select the exclusion, while a producer whose marketing plan made the higher guarantee central had reason to pay for Revenue Protection in its full form.

The three options therefore formed a spectrum of price involvement rather than three unrelated products. Yield Protection excluded price from the guarantee almost entirely and insured quantity. Revenue Protection with the Harvest Price Exclusion included price in the revenue calculation but held the guarantee to the projected price level for the harvest price feature. Revenue Protection included price in both the calculation and the guarantee, allowing the guarantee to reset upward with the harvest price. Premium differences among the options reflected those differences in promised protection, and the choice among the options forced a producer to state, in contract form, how much price risk the insurance was expected to carry. The consolidation of 2011 made that spectrum legible inside a common policy, and the common structure made comparison across the spectrum more direct than comparison across the older patchwork of named plans had been. Revenue coverage did not guarantee a profit and did not remove the deductible embedded in a coverage level, and losses within the deductible band remained with the producer unless another product filled part of that band.

Alongside the farm level products, area plans measured loss over a county or another defined area rather than over a single farm alone. Such a design tied payment to a broader outcome that a producer could not influence alone, while introducing a different imperfection, since an individual producer could suffer a severe loss while the area as a whole performed well enough to produce little or no payment. Each product chose a unit of measurement, and each choice gained something in simplicity or breadth while surrendering something in exact correspondence to the loss of a particular farm.

The Supplemental Coverage Option gave that layered pattern a precise statutory form. The Supplemental Coverage Option was authorized by the Agricultural Act of 2014, commonly called the 2014 farm bill, and carried a premium subsidy of 65 percent, as the Congressional Research Service recorded. The option covered part of the deductible band of an underlying policy, up to 86 percent of expected revenue or yield at the county level, as the Congressional Research Service also recorded. The structure deserves close attention because the option addressed the losses that the underlying policy deliberately left uninsured. A producer with an underlying policy at a coverage level below 86 percent carried the band between the selected coverage level and full expected value as a form of retention. The Supplemental Coverage Option reached into a portion of that retained band, from the coverage level of the underlying policy upward toward the 86 percent county level, and paid on a county basis rather than on the result of the individual farm alone. Losses of that size were often described as shallow losses, meaning losses too small to trigger the underlying policy but large enough to matter to an operation across many acres or across repeated seasons. The county basis separated the option from the underlying contract even when the two were purchased together, since the option responded when the county result fell into the covered band, measured against expected county revenue or expected county yield depending on the underlying election. Layering divided the range of possible losses into bands and assigned different instruments to different bands.

Whole farm and multicounty structures extended the catalog in a different direction, toward breadth of the insured unit rather than toward a supplemental band. Enterprise units were authorized by the 2008 farm bill, whole farm units also dated from that period, and multicounty enterprise units were introduced by the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018, as the Congressional Research Service recorded. An enterprise approach treated all of a crop in a county as one unit, pooling the production experience across fields that might otherwise have been divided into smaller units. A whole farm approach widened the unit further, bringing the operation together under a single structure in which stronger performance in one part of the operation could offset weaker performance in another part before an indemnity arose. A multicounty enterprise unit widened the geography again, treating a crop across county lines as a combined enterprise under the structure introduced in 2018. Each widening changed the risk calculation. A larger unit diversified production outcomes internally, so isolated field losses were less likely to trigger payment, while widespread losses that affected the whole combined unit retained a clearer path to indemnity. Producers weighed the lower cost and administrative simplicity of a combined unit against the loss of payment sensitivity for a single damaged field or a single county pocket of poor performance. Federal choices about which units were recognized guided producers toward some forms of risk management, even though the final election rested with the producer and the companies were required to accept every eligible applicant, a standard term of the Standard Reinsurance Agreement recorded in the verified record.

By the measure of producer choice, revenue coverage won the catalog. Revenue coverage accounted for the largest share of policies sold within a decade of the Agricultural Risk Protection Act of 2000, as the Congressional Research Service reported. That finding carried significance beyond market share among insurance products. The 2000 act had raised premium subsidies and had strengthened the framework within which revenue products operated, and the following decade showed producers selecting revenue protection in numbers sufficient to make revenue the leading form. The program, judged by the policies that participants actually bought, insured income rather than production for most participants. A statute that had begun in 1938 with production loss as the organizing idea had become, through successive amendments and product development, a system whose dominant contract measured the dollar value of a crop.

Breadth of participation reinforced the weight of that shift. About 80 percent of corn, wheat, soybean, and cotton acres carried some form of federally subsidized crop insurance by the middle of the decade preceding the article date, as the Economic Research Service of the Department of Agriculture reported in 2014. Those four crops occupied a central place in American field crop production, and coverage across roughly four fifths of their acres meant that the product choices described in the catalog governed risk management for the greater part of the major commodity sector. A change in the definition of revenue, in the setting of a projected price or a harvest price, or in the treatment of an enterprise unit reached far beyond a marginal group of insured producers. Such a change touched planting finance, marketing decisions, and lender expectations across the regions where those crops dominated.

The edges of the catalog revealed the limits of the insurance model as clearly as the center revealed its reach. Authority for livestock coverage stood at one edge, extending the insurance idea beyond planted crops to animals whose risks involved mortality, price, and feeding margins rather than a harvested yield from insured acres. Livestock coverage did not fit the production history template that governed the principal field crop products, because an animal operation had no direct equivalent to a bushel yield measured against a historical average from a defined acre. Livestock stood as an authority at the boundary of the catalog rather than as another variation on Yield Protection or Revenue Protection, and that placement is faithful to the structure. The central products measured crops. The livestock authority addressed a different kind of agricultural asset under different measurement problems.

A second edge stood entirely outside the company delivered insurance structure. The Noninsured Crop Disaster Assistance Program covered producers of crops for which no catastrophic coverage was available, was administered by the Farm Service Agency rather than through insurance companies, and held permanent authority under section 196 of the Federal Agriculture Improvement and Reform Act of 1996, codified at 7 U.S.C. 7333, as the Congressional Research Service recorded. The placement of that program mattered. Catastrophic coverage formed the base layer for crops that the insurance system could underwrite, indemnifying yield losses beyond 50 percent of expected production at 55 percent of the expected price, with the premium subsidized at 100 percent and the producer paying only an administrative fee, as the verified record states. Some crops lacked that catastrophic availability. The Noninsured Crop Disaster Assistance Program supplied a disaster assistance substitute for that uncovered space, delivered through a Department of Agriculture agency with a county office presence rather than through approved insurance providers and agents. The program was therefore adjacent to crop insurance in purpose and separate from crop insurance in legal and administrative form. A producer in the uncovered space dealt with the Farm Service Agency, under the authority of section 196, and not with a company operating under the Standard Reinsurance Agreement.

The closing picture was one of graduated inclusion. At the center stood the three consolidated options for the 2011 crop year. Around that center stood area measurement, the Supplemental Coverage Option filling part of the deductible band up to the 86 percent county level with a 65 percent subsidy, enterprise and whole farm units from the 2008 farm bill period, and multicounty enterprise units from the 2018 farm bill. Beyond the company delivered catalog stood livestock authority and the Noninsured Crop Disaster Assistance Program under the Farm Service Agency. The Congressional Research Service finding on revenue share and the Economic Research Service finding on acreage placed that catalog at the center of American commodity risk management by 2021, and the measure of a bad season had become, for most participants, a shortfall of revenue rather than a shortfall of bushels alone.

## The Asymmetry: Limits in One Title, None in the Other

A farm bill contains more than one theory of how public money should reach a farm. One theory treats support as a payment, counts the payment, caps the payment, and asks whether the recipient earns too much from other sources to qualify at all. Another theory treats support as a share of an insurance premium, pays that share to make a contract affordable, and asks questions about the crop, the county, the coverage level, and the risk being insured. Federal crop insurance law lives inside the second theory. The commodity title, which houses Price Loss Coverage and Agriculture Risk Coverage, is built around explicit limits. The crop insurance title, which rests on the Federal Crop Insurance Act, is built around a subsidy that carries no comparable ceiling. The difference is structural, visible in the statute itself, and confirmed by the way the Government Accountability Office and the Congressional Budget Office have framed reform studies as proposals for limits that the law does not contain.

The discipline imposed on the commodity title under the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 can be stated in two numbers. The first governs how much a recipient may collect. Combined payments under Price Loss Coverage and Agriculture Risk Coverage are capped at 125,000 dollars for each person, and peanuts carry a separate limit of 125,000 dollars, a carve out reflecting the longstanding practice of treating peanuts under distinct program rules. The cap applies to the combined total across the two programs rather than to each program in isolation, so a producer cannot multiply the ceiling by enrolling in both designs. The second number governs who may collect anything at all. Eligibility ends at an adjusted gross income limit of 900,000 dollars, measured as an average across three years. A producer whose three year average rises above that threshold is barred from the payments regardless of the size of the loss the programs were designed to offset, and a producer below the threshold remains subject to the payment cap no matter how many acres enter the calculation.

That three year measurement shows how seriously the commodity title takes the screening function. Farm income swings sharply with weather, yields, and prices, so a single year test would sweep operations in and out of eligibility on the strength of one harvest or one market rally. Averaging across three years smooths that volatility and targets the test at operations with sustained high income rather than operations that had one exceptional year. No official weighs the character of an operation case by case. The statute draws a line, defines the measurement period, and lets the arithmetic decide. The payment cap works the same mechanical way. The cap does not ask whether a large operation suffered a large loss, whether its costs were high, or whether its land is more valuable than a neighbor's land. The cap simply stops the combined payment at the stated figure.

### Why do payment limits apply to commodity programs but not to crop insurance subsidies?

The two titles were written under different statutes with different rules. The commodity title imposes a payment cap and an income test by law, while the crop insurance statute imposes neither on premium subsidies, so a subsidy grows with insured liability unless Congress adds a limit.

The crop insurance title reverses both elements of that design. Premium subsidies carry no per person payment limit, and they carry no adjusted gross income means test at all. A producer is not disqualified from premium support because income crossed a threshold in the preceding three years, and the total subsidy attaching to an operation is not stopped at a fixed dollar figure once a season's policies are written. The subsidy instead follows the premium, and the premium follows the liability insured. Liability grows with the number of acres covered, the value of the crop on those acres, the coverage level selected, and the risk rating of the ground involved. An operation insuring more acres of a valuable crop at a high coverage level therefore draws a larger public contribution than an operation insuring fewer acres of a lower value crop at a basic level, not because any administrator chose to favor the larger operation, but because the subsidy is a percentage of a premium that scales with exposure. The statutory schedule sets that share by coverage level and unit structure. Whatever percentage applies, the dollar amount remains a share of a total, and the total has no statutory ceiling tied to the person receiving the benefit.

The Government Accountability Office and the Congressional Budget Office have each treated that openness as the baseline fact from which any reform discussion must begin. In 2015 the Government Accountability Office studied reducing premium subsidies for the highest income participants, an inquiry that made sense only because no means test stood in the statute to be enforced or tightened. The office reported that the federal government paid an average of about 62 percent of total premiums in 2014, a figure capturing how substantial the public share had become across the program. In 2017 the Congressional Budget Office analyzed imposing an income based limit on premium subsidies and estimated the savings such options could produce, again treating the limit as a change to law rather than a feature of law. The Congressional Budget Office likewise reported that premium subsidies had averaged 62 percent of total premiums over the preceding five years, with statutory rates ranging from 38 percent to 100 percent. A cap on commodity payments could be adjusted by changing a number already in the statute. A cap on premium subsidies had to be invented, modeled, and priced, because the statute contained no number to change.

The scale of the spending sharpens the point. The Government Accountability Office reported that federal crop insurance costs averaged 3.8 billion dollars per year for fiscal years 2004 through 2008 and averaged 8.5 billion dollars per year for fiscal years 2009 through 2014, with the Congressional Budget Office projecting an average of 8.9 billion dollars per year for fiscal years 2015 through 2024. In 2011, a record premium year, total premiums reached 11.8 billion dollars, premium subsidies accounted for about 7.4 billion dollars, and administrative and operating payments to companies added about 1.3 billion dollars. Under a cap, growth in total spending must come mainly from growth in the number of participants, because each participant's share is bounded. Without a cap, total spending can grow from participation, from rising crop prices that inflate liability, from higher coverage elections, and from concentration of insured value in the largest operations, all at once and without a decision point at which the statute intervenes.

The historical route by which support arrived in that uncapped channel matters as much as the channel itself. Commodity support long included direct payments that flowed on a relatively predictable basis tied to historical acreage and yields rather than to a loss in a particular year. The Agricultural Act of 2014 ended the direct payment program, and support shifted in emphasis toward risk management tools, with crop insurance at the center of the design. The legal character of the assistance changed even where the underlying purpose, stabilizing farm income against forces beyond a producer's control, remained continuous. A direct payment had been a transfer of the kind that payment limits were invented to restrain, and the commodity programs that survived the 2014 act retained those restraints. A premium subsidy arrived attached to an insurance contract, was calculated as a percentage, and carried no limit over with it from the payment world it partly replaced. Support therefore migrated from a limited channel to an unlimited one through a change in instrument rather than through any single vote announcing that limits would cease to apply.

Analysts who press the distributional point argue that the consequence follows directly from the arithmetic of acreage. Because the subsidy follows insured liability, and because liability concentrates where acreage and crop value concentrate, the largest operations collect the largest subsidies as a matter of structure rather than as an accident of administration. A farm insuring several thousand acres draws premium support measured against that entire exposure, while a farm insuring a few hundred acres draws support measured against a fraction of the exposure, even though both farms face the same subsidy percentage at the same coverage level. The percentage is equal and the outcome is unequal, and the inequality widens in proportion to the difference in scale. That concentration claim rests in part on [the distributional evidence on farm subsidy policy](/2021/01/15/farm-subsidy-policy-impact/), which traces how payments follow scale. The same farm bill architecture that sets commodity support beside nutrition assistance, a contrast examined in [the commodity and nutrition titles compared](/2021/02/15/farm-bill-commodity-vs-nutrition/), also places a capped commodity title beside an uncapped insurance title, and analysts who emphasize distribution treat that placement as the central policy choice requiring justification.

The strongest answer from the program's defenders begins by refusing to describe a premium subsidy as a payment wearing another name. An indemnity is paid only after a verified loss satisfies the terms of the contract, and a producer whose crop performs as expected collects no indemnity at all despite having received subsidized premium support at planting. The subsidy buys participation in a risk pool and a contract that may return nothing in a good year, and defenders argue that a limit designed for a guaranteed transfer fits that arrangement poorly. Defenders also emphasize the share of cost producers carry themselves. With the federal government paying an average of about 62 percent of total premiums across the years the Government Accountability Office and the Congressional Budget Office examined, producers paid the remaining share, a substantial portion of every premium dollar, out of operating funds before any loss occurred. On top of that share sits the deductible built into every policy. Coverage protecting 75 percent or 80 percent of expected production or revenue leaves the first portion of any shortfall with the producer, so even a claim year begins with the operation absorbing a real loss the policy never touches. A limit on an insurance subsidy, defenders conclude, is unlike a limit on a payment because the two instruments distribute money, risk, and uncertainty in different ways.

That defense deserves to be stated at full strength, because the counter reading answers a genuine problem rather than a manufactured one. Agriculture exposes producers to weather and price risks that arrive together, strike without regard to the quality of management, and can erase the margin on an entire year of work in a single storm, drought, flood, or market collapse. Private insurance markets acting alone historically undersupplied coverage for such correlated risks. By the middle of the last decade, about 80 percent of corn, wheat, soybean, and cotton acres carried some form of federally subsidized crop insurance, according to the Economic Research Service of the Department of Agriculture in 2014, a breadth defenders cite as evidence the design reaches the working center of production agriculture. The serious criticism, on this reading, concerns the absence of limits, not the existence of the program. A reader can accept the insurance rationale, accept that risk pooling requires broad participation, and accept that producers pay real money and absorb real deductibles, and still ask why the commodity title screens recipients by income while the insurance title screens no one, and why one channel stops at 125,000 dollars per person while the other has no stopping point at all. The asymmetry survives the strongest version of the defense, which is what makes the asymmetry, rather than the program, the durable subject of debate.

One condition does attach to premium subsidy eligibility, and its history tracks the larger story of linkage between farm benefits and obligations. Conservation compliance was created by the Food Security Act of 1985, which established highly erodible land conservation requirements, known as sodbuster provisions, and wetlands conservation requirements, known as swampbuster provisions, and attached those requirements to farm program benefits including crop insurance. Sodbuster rules address the cultivation of highly erodible land. A producer who brings such land into production, or who farms highly erodible ground already in production, must do so under an approved conservation system designed to hold soil loss within tolerable bounds, through practices such as residue management, reduced tillage, cover, and rotations matched to the field. The point is that public support should not subsidize the rapid erosion of fragile soil. Swampbuster rules address wetlands. A producer who drains, fills, levels, or otherwise converts a wetland to make crop production possible, or who produces a crop on a converted wetland, risks eligibility for the benefits to which compliance attaches. The point in that case is that public support should not reward the destruction of wetland functions such as water storage, filtration, and habitat.

The link between those requirements and crop insurance was not continuous. The Federal Agriculture Improvement and Reform Act of 1996 removed the compliance link for crop insurance, even though compliance obligations continued in relation to other farm program benefits, and for nearly two decades premium subsidies flowed without that condition. Section 2611 of the Agricultural Act of 2014 reattached conservation compliance to eligibility for crop insurance premium subsidies, the first such link since 1996, and producers were required to have a conservation compliance certification, form AD-1026, on file with the Farm Service Agency by June 1, 2015, to remain eligible for premium support. The timing is difficult to separate from the migration of support described earlier in this section. Once the 2014 act ended direct payments and placed greater weight on crop insurance as the principal safety net instrument, the absence of a compliance link on the insurance side meant that the fastest growing form of support carried fewer attached obligations than the payment programs it was displacing. Reattaching compliance closed that gap, and the restoration illustrates how Congress legislates in this area, moving individual conditions from one title to another as the balance of spending between titles shifts.

The design of the sanction reveals the same care about the distinction between a subsidy and a contract. A producer who fails conservation compliance loses eligibility for the premium subsidy, not the ability to hold a crop insurance policy. The distinction follows from what compliance legally attaches to. The federal contribution is a public benefit, and Congress may condition a public benefit on conservation behavior without extinguishing the underlying private transaction. A noncompliant producer may still purchase coverage by paying the full unsubsidized premium, dealing with an approved insurance provider on the same contractual terms as any other policyholder apart from price, and collecting an indemnity if a covered loss occurs. The policy survives because the policy is a contract for risk transfer that the producer can fund entirely alone. The subsidy disappears because the subsidy is the point at which public money enters the arrangement, and public money is the point at which public conditions properly bite. Companies selling federal crop insurance must accept every eligible applicant, so withdrawal of a subsidy does not become a mechanism for excluding producers from coverage altogether. Compliance operates on the price the public pays, not on the existence of the market.

Readers returning to the statute after following the debate often carry three misreadings with them, and each misreading obscures the asymmetry. The first describes crop insurance as private insurance, full stop. Delivery is genuinely private in important respects, since approved insurance providers sell and service policies, agents work in local communities, and loss adjustment happens through company channels under the Standard Reinsurance Agreement between each company and the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation. Behind that delivery system stands a federal corporation created by the Federal Crop Insurance Act as Title V of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938, capital stock subscribed by the United States, premium subsidies fixed in the statute, administrative and operating reimbursements paid to companies as a percentage of premium, and a reinsurance structure under which the federal government bears most of the risk in the assigned risk fund and shares underwriting gains and losses across the commercial and developmental funds. A program with a public balance sheet, statutory prices, and a federal reinsurer is a public program delivered through private companies, and collapsing that arrangement into the phrase private insurance hides the public contribution the limits debate is about.

The second misreading assumes that payment limits apply to crop insurance because payment limits apply elsewhere in the farm bill. The assumption feels natural, since the 125,000 dollar cap and the 900,000 dollar income test are prominent features of the commodity title and are often described in summaries as farm bill limits without a title attached. The statute draws the boundary more narrowly. The cap governs combined Price Loss Coverage and Agriculture Risk Coverage payments, with the separate peanut limit, and the income test governs eligibility for those payments, while premium subsidies under the crop insurance title carry neither device. The Government Accountability Office study of 2015 and the Congressional Budget Office analysis of 2017 exist precisely because that boundary is real. Treating the limits as universal misstates the law in the direction of greater discipline than the law imposes, and a producer, lender, or policymaker reasoning from that assumption will miscalculate both the benefits available to a large operation and the exposure of the federal budget to concentrated insured value. Precision about which title a limit inhabits is therefore the difference between the farm bill as summarized and the farm bill as enacted.

The third misreading assumes that the commodity title is the largest support channel among the farm bill's agricultural titles, an assumption inherited from an era when direct payments dominated discussion of farm support. The spending record of crop insurance complicates that inheritance. Federal costs averaging 8.5 billion dollars per year across fiscal years 2009 through 2014, projected by the Congressional Budget Office to average 8.9 billion dollars per year across the following decade, with premium subsidies alone reaching about 7.4 billion dollars in the record premium year of 2011, describe a channel of the first rank. Among the channels delivering support to producers for production and price risk, crop insurance had grown into a central pillar by the period covered here. The misreading matters because a debate focused exclusively on capping commodity payments can leave the larger structural question untouched. Limits in one title and none in the other mean that the discipline of the farm bill binds most tightly where payments are most visible, and binds hardly at all where support flows as a percentage of a premium through a contract that may or may not produce an indemnity. Whether that arrangement represents a wise accommodation to the special character of insurance or an unfinished piece of fiscal design is the question the two titles, read side by side, leave open.

## A Map of the Text

A reader who walks the codified text at sections 1501 and following of title 7 meets the program in the order in which the statute builds it. The opening provisions establish the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation and its Board, the body that holds the authority and approves the products the system may sell. The definitions follow in function, fixing the terms on which every later provision operates, from the approved insurance provider to catastrophic coverage and the production history from which a guarantee is built. A researcher tracing that sequence can [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html), because the order of the text is itself an argument about how the program works.

The insurance authority and the policy terms come next, stating what coverage the Corporation may offer and on what conditions a producer may hold it. After that authority, the text sets the subsidy schedule, the percentages in section 1508(e) that divide each premium between the producer and the government by coverage level and unit structure. Delivery and reinsurance follow, because a subsidized policy still requires a seller, a servicing structure, and an agreement that divides premium and risk between the company and the Corporation. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does federal crop insurance actually work?

A producer purchases a policy from an approved insurance provider, a private company approved to sell and service federal crop insurance through local agents. The Federal Crop Insurance Corporation supplies the statutory authority and stands behind the program, the Risk Management Agency administers rates and rules, and the federal government pays a share of the premium under the schedule at section 1508(e) of title 7. The policy builds a guarantee from production history, a coverage level, and a price term, and an indemnity becomes due when production or revenue falls below that guarantee. Companies retain a share of premium and underwriting results under the Standard Reinsurance Agreement, while the Corporation reinsures the remainder.

### Q: Who sells crop insurance policies?

Approved insurance providers sell crop insurance policies. An approved insurance provider is a private insurance company approved to participate in the federal program, and the company delivers policies through commissioned local agents who explain coverage, enroll producers, collect reports and the producer share of premium, and arrange loss adjustment. The Federal Agriculture Improvement and Reform Act of 1996 required the Department of Agriculture to cease selling policies directly where approved provider coverage was available, completing the shift to private delivery. A defining condition accompanies approval. Companies selling federal crop insurance must accept every eligible applicant, and an approved insurance provider cannot refuse an eligible producer, so approval carries a duty to serve the eligible population rather than a freedom to select only the safest farms.

### Q: How much of a crop insurance premium does the government pay?

The government share is written into section 1508(e) of title 7 and varies by unit structure and coverage level. For basic and optional units, the share is 100 percent for catastrophic coverage, then 67 percent at the 50 percent coverage level, 64 percent at 55 percent, 64 percent at 60 percent, 59 percent at 65 percent, 59 percent at 70 percent, 55 percent at 75 percent, 48 percent at 80 percent, and 38 percent at 85 percent. For enterprise and multicounty enterprise units, the share is 80 percent at every level from 50 percent through 70 percent, then 77 percent at 75 percent, 68 percent at 80 percent, and 53 percent at 85 percent. Across all choices, the federal government paid an average of about 62 percent of total premiums in 2014.

### Q: Are there payment limits on crop insurance?

Premium subsidies carry no per person payment limit and no adjusted gross income means test. That absence stands in contrast to the commodity title, where the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 capped combined Price Loss Coverage and Agriculture Risk Coverage payments at 125,000 dollars per person, with a separate 125,000 dollar limit for peanuts, and set an adjusted gross income eligibility limit of 900,000 dollars measured as a three year average. The Government Accountability Office in 2015 studied reducing subsidies for the highest income participants precisely because no means test applied, and the Congressional Budget Office in 2017 analyzed imposing an income based limit as a change to law. A crop insurance subsidy therefore grows with insured liability, while a commodity payment stops at a statutory ceiling.

### Q: What is revenue protection in crop insurance?

Revenue Protection is one of the three options created when, for the 2011 crop year, the Common Crop Insurance Policy combined the earlier farm level plans into Yield Protection, Revenue Protection, and Revenue Protection with the Harvest Price Exclusion. Revenue Protection guarantees revenue using a projected price set before planting and a harvest price set after harvest, and the guarantee rises when the harvest price exceeds the projected price. The design joins quantity and price in a single calculation, so a short harvest sold at a higher price and a full harvest sold at a lower price both register against the same revenue guarantee. The harvest price exclusion variant removes the rise in the guarantee, leaving the revenue structure in place without that upward reset.

### Q: What do companies get from crop insurance beyond premiums?

Companies receive an administrative and operating reimbursement calculated as a percentage of premium, in addition to a retained share of premium and underwriting results. The Congressional Budget Office reported that total administrative and operating reimbursements ranged from 11 percent to 16 percent of total premiums from 2000 through 2016, and companies used that reimbursement to pay agent commissions, loss adjustment, and other delivery costs. In 2011, administrative and operating payments to companies were about 1.3 billion dollars. The Standard Reinsurance Agreement also places companies inside the underwriting result through its commercial, developmental, and assigned risk funds, so a company shares gains and losses on the premium the company retains, against a target rate of return on retained premium of 14.5 percent set in the 2011 agreement.

### Q: Is crop insurance bigger than commodity programs?

The verified record supports a careful comparison rather than a single paired total, because the fact record gives crop insurance costs in detail and gives the commodity title mainly through its limits. Federal crop insurance costs averaged 3.8 billion dollars per year for fiscal years 2004 through 2008 and 8.5 billion dollars per year for fiscal years 2009 through 2014, and the Congressional Budget Office projected an average of 8.9 billion dollars per year for fiscal years 2015 through 2024. Premium subsidies alone were about 7.4 billion dollars in 2011. The structural point accompanies those figures. The largest federal farm support program sits in the insurance title rather than the commodity title, and the cost figures place crop insurance at a scale that commodity payment limits, capped at 125,000 dollars per person, do not describe.

### Q: Does crop insurance require conservation compliance?

Yes. Conservation compliance attaches to eligibility for crop insurance premium subsidies, after a history of attachment, removal, and reattachment. The Food Security Act of 1985 created highly erodible land conservation and wetlands conservation requirements, known as sodbuster and swampbuster provisions, and attached them to farm program benefits including crop insurance. The Federal Agriculture Improvement and Reform Act of 1996 removed the compliance link for crop insurance. Section 2611 of the Agricultural Act of 2014 reattached compliance to eligibility for crop insurance premium subsidies, the first such link since 1996. Producers were required to have a conservation compliance certification, form AD-1026, on file with the Farm Service Agency by June 1, 2015, to remain eligible for premium support. A producer who failed compliance lost the subsidy, while the underlying policy remained a contract the producer could fund without federal support.

### Q: What is catastrophic coverage in crop insurance?

Catastrophic coverage, known as CAT, is the base layer of the federal crop insurance system, created by the Federal Crop Insurance Reform Act of 1994. CAT indemnifies yield losses beyond 50 percent of expected production, and it pays at 55 percent of the expected price. The payment rate was 60 percent when the coverage was created and was later reduced to 55 percent. The CAT premium is subsidized at 100 percent, and the producer pays only an administrative fee for each crop in each county. The design is deliberately thin. A producer holding only CAT absorbs the first half of a production loss and receives payment on a deeper loss at a reduced price rate, so the tier functions as protection against ruin rather than as compensation for an ordinary shortfall.

### Q: What is the Standard Reinsurance Agreement?

The Standard Reinsurance Agreement is the negotiated agreement between the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation and each approved insurance provider, and the agreement sets risk sharing and compensation for the program. Under its commercial, developmental, and assigned risk funds, a company retains a share of premium and of underwriting gains or losses, with the federal government bearing most of the risk in the assigned risk fund. The Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008 directed a renegotiation for the 2011 reinsurance year and limited renegotiation to no more often than once every five reinsurance years beginning in 2011. The 2011 agreement introduced a floor and a ceiling on total administrative and operating subsidy and set a target rate of return on retained premium of 14.5 percent.

### Q: When was the Risk Management Agency created?

The Risk Management Agency traces its creation to the Federal Agriculture Improvement and Reform Act of 1996. That act allowed the creation of the Risk Management Agency, and the agency has administered the federal crop insurance program since 1996. Administration in this program is a technical function. The agency sets the premium rates that attach to crops, counties, coverage levels, and unit structures, establishes the yield measures against which production is judged, and writes the program rules governing eligibility, reporting, and claims. The same 1996 act required the Department of Agriculture to cease selling policies directly where approved insurance provider coverage was available, so the creation of the agency accompanied the completion of private delivery through approved companies and their agents.

### Q: What is an enterprise unit in crop insurance?

An enterprise unit combines all of the insured crop in a county into a single unit, so production across the acreage is measured together and strong production on one part of the acreage offsets weak production on another part before a shortfall is found. Enterprise units were authorized by the farm bill enacted in 2008, and multicounty enterprise units, introduced by the farm bill enacted in 2018, extend that combination across county lines. The statute prices the structure through a richer premium subsidy. For enterprise units, the government share is 80 percent at every coverage level from 50 percent through 70 percent, then 77 percent at 75 percent, 68 percent at 80 percent, and 53 percent at 85 percent, reflecting the lower risk of insuring all of a crop in a county as one unit.

### Q: How are new crop insurance products created through privately developed submissions?

The section 508(h) process is the channel through which private parties develop new crop insurance products for approval by the Board of the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation. The Agricultural Risk Protection Act of 2000 created the process, placing product invention partly outside the government while keeping entitlement inside the statutory system. A private party designs a product, submits the product through the process, and the Board decides whether the product may be sold with federal support. The arrangement matters because participation depends on fit as well as on price, and a standing approval route allows the catalog to develop products fitted to risks that the standard policy forms did not address. Revenue coverage became the largest share of policies sold within a decade of the 2000 act, in a system that had opened that development channel.

### Q: What is the Noninsured Crop Disaster Assistance Program?

The Noninsured Crop Disaster Assistance Program, known as NAP, covers producers of crops for which no catastrophic coverage is available. The Federal Crop Insurance Reform Act of 1994 created the program as part of a package that also created catastrophic coverage and authorized prevented planting coverage. NAP is administered by the Farm Service Agency rather than through insurance companies, so a producer in the uncovered space deals with a Department of Agriculture agency rather than with an approved insurance provider operating under the Standard Reinsurance Agreement. The program holds permanent authority under section 196 of the Federal Agriculture Improvement and Reform Act of 1996, codified at section 7333 of title 7. NAP therefore stands adjacent to crop insurance in purpose and separate from crop insurance in legal and administrative form.

### Q: How much does the federal crop insurance program cost each year?

The Government Accountability Office reported that federal costs averaged 3.8 billion dollars per year for fiscal years 2004 through 2008 and averaged 8.5 billion dollars per year for fiscal years 2009 through 2014, in report GAO-15-356. The same report relayed a Congressional Budget Office projection of an average of 8.9 billion dollars per year for fiscal years 2015 through 2024. The composition of cost in a single record year shows the parts behind such averages. In 2011, total premiums reached 11.8 billion dollars, premium subsidies were about 7.4 billion dollars, and administrative and operating payments to companies were about 1.3 billion dollars. Cost therefore followed the premium base that producer coverage choices created, rather than a fixed annual sum set in advance.

### Q: Why did Congress require farmers to buy crop insurance in the past?

Congress imposed the requirement through the linkage provision of the Federal Crop Insurance Reform Act of 1994, which required producers to obtain at least catastrophic coverage as a condition of receiving commodity program benefits. The requirement, codified at the time at section 1433f of title 7, attacked a participation problem that voluntary purchase had not solved, because a thin pool of insured producers weakened the insurance model and left ad hoc disaster payments as the standing response to crop failure. The response showed the force of the device. In 1995, with linkage in force, participation rose above 80 percent of the eligible area, and over half of the insured area sat at the catastrophic level. The Federal Agriculture Improvement and Reform Act of 1996 repealed the linkage requirement after that single year.

### Q: What happens to crop insurance companies in a severe drought year?

In a severe drought year, indemnities rise across entire regions at once, because crop losses are correlated and claims arrive together rather than in a scattered pattern. A company feels that year through the share of premium and underwriting result the company retained under the Standard Reinsurance Agreement, and a retained book can move from underwriting gain to underwriting loss when indemnities exceed retained premium. The agreement grades that exposure through its commercial, developmental, and assigned risk funds, and the federal government bears most of the risk in the assigned risk fund. The Federal Crop Insurance Corporation reinsures the remainder behind the company position, so the correlated shock spreads across the federal account. The design gives a company exposure without ruin, leaving a financial interest in careful servicing while placing the full weight of a national crop failure on the public reinsurer.

### Q: Does crop insurance cover livestock?

The central catalog of the program covers crops, and livestock stands at the edge of that catalog under separate authority rather than as a variation on the principal field crop products. Livestock risks involve mortality, price, and feeding margins, and an animal operation has no direct equivalent to a bushel yield measured against a historical average from a defined acre. The production history template that governs Yield Protection and Revenue Protection therefore does not transfer to animals without a different measurement structure. A producer reading the statute should treat livestock coverage as a distinct authority addressing a different kind of agricultural asset, while the crop products, the subsidy schedule at section 1508(e) of title 7, and the company delivery system described in the crop insurance text govern the planted acreage at the center of the program.

### Q: How does crop insurance differ from disaster assistance payments?

Crop insurance is a contract purchased before a loss, with a premium divided between the producer and the federal government, a guarantee fixed by production history, coverage level, and price terms, and an indemnity measured by the policy when production or revenue falls below that guarantee. Disaster assistance, in the history of the program, arrived after a loss had occurred, under rules written for a particular emergency, without a premium paid in advance and without a guarantee established at planting. That after the fact pattern weakened the case for buying insurance and gave a producer no certainty at planting time. The Noninsured Crop Disaster Assistance Program occupies a defined middle position, supplying a standing disaster assistance substitute, administered by the Farm Service Agency, for crops for which no catastrophic coverage is available.

### Q: Where is federal crop insurance law codified?

Federal crop insurance law is codified at sections 1501 and following of title 7 of the United States Code. The source statute is the Federal Crop Insurance Act, which is Title V of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938, Public Law 75-430, enacted on February 16, 1938. Within that codified sequence, section 1503 establishes the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation as an agency within the Department of Agriculture, and section 1504 gives the Corporation capital stock of 500 million dollars subscribed by the United States. Section 1508(e) carries the premium subsidy schedule that divides each premium between the producer and the government. A reader who begins at section 1501 therefore enters the same structure the program itself uses, moving from the Corporation and its definitions toward insurance authority, subsidy, delivery, and reinsurance.
