The Advocates Act 1961, Act 25 of 1961, is the statute that turned a crowd of differently named legal practitioners into one profession. It created a single class of advocates, placed their names on rolls kept by elected Bar Councils, gave those councils charge of admission, standards, legal education and discipline, and reserved a small number of decisions to Parliament itself, among them the fee a new advocate pays to enter the profession.

![The Advocates Act 1961 and the self-governing legal profession in India - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-69.webp)

Within the wider body of [laws governing India's judicial system](/2021/04/01/judicial-system-laws-india-guide/), this Act is the one that decides who may stand up in a courtroom and speak for another person, and who decides whether that person may continue to do so. Its answer to the second question is the profession itself. Standards, enrolment and discipline sit with councils of advocates elected by advocates, and the Bar Council of India, the apex council, is built from members the State Bar Councils elect from among themselves. Yet the self-government is not complete, and the point at which it stops is written into Section 24 of the Act: the enrolment fee is a figure in the statute, fixed by Parliament, and no council may charge a rupee more. That single reservation explains how the Supreme Court could intervene on fees in 2024 while leaving the profession's examination, upheld a year earlier, in the profession's own hands.

The article follows the Act in its own order. It begins with the divided profession the Act replaced and the staged commencement by which the replacement happened, then takes the councils, their functions and their hold over legal education, the enrolment gate in Chapter III, the fee written into Section 24(1)(f), the training clause that Parliament removed and the bar examination that grew in its place, the right to practise in Chapter IV, the disciplinary machine in Chapter V, the certificate of practice verification, the foreign lawyer question, the rule-making powers and the repeal scheme, the amendment arc from 1964 to 2023, an assessment of self-regulation argued from both sides, and a step table that carries a new entrant from a law degree to the right to appear.

Four readers will use the article differently, and the sections are arranged so each can find a payload without reading past the need. The law student and the new entrant need the enrolment path, the fee position and the examination, which the enrolment, fee and training sections and the closing step table carry. The practising advocate needs the disciplinary route and the conduct rules, carried by the conduct section, the complaint journey and the verification section. The foreign firm and its adviser need the registration position, carried by the foreign lawyers section. The researcher needs the structure of self-regulation and the evidence on both sides of it, carried by the councils sections and the assessment.

## The divided profession the Advocates Act 1961 replaced

### What did the Advocates Act 1961 replace?

The Act replaced a patchwork. The Legal Practitioners Act 1879 governed pleaders, mukhtars and revenue agents in the lower courts, and the Indian Bar Councils Act 1926 gave each High Court a Bar Council with advisory functions only. Discipline stayed with the High Courts, enrolment differed from court to court, and no all-India body spoke for the profession.

Before 1961, the right to practise law in India depended on which door a practitioner entered by. The Legal Practitioners Act 1879, Act 18 of 1879, regulated the classes who worked mainly in the subordinate courts: pleaders, who held certificates tied to examinations and to the courts that issued them, mukhtars, who practised without a law degree under a different certificate, and revenue agents, who appeared in revenue offices. Above them stood the advocates and vakils of the High Courts, enrolled under the Letters Patent and the rules of each court, and in Bombay and Calcutta the attorneys, who worked on the original side under a dual system borrowed from England, instructing counsel rather than appearing in every matter themselves. Each class had its own gate, its own certificate and its own disciplinary master.

The Indian Bar Councils Act 1926, Act 38 of 1926, added a council to each High Court but withheld the powers that would have made it a regulator. A Bar Council under the 1926 Act could be consulted by the High Court, could frame rules with the High Court's approval on matters such as the rights and duties of advocates, and maintained the roll of advocates of that court. Enrolment remained an act of the High Court, discipline remained with the High Court, and the councils had no authority over legal education and no existence above the level of a single court. There was no Bar Council of India, no common roll, and no single answer to the question of who counted as a lawyer.

The district courts, where most litigation lived, ran on the 1879 Act's certificate system. A pleader's certificate was issued for a court or a group of courts and renewed periodically, and the High Court held disciplinary power over pleaders and mukhtars under the suspension and removal provisions of that Act, the provisions Section 50(4) of the Advocates Act later repealed when its own conduct chapter began. A practitioner could therefore be fully qualified for the courts of one district and a stranger in the next, and the gradation of pleader below vakil and vakil below advocate priced the hierarchy into every brief. The attorneys of Bombay and Calcutta added a further split inside the High Courts themselves, dividing the work of instructing counsel from the work of appearing, on the English model of solicitor and barrister. Unification was thus not tidying; it was the abolition of a ranked labour market in legal services, and the resistance it met explains much of the caution in the Act's transitional chapter.

The defects of the arrangement were structural rather than personal. A practitioner qualified in one High Court had no automatic standing in another. Standards of entry varied with the court. The classes below the advocates, the pleaders and mukhtars, were graded as inferior although they carried much of the litigation in the district courts, and their position froze a hierarchy that a unified profession could not keep. The All India Bar Committee, appointed in 1951 under the chairmanship of Justice S. R. Das, examined the structure and recommended a unified and autonomous Bar, a common roll of advocates, and Bar Councils at the State and national levels with real powers over enrolment, discipline and legal education. The Law Commission of India, in its Fourteenth Report of 1958 on the reform of judicial administration, supported the same direction. The Advocates Act 1961 is the statute that carried those recommendations into law.

The timing was dictated by the Constitution's settlement. The Republic had a Supreme Court at the top of a single judicial hierarchy, fundamental rights litigation that required counsel able to move between the district courts and the apex court, and a Parliament competent to legislate for the legal profession across the Union. A profession fragmented by province and by class fitted that structure badly: the advocate of one High Court was a foreigner in another, although the appeals from both converged on the same Supreme Court. Unification was the professional counterpart of the integrated judiciary, and the common roll the committee proposed was meant to make the practitioner's standing as national as the court system itself.

The committee's programme, which the Act follows closely, had four limbs: one integrated bar for the whole country in place of the graded classes; a common roll on which every practitioner's name would stand; autonomous Bar Councils, elected by the profession, holding the powers of admission and discipline that the High Courts had exercised; and the end of the separate tracks, the pleader's certificate, the mukhtar's licence and the attorney's articles, as routes into practice. The Act kept one distinction the committee's logic might have erased, the division between senior advocates and other advocates in Section 16, and it preserved the High Courts' rule-making over practice in their own courts in Section 34, concessions that show the unification was negotiated with the institutions it displaced rather than imposed on them.

The Act did not abolish the older world in a single stroke, and its manner of replacing it matters for reading the text. Section 50 repeals the older enactments in slices that follow the chapters: the Indian Bar Councils Act 1926 falls in the territory of a State Bar Council when that council is constituted, the enrolment provisions of the Legal Practitioners Act 1879 fall when Chapter III begins, the practice provisions fall when Chapter IV begins, and the disciplinary provisions fall when Chapter V begins. Chapter VII then protects the people caught in the changeover, saving the practice rights of pleaders, vakils, mukhtars and revenue agents who did not enrol as advocates, and transferring pending disciplinary proceedings from the old councils to the new ones. The replacement was therefore a sequence, not an event, and the Act's commencement record, taken next, shows how long the sequence ran.

## The Advocates Act 1961 at a glance: extent, commencement and structure

The Act received the assent of the President on 19 May 1961 and extends to the whole of India. Section 1(3) left commencement to notification, and the Central Government brought the Act into force in stages spread across fifty years. Chapters I, II and VII came into force on 16 August 1961, which means the councils could be constituted and the transitional provisions operated from that date. Chapter III, on admission and enrolment, followed on 1 December 1961, with sub-section (2) of Section 50. Sub-section (1) of Section 50 followed on 15 December 1961, Sections 51 and 52 on 24 January 1962, and Section 46 on 29 March 1962. Section 32 and Chapter VI began on 4 January 1963, and Chapter V, the conduct chapter, on 1 September 1963. The heart of Chapter IV arrived later still: Sections 29, 31, 33 and 34 commenced on 1 June 1969, and Section 30, the section that states the advocate's entitlement to practise as of right in every court in India, was not notified until 15 June 2011, by notification S.O. 1349(E) dated 9 June 2011. A statute of 1961 therefore contains a right that became operative law only half a century after enactment, a fact flagged again in the discussion of Chapter IV below.

Territorial extension ran on its own timetable as well. The Act was applied to Goa on 1 June 1979 and to Jammu and Kashmir on 1 August 1986, in each case without Section 30, and the reorganisation of 2019 carried it into the Union territories of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh by notification S.O. 3912(E) dated 30 October 2019. The pattern is consistent with the commencement scheme: the councils and the rolls could be built territory by territory, while the unifying right in Section 30, which presupposes a complete national roll, was withheld the longest, first in the late-joining territories and in the end everywhere until 2011.

The staged commencement explains a term that runs through the text. Section 2 measures the appointed day provision by provision: in relation to any provision of the Act, it is the day on which that provision comes into force. The definitions in Section 2 also fix the vocabulary of everything that follows. An advocate is a person entered in any roll under the Act. A legal practitioner, the older and wider expression the Act was retiring, means an advocate or vakil of any High Court, a pleader, a mukhtar or a revenue agent. A roll is a roll of advocates prepared and maintained under the Act, and the State roll is the roll a State Bar Council maintains under Section 17. A law graduate, the raw material of the whole scheme, is a person who has obtained a bachelor's degree in law from any university established by law in India, and a High Court, for the purposes of the Act, is the High Court for the State or the group the council serves, the High Court of Delhi in the case of the Bar Council of Delhi. The definitions are unglamorous and load-bearing: the difference between the advocate of Section 2 and the legal practitioner of the older law is the difference between the unified profession and the graded one, and the Act's choice to define its central figure by entry on a roll, rather than by qualification, degree or employment, is the choice that makes the roll, and the councils that keep it, the centre of everything that follows.

The structure is seven chapters holding 58 sections in their original numbering, with later insertions lettered into place. Chapter I, Sections 1 and 2, carries the title, extent, commencement and definitions. Chapter II, Sections 3 to 15, constitutes the Bar Councils and states their functions. Chapter III, Sections 16 to 28, governs admission and enrolment. Chapter IV, Sections 29 to 34, confers the right to practise. Chapter V, Sections 35 to 44, is the conduct chapter. Chapter VI, Sections 45 to 52, collects the miscellaneous provisions, including the penalty for unauthorised practice, the rule-making power of the Bar Council of India in Section 49, the Central Government's rule power in Section 49A and the repeals in Section 50. Chapter VII, Sections 53 to 58 with their lettered additions, holds the temporary and transitional provisions. The argument of this article, that the profession regulates itself within boundaries the statute draws, can be tested chapter by chapter against that map.

Two features of the text reward a reader's attention before the detail begins. The first is the lettering: sections inserted by later amendments carry letters, Section 8A, Section 9A, Section 24A, Section 36A and 36B, Section 42A, Section 45A, Section 46A, Section 48A, 48AA and 48B, Section 49A, and the run of special provisions from Section 58A onwards, so that the density of lettered sections in a region of the Act is a quick map of where Parliament has had to return and repair. The second is the Schedule, to which Section 50(5)(b) gives effect for repeals not carried in the section itself. An Act whose bare text shows its own amendment history in its numbering is an Act that announces, before any commentary, that its settlement has been renegotiated repeatedly.

## The two councils: composition and elections under Chapter II

### How many elected members does a State Bar Council have?

Section 3(2)(b) fixes elected strength by electorate size. A State Bar Council with an electorate not exceeding five thousand elects fifteen members, one with an electorate exceeding five thousand but not exceeding ten thousand elects twenty members, and one with an electorate exceeding ten thousand elects twenty-five members, by proportional representation through the single transferable vote.

Section 3 constitutes a Bar Council for every State, with some councils serving combinations of States and Union territories: the Bar Council of Maharashtra and Goa also covers Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu, the Bar Council of Punjab and Haryana covers Chandigarh, the Bar Council of Madras covers Tamil Nadu and Pondicherry, the Bar Council of Kerala covers Lakshadweep, and a single council serves Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Mizoram and Nagaland. The Union territory of Delhi has its own Bar Council. The composition in Section 3(2) has two elements. The first is ex officio: the Advocate-General of the State sits on its Bar Council by virtue of office, and for Delhi the ex officio member is the Additional Solicitor General of India. The second and larger element is elected, in the bands of fifteen, twenty and twenty-five set by the size of the electoral roll. A proviso to Section 3(2) adds a seniority condition: as nearly as possible, one-half of the elected members must be persons who have been advocates on a State roll for at least ten years, counting in any period of enrolment under the Indian Bar Councils Act 1926. Section 3(4) leaves the qualifications for voting and for membership to rules made by the Bar Council of India, and each State Bar Council prepares and revises the electoral roll of its voters. Every council elects its own Chairman and Vice-Chairman.

### Who elects the Bar Council of India?

The Bar Council of India is elected indirectly. Section 4 builds it from the Attorney-General of India and the Solicitor-General of India, who sit ex officio, and one member elected by each State Bar Council from amongst its own members, each such member carrying the ten-year qualification of the proviso to Section 3(2).

The national council's membership turns over on a rhythm set by Section 4(3). A member of the Bar Council of India who sits on his State Bar Council ex officio holds the national seat for two years from election or until he ceases to be a member of the State council, whichever is earlier; any other elected member holds it for the period of his State council membership, continuing in office until his successor is elected. The Bar Council of India therefore renews itself continuously as State councils change, rather than at a single general election, a design that keeps the apex body close to the State tier's politics for good and ill: close enough to command the State councils' compliance, and close enough to import their factions into national decisions.

The two-tier design is the Act's federal bargain. The State Bar Council is elected directly by the advocates on its roll, and the national council is a council of councils, accountable to the profession through the State tier rather than to any government. Section 5 makes every Bar Council a body corporate with perpetual succession and a common seal, able to hold property, to contract and to sue and be sued in its own name. The term of an elected member of a State Bar Council under Section 8 is five years from the publication of the election result, and where a council fails to hold its election before the term expires, the Bar Council of India may extend the term by order, for reasons recorded in writing, by a period not exceeding six months. If the term and the extension both run out without an election, Section 8A requires the Bar Council of India to constitute a Special Committee, chaired by the ex officio member with two members nominated by the Bar Council of India from the State electoral roll, which discharges the council's functions, takes over its property and pending proceedings, and must hold elections within six months unless the Bar Council of India extends that period for recorded reasons. An elected member who absents himself from three consecutive meetings without sufficient excuse, whose name is removed from the roll, or who is disqualified under the Bar Council of India's rules is deemed by Section 10B to have vacated office.

Elections are the legitimacy of the whole structure, and the Act guards them at three points. The franchise itself is conditional: under Section 3(4) an advocate's right to vote, and to stand, depends on qualifications and conditions the Bar Council of India prescribes, and the State Bar Council prepares and revises the electoral roll on that basis, so that the roll of advocates under Section 17 and the roll of voters are related but distinct documents. The election machinery is regulated by Section 15(2)(a), under which council rules provide for election by secret ballot, including the conditions for postal ballots, the preparation and revision of electoral rolls and the publication of results. And the validity of the outcome is protected by Section 14, which bars a challenge to an election on the sole ground that due notice was not given to a person entitled to vote, where notice of the date was published in the Official Gazette at least thirty days beforehand, while Section 13 provides that no act of a council or committee is invalid merely for a vacancy or a defect in its constitution. The verification regime described later in this article feeds directly into this machinery, because a name carried on the list of non-practising advocates is a name kept off the electoral roll, and the cleanliness of the Section 17 roll therefore decides the integrity of the councils' own elections.

The councils work through committees, and the committee map in Sections 9 and 10 shows where the real business sits. Every Bar Council constitutes one or more disciplinary committees under Section 9, each of three persons: two elected by the council from among its members and one co-opted by the council from among advocates who hold the ten-year qualification and are not members of the council, with the senior-most advocate on the committee as its Chairman. Section 9A, inserted in 1973, permits legal aid committees of five to nine members. Under Section 10, a State Bar Council maintains an executive committee of five elected members and an enrolment committee of three elected members, while the Bar Council of India maintains an executive committee of nine and a legal education committee of ten, five elected from its members and five co-opted from outside them. Section 11 provides for staff headed by a secretary, Section 12 for accounts audited by auditors qualified under companies law, with a State Bar Council sending its audited accounts to the Bar Council of India and the Bar Council of India sending its accounts to the Central Government, each to be published in the Gazette. Section 15 gives both councils rule-making power for the purposes of Chapter II, covering elections by secret ballot, electoral rolls, funds, meetings and staff, and no rule of a State Bar Council under that section has effect unless the Bar Council of India has approved it. Approval of the State tier's rules by the national tier is a pattern that recurs in Sections 28 and elsewhere, and it is one of the quiet mechanisms by which the apex body governs the system.

The housekeeping provisions carry a point about the councils' character that the functions lists alone would miss. A council must meet, under Section 10A, at New Delhi in the case of the Bar Council of India and at its headquarters in the case of a State Bar Council, unless it records reasons in writing for another place; its committees other than the disciplinary committees meet at the council's headquarters; and every council and committee must observe the prescribed rules of procedure for its meetings. The secretary appointed under Section 11 is the council's permanent officer, holding the records, the roll correspondence and the accounts between elections. The picture is of a small permanent administration under a rotating elected government, which is why the Act attends so carefully to vacancies, casual and otherwise, to deemed vacation of office, and to the validity of acts done despite defects in constitution: the institution must keep deciding enrolments and complaints while its membership turns over.

## What the councils do: the functions in Sections 6 and 7

Section 6 states the functions of a State Bar Council, and the list defines the State tier as the working regulator. It admits persons as advocates on its roll and prepares and maintains the roll. It entertains and determines cases of misconduct against advocates on its roll. It safeguards the rights, privileges and interests of those advocates, promotes the growth of Bar Associations for the effective working of welfare schemes, promotes and supports law reform, conducts seminars and publishes journals and papers of legal interest, manages and invests its funds, and provides for the election of its members. Under Section 6(2) it may build funds for welfare schemes for indigent, disabled or other advocates, for legal aid and advice, and for establishing law libraries. Legal aid appears in the Act both as a function and as a funded purpose: Section 6(1)(eee) charges a State Bar Council with organising legal aid to the poor in the prescribed manner, and Section 9A gives it legal aid committees to do the work through, although the organised legal services system of the country rests on the separate statute examined in the series article on the [Legal Services Authorities Act 1987](/2021/06/15/legal-services-authorities-act-1987/), which built authorities at the national, State and district levels for the same object.

Section 7 states the functions of the Bar Council of India, and the list is the charter of the apex regulator. It lays down standards of professional conduct and etiquette for advocates. It lays down the procedure to be followed by its own disciplinary committee and by the disciplinary committee of each State Bar Council. It safeguards the rights, privileges and interests of advocates, promotes and supports law reform, deals with any matter referred to it by a State Bar Council, and exercises general supervision and control over State Bar Councils. It promotes legal education and lays down its standards, recognises universities whose law degrees qualify for enrolment, and visits and inspects them, a function examined in the next section. It organises legal aid to the poor in the prescribed manner, recognises foreign qualifications in law on a reciprocal basis for admission as an advocate, manages its funds, provides for the election of its members, and may become a member of international bodies such as the International Bar Association under Section 7A, inserted in 1973. Like the State councils, it may build welfare, legal aid and law library funds under Section 7(2).

Two supervisory provisions give Section 7(1)(g) its teeth. Section 48A empowers the Bar Council of India to call for the record of any proceeding disposed of by a State Bar Council or its committee from which no appeal lies, satisfy itself about the legality or propriety of the disposal, and pass such orders as it thinks fit, after hearing any person the order would prejudice. Section 48B empowers it, in the exercise of general supervision and control, to give directions to a State Bar Council or its committee for the proper and efficient discharge of functions, and the direction binds. Section 48AA adds a sixty-day review power for the Bar Council of India and its committees other than its disciplinary committee. Section 48 indemnifies councils, committees and members against suits for acts done in good faith under the Act. Read together, the provisions arrange the councils in a hierarchy of function rather than of government: the State council admits, keeps the roll and disciplines in the first instance, and the national council sets standards, hears appeals and references, supervises, and answers for the uniformity of the whole system.

One design consequence deserves emphasis, because it shapes every controversy in this field. The Bar Council of India is at once the system's chief rule-maker, through Section 49, and its chief appellate authority, through Sections 36, 37 and 48A. The body that writes the conduct code also sits in appeal over punishments imposed for breaking it, and the body that prescribes the conditions of practice also decides, on reference, whether an applicant refused enrolment should have been admitted. The fusion is deliberate: the Act prefers professional judgment at both levels to a separation of powers, and the corrective the design relies on is external, the appeal to the Supreme Court under Section 38 and the constitutional courts' power of review, rather than internal. When critics of self-regulation speak of the councils judging their own cause, this fusion is the precise institutional feature they mean.

## The councils and the courts

Self-regulation under the Act operates inside a court system that kept defined powers for itself, and the boundary provisions repay attention because they decide hard cases. Section 52 saves the power of the Supreme Court to make rules under Article 145 of the Constitution for laying down the conditions on which a senior advocate may practise in that Court and for determining the persons who shall be entitled to act or plead there. The saving preserves the Supreme Court's own gate, the advocates-on-record system, under which the right to act for a party in that Court is separately regulated, and it means that enrolment under the Act is necessary for practice in the Supreme Court but is not the whole of the entitlement there. Section 34 performs the parallel function for the High Courts, whose rules lay down the conditions on which an advocate practises in the High Court and the subordinate courts and govern the taxation of advocates' fees as costs between parties. During the transition, Section 58(1) even made the High Court the enrolment authority of last resort, performing a State Bar Council's admission functions where the council had not been constituted or could not act. The discipline boundary drawn in Supreme Court Bar Association, discussed in the conduct section below, completes the arrangement: the courts control their courtrooms, their records and their contempt jurisdiction, and the councils control the roll.

The two systems meet in the person of the law officers. The Attorney-General and the Solicitor-General sit ex officio on the Bar Council of India, the Advocates-General sit ex officio on the State Bar Councils, and Section 23's pre-audience order, taken in the right to practise section below, ranks those officers at the head of the bar inside the courtroom. The design is deliberate. The State's own senior lawyers are placed inside the profession's government rather than outside it, which both disciplines the councils with the presence of the court's senior officers and accustoms the officers to the profession's self-government. The Advocate-General also appears inside the disciplinary process, receiving notice of every Section 35 hearing and entitled to be heard, so that the public interest in a complaint is represented by someone other than the complainant and the council.

The transition itself was run by the courts before it was run by the councils. Section 58(3) provided that every person who, immediately before 1 December 1961, was an advocate on the roll of a High Court under the 1926 Act, or who had been enrolled under this Act, would, until Chapter IV came into force, be entitled as of right to practise in the Supreme Court, subject to the Supreme Court's rules. Section 58(2) allowed enrolment on the State roll during the interval even where State rules under Section 28 had not been made or approved. The High Courts thus carried the profession across the gap between the old enrolment and the new, and the councils inherited a going concern rather than a blank register, a handover whose smoothness explains why Chapter VII, drafted as temporary, contains some of the Act's most carefully negotiated provisions.

## Legal education under the Advocates Act 1961

The Act makes the Bar Council of India the standards authority for the degree that feeds the profession. Section 7(1)(h) charges it with promoting legal education and laying down standards of such education in consultation with the universities in India imparting it and with the State Bar Councils. Section 7(1)(i) charges it with recognising universities whose degree in law shall be a qualification for enrolment as an advocate, and for that purpose with visiting and inspecting universities, or causing State Bar Councils to visit and inspect them under its directions, a power the State councils carry in Section 6(1)(gg). Section 49(1)(d) supports the function with rule-making power over the standards of legal education to be observed by universities and their inspection, and Section 49(1)(af) adds power over the minimum qualifications required for admission to a course of degree in law in a recognised university. The legal education committee constituted under Section 10(2)(b), half elected and half co-opted, is the standing instrument of this jurisdiction, and the Bar Council of India has exercised the power through its Rules of Legal Education, which prescribe the structure of the three-year and five-year degree courses, the subjects to be taught, and the conditions institutions must meet to keep recognition. The composition of that committee is itself a small constitutional statement: it is the one standing committee of either council in which the Act reserves half the seats to persons who are not members of the council at all, an acknowledgement written into the statute that the education of lawyers is a subject on which the profession's elected representatives should sit with outsiders, teachers and others the council co-opts, rather than deliberate alone.

The arrangement gives the Indian law degree two regulators with different interests. A university answers for its academic standards to the university system supervised under the [University Grants Commission Act 1956](/2015/02/15/ugc-act-1956-explained/), and it answers for the professional sufficiency of its law degree to a council of practitioners, because the degree is not merely an academic award: under Section 24 it is the principal key to enrolment. The dual control explains recurring friction in this field, over inspection of colleges, over the affiliation of new institutions, and over the pace at which law colleges have multiplied, and it explains why the quality of legal education is debated as a question of professional regulation rather than of higher education policy alone. Students meet the system in its material form, in lecture notes, case compilations and slide decks passed from teacher to student, often opened on whatever device is at hand; for files of that kind a [browser-based presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) opens the deck without installed office software, which is the level at which most entrants first handle the course content the council prescribes. The standards question returns in the assessment of self-regulation below, because the body that benefits from a large profession also controls the gates of the degree that feeds it.

Recognition carries a practical consequence that students bear directly. Section 24(1)(c) makes the degree a qualification only where the university is recognised for the purposes of the Act by the Bar Council of India, so a degree from an institution whose recognition has been refused or withdrawn does not open the enrolment gate, however valid the degree may be as an academic award. Inspection under Section 7(1)(i) is therefore not an accreditation ritual; it is the mechanism by which the council decides whose graduates may enter the profession at all, and the lists of recognised institutions the council maintains are, for an applicant choosing a college, the operative document in Indian legal education. The same recognition logic governs foreign degrees under Section 24(1)(c)(iv) and Section 49(1)(e): the council decides which foreign qualifications count, subject in that case to the Central Government's approval of its rules, and an Indian citizen holding an unrecognised foreign degree holds, for enrolment purposes, no degree at all.

## Enrolment: the State roll and Section 24 eligibility

### What is the difference between enrolment and the right to practise?

Enrolment is the entry of a name on a State roll under Chapter III, and it makes the person an advocate. The right to practise is the separate entitlement conferred by Chapter IV, held subject to the Act and to conditions the Bar Council of India's rules impose, such as the bar examination and the certificate of practice.

A name can therefore stand on a roll while the entitlement to appear is suspended, unverified or not yet earned, and much of the litigation under this Act lives in the space between the two concepts.

Chapter III opens by dividing the profession it has just unified. Section 16 maintains two classes, senior advocates and other advocates: a senior advocate is designated, with his consent, by the Supreme Court or a High Court in recognition of ability, standing at the Bar or special knowledge or experience in law, and senior advocates practise subject to restrictions the Bar Council of India prescribes under Section 16(3) and Section 49(1)(g).

Designation as a senior advocate is an honour with working consequences. The designation is conferred by the Supreme Court or a High Court, on the court's own assessment of the advocate's ability, standing or special knowledge or experience, and the Supreme Court in Indira Jaising v. Supreme Court of India (2017) laid down guidelines to make the process structured and open, including assessment by a permanent committee and a point-based consideration of years of practice, reported judgments, publications and the interview. The roll reflects the distinction physically: Section 17(2) keeps senior advocates in the first part of the State roll and all other advocates in the second. The restrictions that attend the rank, prescribed in the Bar Council of India Rules, fence the senior off from the solicitor's side of the work: a senior advocate shall not file a vakalatnama or act on behalf of a client, shall not appear without an advocate-on-record in the Supreme Court or without a junior advocate in other courts, shall not accept instructions to draw pleadings or affidavits or to undertake conveyancing work directly from a client, and shall not accept a brief directly, the instructions reaching him through the instructing advocate. The rank therefore reproduces, inside the unified profession, a moderated version of the old counsel and attorney division, kept as a distinction of function rather than of enrolment.

The State roll itself is created by Section 17. Every State Bar Council must prepare and maintain a roll of advocates carrying the names and addresses of two groups: persons who stood enrolled as advocates on the roll of a High Court under the Indian Bar Councils Act 1926 immediately before the appointed day and who express the intention to practise within the council's jurisdiction, and all persons admitted as advocates on the State roll under the Act after that day. The roll is kept in two parts, senior advocates first and other advocates second, with entries arranged by seniority under the rules Section 17(3) prescribes, and Section 17(4) states the unity rule: no person shall be enrolled as an advocate on the roll of more than one State Bar Council. Section 19 obliges each State Bar Council to send the Bar Council of India an authenticated copy of its roll and to communicate every alteration and addition, which is how the national picture of the profession is assembled. Section 22 requires a certificate of enrolment to be issued to every person entered on the roll, and requires the advocate to notify any change in the place of permanent residence to the State Bar Council within ninety days.

Admission turns on Section 24. Subject to the Act and the rules made under it, a person is qualified to be admitted as an advocate on a State roll if he fulfils the conditions of Section 24(1). He must be a citizen of India, with a proviso admitting a national of another country where citizens of India, duly qualified, are permitted to practise law in that country, the reciprocity principle that returns in Section 47. He must have completed the age of twenty-one years. He must hold a degree in law on one of the routes Section 24(1)(c) preserves: a degree from an Indian university before 12 March 1967, a pre-Partition degree from a university in the territory then comprised in India, a degree after undergoing the three-year course of study in law from an Indian university recognised for the purposes of the Act by the Bar Council of India, the transitional two-year course that began in the academic year 1967-68 or earlier, a degree from a university outside India recognised by the Bar Council of India, or standing as a barrister called to the Bar on or before 31 December 1976, an articled clerk who has passed the examinations the High Courts at Bombay and Calcutta specified, or the holder of another foreign qualification the council recognises. He must fulfil the other conditions the State Bar Council's rules under Chapter III specify, under Section 24(1)(e). And he must pay, under Section 24(1)(f), the stamp duty chargeable under the Indian Stamp Act 1899, if any, and the enrolment fee the clause fixes, a matter important enough to take separately in the next section. Sub-sections (2) and (3) of Section 24 are transitional doors for the older classes: a vakil or pleader who is a law graduate could apply within two years of the appointed day, and persons who had been vakils, pleaders or mukhtars for at least three years, pre-1937 advocates of High Courts in Burma, and persons entitled under the Bar Council of India's rules could be admitted on satisfying the citizenship, age, conditions and fee requirements.

Section 24A, inserted by the Advocates (Amendment) Act 1973 with effect from 31 January 1974, bars the gate against specified offenders. No person shall be admitted as an advocate on a State roll if he is convicted of an offence involving moral turpitude, if he is convicted of an offence under the Untouchability (Offences) Act 1955, Act 22 of 1955, or if he is dismissed or removed from employment or office under the State on a charge involving moral turpitude, the third ground added by the Advocates (Amendment) Act 1993. The bar is not permanent: the proviso states that the disqualification ceases to have effect after two years have elapsed since release, dismissal or removal, and Section 24A(2) excepts a person dealt with under the Probation of Offenders Act 1958, Act 20 of 1958. The Supreme Court applied the section in Mahipal Singh Rana v. State of Uttar Pradesh, AIR 2016 SC 3302, holding that the enrolment of an advocate convicted of contempt would stand suspended for two years under Section 24A, in a judgment that also set in train the Law Commission review discussed in the assessment below.

The machinery of admission sits around the eligibility rules. An application is made under Section 25 to the State Bar Council within whose jurisdiction the applicant proposes to practise. Under Section 26 the council refers every application to its enrolment committee, which disposes of it in the prescribed manner; if the committee proposes to refuse, it must refer the application for the opinion of the Bar Council of India and dispose of it in conformity with that opinion, and the refusal is then intimated to all other State Bar Councils with the applicant's name, address, qualifications and grounds. A proviso to Section 26 lets the Bar Council of India remove from the roll, after giving an opportunity of being heard, a name entered by misrepresentation as to an essential fact or by fraud or undue influence. Section 27 stops forum-shopping after refusal: no other State Bar Council may entertain the refused person's application except with the previous written consent of the refusing council and of the Bar Council of India. Section 28 gives a State Bar Council rule-making power for the chapter, including the conditions of admission and the instalments in which the enrolment fee may be paid, with the usual condition that its rules need the Bar Council of India's approval. Section 18 completes the picture of a single national profession divided into State rolls: an advocate may apply to the Bar Council of India for transfer of his name from one State roll to another, the transfer is made without fee and seniority travels with the name, and the council may reject the application after hearing the applicant where a disciplinary proceeding is pending or where the application appears not to have been made bona fide. Section 20 made special provision for advocates entitled to practise in the Supreme Court before the appointed day, whose names were entered on a State roll of their choice, or on the Delhi roll if they expressed no choice, and Section 21 settles seniority disputes, by age where dates tie and otherwise by reference to the State Bar Council. Section 26A, in the form substituted in 1973, lets a State Bar Council remove from the State roll the name of any advocate who is dead or who has asked for the removal, the routine exit that keeps the roll honest between elections.

The conditions power in Section 24(1)(e) has its own judicial boundary, drawn in Indian Council of Legal Aid and Advice v. Bar Council of India (1995). The Bar Council of India had made a rule barring the enrolment of a person who had completed forty-five years of age and had been in the service of the government or another employer, in substance reserving late entry to those who had never worked elsewhere. The Supreme Court struck the rule down: Section 49(1)(ag), the power to prescribe the class or category of persons entitled to be enrolled, does not authorise the exclusion of a class defined by age in that manner, and the rule was discriminatory and unreasonable in singling out one group of late entrants while leaving others free to enrol or resume practice past the same age. The case stands with Gaurav Kumar and V. Sudeer in the line of decisions that mark where the councils' conditions power ends: conditions must trace to a clause of Section 49, must operate on the qualifications the Act itself makes relevant, and must survive Articles 14 and 19(1)(g) like any other professional gate.

## The fee written into Section 24(1)(f)

Of all the conditions in Section 24, the fee is the one the profession did not set. Section 24(1)(f) provides that the applicant must have paid, in respect of the enrolment, the stamp duty, if any, chargeable under the Indian Stamp Act 1899, and an enrolment fee payable to the State Bar Council of six hundred rupees and to the Bar Council of India of one hundred and fifty rupees, by bank draft drawn in favour of the council. A member of the Scheduled Castes or the Scheduled Tribes producing the prescribed certificate pays one hundred rupees to the State Bar Council and twenty-five rupees to the Bar Council of India. The total is therefore Rs 750 for a general applicant and Rs 125 for a Scheduled Caste or Scheduled Tribe applicant, before stamp duty. The figures have a legislative history. Clause (f) was substituted by the Advocates (Amendment) Act 1973, and the Advocates (Amendment) Act 1993, Act 70 of 1993, with effect from 26 December 1993, raised the figures from two hundred and fifty rupees and one hundred and twenty-five rupees to the present six hundred and one hundred and fifty. Section 46 of the Act, which had dealt with the payment of part of the enrolment fee to the Bar Council of India, was omitted by the same 1993 amendment, and the present scheme leaves each council's share stated in the clause itself. The clause has an Explanation deeming the degree to have been obtained on the date the university publishes the examination results declaring the applicant passed, which settles, in the statute's favour, the common dispute about when a fresh graduate becomes eligible to apply.

For years the statutory figure did not describe what new entrants actually paid. State Bar Councils levied sums far above the statutory amount under heads such as identity cards, welfare contributions, building funds and training and verification charges, sometimes reaching many times the figure the Act states, and made payment a condition of enrolment. The Supreme Court settled the matter in Gaurav Kumar v. Union of India, W.P.(C) 352 of 2023, decided on 30 July 2024 by a bench of Chief Justice D. Y. Chandrachud and Justices J. B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra. The Court held that neither a State Bar Council nor the Bar Council of India may charge an enrolment fee beyond the amount Section 24(1)(f) specifies. Stamp duty, if the law imposes it, stands apart from the fee and may be recovered. Miscellaneous charges, including charges in the name of identity cards, welfare funds or training, may not be made a pre-condition of enrolment. The practice of charging more than the statute permits, the Court held, violates Article 14's guarantee of equality and Article 19(1)(g)'s guarantee of the freedom to practise a profession, and the ruling was given prospective effect, so that fees already collected were not required to be refunded.

The councils were not left without income by the ruling, and the Act's own finance provisions show the intended channels. A State Bar Council builds its funds under Section 6(2) for welfare schemes, legal aid and law libraries, and the Bar Council of India does the same under Section 7(2); both may receive grants, donations, gifts and benefactions for those purposes. Section 46A, inserted in 1973, empowers the Bar Council of India to give financial assistance by grant or otherwise to a State Bar Council satisfied to be in need of funds for performing its functions. Section 28(2)(e) lets a State Bar Council's rules provide for the enrolment fee to be paid in instalments, which softens the statutory figure for the entrant without altering it. What Gaurav Kumar forecloses is only the conversion of those funding needs into compulsory charges on the one person who cannot refuse, the applicant at the gate; a charge for a service genuinely optional, an identity card an advocate chooses to buy or a welfare scheme an advocate chooses to join, stands on a different footing from a precondition of enrolment, and it is the precondition that the judgment strikes at.

The judgment is the working demonstration of the article's claim. Standards of conduct, standards of education, the procedure of discipline and the conditions of admission are all entrusted to councils the profession elects, and within those domains the profession's word is the last word subject to appeal and constitutional review. The fee is the exception, a figure Parliament wrote into the section, and on that one point a new graduate has a right against the councils enforceable in court. Self-regulation ends where the statute's arithmetic begins.

## The training that Parliament removed and the examination that followed

Clause (d) of Section 24(1) is absent from the current text, and its absence is a deliberate decision of Parliament rather than a drafting slip. As enacted in 1961, the clause required the applicant to have undergone a course of training in law prescribed by the Bar Council of India and to have passed an examination after such training. The Advocates (Amendment) Act 1973 omitted the clause with effect from 31 January 1974, ending the statutory demand for a period of practical training before enrolment. The omission was the legal foundation for the Supreme Court's decision in V. Sudeer v. Bar Council of India, (1999) 3 SCC 176, which struck down the Bar Council of India's Training Rules that had sought to reintroduce a year of practical training as a condition of enrolment: what Parliament had removed by amendment, the council could not restore by rule.

The Bar Council of India tried a different route, and this time it stayed within the powers the Act gives it. In 2010 it introduced the All India Bar Examination, not as a requirement to be met before enrolment, but as a condition of the right to practise to be met after enrolment, framed under its rule-making power. The examination's validity was contested, and the question reached a Constitution Bench of five judges in Bar Council of India v. Bonnie Foi Law College, 2023 INSC 116, decided on 10 February 2023 by Justices Sanjay Kishan Kaul, Sanjiv Khanna, Abhay S. Oka, Vikram Nath and J. K. Maheshwari. The Court held that the Bar Council of India has adequate power under the Act to prescribe the examination. The power was located in the rule-making authority read with the enrolment and practice scheme, principally in Section 49(1)(ah), which empowers rules on the conditions subject to which an advocate shall have the right to practise and the circumstances in which a person shall be deemed to practise as an advocate in a court, read with the enrolment provisions of Section 24. Whether the examination is held before or after enrolment is a matter for the Bar Council of India to decide. The interdict in V. Sudeer against pre-enrolment training was not to be treated as sustained in the changed statutory and institutional setting, though the Court added that its decision was not an imprimatur for a pre-enrolment training scheme. Final-semester students may appear for the examination subject to passing the degree course, and the judgment was made prospective, disturbing nothing done before it.

The operating scheme the judgment validated runs as follows. Enrolment comes first: the graduate enrols on a State roll under Section 24 and practises on the strength of the enrolment for a limited window while the examination is cleared. The Bar Council of India's framework applies the examination to advocates who graduated in the academic year 2009-10 onwards, and the Allahabad High Court, reading that framework with the 2015 verification rules, has stated the consequence in terms: an advocate of that cohort who does not qualify in the examination within two years of provisional enrolment is not entitled to practise in the civil, criminal or revenue courts, and a presiding officer may refuse to hear such an advocate or to honour the vakalatnama. On passing, the advocate receives the certificate of practice that the verification regime then keeps current. The sequence matters for reading Section 30: the statutory entitlement to practise as of right is real, but for the post-2010 entrant it is an entitlement held on a condition subsequent, earned in two stages, the roll first and the examination after.

Administration follows the same federal pattern as the rest of the Act. The examination is the Bar Council of India's, set under its rules and held at centres across the country in the subjects of the law degree and of practice; the enrolment that precedes it and the certificate of practice that follows it are the State Bar Council's, issued on the roll the State council keeps. An entrant therefore deals with two councils in sequence for a single entitlement, the State council for admission and the national council for the test of readiness to practise, and a failure at the second stage leaves the first intact: the name stays on the roll, the advocate remains an advocate, and what is withheld until the examination is cleared is the appearance in court. That separation, between status and licence to appear, is the doctrinal hinge on which Bonnie Foi turned, since a condition on appearing is a condition the practice power in Section 49(1)(ah) can carry, where a condition on the status itself would have to be found in Section 24.

The precision of the holding matters more than its result. The examination is a creation of the Bar Council of India's rules under Section 49, not a provision of the Act's text, and that is why the common statement that the Advocates Act requires an entrance examination misreads the law. The Act requires citizenship, age, a degree and a fee; the examination is the profession's own addition, authorised by the Act but not written in it, and its survival depended on the Court finding that addition inside the rule-making power Parliament granted. Where Parliament withheld power, as in the fee, the Court policed the boundary; where Parliament granted it, as in Section 49(1)(ah), the Court deferred to the profession's judgment on the shape of the gate.

## The right to practise: Chapter IV

Chapter IV states the bargain of the single class. Section 29 provides that, subject to the Act and the rules made under it, there shall, as from the appointed day, be only one class of persons entitled to practise the profession of law, namely, advocates. Section 30 gives that class its content: subject to the Act, every advocate whose name is entered in the State roll is entitled as of right to practise throughout the territories to which the Act extends, in all courts including the Supreme Court, before any tribunal or person legally authorised to take evidence, and before any other authority or person before whom the advocate is by or under any law for the time being in force entitled to practise. Section 30 is the clause that makes the roll national in effect: enrolment in one State opens every court in the country.

### What is the right of pre-audience?

Section 23 fixes the order in which advocates are heard when several rise, the right of pre-audience. The Attorney-General of India stands first, followed by the Solicitor-General of India, the Additional Solicitor-General and the second Additional Solicitor-General. The Advocates-General of the States follow, ranked by seniority; then senior advocates, and then other advocates, seniority deciding order within each class.

The chapter's exclusivity is stated twice, from two directions. Section 33 provides that, except as otherwise provided in the Act or in any other law for the time being in force, no person shall, on or after the appointed day, be entitled to practise in any court or before any authority or person unless he is enrolled as an advocate under the Act. Section 45, in Chapter VI, punishes the other side of the line: a person who practises in a court or before an authority or person before whom he is not entitled to practise under the Act is punishable with imprisonment for a term which may extend to six months. The exclusivity is not absolute. Section 32 permits any court, authority or person to permit a person not enrolled as an advocate to appear before it in any particular case, the narrow door through which a non-advocate may address a court with leave. Section 34 gives the High Court rule-making power to lay down the conditions subject to which an advocate may practise in the High Court and the courts subordinate to it, and to fix and regulate, by taxation or otherwise, the fees payable as costs by a party in respect of his adversary's advocate. Section 31, which had carried a special provision for attorneys, was omitted by the 1976 amendment. Section 30's delayed commencement, recorded above, means that the chapter's central promise became operative on 15 June 2011; until that notification, the entitlement the section states in such absolute terms did not exist in law.

Section 30 also carries the scar of the Act's largest design change. As enacted in 1961, the section spoke of the advocate whose name is entered in the common roll, because the original scheme contemplated a single common roll of advocates for the whole of India, maintained at the national level, alongside the State rolls. The Advocates (Amendment) Act 1973 substituted the words "State roll" for "common roll" in the section, and omitted the common roll references from Section 36 as well, abandoning the single national register in favour of the State rolls as the only rolls the Act keeps. The unity of the profession after 1973 therefore rests not on a common register but on the portability Section 30 confers and the transfer mechanism of Section 18: the advocate belongs to one State roll, and the right travels even though the name does not. Section 19's duty, that every State Bar Council send the Bar Council of India an authenticated copy of its roll and communicate every alteration, is the residue of the common roll idea, an information union in place of a documentary one.

The third limb of Section 30 deserves a reader's care, because it is where the Act's exclusivity meets the rest of the statute book. An advocate is entitled as of right before any other authority or person before whom he is by or under any law for the time being in force entitled to practise, which means the entitlement before a tribunal created by a special statute depends on that statute. Several fiscal and regulatory statutes admit authorised representatives who are not advocates: the Income-tax Act 1961, in Section 288, permits an assessee to be represented by an authorised representative, a class that includes legal practitioners but also accountants and other prescribed persons. Section 33's monopoly is therefore a monopoly over practice in courts and before authorities where no other law provides otherwise, and the advocate's nationwide right, sweeping as its first two limbs are, is completed rather than contradicted by the special statutes that share particular forums with neighbouring professions.

## Conduct and discipline: Chapter V

Chapter V is the chapter the profession judges itself by, and it opens with the words that define the disciplinary standard: professional or other misconduct. The Act never defines the expression, and the courts have refused to fence it. In Noratanmal Chouraria v. M. R. Murli, (2004) 5 SCC 689, the Supreme Court held that misconduct envisages breach of discipline, that it would not be possible to lay down exhaustively what constitutes misconduct and indiscipline, and that the expression is wide enough to include wrongful omission or commission, whether intentional or unintentional. The facts themselves made the second point of the jurisprudence: the conduct complained of was an assault by an advocate who was not acting in his professional capacity, and the Court held that an advocate remains answerable to the disciplinary jurisdiction even for conduct outside practice, because the test is whether the advocate has conducted himself in a manner that makes him unworthy of the confidence of the community in him as an officer of the court. Misconduct under this Act is therefore not a list of offences; it is a standard of fitness measured against the expectations of the profession and the court, given content by the standards of conduct in the Bar Council of India Rules described below.

The process begins under Section 35. Where, on receipt of a complaint or otherwise, a State Bar Council has reason to believe that any advocate on its roll has been guilty of professional or other misconduct, it shall refer the case for disposal to its disciplinary committee; the words "or otherwise" preserve a power to act without a complainant, on the council's own information. Section 35(1A), inserted in 1973, allows the State Bar Council, of its own motion or on application by any person interested, to withdraw a proceeding from one of its disciplinary committees and direct the inquiry before another of them. The committee fixes a hearing date, gives notice to the advocate and to the Advocate-General of the State, and hears both; the Advocate-General may appear in person or through an advocate. After hearing, the committee may dismiss the complaint or direct that proceedings initiated at the council's instance be filed, reprimand the advocate, suspend the advocate from practice for such period as it deems fit, or remove the name of the advocate from the State roll. A suspended advocate is debarred by Section 35(4) from practising in any court or before any authority or person in India during the suspension. This account is legal information about the scheme of the Act; a person with an individual grievance needs advice on the facts of the grievance, and the first step in the scheme is a complaint to the State Bar Council on whose roll the advocate's name stands.

The committee that conducts this inquiry is not an informal body. Section 42 gives a disciplinary committee the same powers as a civil court under the Code of Civil Procedure 1908 for summoning and enforcing the attendance of witnesses and examining them on oath, requiring discovery and production of documents, receiving evidence on affidavits, requisitioning public records, and issuing commissions for examining witnesses or documents; summoning the presiding officer of a court requires the previous sanction of the High Court, and of a revenue court officer, the State Government. Proceedings before the committee are judicial proceedings for the purposes of the provisions of the penal law on false evidence and insult to a public servant, and the committee may pass orders on costs under Section 43, executable as an order of the High Court or, for the Bar Council of India's committee, of the Supreme Court. Section 42 also solves the committee's internal failure modes: the absence of the Chairman or a member on a hearing date does not invalidate the proceedings, though no final order of the Section 35(3) kind may be made unless the Chairman and members are present; and where the committee cannot produce a majority view, the case, with the competing opinions, goes to the Chairman of the Bar Council, or to the Vice-Chairman where the Chairman sat on the committee, whose opinion the final order follows. Section 42A extends the Section 42 powers, so far as may be, to the Bar Council of India and to enrolment, election, legal aid and other committees, so that the coercive apparatus of summons and document production is available across the councils' committee system and not in discipline alone.

Above the State committee stands the Bar Council of India, and Section 36 gives it two distinct powers. Its disciplinary committee takes original cases of advocates whose names are not entered on any State roll. More consequential is Section 36(2): the committee may, of its own motion, or on a report by any State Bar Council, or on application by any person interested, withdraw for inquiry before itself any disciplinary proceeding pending before a State disciplinary committee and dispose of it, following the procedure of Section 35 with references to the Advocate-General read as references to the Attorney-General of India, and may pass any order a State disciplinary committee could pass. Section 36A keeps the machinery continuous when a committee's constitution changes, letting a successor committee continue from the stage its predecessor reached. Section 36B then imposes the system's one hard deadline. A State disciplinary committee must dispose of each complaint expeditiously, and the proceedings must be concluded within one year from the date of receipt of the complaint or from the date the State Bar Council initiated them, failing which the proceedings stand transferred to the Bar Council of India, which disposes of them as if withdrawn under Section 36(2). The provision was inserted by the Advocates (Amendment) Act 1973; Parliament, within a dozen years of the Act, found it necessary to legislate against disciplinary delay, and the transfer is automatic on the deadline passing, not dependent on any party applying for it.

Appeals run in two stages, and the time limits are short. Section 37 gives any person aggrieved by an order of a State disciplinary committee under Section 35, and the Advocate-General of the State, a right of appeal to the Bar Council of India within sixty days of the date the order was communicated. The appeal is heard by the Bar Council of India's disciplinary committee, which may pass such order as it deems fit, including an order varying the punishment, with the protection, added in 1973, that no order may be varied so as to prejudice the appellant without giving him a reasonable opportunity of being heard. Section 38 takes the chain one step higher: any person aggrieved by an order of the Bar Council of India's disciplinary committee under Section 36 or Section 37, and the Attorney-General of India or the Advocate-General of the State, may appeal to the Supreme Court within sixty days of communication, and the Court may pass such order, including an order varying the punishment, as it deems fit, under the same hearing protection. Section 39 applies Sections 5 and 12 of the Limitation Act 1963 to these appeals, so that delay may be condoned for sufficient cause and the time taken in obtaining the order excluded. An appeal does not by itself stay the order: Section 40 provides that the order operates unless the appellate body, for sufficient cause, directs a stay on terms, and the committee that passed the order may itself stay it where an application for stay is made before the appeal period expires. Section 41 makes the punishment visible on the record: a reprimand or suspension is entered against the advocate's name in the State roll, a removal strikes the name off the roll, and on suspension or removal the enrolment certificate issued under Section 22 is recalled. The four orders of Section 35(3) are the whole of the punitive repertoire, and their graduation is the chapter's theory of proportionality: a complaint may fail entirely, a fault may be marked by reprimand without touching the right to practise, suspension withdraws the right for a measured period across the whole of India, and removal ends the professional existence the roll records. The Act gives the committees no power to fine an advocate for misconduct; the only monetary order in the chapter is costs under Section 43, which compensates the proceeding rather than punishing the person. Appellate variation under Sections 37 and 38 is the mechanism by which the calibration is corrected, each appellate level holding the power to substitute its own assessment of the punishment, subject to hearing the person a worsening would prejudice. Section 44 closes the chapter with a review power: a disciplinary committee may, of its own motion or otherwise, review an order it has passed under the chapter within sixty days of its date, and a State committee's review order has effect only if the Bar Council of India approves it. The transfer of a pending case, it should be noted for accuracy, is the work of Section 36B and Section 36(2); Section 44 is the review power, and the two are frequently misdescribed as one another.

Discipline under the Act coexists with the courts' own powers, and the boundary between them is settled law. An advocate facing a client in court is also an officer of that court, and misconduct in the courtroom may draw the contempt jurisdiction examined in the series article on the [Contempt of Courts Act 1971](/2021/07/15/contempt-of-courts-act-1971-explained/). The Supreme Court drew the line in Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of India (1998): a Constitution Bench held that the power to punish an advocate for professional misconduct by suspending or removing the licence to practise is the jurisdiction the Advocates Act vests in the Bar Councils, and that a court punishing for contempt cannot itself impose that professional penalty. The result is a division of labour the Act intends: the court protects its proceedings, and the profession, through the councils, decides who may remain in it.

The standards the committees enforce are written in rules, not in the Act. Section 49(1)(c) empowers the Bar Council of India to prescribe the standards of professional conduct and etiquette to be observed by advocates, and rules made under that clause have no effect unless approved by the Chief Justice of India. Chapter II of Part VI of the Bar Council of India Rules, made under that power, is the profession's code: it states the advocate's duties to the court, to the client, to the opponent and to colleagues, and it carries the restrictions on other work. Rule 36, the advertising rule, provides that an advocate shall not solicit work or advertise, directly or indirectly, whether by circulars, advertisements, touts, personal communications, interviews not warranted by personal relations, or furnishing or inspiring newspaper comments or photographs in connection with cases in which he has been engaged; the sign-board or name-plate must be of reasonable size and must not indicate a past presidency or membership of a Bar Council or association, an association with any person, organisation, cause or matter, a specialisation, or past service as a Judge or Advocate-General. In 2008 the rule was amended to carve out one channel: an advocate may furnish website information in the form the Schedule to the rule prescribes, under intimation to and as approved by the Bar Council of India, covering name, address, telephone numbers and email, enrolment number and date of enrolment, the State Bar Councils of original enrolment and of the roll on which the name stands, the Bar Association of membership, professional and academic qualifications, and areas of practice, with a declaration that the information is true. Anything beyond the Schedule is a breach of Rule 36 and is punishable as misconduct under Section 35. The same Part VI code states the advocate's duty to render legal aid in Rule 46: every advocate shall bear in mind that anyone genuinely in need of a lawyer is entitled to legal assistance even if the person cannot pay fully or adequately, and that free legal assistance to the indigent and the oppressed, within the limits of the advocate's economic condition, is among the highest obligations the advocate owes to society, a professional duty that matches the councils' institutional legal aid functions in Sections 6 and 7. Rules 47 to 49 govern other engagements: an advocate shall not personally engage in any business, though he may be a sleeping partner in a business the State Bar Council considers consistent with the dignity of the profession; he may be a director or chairman of a company's board without executive duties but not a managing director or secretary; and under Rule 49 an advocate shall not be a full-time salaried employee of any person, government, firm, corporation or concern so long as he continues to practise, must intimate the fact to the Bar Council on whose roll his name appears on taking up such employment, and must thereupon cease to practise for as long as the employment lasts.

## How a complaint travels through the Act

The scheme of Chapter V is easier to hold as a journey than as a list of sections. A complaint begins as information: any person may lay the facts before the State Bar Council on whose roll the advocate stands, and the council may equally act on information from any other source, because Section 35 is triggered by reason to believe and not by the standing of the informant. The council refers the case to a disciplinary committee of three, composed under Section 9, and the committee fixes the hearing and notifies the advocate and the Advocate-General. The hearing is a judicial proceeding in substance: evidence may be taken on oath or on affidavit, documents may be compelled, commissions may issue, and the parties are heard. The committee's first decision is disposal within the discipline of Section 36B: conclude within a year of the complaint or of the council's own initiation, or lose the case by automatic transfer to the Bar Council of India. At any point the Bar Council of India may also reach down and withdraw the proceeding under Section 36(2), of its own motion, on the State council's report or on an interested person's application, and a State council may move the case between its own committees under Section 35(1A) where fairness or convenience requires it.

The order that ends the inquiry takes one of the four forms Section 35(3) lists, dismissal, reprimand, suspension or removal, and each has its record consequence under Section 41: the punishment is entered on the roll, removal strikes the name off, and suspension or removal recalls the enrolment certificate. The order is not self-staying: it operates while the sixty-day windows of Sections 37 and 38 run, unless a stay is granted under Section 40, first by the committee that passed the order if asked in time, and then by the appellate body. The appeal to the Bar Council of India reopens the merits, including the quantum of punishment, under the protection that a punishment cannot be worsened against an appellant unheard; the appeal to the Supreme Court under Section 38 does the same at the summit, and both appeals borrow the condonation and exclusion rules of the Limitation Act through Section 39. A committee that thinks better of its own order has sixty days to review it under Section 44, and a State committee's review needs the Bar Council of India's approval to take effect. Costs follow the event at the committee's discretion under Section 43, recoverable as a court order. The chain therefore has three decision levels, two appeal levels, one internal clock and one review, all inside a profession-administered system whose final word, by design, belongs to a court.

The complainant's own position in the chain is worth stating, because it is commonly misunderstood. The complainant sets the machine in motion and gives evidence, but the proceeding is the council's: the charge is framed by the council's reason to believe, the Advocate-General is heard as the public voice, and the punishment, if any, is imposed for the profession and the court rather than as compensation to the complainant, for whom the Act provides no damages remedy in the disciplinary forum. What the complainant holds is standing at the two exits: as a person aggrieved by an order under Section 35, the complainant may appeal to the Bar Council of India under Section 37 where the committee has dismissed the complaint or punished too lightly for the grievance alleged, and onward under Section 38. A client whose loss is money must pursue it in the civil courts; the disciplinary route protects the public against the advocate, and only incidentally vindicates the client.

## The certificate of practice and verification

Enrolment under this Act is the beginning of an advocate's record, not the end of it. The Bar Council of India has used the same practice-conditions power that supports the bar examination, Section 49(1)(ah), to build a continuing verification regime, first through the Certificate of Practice and Renewal Rules 2014 and in their place through the Bar Council of India Certificate and Place of Practice (Verification) Rules 2015, which took effect on 13 January 2015. The object stated for the regime is housekeeping of the roll: the councils' records had filled with advocates who had left practice for employment or business, had died, or in some cases had enrolled on credentials that did not bear scrutiny, and a roll carrying non-practising names distorts everything built on it, including the electoral rolls from which the councils themselves are elected.

The 2015 rules also corrected an overreach of their predecessor. The Certificate of Practice and Renewal Rules 2014 had required, in their Chapter III, a minimum of five years of practice in the lower courts before an advocate could practise in the Supreme Court, a condition that drew writ challenges, including a petition in the Madras High Court against the rule, and sharp criticism across the profession. The 2015 Verification Rules repealed the 2014 rules and dropped that condition, keeping the regime to its housekeeping object of identifying who is in practice and where. The episode belongs in the record of self-regulation's boundaries: a condition on the Section 30 right to practise in the Supreme Court, imposed by rule, was withdrawn by the rule-maker itself under pressure of litigation and professional opinion, without a court needing to strike it down.

The mechanics, at headline level, are these. Every advocate on a State roll must hold a certificate of practice, and must apply in the prescribed form to the State Bar Council for verification of the certificate with a declaration of the place of practice and proof of continued practice. The application carries a process fee; the rules leave a State Bar Council liberty to vary the verification and process fee to its own requirements, subject to the Bar Council of India's approval, so the charge differs between councils while the obligation does not. A certificate verified under the rules runs for five years, after which it must be verified again. An advocate who does not apply within the time allowed is placed on the list of defaulting advocates; an advocate who remains unverified after the further time the rules allow is published in the list of non-practising advocates, and the disability of the regime attaches at that point: the advocate is not entitled to practise, to appear, or to the privileges that ride on the roll, including the vote at council elections, until the name is restored through verification. Reading the rules in Yogendra v. State of Uttar Pradesh, the Allahabad High Court treated the disability as arising on publication of the non-practising list rather than on the mere expiry of five years from the certificate's issue, so that an advocate whose name has not been published may continue to practise while verification is pending. Verification is thus the roll's self-cleaning device, operated by the same councils that enrol, and like the examination it is a creature of rule under the Act rather than of the Act's own text.

## Foreign lawyers: Balaji, the 2022 Rules and the 2025 amendment

The Act itself contains the seed of the foreign lawyer question in its reciprocity provisions. The proviso to Section 24(1)(a) admits a foreign national to a State roll only where Indian citizens, duly qualified, are permitted to practise law in that person's country. Section 47(1) provides that where a country specified by the Central Government prevents citizens of India from practising law there or subjects them to unfair discrimination, no subject of that country shall be entitled to practise law in India. Section 47(2) and Section 49(1)(e) empower the Bar Council of India to prescribe the conditions on which foreign qualifications in law held by non-citizens shall be recognised for admission as an advocate, rules under that clause requiring the Central Government's approval, and Section 7(1)(ic) lists the recognition of foreign qualifications on a reciprocal basis among the council's functions. What the Act does not do is provide for the practice of foreign law in India by persons who are not enrolled at all, and it is in that space that the litigation happened.

The route ran through the High Courts first. In Lawyers Collective v. Bar Council of India, the Bombay High Court held in 2009 that the Reserve Bank's permission for foreign law firms to open liaison offices did not authorise the practice of law, and that practice includes non-litigious work. The Madras High Court, in the judgment of 21 December 2012 from which the Supreme Court appeal arose, likewise held that foreign lawyers and firms had no right to practise in India, while accepting that casual visits to advise on foreign law and participation in international arbitration seated in India stood on a different footing. In Bar Council of India v. A. K. Balaji, the Supreme Court decided the question on 13 March 2018, AIR 2018 SC 1382. The Court held that foreign law firms and foreign lawyers are not entitled to practise law in India, on the litigation side or the non-litigation side; that the expression practising the profession of law in Sections 29 and 33 covers advisory and transactional work and not court appearances alone, so that drafting, conveyancing, opinion work and chamber practice for Indian clients fall within the prohibition as much as pleading does, because the Act's single class admits no second channel of unregulated practice; that nothing bars a foreign lawyer from visiting India on a temporary fly-in fly-out basis to advise a client on foreign law or on the client's home law; and that foreign lawyers may participate in international commercial arbitration conducted in India, subject to the rules of the arbitral institution and the code of conduct, the space occupied in this series by the [Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996](/2021/05/01/arbitration-conciliation-act-1996-guide/) on the domestic side of the same work. The Court left the Bar Council of India free to frame rules for the permissible space.

The Bar Council of India used that space to build a registration regime: the Bar Council of India Rules for Registration and Regulation of Foreign Lawyers and Foreign Law Firms in India, 2022, notified on 10 March 2023. Registration with the council is mandatory, reciprocity with the home country is the entry condition, and the permissible field is advice on foreign law and international law in non-litigious matters and appearance in international commercial arbitration where foreign or international law is involved. Practice of Indian law, appearance before Indian courts and tribunals, and litigation work remain barred. By an amendment notified in the Gazette on 13 May 2025, the council adjusted the regime: fly-in fly-out advisory visits are capped at sixty days in a twelve-month period, and Indian advocates and firms may themselves register for foreign law practice, opening a two-way street inside the same registration system. The position should be stated exactly: foreign lawyers may be present in India, registered and regulated, practising foreign and international law; they may not practise Indian law, and the door the Supreme Court opened in Balaji is a door into arbitration and advice, not into the Indian courts.

The registration regime deserves one further precision, because it is misstated in both directions. Registration under the 2022 Rules is a regime for foreign practice, not a second enrolment: it confers no entry on any State roll, no right under Section 30, and no standing in an Indian court, and it is granted and supervised by the Bar Council of India rather than by any State Bar Council. An Indian advocate needs no registration of this kind to practise Indian law, which is the advocate's by enrolment; the 2025 amendment's opening of registration to Indian advocates and firms concerns their practice of foreign law, placing the Indian firm advising on English or Singapore law in India under the same regulatory umbrella as the foreign firm advising on the same law. The reciprocity inquiry runs through the home jurisdiction in each case: the question is whether that jurisdiction permits Indian lawyers the corresponding space, and where it does not, Section 47's bar and the rules' refusal follow.

The argument on this question has a genuine second side, and the stance of this article is to record it without deciding it. The Bar Council of India and the domestic profession argue protection on reciprocity: Indian firms face establishment restrictions in many jurisdictions, entry without reciprocity surrenders the only bargaining asset the profession holds, and the Indian litigation market sustains a very large number of independent practitioners who cannot absorb competition from global firms on equal terms. The contrary case, put by Chief Justice of India J. S. Khehar in 2017 when he favoured the entry of foreign lawyers and law firms and said the opportunity of economic globalisation for the profession should never be missed, is that cross-border clients require integrated advice across jurisdictions, that Indian firms have grown through exposure to international work, and that a restriction which keeps foreign firms out also pushes the India-facing part of global transactions to offices in Singapore, Dubai and London. The registration rules of 2022, amended in 2025, are the working compromise between those cases: the door is open, measured, registered and reciprocal, and the disagreement continues over its width.

## Rule-making, repeals and the transitional scheme

Section 49 is the engine room of the Act's subordinate law, and its catalogue repays reading in full because the contested instruments of the profession all trace to one of its clauses. The section is headed "General power of the Bar Council of India to make rules". Rules may prescribe the conditions on which an advocate may vote at a State Bar Council election and the preparation of electoral rolls, the qualifications and disqualifications for membership of a Bar Council, the manner of giving effect to the ten-year proviso in Section 3(2), the manner of preventing enrolment on more than one State roll, the determination of seniority, the minimum qualifications for admission to a course of degree in law in a recognised university, the class or category of persons entitled to be enrolled as advocates, and, in clause (ah), the conditions subject to which an advocate shall have the right to practise and the circumstances in which a person shall be deemed to practise as an advocate in a court. Rules may further prescribe the form of application for transfer of a name between State rolls, the standards of professional conduct and etiquette in clause (c), the standards of legal education and the inspection of universities in clause (d), the recognition of foreign qualifications in clause (e), the procedure of disciplinary committees in clause (f), the restrictions on senior advocates in clause (g), the robes and dresses of advocates in clause (gg), the fees leviable in respect of any matter under the Act in clause (h), general principles for the guidance of State Bar Councils and the enforcement of the Bar Council of India's directions, and any other prescribed matter. Two approvals discipline the power: rules under clause (c) or clause (gg) have no effect unless approved by the Chief Justice of India, and rules under clause (e) have no effect unless approved by the Central Government.

Section 49A, inserted by the 1964 amendment, arms the Central Government with a parallel rule power for carrying out the purposes of the Act, including on matters for which a Bar Council also has power, and resolves conflicts in the government's favour: where a Bar Council rule is repugnant to a Central Government rule under the section, the Central rule prevails and the council's rule is void to the extent of the repugnancy. Central rules must be laid before Parliament and may be modified or annulled by both Houses. The existence of Section 49A is a standing qualification on the self-regulation story: Parliament kept a legislative override inside the statute, even if the everyday instruments, the conduct code, the legal education rules, the examination and the verification regime, have all issued from the councils.

The rule powers are in fact layered through the Act, and mapping them explains where each instrument lives. Section 15 gives every Bar Council power to make rules for Chapter II, the councils' own constitution and elections, with State rules needing the Bar Council of India's approval. Section 28 gives a State Bar Council power to make rules for Chapter III, the enrolment chapter, again under the national council's approval. Section 49 is the Bar Council of India's general power, reaching conduct, education, enrolment categories and the conditions of practice across the whole Act. Section 34 reserves a rule power to the High Courts over practice in their own courts, and Section 49A overlays the Central Government's power on all of it. Sub-section (2) of Section 49, inserted in 1973, saved the conduct and dress rules in force immediately before the 1973 amendment, so that the professional code continued without a gap while the section was being renumbered around it. A challenge to any instrument in this field therefore begins with a vires question in a specific clause, which is precisely how Bonnie Foi was argued and decided.

Section 50 then dismantles the old law on a trigger mechanism tied to the chapters, in the sequence described earlier: sub-section (1) repeals the core of the Indian Bar Councils Act 1926 in a territory when its State Bar Council is constituted; sub-section (2) repeals the enrolment provisions of the Legal Practitioners Act 1879, the corresponding sections of the Bombay Pleaders Act 1920, the enrolment part of Section 8 of the 1926 Act and the Letters Patent provisions on admission when Chapter III begins; sub-section (3) repeals the practice provisions of the same instruments and the Supreme Court Advocates (Practice in High Courts) Act 1951, Act 18 of 1951, when Chapter IV begins; sub-section (4) repeals the disciplinary provisions when Chapter V begins; and sub-section (5) repeals the remainder when the whole Act is in force, saving Sections 1, 3 and 36 of the Legal Practitioners Act 1879, the sections dealing with touts and the Act's own frame. Section 51 construes references in any enactment to an advocate enrolled by a High Court as references to an advocate enrolled under this Act, and Section 52 saves the Supreme Court's power to make rules under Article 145 of the Constitution on the conditions for senior advocates and on who may act or plead in that Court. The 2023 amendment finished the repeal story Section 50 had left open: Section 50(6), inserted by Act 33 of 2023, repeals Sections 1, 3 and 36 of the Legal Practitioners Act 1879 from the date Section 45A comes into force, so that the tout regime of 1879 moved bodily into this Act before its parent sections vanished.

Chapter VII manages the human transition. Section 53 governed elections to the first State Bar Councils, held among advocates, vakils, pleaders and attorneys entitled to practise in the High Court, and Section 54 gave the first elected members a two-year term. Section 55 saved the practice rights of pleaders, vakils, mukhtars and revenue agents who did not elect to enrol, or were not qualified to enrol, as advocates: they continued with the same rights of practice and remained subject to the disciplinary jurisdiction they had before, under the old law as if unrepealed. Section 56 dissolved the existing Bar Councils on the constitution of the new ones, vesting their property, rights and obligations in the successors and transferring all pending proceedings, disciplinary or otherwise, to the new State Bar Council, a transfer device that sits alongside Sections 8A and 36B in the Act's family of continuity provisions. Section 57 let the Supreme Court and the High Courts exercise a Bar Council's rule-making power until the council was constituted, and Section 58 carried special provisions for the transitional period, including the performance of enrolment functions by the High Court where a State Bar Council had not been constituted or could not function. Lettered sections that follow, from Section 58A onwards, made special provision for particular groups, advocates of the Allahabad and Oudh courts, of Hyderabad, of Bombay and Gujarat, and of the Judicial Commissioners' courts, and for Pondicherry and for persons enrolled by the Mysore State Bar Council, each a repair of some anomaly the unification would otherwise have created.

One survival inside the transitional scheme outlasted the transition. The attorneys of Bombay and Calcutta lost their separate enrolment when the unified roll came, but the articled clerks training in their offices were mid-course in a qualification the old system had promised them. Section 24(1)(c) preserves the route in its text, counting the article clerk's examination specified by the High Court at Bombay or Calcutta among the qualifications for admission, Section 34(2) preserves the Calcutta High Court's power to make rules for the Intermediate and Final examinations for the articled clerks referred to in Section 58AG, and the lettered transitional sections carry the corresponding saving. The detail is small, but it shows the method of the whole chapter: the Act abolishes a class only after it has provided for the last person training to enter it.

## The amendment arc: 1964 to 2023

The Act has been rebuilt several times, and the amendments mark the points where the original settlement failed. The Advocates (Amendment) Act 1964, Act 21 of 1964, in force from 16 May 1964, began the cycle of repairs: it recast the disciplinary committee in Section 9, inserted the member-disqualification provision that became Section 10B, added the Central Government's rule power in Section 49A, inserted the Bar Council of India's revision power in Section 48A, and added the proviso letting the council reject a transfer application made while discipline was pending or in bad faith. The Advocates (Amendment) Act 1973, Act 60 of 1973, in force from 31 January 1974, was the great reconstruction. It ended the common roll the Act had originally contemplated and anchored every advocate to a State roll; it omitted the training clause from Section 24(1); it inserted the disqualification in Section 24A; it gave the State councils' disciplinary proceedings the withdrawal, succession and one-year-transfer machinery of Sections 35(1A), 36A and 36B; it added the hearing protection for appellants whose punishment an appellate body proposes to worsen; it recast Section 49 as a numbered sub-section with the clause structure described above; and it inserted provisions as varied as legal aid committees and membership of international bodies. The Advocates (Amendment) Act 1976, Act 107 of 1976, removed the special provision for attorneys in Section 31 and other relics of the dual system. The Advocates (Amendment) Act 1980, Act 47 of 1980, adjusted seniority for attorneys and refined the pre-audience order in Section 23. The Advocates (Amendment) Act 1993, Act 70 of 1993, in force from 26 December 1993, raised the Section 24(1)(f) fee figures, added the dismissal ground to Section 24A, substituted the five-year term provision in Section 8 with the Special Committee machinery in Section 8A, added the welfare and Bar Association functions to Sections 6 and 7, and omitted Section 46. Adaptations followed the reorganisations of States, adjusting the Section 3 table of councils as Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand and the reorganised Union territories of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh took their places, the Act applying to the two new Union territories by notification S.O. 3912(E) of 30 October 2019.

The most recent enacted change is the Advocates (Amendment) Act 2023, Act 33 of 2023, which received the President's assent on 8 December 2023 and came into force on 30 September 2024. It is a narrow Act with a single object, the tout. It inserted Section 45A, empowering every High Court, District Judge, Sessions Judge, District Magistrate and Revenue officer not below the rank of a Collector to frame and publish lists of persons proved, by evidence of general repute or otherwise, habitually to act as touts, after giving the person an opportunity to show cause against inclusion; the lists are displayed in the courts, a listed person may be excluded from the precincts of the court, and a person who acts as a tout while his name stands in a list is punishable with imprisonment extending to three months, or a fine extending to five hundred rupees, or both. The same Act, through Section 50(6), repealed the surviving tout provisions of the Legal Practitioners Act 1879 from the commencement of Section 45A, closing the oldest file the Advocates Act had left open.

Reform proposals beyond the 2023 Act exist only as drafts, and their status should be kept exact. The Law Commission's draft Advocates (Amendment) Bill 2017, annexed to its Report No. 266, was never introduced in Parliament. A draft Advocates (Amendment) Bill was published by the Department of Legal Affairs for public consultation on 13 February 2025; it proposed changes to the definitions of legal practitioner and law graduate, among other matters, drew sustained protest from the Bar Council of India and bar associations, and on 22 February 2025 the Ministry of Law and Justice announced that the consultation process was concluded and that the draft Bill, as revised, would be processed afresh for consultation with stakeholders. A further consultation draft was circulated by the Bar Council of India in 2026. None of these texts is law, and the Act described in this article is the Act of 1961 as amended by the enacted amendments recorded above.

What has not been amended is as instructive as what has. The four punishments of Section 35(3) stand in the words of 1961. The composition bands of Section 3(2), fifteen, twenty and twenty-five, have survived every reorganisation of the States they serve. The exclusivity of Section 33 and the one-class rule of Section 29 have never been reopened. Parliament's interventions have clustered at the machinery, enrolment's disqualification, discipline's timetable, the councils' elections and the fee, which is to say at the points where the original settlement met a constituency it had injured: the convicted applicant, the complainant waiting years for a hearing, the advocate facing an election that never came, and the graduate charged more than the statute allowed. The architecture of 1961 has not been revisited because, on the evidence of the amendment record, the architecture is not where the failures were.

## Self-regulation assessed

The case for self-regulation begins with the structure of the advocate's work. The profession that must appear against the State, in challenges to detention, taxation, prosecution and executive action, cannot take its licence from the State without putting the adversary relationship under the licensor's shadow. The Act answers that concern with election: the regulators are advocates chosen by advocates, on the same model as the medical and legal professions in most common law jurisdictions, and the constitutional courts have respected the boundary the model implies. The Constitution Bench in Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of India (1998) refused to let even a contempt court take away the licence to practise, leaving that professional death sentence to the councils the Act creates. The Constitution Bench in Bonnie Foi treated the profession's judgment on the competence gate, the bar examination, as entitled to weight inside its own sphere. And the empirical card the defenders play is the fee case in reverse: where Parliament wanted control, it took it expressly, in Section 24(1)(f), which suggests that the domains left to the councils were left deliberately, after argument, as the price of an independent bar.

The welfare record belongs on the same side of the ledger. The councils are not only regulators; under Sections 6(2) and 7(2) they are the profession's mutual insurers, building funds for indigent and disabled advocates, for legal aid and for law libraries, and Section 6(1)(dd) charges the State councils with promoting Bar Associations precisely so that welfare schemes reach the district bars where hardship concentrates. A State licensing department would not perform that function with the same knowledge of the profession's casualties, and the elected councils' familiarity with their own electorate, the same familiarity the critics count as capture, is what makes the welfare machinery work at the level of the individual advocate's distress.

The case against begins with incentives. A body elected by practitioners is judged, at election time, by the practitioners it may have to discipline, and the Act's own history records the consequence. Parliament inserted Section 36B in 1973 because State disciplinary proceedings were not finishing; a one-year limit with automatic transfer is a legislative confession that the first-instance machinery could stall indefinitely. The Supreme Court in Mahipal Singh Rana v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2016), a bench of Justices Anil R. Dave, Kurian Joseph and Adarsh Kumar Goel, described an urgent need to review the provisions of the Act dealing with the regulatory mechanism and requested the Law Commission of India and the Central Government to act. The Law Commission's Report No. 266, The Advocates Act 1961 (Regulation of Legal Profession), submitted on 23 March 2017, recorded that an overwhelming number of the responses it received, from judges, councils, associations, lawyers and academics, pointed to the failure of the existing mechanism of self-regulation, and it recommended extensive amendment, with a draft Bill annexed. The Bar Council of India rejected the Commission's report and resolved to frame its own recommendations instead. In Ex-Capt. Harish Uppal v. Union of India, (2003) 2 SCC 45, the Supreme Court had already held that lawyers possess no right to strike or boycott courts and had called on the Bar Councils to act against those who do, a judgment whose premise is that the councils had not been acting. On legal education, the critics' point is the one made in the education section above: the body that licenses the degree also gains membership from the profession's growth, and the multiplication of law colleges proceeded under its recognition regime.

The recurring reform answer to these failures has been to qualify the self-government rather than abolish it, and the drafts show the direction of pressure. The Law Commission's 2017 draft Bill kept the councils but proposed a statutory definition of misconduct, wider disciplinary powers and consequences for organised abstention from court work; it was not introduced. The February 2025 consultation draft went further in the direction of the State, proposing among its changes a government presence inside the councils through nominated members and a power of direction over the Bar Council of India, and it was those features, reported widely in the profession's press, that the Bar Council of India and the bar associations fastened on in the protests that closed the consultation within nine days. The pattern across six decades is consistent: the profession's autonomy is the settlement every reform must negotiate with, and the negotiation happens at the boundary Parliament draws, not inside the council chamber.

The record supports a measurement rather than a slogan. Self-regulation under this Act is real, extensive and democratically anchored inside the profession, and every serious attempt to displace it, by contempt court, by training rules struck down in litigation, or by Law Commission draft, has failed against the statute's design. It is also bounded, and the boundaries are where the system's failures get corrected: the fee was corrected by the statute's own figure; the examination was disciplined by constitutional review of vires; the disciplinary machine was given a statutory clock by Parliament in 1973 and an automatic transfer it cannot waive; and the appellate chain of Sections 37 and 38 delivers the councils' gravest decisions to the Bar Council of India and then to the Supreme Court. The honest verdict is that the Act trusts the profession with its own government and trusts it incompletely, and that the incompleteness, the fee, the clock, the appeals and Section 49A's unused override, is not an accident of drafting but the settlement itself.

## The money of the profession

Money runs through the Act in four channels, and separating them prevents the common error of treating every sum an advocate pays as an enrolment fee. The first channel is the price of entry: the Section 24(1)(f) fee, fixed by Parliament, divided between the State Bar Council that enrols and the Bar Council of India, with the stamp duty the Stamp Act imposes standing outside the fee, as Gaurav Kumar confirmed. The second channel is the councils' own charges for the business of the Act: Section 49(1)(h) empowers the Bar Council of India to prescribe by rule the fees which may be levied in respect of any matter under the Act, a power that operates on matters other than enrolment, since enrolment's price is taken by the statute itself; some transactions the Act expressly makes free, transfer of a name between State rolls under Section 18 being the clearest, granted without the payment of any fee. The third channel is the profession's collective funds: the welfare, legal aid and law library funds of Sections 6(2) and 7(2), fed by the councils' income and by grants, donations and benefactions, with the Bar Council of India's power under Section 46A to assist a State Bar Council in need, and the discipline of Section 12 behind them, audited accounts, publication in the Gazette, and transmission upward, State accounts to the Bar Council of India and its accounts to the Central Government. The fourth channel is the money of litigation itself: the fees an advocate charges a client are a matter of engagement, but the fees recoverable as costs by a successful party from the adversary are regulated by the High Court's rules under Section 34(1A), which fix and regulate by taxation or otherwise the fees payable as costs in respect of the adversary's advocate. Gaurav Kumar policed the first channel only; the judgment does not touch what a council may charge for a matter the Act leaves to its rules, what a fund may raise by voluntary contribution, or what a court may allow as costs, and reading it as a general charter against professional charges misstates its scope.

## The verdict on the Advocates Act 1961

The Act's design can be stated in one sentence: Parliament built a profession that governs itself and then fixed, in the statute, the single price of entry into it. Admission, standards, education, conduct and discipline all run through councils of advocates elected by advocates, and the Supreme Court has guarded that allocation against courts reaching for the licence, against a council exceeding its rule power, and against a government draft that the profession resisted. The two contested questions of the last decade were both settled on the statute's terms: the examination survived because Section 49(1)(ah) grants the power in words, and the fee fell because Section 24(1)(f) grants no power to charge beyond its figures. The question that remains open, the width of the door for foreign lawyers, is being settled the same way, by rule-making inside the boundary Balaji drew. For the student, the Act is the map of entry; for the practitioner, it is the code of accountability; for the reader of the series, it is the clearest Indian example of the series thesis that the boundary of self-regulation is set by whatever the statute reserves, and that here the reservation was a number.

That is also the practical reading for anyone advising on this Act. A question about the profession is answered in three places in a fixed order: first in the section, to see whether Parliament has spoken, as it has on the fee, the punishments and the appellate chain; then in the rules under the section, where the profession's own answers live, the examination, the conduct code and the verification regime; and then in the constitutional case law that patrols the junction between the two, Bonnie Foi on the rule side of the line and Gaurav Kumar on the statute side. Most errors in writing about this Act come from collapsing those levels, attributing the examination to the statute or the fee to the councils. The Act rewards the reader who keeps its levels apart, because its whole design is the allocation of questions between them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Advocates Act 1961 regulate?

The Advocates Act 1961, Act 25 of 1961, regulates the legal profession in India as a single system. It constitutes the Bar Council of India and the State Bar Councils, prescribes who may be admitted as an advocate on a State roll, confers the right to practise law in every court in India, lays down the disciplinary machinery for professional and other misconduct, and empowers the councils to govern legal education, professional conduct and the conditions of practice by rules. It replaced the fragmented regime of the Legal Practitioners Act 1879 and the Indian Bar Councils Act 1926, under which pleaders, mukhtars, vakils, attorneys and advocates held separate and unequal rights.

### Q: Who can be enrolled as an advocate?

Under Section 24, a person may be admitted as an advocate on a State roll if the person is a citizen of India, has completed twenty-one years of age, holds a degree in law from a university recognised for the purposes of the Act by the Bar Council of India or another qualification the section preserves, fulfils the conditions the State Bar Council's rules specify, and pays the enrolment fee Section 24(1)(f) fixes, with stamp duty if chargeable. A foreign national may be admitted where Indian citizens are permitted to practise law in that person's country. Section 24A bars enrolment of a person convicted of an offence involving moral turpitude, subject to the two-year proviso the section carries.

### Q: What does the Bar Council of India regulate?

Section 7 gives the Bar Council of India the apex functions of the profession's government. It lays down standards of professional conduct and etiquette, prescribes the procedure of disciplinary committees, promotes legal education and fixes its standards in consultation with the universities and the State Bar Councils, recognises universities whose law degrees qualify for enrolment and inspects them, recognises foreign qualifications on a reciprocal basis, exercises general supervision and control over State Bar Councils, and hears appeals against State disciplinary orders. Under Section 49 it makes the rules through which much of this regulation operates, including the conditions on which an advocate has the right to practise.

### Q: Is the All India Bar Examination valid?

Yes. The examination is a condition of the right to practise imposed by the Bar Council of India through its rules under the Advocates Act, principally Section 49(1)(ah); it is not a provision written into the Act itself. Its validity was contested for years, and a Constitution Bench of five judges of the Supreme Court upheld it in Bar Council of India v. Bonnie Foi Law College, decided on 10 February 2023. The Court held that the council has adequate power under the Act to prescribe the examination, that whether it is held before or after enrolment is for the council to decide, and that the contrary interdict in V. Sudeer could not be sustained.

### Q: How is professional misconduct dealt with?

A complaint, or the State Bar Council's own information, starts the process under Section 35: the council refers the case to its disciplinary committee, which hears the advocate and the Advocate-General and may dismiss the complaint, reprimand the advocate, suspend the advocate from practice, or remove the name from the State roll. The Bar Council of India may withdraw a pending case to itself under Section 36, and a case not concluded within one year stands transferred to it under Section 36B. Appeals lie to the Bar Council of India under Section 37 and then to the Supreme Court under Section 38, each within sixty days.

### Q: Can advocates advertise?

No, subject to one narrow exception. Rule 36 of the Bar Council of India Rules, made under Section 49(1)(c) of the Act, forbids an advocate to solicit work or advertise directly or indirectly, whether by circulars, advertisements, touts, personal communications or inspired publicity, and restricts what a sign-board or name-plate may state. An amendment of 2008 permits an advocate to furnish information on a website in the particulars the Schedule to the rule prescribes: name, address and contact details, enrolment number and date, the Bar Councils of enrolment, Bar Association membership, professional and academic qualifications, and areas of practice, with a declaration that the information is true. Anything beyond those particulars breaches the rule and is punishable as misconduct.

### Q: Can foreign lawyers practise in India?

Foreign lawyers and foreign law firms may not practise Indian law in India, on the litigation or the non-litigation side: the Supreme Court settled that position in Bar Council of India v. A. K. Balaji (2018). A foreign lawyer may visit on a temporary fly-in fly-out basis to advise on foreign law, and may appear in international commercial arbitration held in India. Under the Bar Council of India Rules for Registration and Regulation of Foreign Lawyers and Foreign Law Firms in India, 2022, notified in 2023 and amended in 2025, a foreign lawyer or firm may register with the council, on a reciprocal basis, to practise foreign and international law in non-litigious matters and in international commercial arbitration; fly-in fly-out visits are capped at sixty days in twelve months.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide about enrolment fees?

In Gaurav Kumar v. Union of India, decided on 30 July 2024, the Supreme Court held that a State Bar Council and the Bar Council of India cannot charge an enrolment fee beyond the amount Section 24(1)(f) of the Act specifies: six hundred rupees to the State Bar Council and one hundred and fifty rupees to the Bar Council of India, Rs 750 in all, and one hundred rupees and twenty-five rupees, Rs 125 in all, for Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe applicants. Stamp duty, where the law imposes it, stands apart. Charges under heads such as identity cards, welfare funds or training cannot be made a condition of enrolment, and the practice of charging more violated Articles 14 and 19(1)(g). The ruling operates prospectively, so fees already collected were not ordered to be refunded.

### Q: What is the State roll of advocates?

The State roll is the register of advocates that every State Bar Council must prepare and maintain under Section 17 of the Act. It carries the names and addresses of the advocates admitted in that State, arranged in two parts, senior advocates in the first part and other advocates in the second, with entries ordered by seniority. A person may stand on one State roll only. The roll is the foundation document of the profession in the State: enrolment on it makes a person an advocate, the certificate of enrolment under Section 22 evidences the entry, punishments are recorded against the name under Section 41, and the electoral roll for council elections is built from it.

### Q: Can an advocate enrolled in one State practise in another State?

Yes. Section 30 entitles every advocate whose name is entered in a State roll to practise as of right throughout the territories to which the Act extends, in all courts including the Supreme Court, before tribunals and authorities legally authorised to take evidence, and before any other authority the advocate is entitled by law to appear before. Enrolment in one State therefore opens every court in India, and no fresh enrolment is needed to appear in another State. What enrolment in one State does not transfer is the home roll itself: an advocate who shifts the centre of practice permanently may move the name to the other State's roll by transfer under Section 18, which the Bar Council of India directs without fee, seniority travelling with the name.

### Q: What is the penalty for practising law without enrolment?

Section 45 of the Act makes unauthorised practice a criminal offence. A person who practises in any court or before any authority or person, in or before whom the person is not entitled to practise under the Act, is punishable with imprisonment for a term which may extend to six months. The provision is the criminal edge of Section 33, which confines the entitlement to practise to persons enrolled as advocates, subject to exceptions in the Act or another law. Section 32 preserves a narrow opening: a court, authority or person may permit a person who is not enrolled to appear before it in a particular case, so appearance with leave in a single matter is not the offence Section 45 punishes.

### Q: Can a punished advocate appeal against the order?

Yes, in two stages. Under Section 37, a person aggrieved by an order of a State Bar Council's disciplinary committee, and the Advocate-General of the State, may appeal to the Bar Council of India within sixty days of the order's communication; the Bar Council of India's disciplinary committee hears the appeal and may vary the punishment, though it may not worsen the appellant's position without giving a reasonable opportunity of being heard. Under Section 38, an appeal lies from the Bar Council of India's disciplinary committee to the Supreme Court, again within sixty days, and the Court may pass any order, including one varying the punishment, under the same hearing protection. Sections 5 and 12 of the Limitation Act apply to both appeals under Section 39, and an appeal does not itself stay the order unless a stay is granted under Section 40.

### Q: Who can file a complaint of misconduct against an advocate?

Any person may bring the facts to the State Bar Council on whose roll the advocate's name stands. Section 35 is set in motion where the council, on receipt of a complaint or otherwise, has reason to believe that an advocate on its roll has been guilty of professional or other misconduct, so a complaint from a client is the usual beginning but not the only one: the council may act on a court's report, on information from any source, or on its own motion. The proceeding then belongs to the council rather than to the complainant, who gives evidence but does not control the charge. A complainant dissatisfied with the outcome is a person aggrieved for the purposes of the appeal to the Bar Council of India under Section 37.

### Q: What counts as professional misconduct under the Act?

The Act does not define professional or other misconduct, and the courts have declined to define it exhaustively. In Noratanmal Chouraria v. M. R. Murli, (2004) 5 SCC 689, the Supreme Court held that misconduct envisages breach of discipline, that it cannot be catalogued in advance, and that it is wide enough to cover wrongful acts and omissions, intentional or unintentional. The working test is fitness: conduct that shows the advocate to be unworthy of the confidence of the community as an officer of the court, measured against the standards of professional conduct and etiquette in the Bar Council of India Rules. It is not confined to conduct in practice; the conduct in Noratanmal itself was an assault committed outside the advocate's professional capacity, and discipline still followed.

### Q: What happens if a disciplinary case is not finished in one year?

Section 36B answers with an automatic transfer. The disciplinary committee of a State Bar Council must dispose of a complaint expeditiously, and the proceedings in each case must be concluded within one year from the date the complaint was received or the date the State Bar Council initiated the proceedings. If the year runs out, the proceedings stand transferred to the Bar Council of India by operation of the section, without any party applying, and the Bar Council of India disposes of them as if they had been withdrawn for inquiry before its own disciplinary committee under Section 36(2), with the full range of punishments available. The provision, inserted by the 1973 amendment, is Parliament's discipline on the disciplinary system itself.

### Q: How does a conviction affect enrolment as an advocate?

Section 24A disqualifies a convicted person from enrolment on a State roll. A person convicted of an offence involving moral turpitude, convicted of an offence under the Untouchability (Offences) Act 1955, or dismissed or removed from employment or office under the State on a charge involving moral turpitude, cannot be admitted as an advocate. The disqualification is temporary: the proviso to the section ends it once two years have elapsed since the person's release, dismissal or removal. A person dealt with under the Probation of Offenders Act 1958 is excepted by Section 24A(2). The Supreme Court applied the provision in Mahipal Singh Rana v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2016), holding that the enrolment of an advocate convicted of contempt stood suspended for the two-year period the section prescribes.

### Q: Can an advocate take up a full-time salaried job?

Not while continuing to practise. Rule 49 of the Bar Council of India Rules provides that an advocate shall not be a full-time salaried employee of any person, government, firm, corporation or concern so long as he continues to practise; on taking up such employment the advocate must intimate the fact to the Bar Council on whose roll his name appears and must thereupon cease to practise for as long as the employment continues. Neighbouring rules soften the edges without removing the rule: Rule 47 allows a sleeping partnership in a business the State Bar Council considers consistent with the dignity of the profession, and Rule 48 permits a directorship or chairmanship of a company without executive duties, though not the office of managing director or secretary.

### Q: Can a newly enrolled advocate practise before clearing the bar examination?

For a time, yes. Under the Bar Council of India's framework, enrolment on the State roll comes first and the All India Bar Examination follows: the advocate enrols under Section 24 and is entitled to practise while the examination is cleared, and the certificate of practice issues on passing. The entitlement is not open-ended. The examination applies to advocates who graduated in the academic year 2009-10 onwards, and the Allahabad High Court has stated the consequence of default in terms: an advocate of that cohort who does not qualify within two years of provisional enrolment is not entitled to practise, and a presiding officer may refuse to hear the advocate or to honour the vakalatnama. The name remains on the roll; what is withheld is the appearance in court.

### Q: What happens if an advocate does not get the certificate of practice verified?

The Bar Council of India Certificate and Place of Practice (Verification) Rules 2015 require every advocate to hold a certificate of practice and to have it verified by the State Bar Council in five-year cycles, with a declaration of the place of practice. An advocate who does not apply within the time allowed is placed on the list of defaulting advocates; an advocate who still does not comply is published in the list of non-practising advocates, and on that publication the disability attaches: the advocate is not entitled to practise or to the privileges that ride on the roll, including the vote at council elections, until verification restores the name. The Allahabad High Court has held that the disability bites on publication of the list, not on the mere passage of five years, so practice may continue while a verification application is pending.

### Q: When did the right to practise under Section 30 begin?

On 15 June 2011, half a century after the Act was passed. The Advocates Act received assent on 19 May 1961 and was brought into force in stages by notification, and Section 30, which states the advocate's entitlement to practise as of right in all courts including the Supreme Court, was the last major provision to be notified, by S.O. 1349(E) dated 9 June 2011 with effect from 15 June 2011. Other parts of Chapter IV had begun far earlier: Sections 29, 31, 33 and 34 commenced on 1 June 1969, and Section 32 with Chapter VI on 4 January 1963. The delay mattered because Section 30 is the clause that makes enrolment on one State roll a nationwide right in terms, and until its commencement the entitlement rested on the chapter's other provisions and the rules made under them.
