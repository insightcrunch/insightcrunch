On 7 August 1990, Prime Minister Vishwanath Pratap Singh stood in the Lok Sabha and announced that his government would implement the central recommendation of a report that had waited ten years in the files of the Union government: reservation of 27 percent of posts under the Government of India and its public undertakings for the Other Backward Classes. The Mandal Commission's decisive moment in Indian politics is usually dated from that announcement, and the dating is too narrow. The moment runs from the Commission's constitution in December 1978 to the Supreme Court's judgment in Indra Sawhney v Union of India on 16 November 1992, and its substance is still argued over, because the three most serious accounts of what happened in those fourteen years do not merely differ in emphasis. They disagree about what kind of event Mandal was.

![The Mandal Commission report of December 1980 and the three scholarly readings of the Mandal moment in Indian politics - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The puzzle can be stated plainly. Almost every serious student of Indian politics treats the Mandal implementation of 1990 as a turning point, a before and an after in the life of the republic. Yet the turning point has three rival descriptions, each attached to a scholar, a book or a body of essays, and each resting on a different body of evidence. Yogendra Yadav reads Mandal as democratic deepening: the moment when the formal promise of political equality, made in 1950, acquired substance for the backward classes who form the largest bloc of the Indian electorate. Pradeep Chhibber reads Mandal as an accelerant of party-system fragmentation: one of the forces that broke the old national party system into the regionalised, coalition-bound order that governed India from 1989 to 2014. Christophe Jaffrelot reads Mandal twice over, as a silent revolution in the social composition of political power in north India and as the provocation that handed Hindu nationalism its most effective mobilisation opening of the late twentieth century. A descriptive history of the Commission, its report and its implementation cannot choose among these readings, because the facts are largely shared and the disagreement is about what the facts did.

The claim defended in this article is that the three readings complement rather than fully compete, and that Mandal earns the name of decisive moment precisely because all three describe real and simultaneous effects of a single reframe. Yadav's reading addresses the substantive dimension: what changed for the backward classes themselves, in representation, in office and in the content of political appeals. Chhibber's reading addresses the institutional dimension: what changed for the party system that processes those appeals. Jaffrelot's reading addresses the strategic dimension: what changed for the Bharatiya Janata Party, which learned to answer caste assertion with religious consolidation. A reframe that altered the substance of democratic citizenship, the architecture of party competition and the strategy of the rising opposition at the same time is not a policy episode. It is a settlement change, a revision of the terms on which the post-Independence order distributed recognition and power. The sections that follow reconstruct the chronology, examine the Commission's own evidence, take each reading on its strongest terms, and then adjudicate.

## The Mandal Commission Decisive Moment: December 1978 to November 1992

The moment begins inside the first non-Congress government of the republic. The Janata Party, the coalition that won the general election of March 1977 after the Emergency, had promised in its election manifesto to appoint a commission on the backward classes, and its government under Prime Minister Morarji Desai acted on the promise late in its life: the appointment of the Second Backward Classes Commission was announced on 20 December 1978, and the Commission was formally constituted by presidential order on 1 January 1979 under Article 340 of the Constitution. The government that appointed it was already fracturing, a condition examined in the account of [the 1977 Janata coalition's institutional fragility](/2019/04/28/1977-janata-verdict-explained/), and it fell in July 1979, months into the Commission's work. The chairman was Bindeshwari Prasad Mandal, a former chief minister of Bihar and a sitting member of the Lok Sabha from Madhepura, a Yadav by caste and a socialist by political formation, whose own career had been made inside the backward-class politics of his state. The Commission took his name in public usage, as the First Backward Classes Commission of 1953 had taken the name of its chairman, Kaka Kalelkar.

### When was the Second Backward Classes Commission constituted?

Prime Minister Morarji Desai announced the appointment in December 1978, and the Commission was formally constituted by presidential order on 1 January 1979 under Article 340, chaired by Bindeshwari Prasad Mandal, a former chief minister of Bihar and the Lok Sabha member from Madhepura.

Its terms of reference required it to determine the criteria for defining socially and educationally backward classes, to recommend steps for their advancement, and to examine reservation in services and in educational institutions.

The Commission's membership mattered to its reception. Beside Mandal sat Dewan Mohan Lal, R. R. Bhole and K. Subramaniam, with S. S. Gill as member-secretary; Dina Bandhu Sahu, an original member, left during 1979 and was replaced by L. R. Naik, the only member drawn from the Scheduled Castes. Four of the five members belonged to the backward classes themselves, a composition that admirers read as experiential authority and that detractors read as partisan design, and the dispute over composition trailed the report for a decade. The Commission worked through 1979 and 1980 under three governments: the Janata government that appointed it, the caretaker administration of Charan Singh, and, from January 1980, the returned Congress government of Indira Gandhi, which received its report. It held sittings, issued questionnaires to the Union and state governments and to the public, took evidence from sociologists and administrators, and conducted a socio-economic field survey in February 1980 that covered two villages and one urban block in 405 of the country's 406 districts, an evidentiary sweep no earlier commission on the subject had attempted.

The report was submitted to the President of India on 31 December 1980. By then the political conditions that had produced the Commission had dissolved. Indira Gandhi's government, restored in the election of January 1980 with 353 Lok Sabha seats, had no dependence on the Janata coalition's promises and no appetite for a report whose implementation would antagonise the upper-caste constituencies the Congress still courted in north India. The report was tabled and discussed fitfully in Parliament during 1982, and members of the Lok Sabha pressed the government for a decision; the government replied that the question of reservation for backward classes at the centre should be settled in consultation with the states, and the file went quiet. What followed was a nine-year shelving, examined in a later section, during which the report existed as a printed text, an opposition demand and a standing reproach, but not as policy.

The second act of the moment opened with the general election of November 1989. The National Front government of V.P. Singh, formed in December 1989 with outside support from the Bharatiya Janata Party and the Left Front, carried in its manifesto a commitment to implement the Mandal recommendations, and on 7 August 1990 V.P. Singh announced the implementation in Parliament, a decision treated in full in the account of [the V.P. Singh government's 1990 Mandal implementation](/2019/05/20/vp-singh-1989-verdict-india/). The office memorandum giving effect to the announcement was issued by the Department of Personnel and Training on 13 August 1990. Within weeks the announcement had set off the largest urban protest movement of the decade, a counter-mobilisation on the Ayodhya question, the withdrawal of Bharatiya Janata Party support on 23 October 1990 after L. K. Advani's arrest at Samastipur, and the fall of the V.P. Singh government in a confidence vote on 7 November 1990. The successor government of Chandra Shekhar kept the reservation in place, the Congress government of P. V. Narasimha Rao that took office in June 1991 modified the memorandum in September 1991 by adding a 10 percent quota for economically weaker sections among the unreserved and a preference for the poorer sections within the backward classes, and the Supreme Court, which had stayed the operation of the memoranda while it heard the challenge, delivered its verdict on 16 November 1992. The Court upheld the 27 percent reservation, subject to the exclusion of the creamy layer, a ceiling of 50 percent on total reservation, and the bar on reservation in promotions. The moment that began with a commission's appointment in the last days of 1978 closed, institutionally speaking, with that judgment fourteen years later.

## What the Commission Found: 3,743 Castes, 52 Percent, and the 27 Percent Answer

Any adjudication among the three readings must begin from what the Commission actually said, because later argument repeatedly conscripted a report that few of the arguers had read closely. The report of December 1980, running through seven volumes, opened with a sentence that stated its philosophical premise: there is equality only among equals, and to equate unequals is to perpetuate inequality. From that premise the Commission built an empirical case in three parts: a method for identifying backwardness, a measurement of its scale, and a remedy calibrated to constitutional limits it anticipated with notable precision.

The method came first, and it was the Commission's answer to the Supreme Court's insistence, in M. R. Balaji v State of Mysore in 1962, that caste could not be the sole test of backwardness and that any classification required objective criteria. The Commission adopted eleven indicators of backwardness, grouped under three heads and weighted to express a theory of causation. The four social indicators, worth three points each, captured caste standing as others perceived it, dependence on manual labour, early marriage, and women's participation in work outside the home. The three educational indicators, worth two points each, captured children's non-attendance at school, dropout rates, and matriculation rates against state averages. The four economic indicators, worth one point each, captured family assets, housing, distance from drinking water, and consumption debt. A caste scoring eleven points or more of the maximum twenty-two, which is to say half the scale, was classified as socially and educationally backward. The weighting was itself an argument: social and educational disadvantage were treated as the causes, economic disadvantage as their consequence, and a commission that had weighted income most heavily would have produced a different India on paper.

Applied across the states, the method yielded a list of 3,743 castes and communities as Other Backward Classes among Hindus and non-Hindus combined, with a second, more deprived tier of 2,108 communities the report called depressed backward classes. The population arithmetic rested on the census of 1931, the last enumeration to record caste across India, worked forward with the ratios the Commission derived from state lists and its own survey. The conclusion was that the backward classes constituted 52 percent of the population: 44 percent counted as Hindu backward classes and an estimated further 8 percent among non-Hindu communities, set against 22.5 percent for the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes and roughly 17.5 percent for the forward castes. The Commission set that 52 percent against representation. Its tables showed the backward classes holding 4.83 percent of Class I posts in the central services and 10.63 percent of Class II posts, figures the report deployed to convert an abstract claim of under-representation into an audited deficit. Whatever later scholars disputed about Mandal, the dispute started from this arithmetic: a majority of the population, identified by name and caste, holding under a twentieth of the senior posts of the Union government.

The remedy followed from the diagnosis but was deliberately smaller than it. The Commission recommended reservation of 27 percent of posts in the services of the Union government and its public undertakings for the backward classes, the same proportion in educational institutions aided by the state, and 27 percent in promotions as well, together with age relaxation on the pattern available to the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, carry-forward of unfilled reserved vacancies for three years, and reservation in banks and in private undertakings receiving government assistance. The 27 percent figure was not derived from the 52 percent population share. It was derived from the ceiling the Supreme Court had indicated in Balaji, that reservation should in general remain below half of the posts available. The Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes already held 22.5 percent; 27 percent brought the total to 49.5 percent, inside the ceiling with half a point to spare. The Commission also recommended measures beyond reservation, including land reform, restructuring of production relations, and special educational schemes, but it understood which of its recommendations would be fought over, and it built the fight into the arithmetic from the start.

### What did L. R. Naik's note to the report propose?

L. R. Naik, the Commission's only member from the Scheduled Castes, appended a note proposing that the backward classes be divided into two lists, intermediate backward classes and depressed backward classes, with the reservation divided between them, so that the more advanced backward castes could not absorb the entire benefit.

The majority declined the division. The note anticipated by over a decade the sub-categorisation argument that state governments and courts would later take up in earnest.

The report's most neglected stratum is its evidence of consultation. The Commission toured the states, recorded testimony from state-level hearings through 1979 and 1980, and preserved the articulations of the populations it was classifying: village witnesses describing exclusion from wells, from schools and from the services in terms that the eleven indicators then translated into points. Those state-level testimony documents, preserved at the archives of the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment, are the closest the republic's archive comes to a verbatim record of how the backward classes described their own condition at the moment of their enumeration, and they deserve the preservation discipline that any sensitive testimony requires, the discipline described in [this account of how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) and applicable well beyond journalism. The three readings of Mandal all cite the report's conclusions; the testimony volumes explain the conclusions in the claimants' own words, and any future restudy of the moment will have to begin there.

## The Evidence Under Attack: 1931 Data and the Method's Critics

No part of the report drew more fire than its evidence, and the hub of a pillar devoted to the subject must try the evidence before it tries the interpretations. The charges came in three forms. The first was staleness: the Commission's population arithmetic rested on the census of 1931, the last enumeration to count caste across the whole of India, so that a report submitted in 1980 and implemented in 1990 distributed the offices of a modern republic by ratios older than the republic itself. The second was construction: the eleven indicators weighted social standing at three points, education at two and economics at one, so that caste, the very thing to be proved, supplied half the scale by design, and a sceptic could say the method found backwardness where it had been instructed to look. The third was composition: a commission staffed in its majority by members of the backward classes, delivering a verdict in favour of the backward classes, invited the charge that the inquiry was a brief dressed as a bench. Each charge was made in Parliament, in the press and in the litigation of 1990 to 1992, and each deserves its strongest answer rather than its weakest.

The staleness charge meets a fact the accusers rarely faced. India possessed no newer all-India caste count because the republic had chosen, at the census of 1951, to stop counting caste beyond the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and every subsequent census repeated the refusal. The Commission's alternatives were to use the 1931 ratios, adjusted against the state lists and its own 1980 field survey of 405 districts, or to abandon enumeration altogether and classify by impression. It chose the auditable path and disclosed the basis in its own text, an honesty its critics seldom matched with a rival number. In the decades since, no official enumeration has displaced the 52 percent figure with a better-evidenced alternative at the all-India level; state-level surveys and commissions have revised lists at the margins, adding and occasionally deleting communities, while the central list administered by the National Commission for Backward Classes, given statutory form in 1993, grew by accretion rather than correction. A number can be old and still be the best measurement a state that refuses to measure has left standing, and the burden the staleness charge must carry is to produce the truer count it implies exists.

The construction charge is the more serious one, and the sociologist André Béteille gave it its most formidable academic form across his essays on reservation and caste: a classification that weights social perception so heavily will certify as backward many communities whose material condition has advanced, will freeze a hierarchy it claims to remedy into an administrative grid, and will teach citizens to present themselves to the state in the idiom of caste because the state has announced that caste is the idiom it rewards. The Commission's answer is written into the weighting itself. It held, as a finding about Indian society and not as a bookkeeping convenience, that economic disadvantage among the backward classes flowed from social and educational exclusion rather than the reverse, so that an income-weighted index would have mismeasured the disease it was appointed to diagnose; the depressed weaver with assets marginally above a line remained excluded from the school, the well and the interview board. The disagreement is therefore not between rigour and its absence but between two theories of what backwardness is, one distributive and one recognitional, and the report's theory has the merit of explaining the datum the distributive theory cannot: why communities of modest prosperity remained, in the Commission's testimony record, shut out of the services and the professions at rates their incomes do not predict.

Against all three charges stands the part of the report that no critic succeeded in shaking, because it was not built from 1931 at all. The representation tables, the 4.83 percent of Class I posts and 10.63 percent of Class II posts held by the backward classes, were compiled from the service records of the Government of India in the Commission's own time. Whatever doubts attach to the population denominator, the numerator was the state's own count of its own officers, and it established a deficit of presence in the senior services so wide that no plausible correction to the population share could argue it away: halve the estimated backward-class population to 26 percent and the Class I share remains under a fifth of proportionality. This is why the report survived its decade in the drawer with its authority dented but intact, and why the Supreme Court in 1992, while disciplining the remedy with a ceiling and a creamy layer, accepted the classification project itself. The evidence was imperfect in the ways its critics named. It was sufficient in the way that mattered, and the readings that follow in this article are readings of a moment whose factual floor, the deficit, was never successfully contested.

## The Decade in the Drawer: 1980 to 1989

The shelving of the report is the part of the story that the decisive-moment framework must explain rather than skip, because a reframe that slept for ten years invites the objection that 1990, not 1980, made it. The objection is half right and wholly instructive. Between January 1980 and December 1989 the Union government was held by the Congress under Indira Gandhi and then Rajiv Gandhi, and neither prime minister would touch the report. The reasons were structural rather than personal. The Congress of the 1980s still drew its north Indian organisation from the upper castes and its electoral arithmetic from a broad vertical coalition in which backward-class assertion at the centre threatened more votes than it promised. A commission's report has no self-executing force; it waits on a government whose incentives align with its recommendations, and for a decade no such government existed in New Delhi. The report nonetheless worked underground in three ways during the 1980s. It standardised a vocabulary, giving political organisers across the Hindi belt a single name, Mandal, and a single number, 27 percent, around which to mobilise. It supplied a documentary warrant, a printed official text that state-level movements could cite against the charge of mere casteism. And it set a deadline in opposition manifestos, so that any future non-Congress government in Delhi would inherit the commitment as a debt.

While the centre shelved the question, several states had already answered it, and the contrast between the state record and the central silence is essential evidence for the adjudication. Tamil Nadu's lineage ran back to the communal government orders of the Madras Presidency in 1921, issued under the Justice Party, and the state's post-Independence governments had maintained backward-class reservation continuously: the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam government raised the backward-class share to 31 percent in 1971, and in 1980 M. G. Ramachandran's government, after withdrawing an income-ceiling order that had cost it dearly in that year's Lok Sabha election in the state, raised the backward-class quota to 50 percent, taking the state's total reservation to 68 percent. In 1989 the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam government of M. Karunanidhi divided that 50 percent into 30 percent for backward classes and 20 percent for most backward classes, taking the total to 69 percent. Karnataka's route ran through the Havanur Commission, appointed in 1972 under Chief Minister Devaraj Urs and reporting in 1975, after which Karnataka operated an extensive reservation regime in state services through the late 1970s. Bihar, the chairman's own state, adopted the Mungeri Lal Commission's recommendations under Chief Minister Karpoori Thakur in 1978, reserving 26 percent of state posts across backward-class categories, a state-level settlement whose later career is traced in the study of Bihar's Mandal politics within this pillar. Kerala and Andhra Pradesh each maintained their own state lists and quotas. By 1990, therefore, reservation for backward classes was unremarkable as state practice across the south and in Bihar; what remained unthinkable, and what Mandal proposed, was its extension to the services of the Union itself, the commanding heights of the Indian state.

### Which states already reserved posts for backward classes before 1990?

Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Kerala, Andhra Pradesh and Bihar all operated backward-class reservation in state services well before the Union acted. Tamil Nadu's total reached 68 percent in 1980 and 69 percent in 1989. Karnataka built its regime on the Havanur Commission report of 1975, and Bihar adopted its scheme in 1978.

The legal ground had also been prepared during the shelving decade, largely by the scholarship that surrounded the report rather than by litigation. Marc Galanter's Competing Equalities: Law and the Backward Classes in India, published in 1984, assembled the fullest account of how the compensatory discrimination regime had actually operated since 1950: the constitutional provisions of Articles 15(4) and 16(4), inserted and sustained through the First Amendment of 1951 after the Supreme Court's judgment in State of Madras v Champakam Dorairajan, the line of cases from Balaji onward that disciplined the states' quotas, and the persistent gap between the paper promise and the delivered share. Galanter's study demonstrated, with the regime's own statistics, that three decades of formal provision had left the backward classes' hold on public employment far below their population share at the centre, precisely the deficit the Mandal tables quantified. When the 1990 challenge reached the Supreme Court, the legal argument on both sides was conducted in a vocabulary Galanter had done much to create. The decade in the drawer was therefore not a decade of nothing. It was the decade in which the report became a programme, a legal brief and a mobilisation banner, waiting for a government weak enough, or committed enough, to sign it.

## August 1990: Implementation and Conflagration

The announcement of 7 August 1990 was a parliamentary act with an administrative shadow. V.P. Singh informed the Lok Sabha that the government had decided to implement the Mandal recommendations on reservation in the services of the Union, and the office memorandum of 13 August 1990 from the Department of Personnel and Training converted the announcement into instruction: 27 percent of vacancies in civil posts and services under the Government of India and in public sector undertakings would be reserved for the socially and educationally backward classes, filled by direct recruitment. The memorandum did not extend the reservation to promotions, and it left the identification of beneficiary castes to the common list the government would notify. The prime minister who signed it governed with 143 Janata Dal seats, outside support from the Bharatiya Janata Party's 85 and the Left's bloc, and a restive deputy prime minister in Devi Lal, whom he had dismissed on 1 August 1990, six days before the announcement. The timing has sustained an argument about motive that the evidence cannot fully close: critics then and since have read the announcement as a defensive stroke in an internal party struggle, a reading V.P. Singh and his defenders contested by pointing to the manifesto commitment and to the fact that the decision was taken by the cabinet and announced in Parliament as the fulfilment of a public pledge. The dispute over motive is marked here as disputed; the effects are not in dispute at all.

The protests began within days and crested in September. Students and job aspirants from the unreserved categories, concentrated in Delhi and the towns of the Hindi belt, organised marches, strikes and blockades under banners that framed 27 percent reservation as the confiscation of merit; the agitation turned to self-immolation on 19 September 1990, when Rajiv Goswami, a student at Deshbandhu College in Delhi, set himself alight and survived with grave burns, and imitators followed across north India through the autumn, several of whom died. The protest repertoire was aimed at the state that had issued the memorandum: government buildings, buses and railway property were attacked, universities closed, and the capital's examination calendar collapsed. Arrayed against the protests, and less visible to the metropolitan press, were pro-Mandal rallies by backward-class organisations and the parties that spoke for them, which read the same memorandum as the first instalment of a debt contracted in 1950. Any honest account must hold both mobilisations in view, because the two together are the texture of the moment: the announcement did not merely change a recruitment rule, it rearranged the visible order of who could claim the Indian state as theirs.

Running on a parallel track was the mobilisation that would decide the government's fate. L. K. Advani began his Rath Yatra from Somnath on 25 September 1990, driving toward Ayodhya in a campaign whose context is set out in [the post-1990 Rath Yatra context](/2019/10/10/advani-rath-yatra-ayodhya-movement/), and on 23 October 1990 the Bihar government of Lalu Prasad Yadav had him arrested at Samastipur. The Bharatiya Janata Party withdrew its support the same day. V.P. Singh lost the confidence of the Lok Sabha on 7 November 1990 and resigned, having held office for eleven months. Chandra Shekhar succeeded him on 10 November 1990 at the head of a breakaway faction sustained by Congress support, and his government let the reservation stand while the Supreme Court heard the challenge that the advocate Indra Sawhney and others had filed against the memoranda. The general election of May and June 1991, interrupted by the assassination of Rajiv Gandhi, returned the Congress as the largest party with 232 seats, and P. V. Narasimha Rao became prime minister on 21 June 1991. His government neither repealed Mandal nor froze it. The modifying office memorandum of 25 September 1991 retained the 27 percent, added a 10 percent reservation for economically weaker sections among those not covered by any existing scheme, and directed that within the backward-class quota the poorer sections receive preference, an attempt to answer the Court's anticipated objections before the Court spoke.

### How long did the V.P. Singh government last after the Mandal announcement?

The government survived the announcement by three months. Advani's arrest at Samastipur on 23 October 1990 ended Bharatiya Janata Party support, and the Lok Sabha withdrew its confidence on 7 November 1990, eleven months after V.P. Singh took office in December 1989.

The government that announced the implementation thus fell in the implementation's first season, while the policy it had announced survived it under two successor governments of opposed parties.

The Court spoke on 16 November 1992. A nine-judge bench in Indra Sawhney v Union of India upheld, by a majority of six to three, the constitutionality of reservation for backward classes in the services of the Union, and in doing so converted a political conflagration into constitutional doctrine. The majority, in the judgment delivered by Justice B. P. Jeevan Reddy, held that caste could serve as a starting point for identifying a backward class, that the 27 percent reservation was valid, that total reservation should ordinarily not exceed 50 percent, that the socially advanced persons within a backward class, the creamy layer, must be excluded, that reservation in promotions was impermissible, and that the 10 percent quota for economically weaker sections added in 1991 was unconstitutional. The verdict disappointed the maximalists on both sides and settled the matter against both: the anti-Mandal litigants lost the proposition that caste-based reservation was itself unconstitutional, and the pro-Mandal governments lost the proposition that the share could follow population without a ceiling. With the verdict, the Mandal moment acquired its third institutional form: after the commission report of 1980 and the executive memoranda of 1990 and 1991, a constitutional settlement, delivered by the largest bench the question had ever received.

## September 1990: The Two Publics of the Mandal Season

The autumn of 1990 presented the republic with two crowds claiming to be its conscience, and the readings of Mandal divide partly according to which crowd the reader stood with. The first crowd is the one the cameras kept: students of the universities and the professional colleges, aspirants to the very services the memorandum had just divided, marching in Delhi and the provincial capitals under the vocabulary of merit. Its emblematic act was self-immolation, Rajiv Goswami's on 19 September 1990 the first and the most reported, and its emblematic institution was the elite campus, the University of Delhi above all, where the agitation shut teaching for weeks. The second crowd assembled in the district towns of Bihar and Uttar Pradesh and in the rallies of the Janata Dal and the Left parties: backward-class students, cultivators' sons, party workers of the new assertion, marching under the vocabulary of social justice for a decision they described as the republic's debt finally acknowledged. Its emblematic acts were the counter-rallies that the metropolitan press covered thinly, and its emblematic figure was not a protester at all but a chief minister, Lalu Prasad Yadav in Patna, who treated the memorandum's defence as his government's founding purpose and arrested the Rath Yatra's leader on his state's soil to underline the point.

The two publics did not merely disagree about a recruitment rule; they disagreed about what the Indian state was for and whose it was, and each experienced the other as an occupying power. The anti-Mandal public spoke of a nation of merit being partitioned by birth, and experienced the 27 percent as expropriation of a future its families had planned around examinations. The pro-Mandal public spoke of a state that had been captured at birth by the twice-born and administered in their accent for forty years, and experienced the same 27 percent as a modest repossession. Yogendra Yadav, writing in the season and returning to it in later essays, charged the metropolitan press with reporting the first public as the country and the second as a mob, a charge media analysts have since examined in the coverage of the English-language dailies of September and October 1990 and found substantially borne out in column inches and in framing. The asymmetry of coverage matters to this article's adjudication for a precise reason: the first reading of Mandal to harden into common sense, the reading of a nation vandalised by caste, was produced inside that asymmetry, and a hub article that failed to record the second public would repeat the original distortion.

The death toll of the season remains, fittingly and grimly, a disputed fact, and the dispute is itself evidence of how completely the two publics failed to share a record. Official statements minimised the self-immolation deaths; the agitation's chroniclers counted scores of attempts and a toll in the dozens; hospitals in Delhi treated burn cases through October whose political meaning each side narrated in opposite directions. What is not disputed is the sequence's end. The street did not repeal the memorandum. The anti-Mandal mobilisation, for all its intensity in the capital, could not assemble a parliamentary majority for repeal, and the political class that had sympathised with it in September discovered by the election of 1991 that the second public voted in larger numbers than the first. The conflagration of 1990 settled, by demonstration, the question that the shelving decade had left open: whether any government could implement Mandal and survive. The answer delivered by events was subtler than either crowd expected. The government that implemented it fell, on an allied question, within three months. The implementation did not fall, then or under any successor, which is the difference between a ministry and a settlement, and the difference on which this article's claim rests.

## The Yadav Reading: Mandal as Democratic Deepening

Yogendra Yadav's reading was built across the 1990s in essays in the Economic and Political Weekly, including his analysis of the state assembly elections of 1993 to 1995, and stated in canonical form in his essay on the second democratic upsurge at the end of that decade, later gathered into his Making Sense of Indian Democracy in 2020. The reading begins from a distinction between the two upsurges of Indian democracy. The first democratic upsurge, of the 1960s, had drawn the intermediate castes into electoral politics as voters and as clients of the Congress system. The second, which Yadav dated from the closing years of the 1980s, was an upsurge of assertion: the plebeian orders of Indian society, the backward classes, the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes, entered politics not as vote banks managed from above but as claimants organising on their own account, with their own parties, their own leaders drawn from their own communities, and their own vocabulary of self-respect and share. On Yadav's account, Mandal was the hinge of that upsurge, the act that converted a social fact, the numerical weight of the backward classes, into a recognised political title.

The evidence Yadav marshalled was electoral and representational before it was programmatic. Election studies conducted by the Centre for the Study of Developing Societies, with which Yadav was centrally associated, documented through the 1990s that turnout among Dalit, Adivasi and backward-class voters, historically below the turnout of the privileged, had risen to match and in places surpass the turnout of the upper castes, an inversion of the participation gradient that had held since Independence. The descriptive composition of legislatures moved in the same direction, a movement tracked constituency by constituency in [the post-1990 OBC-political-consolidation laboratory](/2020/01/06/uttar-pradesh-bellwether-laboratory/) of Uttar Pradesh and, at the parliamentary level, in the figures Jaffrelot assembled and this article uses below: among Lok Sabha members returned from the Hindi belt, the backward-class share doubled from 11.1 percent in 1984 to 20.9 percent in 1989 and continued climbing through the 1990s, while the upper-caste share fell below 40 percent in 1989 for the first time in the republic's history. Yadav's reading treats such numbers not as decoration but as the substance of democracy itself: a political order in which the governed increasingly resemble the governors has undergone a democratic change of kind, not merely of personnel.

The reading's strongest form insists on the direction of causation that the chronology supports. The surge in backward-class representation in the 1989 Parliament preceded the implementation announcement by nine months; the Janata Dal had distributed tickets to backward-class candidates at a rate no national party had attempted, and V.P. Singh's mandate, whatever else it was, was delivered substantially by that mobilisation. Mandal on this account did not manufacture a constituency. It answered one, and then it armed that constituency with a durable institutional claim: a reserved share of the state's own employment, won in Parliament in 1990 and entrenched by the Supreme Court in 1992, which no subsequent government of any party has dared to withdraw. The post-1990 careers of backward-class leadership in the two largest states give the reading its narrative flesh. In Bihar, Lalu Prasad Yadav, a leader whose authority rested on the Yadav and wider backward-class base the state had organised since the Karpoori Thakur years, held the chief ministership from 1990 and made Patna the capital of a politics of dignity whose programmatic limits were real and whose representational rupture was equally real, a career examined in the pillar's study of [Bihar's Mandal politics](/2020/01/12/bihar-mandal-politics-reanalysed/). In Uttar Pradesh, Mulayam Singh Yadav founded the Samajwadi Party on 4 October 1992 and built it into the state's principal alternative pole. For the democratisation reading, these are not regional footnotes. They are the upsurge institutionalised, the evidence that after 1990 the backward classes of the Hindi belt possessed parties of their own that no Delhi leadership could summon or dismiss.

The upsurge had a second institutional theatre that the democratisation reading rightly claims: local government. The Constitution (Seventy-third Amendment) Act of 1992 and its companion for municipalities, passed in the same parliamentary season in which the Supreme Court settled Mandal, rebuilt the panchayats and municipalities as elected institutions and, through Articles 243D and 243T, empowered state legislatures to reserve seats and chairperson offices in them for backward classes, a power the states used extensively through the 1990s. The conjunction is not decorative. At the very moment the Union's services were opened by quota at the top, the village republic was opened by election and reservation at the base, and the two movements fed one another: the panchayat became the training ground and the first prize of the backward-class assertion that Mandal had licensed at the summit, and the sarpanch of 1995 was frequently the legislative candidate of 2005. Yadav's framework, which measures democratisation by who actually enters the rooms where decisions are taken, counts the panchayat revolution as part of the same wave, and the counting is just: the second upsurge is visible in the rosters of a million local offices as plainly as in the caste tables of the Lok Sabha.

Yadav's reading also carries a normative edge that its author does not disguise, and stating the reading at its strongest requires stating the edge. The argument holds that the quality of a democracy is mismeasured when it is assessed by the smoothness of its elite circulation, and that the turbulence of the 1990s, the unstable coalitions, the assertive caste parties, the noisy claim-making, was the sound of the democratic promise being cashed by those to whom it had been longest deferred. On this view, the objection that Mandal politicised caste mistakes the sequence: caste had always structured access to office and education; Mandal politicised the structure itself, making it contestable in the open. The reading concedes the fragmentation facts that Chhibber assembles and replies that fragmentation was the institutional price, and partly the institutional form, of inclusion arriving faster than the old parties could absorb it. Where the reading is vulnerable is in its treatment of outcomes beneath representation: the upsurge changed who ruled more readily than it changed schools, hospitals or land relations in the backward-class countryside, and the distance between descriptive representation and material transformation is the space its critics occupy. That vulnerability is registered here and taken up in the adjudication.

## The Chhibber Reading: Mandal as Party-System Fragmentation

Pradeep Chhibber's Democracy without Associations, published in 1999, approached the 1990s from the opposite end: not from the society pressing upward but from the party system failing to organise what pressed. Chhibber's central claim was that Indian parties, the Congress above all, had allowed their organisational and associational infrastructures to decay: parties neither enrolled members in depth nor anchored themselves in the unions, cooperatives, caste associations and civic bodies through which interests are aggregated and compromises pre-negotiated. A party system so hollowed could not absorb new claimants into existing structures. New claimants therefore built parties of their own, and the claims arrived in the legislature unaggregated, as the demands of single communities and single states, to be settled by coalition bargaining rather than by programme. Mandal enters this account as the great accelerant. By making caste the explicit currency of one of the Union's largest distributive decisions, the implementation of 1990 raised the return on organising politically as a caste, and it did so just as the old national parties lost the capacity to mediate among castes within a single tent.

The evidence for the fragmentation reading is the starkest in the entire debate, because it can be read off the election results. Between the general election of 1984, in which the Congress won 404 seats, and the general election of 2014, no single party won a majority in the Lok Sabha. The elections of 1989, 1991, 1996, 1998, 1999, 2004 and 2009 produced hung parliaments and coalition or minority governments of every stripe: the National Front of 1989, the thirteen-day Bharatiya Janata Party government and then the United Front of 1996 and 1997, the National Democratic Alliance governments of 1998 and 1999, and the United Progressive Alliance governments of 2004 and 2009, the whole span forming [the coalition era](/2019/05/15/coalition-era-1989-2014-explained/) that a companion study in this series reconstructs. The party system regionalised as it fragmented. The Janata Dal, the vehicle of 1989, disintegrated through the 1990s into state parties: the Samajwadi Party in Uttar Pradesh in 1992, the Samata Party in Bihar in 1994, the Rashtriya Janata Dal in 1997 after Lalu Prasad Yadav's expulsion from the Janata Dal, the Biju Janata Dal in Odisha in 1997, and the Janata Dal (United) and Janata Dal (Secular) by 1999. Each successor held a state, a caste coalition and a veto in Delhi. Chhibber's reading explains this pattern as the predictable output of Mandal's incentive structure meeting organisational decay: when the state distributes by community and no encompassing party can aggregate communities, politics reorganises at the scale at which communities can win, which in India is the state and the caste bloc.

Stated at its strongest, the fragmentation reading is not a lament for the Congress system. Chhibber's argument is institutional, not nostalgic: aggregation is a function democracies require from somewhere, and where parties do not perform it, the performance migrates to coalition managers, to courts and to the street. The reading can also absorb the democratisation evidence without strain, since nothing in the fragmentation account denies that the fragmenting parties carried new social groups into power; its claim is about the form their arrival took and the governability costs of that form. Its predictive edge was real: a reader of Democracy without Associations in 1999 would not have been surprised by the coalition decades that followed, nor by the premium the system placed on state-level satraps. Where the reading is exposed is at its point of origin. Fragmentation of the national party system predates the Mandal implementation by at least a year in the verdict of 1989 and by two decades in the Congress's organisational decline, and the causal weight the reading assigns to a single distributive decision can be questioned: Mandal may have named and accelerated a fragmentation whose drivers, the decay Chhibber himself documented, were already in motion. The reading must therefore present Mandal as catalyst rather than cause, and the adjudication below holds it to that formulation, which is also the formulation its own evidence best supports.

Chhibber's later collaborative work sharpened the mechanism. In his 2004 book with Ken Kollman, on how national party systems take shape, the argument became comparative and conditional: party systems aggregate nationally where political authority is centralised, and fragment along regional lines where authority and the salient distributive questions migrate to the states. India of the 1990s was the theory's exhibit. As economic and distributive initiative passed downward and as the great national questions of the founding era closed, the incentives facing any ambitious politician pointed to the state arena and the state-sized coalition, and Mandal, by locating its benefits and its beneficiary lists substantially in state politics even while the Union acted, fed the migration. The fragmentation reading thus explains a fact the democratisation reading leaves untouched: why the new claimants built state parties that endured for decades rather than factions that a recovering national party might reabsorb, as the Congress had reabsorbed claimants in the 1950s. The answer, that there was no longer an encompassing organisation with the associational depth to do the absorbing, is Chhibber's most durable contribution, and it converts Mandal from a distributive episode into a constitutional fact of the party system.

The two readings thus far set the terms of a genuine argument. Yadav's democrats and Chhibber's institutionalists look at the same decade and see, respectively, a society arriving and a system dissolving. Both are describing the post-1989 order accurately from their chosen level of analysis, and the appearance of contradiction arises when either claims the whole. The third reading introduces a different protagonist altogether: the party that was neither the author of Mandal nor its intended beneficiary, and that converted the moment into its own ascent.

## The Jaffrelot Reading: The Silent Revolution and the Hindutva Opening

Christophe Jaffrelot's account, built in The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics in 1996 and completed in India's Silent Revolution: The Rise of the Lower Castes in North India in 2003, contains the other two readings and adds a third element neither possesses. The silent revolution is Jaffrelot's name for the long transfer of political power in north India from the upper castes to the lower: silent because it proceeded without insurrection, through the ballot box and the slow sociology of candidacies, and a revolution because it displaced a social order that had governed the region since before Independence. Jaffrelot documented the transfer with a prosopographer's patience, caste-coding the members of the Lok Sabha and the state assemblies across five decades. The figures bear repeating in his terms. From the Hindi belt, upper-caste representation in the Lok Sabha stood above 50 percent through the 1970s and fell to 48.2 percent in 1977, to 38.2 percent in 1989; backward-class representation doubled from 11.1 percent in 1984 to 20.9 percent in 1989 and rose onward to a peak of roughly a quarter of the House in the following decade. In Bihar the shift was violent in speed: backward-class members of Parliament from the state rose from 17 percent in 1984 to 43 percent in 1996. In the Uttar Pradesh assembly, upper-caste members fell from 58 percent in 1962 to 37.7 percent in 1996, while backward-class members rose from 9 percent to 30 percent by 1993. On Jaffrelot's showing, Mandal did not start this revolution. The verdict of 1989 had already registered its parliamentary breakthrough. Mandal legitimised it, extended it into the bureaucracy, and above all announced it, converting a drift that elites could treat as temporary into a settlement they had to treat as permanent.

It is the second movement of Jaffrelot's argument that distinguishes his reading: the Hindu nationalist response. The Bharatiya Janata Party and the wider Sangh Parivar read the Mandal moment as an existential division of the Hindu electorate along caste lines, and answered it with the one appeal that could override caste division, religious consolidation around Ayodhya. The temporal interlock is the reading's central exhibit: the Rath Yatra of September and October 1990 travelled through a country in which the Mandal protests were still burning, and the party's strategists said openly that Mandir was the reply to Mandal, the reconstruction of a Hindu unity that backward-class assertion had fractured. The upper-caste anxiety that the anti-Mandal agitation expressed, the fear of a permanently diminished share in the state's employment and honours, was available for mobilisation by a movement that redirected status resentment toward a religious adversary and a unifying shrine. The electoral sequence followed: the Bharatiya Janata Party rose from 2 seats in 1984 to 85 in 1989, and after the double mobilisation of Mandal and Mandir, to 120 seats in 1991 and 161 in 1996, becoming within six years of the implementation the largest party in the Lok Sabha.

### How many seats did the Bharatiya Janata Party win in the 1991 Lok Sabha election?

The Bharatiya Janata Party won 120 seats in the general election of 1991, up from 85 in 1989 and 2 in 1984, on a campaign run in the immediate aftermath of the Rath Yatra and the Mandal implementation. The same election made it the ruling party of Uttar Pradesh under Kalyan Singh.

Jaffrelot's reading, stated at its strongest, refuses the sentimental version of its own thesis in two ways. First, the silent revolution was socially uneven: its principal beneficiaries in the north were the dominant backward castes, Yadavs above all, and the most backward classes and the Scheduled Castes advanced more slowly and often in rivalry with the new backward-class power, a stratification inside the revolution that the creamy-layer doctrine and L. R. Naik's forgotten note both anticipated. Second, the Hindutva response was never only a reaction of upper-caste anxiety. The Bharatiya Janata Party practised its own social engineering through the 1990s, elevating backward-class leaders such as Kalyan Singh in Uttar Pradesh and Uma Bharti in Madhya Pradesh, and competing for the very electorate Mandal had organised; the party's ascent is therefore not explicable as a pure upper-caste restoration, and Jaffrelot's own text does not claim that it is. The reading's claim is narrower and stronger: Mandal created the strategic field on which Hindu nationalism had to reinvent itself as a cross-caste project, and the reinvention succeeded because the caste order Mandal consolidated gave the Sangh both its problem and its programme. Where the reading can be pressed is on the autonomy it grants to strategy over structure: the Ayodhya movement had its own organisational history reaching back through the 1980s, and a sceptic can argue that Hindutva would have surged on its own timetable without the Mandal provocation. The adjudication must weigh that counterfactual, and it is the hardest of the three to settle, because the evidence is temporal coincidence fortified by the strategists' own admissions.

## The Southern Contrast: The Revolution That Arrived Early

Jaffrelot's title carries a geography that the hub must honour: the silent revolution he chronicled was the rise of the lower castes in north India, and the qualifier matters because the south of the country had undergone its transfer of power generations earlier, by a different road and without a Mandal. In the Madras Presidency, the Justice Party, founded in 1916 as the South Indian Liberal Federation, built the first non-Brahmin government of the presidency and issued the communal government orders of the early 1920s that distributed public employment among communities by quota, the direct ancestors of the Tamil Nadu reservation regime whose career the state record section of this article traced. Periyar E. V. Ramasamy's Self-Respect Movement, launched in 1925, gave the transfer its ideology of caste annihilation and rationalist self-respect, and the Dravidian parties that inherited the movement ended Congress rule in Tamil Nadu in 1967 and never returned it. In the princely state of Kolhapur, Chhatrapati Shahu had reserved half of state service posts for the backward communities as early as 1902, acting on the non-Brahmin politics that Jotirao Phule's Satyashodhak Samaj, founded in 1873, had first organised in Maharashtra. In Karnataka, the politics of the two dominant peasant communities and Devaraj Urs's deliberate backward-class coalition-building in the 1970s redistributed power a decade before Delhi moved. Kerala's caste associations, the Sree Narayana Dharma Paripalana Yogam founded in 1903 in the inspiration of Narayana Guru and the Nair Service Society founded in 1914, had organised community claims on the state before the republic existed.

The contrast explains the moment's geography, and the geography refines the framework. Where the transfer of power to the lower orders had already occurred through provincial movements and state quotas, Mandal arrived as confirmation and extension: Tamil Nadu's governments of every Dravidian shade defended the principle the state had practised for seventy years, and the 69 percent regime needed no rescue from Delhi's example. Where the transfer had been blocked, in the Hindi belt above all, by a Congress system whose brokerage kept backward-class assertion inside an upper-caste-led order, Mandal arrived as detonation, because the claim it carried had accumulated for four decades without a provincial vent. The unevenness is not an embarrassment for the decisive-moment framework; it is one of its predictions. A reframe is decisive where it breaks a blockage, and the same national act registers as earthquake in Patna and as weather in Madras because the two cities stood at different points of the same long process. Yadav's upsurge framework makes the identical point in temporal form: the second democratic upsurge was a northern event of the late 1980s and 1990s partly because the south's plebeian assertion had run its first course in the 1950s and 1960s, in the Dravidian movement's capture of Madras and the linguistic states' first ministries.

The southern record also supplies the framework's control case for the counter-arguments weighed in the adjudication. Tamil Nadu administered the republic's deepest reservation regime for the entire post-Independence period without the administrative collapse the merit argument predicts, while building, across the same decades, one of India's most industrialised state economies and a public health and education infrastructure that richer and less reserved states measured themselves against. Karnataka's and Kerala's trajectories differ in detail and converge in the same refusal to corroborate the prophecy of ruin. None of this settles the design questions, of shares, ceilings and creamy layers, that the Indra Sawhney bench and the sub-categorisation inquiries exist to argue. It settles the prior question on which the 1990 street argument was fought and lost: whether a state that reserves on this scale can govern at all. The south had answered that question in the affirmative before the question was put to the Union, and Mandal, in the framework defended here, is the moment the Union's answer finally aligned with the answer its own southern states had been giving for half a century.

## The Counter-Argument at Full Strength: Merit, Entrenchment and Capture

An adjudication that never faces the case against its subject is advocacy in judicial dress, and the case against Mandal, made on the streets in 1990 and in the seminar rooms across the decade, had three heads, each of which must be stated as its holders stated it before it is weighed. The first head was merit. The anti-Mandal agitation's central claim, repeated in its pamphlets and its court pleadings, was that reservation in the services of the Union traded competence for birth: that a state which fills its posts by category will be administered worse, that the beneficiaries themselves are stigmatised as quota appointees regardless of their ability, and that the truly injured party is the poor applicant from an unreserved caste whose marks exceed those of the beneficiary who displaces him. The argument drew strength from an intuition widely shared in the professional middle class that examinations are neutral measuring instruments, and it reached the Supreme Court as a constitutional claim under the efficiency clause of Article 335 by analogy and the equality code of Articles 14 and 16.

The second head was entrenchment, and Béteille again supplied its most considered statement. A republic that aspired, in its founding documents, to the eventual irrelevance of caste had, on this argument, chosen the one policy guaranteed to make caste permanent: by attaching the state's largest distributive benefits to caste membership, Mandal taught every community to organise as a caste, to police its boundaries, to resist the intermarriage and occupational drift by which caste might otherwise have thinned, and to convert legislatures into auctions of communal claims. The policy thus perpetuated the disease in the act of compensating for it, and its defenders were mistaking the organisation of caste for the emancipation of the castes' members. The third head was capture, pressed from within the backward-class camp as much as from outside it: that the benefits of any caste-based quota flow uphill within the category, to the advanced, landholding, politically organised backward castes, and within them to their urban, educated strata, leaving the most backward exactly where they stood. L. R. Naik's dissenting note of 1980 is the capture argument's founding document, and the long queue of most-backward grievance that produced sub-categorisation movements in state after state is its continuing evidence.

Weighed in the adjudication's scales, the three heads fare differently. The capture argument is the strongest, because the settlement's own custodians conceded it in practice: the Supreme Court wrote the creamy layer into the doctrine in 1992 precisely to strip the advanced stratum from the benefit, and the sub-categorisation question that a later study in this pillar examines is the capture argument institutionalised as a reform agenda. A framework that absorbs its best objection as an internal amendment is strengthened, not weakened, and the decisive-moment reading does exactly that: capture describes a pathology of the settlement's operation, not a refutation of the reframe that created it. The entrenchment argument identifies a real mechanism and overstates its counterfactual. Caste organised Indian politics before Mandal because caste organised Indian life chances before Mandal; the choice the republic faced in 1990 was not between caste politics and castelessness but between a caste order that distributed office by inertia to its summit and one that distributed a defended share downward by law. That the downward distribution entrenched the categories it used is a cost the argument is entitled to record; that the summit's quieter arrangement was category-free is a premise the Mandal representation tables refute.

The merit argument is the weakest of the three at the level of evidence, however powerful its hold on the imagination of 1990. Reservation under the 1990 memorandum and the 1992 doctrine operated within minimum qualifications for each post; no unqualified candidate was appointed to any office, and the services of the Union, staffed for three decades since under the settled regime, exhibit no collapse of function that the argument's logic predicts and its holders have never documented with the regime's own output measures. The deeper premise, that examination marks measure an aptitude independent of inherited advantage, was the very proposition the Mandal report's opening philosophy contested, and Galanter's research had already shown how the unreserved competition of the pre-1990 decades reproduced the social composition of the examining classes. The majority in Indra Sawhney addressed the efficiency concern in terms, held that Article 16(4) was itself part of the equality code rather than an exception devouring it, and capped the experiment at half the posts. What remains of the merit argument after the evidence is heard is a question about the proper size and design of the share, which is the capture argument's territory again. The counter-case, taken whole, therefore refines the settlement it failed to prevent, and the history of the decades since bears the refinement out: political argument migrated from repeal to repair, from whether Mandal to how Mandal, which is the migration this article's framework predicts for a reframe that has taken hold.

## The Adjudication: Three Dimensions of One Reframe

The three readings can at this point be placed side by side, and the first thing the placement shows is that they rarely contradict one another on any proposition that all three actually assert. Yadav asserts that after 1990 the backward classes held a larger and more autonomous share of political power, and that the change was democratic in character. Chhibber asserts that after 1989 the party system lost its national aggregating capacity and reorganised regionally, and that Mandal raised the returns to caste-based party formation. Jaffrelot asserts that power's social composition shifted durably toward the lower castes in the north, and that Hindu nationalism grew by answering the division Mandal deepened. A democratisation of personnel, a fragmentation of organisation and a strategic reinvention of the opposition are not rival descriptions of one variable. They are descriptions of three variables that happened to move together, and the analytical error the decisive-moment framework corrects is the habit of forcing them onto a single scale, as though Mandal had to be judged, in the end, as good or bad, unifying or divisive, progressive or regressive, on one axis of appraisal.

The complementarity can be made precise dimension by dimension. On the substantive dimension, Yadav is right in the way the evidence compels: the doubling of backward-class parliamentary representation from the Hindi belt in 1989, the entrenchment of a reserved share in Union employment that survived three governments and a nine-judge bench, and the installation of backward-class leadership in the two largest states constitute a change in what Indian democracy delivered to its largest constituency, and no account that treats this as surface noise survives contact with the figures. On the institutional dimension, Chhibber is right with the qualification his own evidence imposes: the party system did fragment along the lines Mandal incentivised, the Janata Dal's successor parties are the organisational fossils of that incentive, and the coalition decades that followed are unintelligible without it; but fragmentation's organisational drivers predate the implementation, so Mandal stands as catalyst and legitimator of the fragmentation, not its sole author. On the strategic dimension, Jaffrelot is right in the strong form his evidence supports and the weak form must be refused: the Bharatiya Janata Party's rise from 85 seats in 1989 to 161 in 1996 was not caused by Mandal in the sense of a sufficient condition, since the Ayodhya mobilisation and the party's organisation had independent histories; but Mandal set the problem to which the party's 1990s strategy was the answer, the problem of a Hindu electorate sorting itself by caste, and the party's own conduct in the Mandal season, the Yatra's timing, the withdrawal of support, the subsequent social engineering of candidacies, is the behaviour of an organisation that understood itself to be answering that problem.

Table 1 states the complementarity in its sharpest available form, and one feature of the table deserves emphasis: no row of it can be deleted without leaving the others unexplained. Delete Yadav's row, and the fragmentation reading loses the claimant whose arrival fragmented the system, while the Hindutva reading loses the assertion to which the Yatra responded. Delete Chhibber's row, and the democratisation arrives without an account of why it produced state parties and coalition vetoes rather than a renewed national party, while the Hindutva reading loses the vacuum into which a disciplined cadre party expanded. Delete Jaffrelot's row, and the other two cannot explain the largest single beneficiary of the decade, the party that won the 1990s without being either Mandal's author or its intended heir. A framework is vindicated differently from a reading: each reading is vindicated by its own evidence, while the framework that orders them is vindicated by the mutual dependence the table displays. That mutual dependence is what the word reframe means in the claim this article defends. The post-Independence settlement had distributed recognition through a Congress-brokered hierarchy in which the backward classes were promised eventual inclusion and delivered gradual patronage. The Mandal moment replaced the promise with a title, the patronage with a share, and the brokerage with direct organisation, and every institution that had lived off the old distribution, the national party system and the Hindu nationalist project included, had to be rebuilt around the replacement.

A framework that cannot say what would count against it is a mood, and the three readings can each be given a falsifying test that this article's evidence happens to pass. The democratisation reading would fail if backward-class representation in Parliament and the assemblies had subsided to its pre-1989 levels once the agitation cooled; the caste tables show a ratchet instead, with the 1989 doubling consolidated through the 1990s and never surrendered. The fragmentation reading would fail if a national party had re-aggregated the system on the old encompassing model within a decade, restoring single-party majority government by the brokerage the Congress once practised; the verdicts of 1996 to 2009 show coalition and minority governments of every combination instead, and the majority that eventually returned in 2014 was assembled by a cadre party on a post-Mandal caste arithmetic, not by the old brokerage restored. The Hindutva-enabling reading would fail if the Bharatiya Janata Party's growth had proceeded independently of the Mandal season, on a timetable set before August 1990; the party's own sequencing, two seats in 1984, 85 in the pre-implementation verdict of 1989, then 120 and 161 in the two verdicts that followed the double mobilisation, together with its strategists' own account of Mandir as the answer to Mandal, ties the ascent to the season too tightly for coincidence to carry. Tests stated in advance and met on the record are what distinguish an adjudication from a survey, and the complementarity claimed here is of the disciplined kind: three readings, three dimensions, three tests, one reframe.

One more body of evidence disciplines all three readings, and it is the evidence of irreversibility. Between 1992 and 2014, governments of the National Democratic Alliance and the United Progressive Alliance, led by parties that had respectively ridden the counter-mobilisation and inherited the old Congress coalition, left the 27 percent and the Indra Sawhney framework untouched. When distributive conflict over reservation returned to the centre of politics, it returned as argument about the framework's edges: sub-categorisation within the backward classes, the creamy layer's income line, the claims of groups outside the lists, and eventually the economically weaker sections amendment of 2019, each a dispute inside the Mandal settlement rather than an attempt to repeal it. A policy that its opponents administer for three decades has ceased to be a policy and become part of the order. The point will matter for the final assessment of the continuity objection, since an objector must explain not only why the moment looks like earlier episodes but why this episode, unlike the Kalelkar report of 1955 or the state quotas of the 1970s, proved impossible to reverse.

## Why a Decisive Moment and Not a Turning Point

The category itself requires defence, because Indian political commentary distributes the phrase turning point freely, and an inflated category explains nothing. A decisive moment, as this pillar uses the term, must satisfy three tests that a mere turning point need not. The first is simultaneity across dimensions: within a bounded span, the event must alter the substantive distribution of power, the institutional machinery that processes power, and the strategic situation of the principal contenders, all at once and in interaction. The second is irreversibility under opposed governments: the change must survive administration by the forces that opposed it, because a change that awaits its author's survival is a programme, not a settlement. The third is generativity: the moment must supply the questions that later politics is obliged to answer, so that subsequent decades argue in its vocabulary even when they argue against it. Measured against these tests, most celebrated turning points of the republic qualify on one test or two. The Emergency of 1975 to 1977 scores on generativity and fails irreversibility, since the electorate of 1977 reversed its author. The economic reforms of 1991 score on irreversibility and generativity, having been administered and extended by every successor government; their simultaneity is weaker, since the party system and the social distribution of office were not remade in the same season.

Mandal passes all three tests jointly, and the joint passage is the argument. Simultaneity: between August 1990 and November 1992, the substantive claim of the backward classes was conceded in the services of the Union, the party system completed its passage into the regionalised coalition form, and the Hindu nationalist project executed the strategic reinvention that carried it to plurality by 1996, each movement conditioning the others in ways the three readings document. Irreversibility: the settlement of 1992 was administered without amendment of its core by the National Democratic Alliance governments of 1998 to 2004 and the United Progressive Alliance governments of 2004 to 2014, the two formations whose founding constituencies had stood on opposite sides of the 1990 street. Generativity: the political argument of the following decades ran on Mandal's rails, through the sub-categorisation demands of the most backward classes, the litigation over the creamy layer, the extension of reservation to central educational institutions in 2006 under the Ninety-third Amendment, and the caste census demand that a later study in this pillar examines. The counterfactual buried in [the post-Mandal arithmetic break context](/2019/07/03/2014-inflection-election-reanalysed/) of the 2014 verdict makes the same point from the far side: when a single party next won a Lok Sabha majority, it did so by mastering the post-Mandal arithmetic, assembling a coalition of the backward and forward under one banner, not by restoring the arithmetic Mandal had destroyed. Generativity has a linguistic form that can be checked in the public record. After 1992, every major distributive proposal in Indian politics arrived phrased as a Mandal question, including the proposals of Mandal's opponents. The demand for sub-categorisation asks how the Mandal share is divided. The demand for a caste census asks for the Mandal measurement to be taken again. The economically weaker sections reservation of 2019 asked whether the Mandal ceiling could be crossed for a category defined against the Mandal categories. The women's reservation debates carried a sub-quota argument that is Mandal's logic applied within another reservation. Even the vocabulary settled: social justice, samajik nyay, became the obligatory phrase of party manifestos across the spectrum, used by the party that rose against the implementation as readily as by the parties the implementation created, because after the moment there was no other language in which a claim on the Indian state could be respectably made. A settlement is generative when its adversaries must speak its language to oppose it, and by that test the Mandal moment's generativity is a matter of documentary record rather than of interpretation. Decisive moments are recognised by the obligation they impose on their successors, and by that recognition Mandal has few rivals in the life of the republic.

## The Settlement Administered: 1993 to 2006 and the Second Extension

A decisive moment proves itself in the administration of its enemies, and the proof began within a year of the verdict. The Supreme Court had directed that the creamy layer be identified and excluded, and the government of Narasimha Rao appointed an expert committee under Justice Ram Nandan Prasad to define the exclusion. On the committee's criteria, the Union government issued the operative office memorandum of 8 September 1993, and the 27 percent reservation for the backward classes in central services passed from announcement into the routine machinery of rosters, vacancy registers and departmental instructions, where it has remained through every change of regime since. The National Commission for Backward Classes received statutory form in 1993 as the standing authority on the central list, converting the Commission's one-time enumeration into permanent administration: communities petition for inclusion, the Commission advises, the government notifies, and the list that began as 3,743 entries in a report became a living register of the state's recognition. The significance of this bureaucratic sedimentation is easy to miss and impossible to overstate. What had been a manifesto promise in 1989 was, by the middle 1990s, an item of establishment business, defended in court by governments of the United Front, the National Democratic Alliance and the United Progressive Alliance in turn, each inheriting the settlement as part of the furniture of the state it had campaigned to capture.

The second extension carried the settlement from employment into the commanding heights of education. The Constitution (Ninety-third Amendment) Act of 2005 inserted clause (5) into Article 15, empowering the state to make special provision for the backward classes in aided and unaided educational institutions, and the Central Educational Institutions (Reservation in Admission) Act of 2006 applied the 27 percent to the Indian Institutes of Technology, the Indian Institutes of Management and the central universities, phased in with expanded seats so that the unreserved intake would not shrink in absolute terms. The challenge reached a constitution bench in Ashoka Kumar Thakur v Union of India, and on 10 April 2008 the Supreme Court upheld the amendment and the Act, again with the creamy layer excluded. The sequence deserves emphasis for what it reveals about the settlement's centre of gravity: the fiercest political battle of Mandal's second decade was fought not over whether the backward classes held a title to the state's institutions, but over whether the title reached the institutions that manufacture the elite. The state's answer, delivered through a Congress-led government, sustained by the Court, and unresisted in office by the opposition that had ridden the anti-Mandal season, was that it did. Mandal's first life had been about who clerks for the Union and who officered it; its second life decided who would be educated at its summit, and the ratchet moved in one direction only.

The coda of the administered decades returns the argument to its edges, which is where living settlements do their arguing. In October 2017 the President appointed a commission under Justice G. Rohini to examine the sub-categorisation of the backward classes within the central list, the capture objection translated into an official inquiry into whether the 27 percent is shared with anything like equity among the thousand-plus communities it covers. In January 2019 the Constitution (One Hundred and Third Amendment) Act created a reservation of up to 10 percent for economically weaker sections among those outside every existing category, breaching, for that category, the 50 percent ceiling the Court had set in 1992, and its validity was upheld by the Supreme Court in November 2022 by a majority of three to two. Both developments are disputes inside the world Mandal made: the first asks how the backward share is divided, the second asks whether the ceiling survives contact with a claim framed in the forward castes' own name, and neither asks whether the 27 percent should exist. The hub of this pillar records the pattern without adjudicating the later questions, which belong to their own studies within the pillar, and draws from it the single inference the framework needs: three decades of administration by opposed parties, two extensions of scope, and a queue of reform proposals at the margins add up to irreversibility demonstrated, not merely asserted.

## The Inheritance in the States: The Deepening of the 1990s

The administered settlement at the centre had a wilder twin in the states, where the Mandal inheritance was fought over, deepened and institutionalised through the 1990s, and the hub must register the state record because all three readings draw their strongest confirming instances from it. Uttar Pradesh supplied the decade's emblematic sequence. In December 1993 the Samajwadi Party and the Bahujan Samaj Party, the parties of backward-class assertion and of Dalit assertion respectively, formed a government together in Lucknow under Mulayam Singh Yadav, an alliance of the very constituencies the Mandal moment had organised, governing the republic's largest state on an explicit social-justice programme. The alliance's collapse in June 1995, in the violence and recrimination of the Lucknow guest house episode, and Mayawati's installation as chief minister with Bharatiya Janata Party support, taught the decade's second lesson: the new constituencies were sovereign facts that every older party, including the party of the counter-mobilisation, would court, split and serve in turn rather than confront. Through the rest of the decade Uttar Pradesh alternated among the Mandal inheritance's claimants and its Hindu nationalist answer, and no government of any composition proposed to govern the state against the backward-class arithmetic that 1990 had made permanent.

Bihar, the Commission chairman's state and the laboratory Karpoori Thakur had opened in 1978, consolidated the inheritance longest. Lalu Prasad Yadav's Rashtriya Janata Dal and its predecessor formation won the assembly elections of 1990, 1995 and 2000, and the state's politics reorganised itself for a generation around the questions Mandal posed: the backward classes in possession of the state, the most backward classes pressing their sub-claim against the dominant backward castes, and the forward castes negotiating their reduced share from opposition. The governance indictments of those years, the fodder scam prosecutions that forced Lalu Prasad's resignation in 1997 and raised Rabri Devi to the chief ministership, belong to the pillar's Bihar study; for the hub's argument the salient fact is simpler, that the settlement's political form in its heartland survived scandal, prosecution and the imprisonment of its leader without the form itself coming into question, because the constituency it organised had no alternative vehicle and every rival had to bid for the same voters on the same terrain.

The inheritance also worked inside the parties that had resisted it, which is the surest sign of a reframe completing itself. The Congress, whose central leadership had shelved the report and then administered it, spent the 1990s Mandalising its state units by necessity: Ashok Gehlot, drawn from Rajasthan's Mali community of the backward classes, became that state's chief minister in 1998, and in Madhya Pradesh the Congress government of Digvijaya Singh convened the Bhopal conference of January 2002 whose document committed the party in the state to a Dalit and backward-class agenda of land, education and representation, an exercise in catching up with a constituency the party had ceded a decade earlier. The Bharatiya Janata Party's state record ran on the parallel track Jaffrelot's reading predicts. Uma Bharti, a leader of the Lodhi backward caste, carried the party to power in Madhya Pradesh in 2003. In Gujarat, Narendra Modi of the Modh Ghanchi community, listed among the state's backward classes, became chief minister in 2001 and built there the party's longest state tenure of the era. A Hindu nationalist party that had entered the Mandal season as the refuge of the unreserved was, within thirteen years, routinely captained in the states by leaders of the classes Mandal had organised, competing for those classes' votes against the parties Mandal had created. The counter-mobilisation had not defeated the reframe; it had been absorbed by it, which is what the absorption of a counter-mobilisation looks like when a settlement, rather than a ministry, is doing the absorbing.

For the adjudication, the state record performs a specific service: it discriminates among the three readings at the level where each is weakest. Yadav's democratisation is confirmed in the states' personnel and embarrassed nowhere else; the upsurge's parties governed, badly at times, corruptly at times, but as the routine sovereigns of the largest states, which is what an upsurge that had failed would not have produced. Chhibber's fragmentation is confirmed as the delivery vehicle and complicated as a lament, since the fragmented state parties proved capable of holding power for decades and of administering the settlement with a consistency the undivided national parties had never shown toward it. Jaffrelot's double reading is confirmed in both its halves within a single state's boundaries, Uttar Pradesh above all, where the silent revolution and the Hindutva answer governed in alternation and occasionally in alliance. A moment whose consequences can be tracked, state by state and ministry by ministry, through the two decades after its closing judgment is not an artefact of commentary. It is the load-bearing fact of the period's politics, and the readings compete, where they genuinely compete, only over the name of the load it bears.

## The Continuity Objection: Kaviraj and the Long Trajectory

The strongest counter to everything argued so far comes from the direction of depth. Sudipta Kaviraj, in essays written across the 1990s on the character of Indian democracy, resisted the dramaturgy of rupture that commentary attached to Mandal, and his resistance deserves statement at full strength because it draws on the longest view any of the participants commanded. On the continuity reading, Mandal was an episode within a trajectory that began with the Constitution itself: universal franchise in a society ordered by caste guaranteed, over time, that democratic politics would be conducted in caste's idiom and would progressively transfer power toward the numerically weighty lower orders, as voters learned the uses of the ballot and as the ballot's logic worked through candidacies, parties and governments. The trajectory had registered earlier instalments long before 1990. The Dravidian movement had displaced Brahmin predominance in the Madras Presidency's successor state across the middle decades of the century. Backward-class organisation in Bihar reached state power with Karpoori Thakur in the 1970s. Kanshi Ram founded the Backward and Minority Communities Employees Federation in 1978 and the Bahujan Samaj Party in 1984, building an autonomous Dalit politics in the north before V.P. Singh had announced anything. The 1977 verdict itself, on Jaffrelot's own figures, had already pushed upper-caste parliamentary representation below half. Read from this altitude, 1990 looks less like a break than like the moment a slow river reached a bend that commentators happened to be watching.

The objection also has a methodological edge. Periodisation flatters the periodiser: to name a decisive moment is to claim that history's joints are where the analyst's knife falls, and Kaviraj's essays warn that the claim of rupture often measures the observer's surprise rather than the process's speed. The elites who wrote the first accounts of 1990 experienced Mandal as a shock because the process it announced had proceeded beneath their attention, in the districts and the state capitals; the silent in Jaffrelot's silent revolution is partly an artefact of who was listening. A framework that begins from metropolitan surprise will date the revolution to the moment the surprise occurred, which is a fact about the observers as much as about the observed.

The decisive-moment framework absorbs the objection by conceding its premise and denying its conclusion. The trajectory is real, and this article's own narrative has relied on it: the state quotas of Tamil Nadu, Karnataka and Bihar, the legal architecture assembled by 1951 and disciplined in Balaji, the parliamentary drift documented from 1977, all belong to the long movement Kaviraj describes. What the concession cannot explain away is the concentration. Trajectories do not implement themselves at the centre of the Union on a single August morning, do not ordinarily provoke a counter-mobilisation that reorders the opposition within one season, and do not usually acquire, within twenty-seven months of implementation, a nine-judge constitutional settlement that forecloses reversal by ordinary politics. The framework's claim is not that Mandal began the trajectory but that Mandal was the trajectory's point of concentration and lock-in, the span in which a tendency became a title, a title became a quota and a quota became constitutional bedrock, while simultaneously the party system and the Hindu nationalist project reorganised themselves around the fact. Continuation at the level of social process and decisiveness at the level of political settlement are compatible claims, and the second is the one a political history is obliged to test. Kaviraj's caution disciplines the framework's rhetoric; it does not dissolve the framework's object.

## Mandal and the Founding Settlement

To see what was reframed, return to the founding. The Constitution of 1950 addressed caste disadvantage through a deliberate asymmetry that [the rights-development founding tension](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) of the Constituent Assembly debates made structural: equality before the law in Articles 14 to 18 as a justiciable right, and the welfare of the weaker sections in the Directive Principles as an unenforceable charge on the state, with Articles 15(4) and 16(4) inserted as enabling exceptions that permitted, without commanding, special provision for backward classes in education and in the services. The Constituent Assembly that wrote this architecture, whose drafting was steered by the committee chaired by [B. R. Ambedkar](/2018/12/06/ambedkar-drafting-committee-constitution/), deliberately declined to enumerate the backward classes or to fix their shares. Article 340 handed the task to future presidential commissions, and the census of 1951 abandoned caste enumeration beyond the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, so that the republic undertook to remedy a condition it declined to measure. Ambedkar's statement on resigning from the Union cabinet in 1951 numbered among his grievances the failure to appoint the commission that Article 340 envisaged; the appointment came in 1953 under Kaka Kalelkar, and its report of 1955 was received by a government that found its caste-based lists unusable and let the matter rest for a quarter of a century.

Two features of the founding choices deserve emphasis because the Mandal moment reversed both. The first is a verb. Article 340 provides that the President may appoint a commission to investigate the conditions of the backward classes; where the Constitution elsewhere commands, on the holding of elections, on the appointment of finance commissions at fixed intervals, here it merely permits, and the permission slept unexercised from 1950 to 1953 and again, after the Kalelkar report was shelved, from 1955 to 1978. The second is a refusal. When the census of 1951, the first conducted by the republic, was designed, the government resolved that caste would not be enumerated beyond the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes, on the reasoning that counting caste would perpetuate it, and every census since has repeated the refusal. The republic thus deprived its largest distributive question of its elementary statistic for the whole period of the settlement, leaving the field to the 1931 ratios and to inference, a deprivation whose consequences for the Mandal evidence the earlier examination of the report's data recorded.

The post-Independence settlement, in the sense the decisive-moment claim requires, was this arrangement of permission without entitlement, measurement refused, and pace entrusted to the discretion of governments drawn substantially from the classes the arrangement was meant to correct. It held for forty years because the Congress system could broker the backward classes' claims inside its own coalitions, delivering patronage, tickets in calibrated numbers and occasional state-level quotas, while reserving the commanding heights of the Union services to a de facto order that the Mandal tables of 1980 exposed in print: 52 percent of the population holding 4.83 percent of Class I posts. Mandal reframed the settlement at each of its joints. Permission became entitlement when the 1990 memorandum converted an enabling clause into an operative share. Refused measurement became official arithmetic when the Commission's 52 percent and 3,743 named castes entered the state's own documents and survived judicial scrutiny in 1992. Discretion became doctrine when Indra Sawhney wrote the ceiling, the creamy layer and the bar on promotions into constitutional law, fixing the terms inside which all later argument must move. The reframe did not abolish the founding tensions; it re-set their balance, transferring the burden of justification from those who claimed a share to those who would deny one. That transfer, more than any election or agitation, is the decisive moment's durable core, and it is why the founding generation's unfinished business, Ambedkar's unappointed commission included, belongs inside this article's frame rather than as antiquarian preface.

## The Pillar This Hub Opens

The framework established here governs nine further studies, and the hub's duty is to state what each will take up without confiscating its argument. The pillar reaches back first to the Poona Pact of 1932, where the questions of separate electorates and reserved seats were fought between Gandhi and Ambedkar in a form the Constitution later inherited. It then examines the constitutional reservation framework as a working system, the articles, commissions and extensions through which the founding permission was administered for four decades. The First Backward Classes Commission under Kaka Kalelkar receives its own study, because the deferral of its 1955 report is the founding settlement's clearest self-portrait. The Mandal Commission's report of 1980 is then read in full, as a text with indicators, arithmetic and a dissenting note, rather than as the slogan it became. The implementation protests of 1990 follow, the street season in which the memorandum met its opposition and its defenders. The post-Mandal party system is reanalysed as a system, asking what kind of order the fragmented pieces compose. The sub-categorisation question within the Scheduled Castes, carried into the courts and decided in a new form by the Supreme Court in 2024, tests the settlement's internal equity. The economically weaker sections reservation introduced by the amendment of 2019 tests its ceiling, being the first reservation in the republic's history addressed to the unreserved. And the debate over a caste census closes the pillar by returning to the founding generation's refused measurement, since a state that distributes by caste while declining to count caste argues with one hand tied. Each study applies the three-reading discipline in its own territory: every claim anchored to a named text, every contested characterisation attributed to its holder at full strength, every settled fact left plain.

## The Claim Restated

Strip the moment to its dates and it is a commission appointed in December 1978, a report delivered on 31 December 1980, an announcement on 7 August 1990, a government fallen on 7 November 1990 and a judgment on 16 November 1992. Strip it to its numbers and it is 3,743 castes, 52 percent of a population, 27 percent of posts, 4.83 percent of Class I office held against that majority, and a ceiling of 50 percent that a nine-judge bench made law. Neither the dates nor the numbers say what kind of thing happened, which is why the republic's scholarship produced three readings of stature, each true to its evidence and each incomplete alone. The democratisation reading is true of the citizens Mandal made. The fragmentation reading is true of the party system Mandal unmade. The Hindutva-enabling reading is true of the opposition Mandal remade. The decisive-moment framework is the claim that these truths are one truth viewed along three dimensions, and that their simultaneity, irreversibility and generativity mark the Mandal moment as the reframe of the post-Independence settlement rather than an episode inside it. The pillar that follows will test that claim against nine territories of evidence, from a prison fast in 1932 to the caste census demanded in the 2020s; the claim is stated here so that the tests have something definite to test.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the Mandal Commission?

The Mandal Commission is the Second Backward Classes Commission, appointed under Article 340 of the Constitution by the Janata Party government of Prime Minister Morarji Desai, announced in December 1978 and formally constituted on 1 January 1979 under the chairmanship of Bindeshwari Prasad Mandal, a former chief minister of Bihar. Its terms of reference required it to determine the criteria for defining socially and educationally backward classes, to recommend steps for their advancement, and to examine reservation in government services and educational institutions. The Commission adopted eleven indicators of backwardness across social, educational and economic heads, conducted a field survey covering 405 districts, and submitted its report on 31 December 1980, identifying 3,743 castes as backward, estimating their population at 52 percent, and recommending 27 percent reservation in the services and educational institutions of the Union.

### Q: How did V.P. Singh's 1989 mandate shape regional politics?

V.P. Singh's mandate in the general election of 1989 was assembled substantially on backward-class support: the Janata Dal, which won 143 seats, distributed tickets to backward-class candidates at a rate no national party had attempted, and the National Front's state alliances carried the claim into the regions. The mandate demonstrated that a Union government could be made on an explicitly backward-class appeal, and V.P. Singh's implementation of Mandal in August 1990 then pushed the politics of that appeal downward into the states, where it proved most durable. When the Janata Dal fragmented through the 1990s, its successor parties, the Samajwadi Party in Uttar Pradesh, the Rashtriya Janata Dal in Bihar and their counterparts elsewhere, inherited state-sized pieces of the 1989 coalition and made regional capitals, rather than Delhi, the principal arena of backward-class politics for a generation.

### Q: When was Mandal implemented?

Mandal was implemented in three stages, and the date depends on which stage is meant. The decision was announced by Prime Minister V.P. Singh in the Lok Sabha on 7 August 1990 and given administrative form by the office memorandum of 13 August 1990, reserving 27 percent of vacancies in the civil services and public undertakings of the Union for the backward classes. The Narasimha Rao government modified the memorandum in September 1991, and the Supreme Court upheld the reservation, with the creamy layer excluded and a 50 percent ceiling, in Indra Sawhney v Union of India on 16 November 1992. The operative memorandum that put the scheme into routine recruitment, applying the creamy-layer criteria of the Ram Nandan Prasad committee, was issued on 8 September 1993. The reservation reached central educational institutions later, under the Act of 2006.

### Q: What is the OBC reservation?

The OBC reservation is the reservation of 27 percent of posts in the services and public undertakings of the Union, and of seats in central educational institutions, for citizens belonging to the Other Backward Classes, the castes and communities notified in the central list maintained on the advice of the National Commission for Backward Classes. It rests constitutionally on Article 16(4) for employment and Articles 15(4) and 15(5) for education, which empower the state to make special provision for socially and educationally backward classes. The 27 percent sits alongside 15 percent for the Scheduled Castes and 7.5 percent for the Scheduled Tribes, a total of 49.5 percent, inside the 50 percent ceiling the Supreme Court laid down in 1992. The socially advanced creamy layer within each listed caste is excluded. States administer their own lists and shares, which differ from the central scheme.

### Q: What was the Indra Sawhney verdict?

The Indra Sawhney verdict is the judgment of a nine-judge bench of the Supreme Court in Indra Sawhney v Union of India, delivered on 16 November 1992, which settled the constitutional law of backward-class reservation. By a majority of six to three, in the lead judgment of Justice B. P. Jeevan Reddy, the Court upheld the 27 percent reservation for the Other Backward Classes in the services of the Union as a valid exercise of Article 16(4). The Court held that caste could serve as a starting point for identifying a backward class, that total reservation should ordinarily not exceed 50 percent, that the creamy layer, the socially advanced persons within a backward class, must be excluded from the benefit, that reservation in promotions was impermissible, and that the additional 10 percent quota for economically weaker sections introduced in 1991 was unconstitutional. The verdict disappointed the maximal claims of both sides and has governed the subject since.

### Q: Why are there three readings of Mandal?

There are three readings of Mandal because the event moved three different dimensions of Indian politics at the same time, and scholars who began from different bodies of evidence arrived at different accounts of what kind of event it was. Yogendra Yadav, working from election studies and representation data, read a democratic deepening in which the backward classes acquired substantive political power. Pradeep Chhibber, working from the party system's organisational record, read an acceleration of fragmentation into regional parties and coalition government. Christophe Jaffrelot, working from the caste composition of legislatures and the Bharatiya Janata Party's trajectory, read a silent revolution in social power that also handed Hindu nationalism its opening. The readings share most of their facts and dispute the interpretation, which is why an adjudication among them, rather than a choice of one, is the method this pillar adopts.

### Q: How did Mandal change Indian politics?

Mandal changed Indian politics along the three dimensions the three readings isolate, and the changes proved cumulative. In representation, the backward classes' share of parliamentary and assembly seats from the Hindi belt doubled across the 1989 verdict and never returned to its earlier levels, and a reserved 27 percent of Union employment was entrenched beyond the reach of any successor government's repeal. In the party system, the Janata Dal's inheritance fragmented into durable state parties, and coalition government at the centre became the norm for twenty-five years. In strategy, the Bharatiya Janata Party rebuilt itself as a cross-caste Hindu nationalist project in answer to caste assertion, rising from 85 seats in 1989 to the largest party by 1996. In law, the Indra Sawhney doctrine of 1992 fixed the ceiling, the creamy layer and the bar on promotions. In vocabulary, social justice became the obligatory language in which claims on the Indian state are made.

### Q: What is Yogendra Yadav's democratisation reading of Mandal?

Yogendra Yadav's democratisation reading holds that Mandal was the hinge of a second democratic upsurge in India, the phase from the late 1980s in which the plebeian orders of society, the backward classes, the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes, entered politics as autonomous claimants rather than as vote banks managed by their social superiors. Developed in his Economic and Political Weekly essays of the 1990s and restated in Making Sense of Indian Democracy in 2020, the reading rests on evidence assembled by the Centre for the Study of Developing Societies: turnout among lower-caste voters rising to match and surpass upper-caste turnout, and the caste composition of legislatures shifting durably toward the backward classes. On this reading, Mandal did not create a constituency; it answered one that had already voted its way into visibility in 1989, and converted its presence into an institutional title that later politics could not revoke.

### Q: What is Pradeep Chhibber's fragmentation reading of Mandal?

Pradeep Chhibber's fragmentation reading, stated in Democracy without Associations in 1999, holds that Mandal accelerated the disintegration of India's national party system into regional parties and coalition government. Chhibber argued that Indian parties, the Congress above all, had let their organisational and associational roots decay, so that when new claimants arrived they could not be aggregated inside existing parties and built parties of their own instead. The Mandal implementation of 1990 raised the return on organising politically as a caste and at the scale of a state, and the Janata Dal's subsequent breakup into the Samajwadi Party, the Rashtriya Janata Dal, the Biju Janata Dal and the two Janata Dal successors is the reading's organisational evidence, set against twenty-five years in which no single party won a Lok Sabha majority. Mandal on this account was the catalyst of fragmentation rather than its sole cause.

### Q: What does Christophe Jaffrelot mean by India's silent revolution?

By India's silent revolution, Christophe Jaffrelot means the transfer of political power in north India from the upper castes to the lower castes across the second half of the twentieth century, accomplished without insurrection, through the ballot box, the distribution of party tickets and the slow change in who sits in legislatures. The phrase titles his 2003 book, India's Silent Revolution: The Rise of the Lower Castes in North India, which documents the transfer by caste-coding legislators over five decades: backward-class representation among Lok Sabha members from the Hindi belt doubled from 11.1 percent in 1984 to 20.9 percent in 1989 and rose onward, while the upper-caste share fell below 40 percent. The revolution is silent because it proceeded by procedure rather than by storm, and a revolution because it displaced a social order that had governed the region since before Independence.

### Q: What is the decisive-moment framework for reading Mandal?

The decisive-moment framework is the interpretive claim that Mandal is best understood as a single reframe of the post-Independence political settlement whose effects ran simultaneously along three dimensions: the substantive, what changed for the backward classes; the institutional, what changed for the party system; and the strategic, what changed for Hindu nationalism. The framework holds that the three scholarly readings of Mandal, Yadav's democratisation, Chhibber's fragmentation and Jaffrelot's Hindutva-enabling account, describe those three dimensions and therefore complement rather than fully compete with one another. An event earns the name decisive moment, on this framework, by passing three tests: simultaneity across dimensions, irreversibility under governments that opposed it, and generativity, supplying the questions later politics must answer. Mandal's claim to the name rests on the record of 1978 to 1992 and on three decades of administration since.

### Q: Why was the Mandal report not implemented during the 1980s?

The Mandal report was not implemented during the 1980s because no government of that decade had an incentive to implement it. The report was submitted on 31 December 1980 to the Congress government of Indira Gandhi, restored that year with a large majority, whose north Indian organisation and electoral coalition still depended heavily on upper-caste support and on a brokerage model that backward-class assertion at the centre threatened more than it rewarded. Her government let the report rest after parliamentary discussion in 1982, answering that reservation at the centre required consultation with the states. Rajiv Gandhi's government after 1984, holding over four hundred seats, had even less need of the backward-class claim and left the file untouched. Implementation waited for a government whose majority was itself built on backward-class mobilisation, which arrived only with the National Front victory of 1989, and even then the announcement of August 1990 was entangled with the internal politics of V.P. Singh's coalition.

### Q: How did OBC representation in Parliament change after 1990?

OBC representation in Parliament rose sharply across the Mandal years and then held its gains. Christophe Jaffrelot's caste-coded series for Lok Sabha members returned from the Hindi belt shows the backward-class share doubling from 11.1 percent in 1984 to 20.9 percent in 1989, the verdict that brought V.P. Singh to office, and continuing to climb through the 1990s as parties of every colour distributed tickets to backward-class candidates in recognition of the new arithmetic. The state figures are starker: backward-class members of Parliament from Bihar rose from 17 percent in 1984 to 43 percent in 1996, and in the Uttar Pradesh assembly the backward-class share of members reached 30 percent by 1993 while the upper-caste share fell from 58 percent in 1962 to 37.7 percent in 1996. All-India, the backward-class share of the Lok Sabha peaked at roughly a quarter of the House in 2004, a plateau rather than a reversal in the decades after.

### Q: Why does Sudipta Kaviraj read Mandal as continuity rather than rupture?

Sudipta Kaviraj reads Mandal as continuity because his essays of the 1990s take the longest available view of Indian democracy: universal franchise in a caste-ordered society made the progressive transfer of power toward the numerically weighty lower orders a structural tendency of the republic from 1950 onward, and Mandal appears, from that altitude, as the moment the tendency became visible to metropolitan observers rather than the moment it began. The tendency had earlier instalments Kaviraj's reading can cite: the Dravidian displacement of Brahmin predominance in the south, backward-class power in Bihar under Karpoori Thakur in the 1970s, Kanshi Ram's autonomous Dalit organisation founded before 1990, and the fall of upper-caste parliamentary representation below half as early as 1977. On this reading, periodising 1990 as a rupture flatters the surprised observer. The decisive-moment framework accepts the trajectory and disputes only the inference, holding that Mandal concentrated and locked in what the trajectory had prepared.

### Q: What did Marc Galanter's Competing Equalities establish about reservation law?

Marc Galanter's Competing Equalities: Law and the Backward Classes in India, published in 1984, established the scholarly baseline for understanding how India's compensatory discrimination regime actually worked, as distinct from how its texts read. Galanter assembled the constitutional architecture, Articles 15(4) and 16(4), the First Amendment of 1951 that followed the Champakam Dorairajan judgment, and the line of cases from M. R. Balaji v State of Mysore onward, and measured the regime's output against its promise using the state's own statistics. His central finding was a persistent implementation gap: three decades of formal provision had left the backward classes' share of public employment and higher education far below their population share, particularly in the services of the Union. The study appeared during the Mandal report's shelving and supplied the legal and statistical vocabulary in which the argument of 1990 to 1992 was conducted on both sides.

### Q: How did the Janata Dal fragment after the Mandal implementation?

The Janata Dal, the party that implemented Mandal, disintegrated across the 1990s into state parties that divided its inheritance by region and caste base. The first break came within three months of the announcement, when Chandra Shekhar led a breakaway faction out in November 1990 to form a government with Congress support. Mulayam Singh Yadav founded the Samajwadi Party in Uttar Pradesh on 4 October 1992. George Fernandes and Nitish Kumar formed the Samata Party in Bihar in 1994. Lalu Prasad Yadav, expelled from the Janata Dal, founded the Rashtriya Janata Dal in 1997, and Biju Patnaik's followers formed the Biju Janata Dal in Odisha the same year. By 1999 the rump had divided into the Janata Dal (United) and the Janata Dal (Secular). Each successor held a state and a caste coalition, and each became a necessary partner in Delhi's coalition arithmetic, which is Pradeep Chhibber's fragmentation thesis written in party genealogy.

### Q: What did the Mandal Commission hear during its public consultations?

During its tours of the states in 1979 and 1980, the Mandal Commission heard testimony from the populations it had been appointed to classify, and it preserved that testimony in state-level records that remain the least consulted part of its archive. The Commission issued questionnaires to Union and state governments and to the public, took evidence from sociologists, administrators and community organisations, and conducted a socio-economic field survey in February 1980 covering two villages and one urban block in 405 of the country's 406 districts. Village witnesses described exclusion from wells and water sources, from schools and from government employment in the concrete terms of their own localities, and the Commission's eleven indicators were designed to translate such testimony into comparable scores. The consultation records, preserved at the archives of the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment, show the report's recommendations arising from articulations by the backward classes themselves, a provenance its critics, who preferred to describe the report as a politician's device, rarely engaged.

### Q: How did the Commission identify backward classes among non-Hindu communities?

The Commission extended its identification beyond Hindu castes by two routes. Communities that had converted from castes treated as untouchable before conversion, among Muslims, Sikhs, Christians and Buddhists, were counted as backward on the ground that conversion had not dissolved the social and occupational disabilities the caste had carried. Communities bearing the same occupational names and functions as Hindu castes classified as backward, the dhobi or launderer, the lohar or iron worker, the nai or barber and the teli or oil presser among them, were classified by the equivalence of their occupation and standing. Applying these routes, the Commission estimated that backward classes among non-Hindus added roughly eight percentage points to the 44 percent it counted among Hindus, producing the report's headline estimate that the Other Backward Classes constituted 52 percent of India's population. The method recognised that caste in India operates across religious boundaries, a recognition the central and state lists have retained.

### Q: How did the Narasimha Rao government carry Mandal forward after 1991?

The Congress government of P. V. Narasimha Rao, which took office in June 1991 after the government that announced Mandal and its successor had both fallen, neither repealed the reservation nor implemented it unchanged. In September 1991 it issued a modifying office memorandum that retained the 27 percent for the backward classes, directed that the poorer sections within them receive preference, and added a 10 percent reservation for economically weaker sections among the unreserved, the element the Supreme Court struck down in 1992 while upholding the rest. Rao's government then defended the scheme before the nine-judge bench, accepted the verdict of November 1992, appointed the Ram Nandan Prasad committee to define the creamy layer, and issued the operative memorandum of 8 September 1993 that put the reservation into routine recruitment. The National Commission for Backward Classes received statutory form the same year. Mandal survived, in short, by being administered by the party that had shelved its report for a decade.

### Q: What did the 1990 Mandal implementation cover, and what did it leave out?

The office memorandum of 13 August 1990 covered direct recruitment to civil posts and services under the Government of India and to posts in public sector undertakings, reserving 27 percent of vacancies for the socially and educationally backward classes identified in the common list the government would notify. It left out three fields the Mandal report itself had addressed. Reservation in promotions, which the report had recommended at the same 27 percent, was not included in the memorandum, and the Supreme Court in 1992 held such reservation impermissible in any case. Reservation in educational institutions, also recommended by the report, was not extended to central institutions until the constitutional amendment of 2005 and the Central Educational Institutions Act of 2006. And the report's recommendations reaching banks and private undertakings receiving government assistance were never enacted. The implementation of 1990 was therefore a partial execution of the report, concentrated on the field of Union employment, with its remaining fields settled later or not at all.
