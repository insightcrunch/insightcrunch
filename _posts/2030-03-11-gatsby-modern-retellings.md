## The Retelling Test

The Great Gatsby entered the public domain in the United States on the first day of 2021, and the months that followed brought a rush of new versions, some licensed and many not, some reverent and some gleefully irreverent. The cluster filed in this series under the keyword the chol hong nguyen retelling and others is the subject of this article: the modern retellings that take Fitzgerald's story apart and rebuild it around the people the original pushed to its edges. The claim this article makes is direct and should be stated before the evidence. The most acclaimed modern retelling of the novel, Nghi Vo's The Chosen and the Beautiful, recasts the story through a queer Vietnamese-American Jordan Baker, and in doing so it recovers the perspectives the original left silent. That recovery is the test every retelling in this cluster must face. A retelling earns its place not by dressing an old plot in new clothes but by showing the reader something the source kept hidden, and the strongest of these new versions pass that test with room to spare.

![Jordan Baker dealing paper cards that turn into living birds at a glittering Jazz Age party - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The distinction matters because the culture produces Gatsby versions the way it produces weather, and not all of them are retellings in the sense this article uses the word. A film that moves the plot faithfully from page to screen is an adaptation. A novel that continues the story past Fitzgerald's last page, inventing new fates for Nick or Daisy or the children they might have had, is a sequel or a prequel, and those belong to a different inquiry. A retelling keeps the known story, the summer of 1922, the parties, the green light, the death on the road to New York, and tells it again from a different center of gravity, so that the familiar events arrive carrying unfamiliar weight. The difference is not a matter of fidelity but of geometry. The adaptation asks how close it can stand to the original. The retelling asks what the original looks like from somewhere the original never stood.

That geometry is the series thread running through this article, and it can be named plainly: reception as illumination. Every retelling is an act of reading made public. The reteller has read Fitzgerald's novel, has felt its force and its evasions, and has answered with a book of her own that says, in effect, here is what I found when I looked at your story from the margin. The best of these answers do not diminish the original. They function the way a strong light functions in a familiar room, showing the dust in corners the householder had stopped seeing and the cracks in walls that were always there. The original remains standing. It is simply harder, afterward, to pretend it was ever a simple house.

The complication must be faced honestly, because it is the first objection any serious reader raises. Retellings are not mere novelty, and they do not replace the original, and any retelling that tries to replace it has misunderstood the assignment. The Chosen and the Beautiful does not ask the reader to forget Fitzgerald's Gatsby; it assumes the reader knows it, leans on that knowledge, and then quietly rearranges the furniture. The pleasure of the book depends on recognition, on feeling the familiar plot arrive through an unfamiliar consciousness. A reader who had never heard of Jay Gatsby could still enjoy Vo's novel as a Jazz Age fantasy, but the book's full charge comes from the double vision, the original glowing through the new version like a watermark. The same holds for every retelling examined here. They are parasites in the honorable sense, organisms that live on a host without killing it, and the healthiest of them give back more than they take, returning the reader to Fitzgerald with sharpened attention.

There is also a second, quieter complication, which is that retelling is an old literary habit wearing new clothes, and the 2021 cluster did not invent it. Writers have been answering one another's books for as long as books have existed. What the public-domain expiry changed was not the impulse but the permission structure. Before 2021, anyone who wanted to publish a Gatsby retelling in the United States needed the blessing of the rights holders, and blessings come with conditions. After the expiry, the only remaining gate was the writer's own nerve. The result was a sudden widening of the field, and the widening favored exactly the writers the old gate would have kept out: the ones who wanted to make the story stranger, queerer, browner, more female, more working-class, more magical than the licensed tradition had allowed. That is why the cluster matters, and why this article treats it as a single phenomenon rather than a shelf of unrelated curiosities. The licensed era's permissions set the baseline the new wave broke from: adaptations that stayed close to the novel's surface, that treated its silences as settled rather than as invitations, because the estate's blessing depended on not embarrassing the property. Nixon's ballet, the licensed era's boldest Gatsby, still told the story straight, in period costume and with reverence. The unlicensed wave kept the period and dropped the reverence, and the difference between those two choices is the difference between an adaptation and a retelling.

The argument that follows moves through the phenomenon in stages. It begins with the silences in Fitzgerald's novel that make retelling irresistible, the characters and histories the original gestures toward and then abandons. It describes the legal opening that turned a licensed trickle into an unlicensed flood. It gives the center of the stage to Nghi Vo's novel, the retelling against which the others are measured, and examines the machinery of its magic and the precision of its revision. It sets the boundaries of the category, distinguishing retelling from the sequels and prequels handled elsewhere. It surveys the other notable versions, the trans Latinx remix, the watercolor graphic novel, the wordless ballet, the Broadway musical, and asks of each what viewpoint or element it contributes. It gathers those answers into a single table. And it closes by returning to the claim with which it opened, tested now against the full weight of the evidence.

## Why the Story Asks to Be Retold

No novel invites retelling unless it leaves something unfinished, and The Great Gatsby leaves a great deal unfinished by design. Fitzgerald's book is narrated by Nick Carraway, a young Midwesterner of limited self-knowledge and unlimited confidence in his own decency, and everything the reader learns passes through Nick's filter. The filter is the art. Nick tells the reader, in the novel's first pages, that he is inclined to reserve all judgments, and then spends the rest of the book judging everyone, usually wrongly, usually late, and usually in ways that reveal more about Nick than about his subjects. The reteller's opportunity begins exactly there, in the gap between what Nick sees and what the reader suspects. Every character Nick describes is a candidate for a second telling, because Nick's descriptions are performances of his own limitations.

Consider Jordan Baker, the figure at the center of this article's claim. In Fitzgerald's novel she is the most modern person in the room and the least known. She plays championship golf, she moves through the world with a bored competence that the men around her find both attractive and faintly threatening, she lies about a borrowed car and possibly about a golf tournament, and she exits the novel with a sentence of cool finality that Nick never quite recovers from. What the novel never supplies is her interior. No chapter is told from inside her head. No passage renders her thoughts at the wheel of the car she drives too fast, or her calculations at the golf tournaments where her reputation is made and questioned, or her feelings about Daisy, the beautiful friend to whom she is loyal in a way the novel never quite explains. Nick admires her and distrusts her in the same breath, and his narration keeps her at the distance of a man describing a woman he desires and cannot decode. She is, in the strictest sense, a surface with a rumor of depth, and a rumor of depth is an invitation. It is no accident that the most ambitious modern retelling chose her as its narrator. She was the largest unopened room in Fitzgerald's house.

The Wilsons offer a different kind of silence, the silence of class. George and Myrtle Wilson live in the valley of ashes, the industrial wasteland between West Egg and New York, and the novel needs them the way a tragedy needs its machinery: Myrtle must die on the road so that the plot can turn, and George must fire the gun so that it can close. But the novel never grants them the interiority it grants its rich characters. Myrtle's vitality, her hunger for a larger life, her disastrous taste in men, all arrive filtered through Nick's condescension and Tom's contempt. George's grief, the slow curdling of a decent man's mind under the pressure of betrayal and poverty, is rendered in a few stark scenes and then spent. A reteller who wanted to write the working-class Gatsby would find the material waiting, fully staged but never explored, like a set built for a play that was never performed. That no major modern retelling has yet taken the Wilsons as its center is itself a fact worth noting, a measure of which margins the culture is ready to hear from and which it still passes by.

Then there is the silence of race, which in Fitzgerald's novel is not a silence at all but a noise the book makes without examining. Tom Buchanan's dinner-table rant about the rise of the colored races, cribbed from the eugenicist tracts of the period, is the novel's most quoted passage on the subject, and it is usually read as characterization, proof of Tom's brutishness. But the novel's own racial imagination is thinner than its condemnation of Tom. The famous scene in which Nick, Gatsby, and Jordan are passed on the Queensboro Bridge by a limousine full of wealthy Black revelers is rendered in a sentence of startled condescension that the novel never revisits. The Black characters of the novel exist as atmosphere, as signs of a modernity the white characters find exciting and vaguely threatening. A retelling that centers a Vietnamese-American Jordan does not merely add diversity to the cast. It answers a specific evasion, the novel's habit of noticing race only as a disturbance in the white characters' field of vision, and it answers by moving the disturbance to the center of the frame and giving it a voice, a history, and a magic of its own.

The queer subtext is the fourth silence, and perhaps the most productive for retellers. Fitzgerald's novel is saturated with a desire it never names. Nick's narration lingers on Gatsby's body, on the "gorgeous" shirts, on the physical charge of the men's encounters, with an intensity that exceeds the requirements of friendship. The drunken afternoon in the McKees' apartment, with its suggestive ellipses and its famous elevator scene, has been read for decades as the novel's open secret. Gatsby himself, the self-invented man, the man who remade his name and his past out of longing, reads in the modern retellings as a figure of queer self-creation, a man performing a masculinity he assembled from borrowed parts. The retellings that make this subtext text, that let Nick and Gatsby fall for each other or that make Gatsby a transgender boy building himself from nothing, are not importing an alien concern into the novel. They are developing a photograph the original left in the bath too short a time, bringing up an image that was always latent in the negative.

Finally there is the silence of Gatsby's own past, the deliberate blank at the novel's center. Fitzgerald gives the reader rumors, a rose-tinted biography from Gatsby himself, a correction from Nick's investigation, and the famous passage about the young James Gatz and his schedule for self-improvement, and then moves on. The novel is interested in the dream, not the dreamer, and it guards that priority by keeping the dreamer slightly out of focus. Every retelling must decide what to do with that blur. Vo fills it with magic, making Gatsby's self-invention literal sorcery. McLemore fills it with the specific labor of a trans boy becoming himself. The graphic novel draws it in watercolor washes that refuse hard edges. Each choice is a reading of what the blur meant, and the variety of the choices is itself an argument about the novel's inexhaustibility. A book that told everything would leave retellers nothing to do. Fitzgerald's genius, for the reteller's purposes, was to build a mansion and leave half the rooms locked.

The queer reading has the most textual tinder, and the retellings that strike the match do so with specifics, not atmosphere. There is the afternoon in the McKees' apartment, where Nick gets drunk with a photographer and his wife, where the narration grows elliptical at exactly the moment of greatest intimacy, and where Nick finds himself standing beside Mr. McKee in the elevator, a scene Fitzgerald renders in a suggestive half-sentence and then abandons. There is Nick's remark, early in the novel, that he was slow to understand a suggestion from a man in his office, a line generations of readers have underlined. There is the scene of Gatsby's shirts, where Nick watches his friend hurl colored fabric into the air and buries his face in the pile, an image of sensual surrender that exceeds any requirement of the plot. And there is the sheer disproportion of Nick's attention: the loving inventory of Gatsby's smile, his tanned skin, his physical grace, set against the thinness of his interest in any woman. None of this proves anything about Fitzgerald's intentions, and the retellings wisely do not claim that it does. What it proves is that the text supports the reading, that the desire is in the sentences whether or not it was in the plan. Vo's decision to let Nick and Gatsby fall for each other is therefore not an imposition but an activation, the development of an image the original left latent. McLemore's decision works the same negative in a different key: her Nick loves Gatsby too, but as a trans boy recognizing a fellow self-maker, which turns the original's erotic charge into a recognition scene. The two retellings disagree about what the charge means, and the disagreement is productive, because it shows the subtext to be inexhaustible rather than merely suggestive.

### Why did Jordan Baker become the favored doorway into the story?

Jordan is the major character Fitzgerald never enters. Nick narrates around her but never inside her, leaving a cool surface of golf, fast driving, and half-admitted lies with no interior behind it. A reteller who takes her as narrator inherits the whole plot and gains the consciousness the original withheld, so every familiar scene replays with new interior weather.

## The Law Opened a Door

On the first day of 2021, the copyright in The Great Gatsby expired in the United States, and the novel entered the public domain. The legal fact is simple and its consequences were not. For ninety-five years the book had been property, administered by the Fitzgerald estate and the publisher, and anyone who wanted to adapt it, quote it at length, or build a new work on its foundations needed permission. Permission is a relationship, and relationships have terms. The licensed adaptations of the twentieth century, the films, the stage versions, the authorized dramatizations, all operated within the estate's sense of what the book was and what it could decently become. The expiry ended that supervision in a single stroke. What followed was not merely more adaptation but a different kind of adaptation, unvetted, unblessed, and free to be disrespectful in the way that only the unauthorized can be. The mechanics of the opening are worth stating plainly. Under the American copyright term then in force, works published in 1925 entered the public domain at the start of 2021, ninety-five years after publication, and Gatsby entered in the company of the year's whole cohort. The term's length is itself a fact about the culture: three generations of readers met the novel as property before any living writer could freely answer it. The writers who came of age under that regime learned to treat the classics as either licensed or untouchable, and the retelling impulse, where it existed, was routed through permission. The 2021 expiry did not create the desire to rewrite Gatsby. It created the conditions under which the desire could be acted on without negotiation, and the pent-up backlog of those desires is one reason the first year's output was so various and so bold.

The timing of the first wave tells the story. K. Woodman-Maynard's graphic novel adaptation, painted in watercolors and published by Candlewick Press, carries a publication date of January 5, 2021, four days after the expiry, which means it was conceived, drawn, and scheduled in anticipation of the opening, a ship waiting at the harbor mouth for the tide. Nghi Vo's The Chosen and the Beautiful followed in June of the same year, the first major prose retelling of the freedom era, and its publisher, Tordotcom, is a science fiction and fantasy house, not a literary-fiction house, which is itself a sign of the shift: the book was acquired as a fantasy novel that happened to be Gatsby, not as a Gatsby book that happened to have magic. The old gate would have asked whether a queer Vietnamese-American Jordan with paper-cutting magic was a proper use of the property. The new dispensation did not ask. It published.

The legal history behind the opening deserves a fuller telling than this article can give it here, and the fuller telling belongs to the article that owns it, the account of [the public-domain entry and the adaptation wave it released](/2029/11/19/gatsby-public-domain-2021/). What matters for the retelling argument is the shape of the permission. Copyright expiry does not merely lower costs, though it does that; it removes the veto. A reteller working under license must negotiate every liberty, must persuade the estate that a transgender Gatsby or a magical Jordan honors the original. A reteller working in the public domain must persuade only readers. That shift in audience, from the guardian to the public, changes what kinds of books get written, because guardians and publics want different things. Guardians want the property protected. Publics want the story to speak to them, in their idiom, about their lives. The 2021 cluster is the sound of that shift, the noise a classic makes when it stops being property and starts being material.

It would be a mistake, though, to treat the public-domain moment as the beginning of Gatsby retelling. The impulse predates the law by decades, and the licensed era produced its own reimaginings, necessarily tamer, necessarily negotiated. The Northern Ballet's full-length Gatsby, choreographed by David Nixon and premiered in 2013, was a licensed stage work, and its existence proves that the desire to re-see the story in a new medium did not wait for the lawyers. What the expiry changed was the center of gravity. Before 2021, retelling was an exception granted by permission. After 2021, it became a right exercised at will, and the exceptions, the licensed and careful versions, became the minority. The flood that followed is the subject of the rest of this article, and its first and deepest current is the novel that put Jordan Baker's consciousness where Nick Carraway's had been.

## Jordan Takes the Pen

Nghi Vo's The Chosen and the Beautiful, published in June 2021 as the author's debut novel, is the retelling against which the modern cluster is measured, and the measurement is not close. The book takes Fitzgerald's plot nearly whole, the summer of 1922, the parties at Gatsby's mansion, the reunion with Daisy, the death on the road, the hollow funeral, and retells it through the consciousness of Jordan Baker, who in Vo's version is a queer Vietnamese-American woman adopted as a child by a wealthy Louisville family. The facts of her adoption are given the weight of history. Found as a small child in Tonkin during wartime, brought to Kentucky by Eliza Baker, a missionary daughter of the Baker family, raised among white wealth that treated her as both ornament and outsider, Jordan grows up suspended between two worlds, claimed by neither. The white Louisvillians of her childhood hid her in her bedroom during parties and compared her, in her hearing, to the servants. The Vietnamese immigrants she meets in New York hear her Kentucky accent and her American self-description and find her foreign. She is, as one strand of the novel's reception has put it, an anomaly among the white rich and a conspiracy among her own, charming in one room and dangerous in another, and the doubleness is not a theme she discusses but the air she breathes.

The revision of Jordan is the book's great engine, and it repays close attention because it shows how a retelling recovers a silenced perspective without falsifying the source. Fitzgerald's Jordan is cool, athletic, dishonest in small ways, and erotically self-possessed. Vo's Jordan is all of those things, and the novel never contradicts the original's facts about her, which is part of its discipline. The golf, the fast driving, the careless charm, the affair with Nick, all survive. What changes is the interior, and the interior changes everything. Where Fitzgerald's Nick sees a woman who is "incurably dishonest," Vo's Jordan narrates the dishonesty as a survival skill learned by a child who understood early that the truth about her origins was not hers to tell, because the Bakers had already told a tidier version. Where Nick sees coolness, Jordan narrates the discipline of a person who learned to be unflappable because flappability would be read as foreignness. The retelling does not argue that Fitzgerald was wrong about Jordan. It argues that he was looking at her from the wrong side of the glass, and then it moves the reader to the other side.

The novel's queerness is handled with the same double fidelity. In Fitzgerald, the erotic charge between the characters is ambient and unnamed: Nick's fascination with Gatsby's body, the suggestive comedy of the McKee apartment, Jordan's mannish independence. Vo makes the charge explicit without making it anachronistic. Jordan moves through gay speakeasies, takes lovers of both sexes with the frankness of a woman whose outsider status has freed her from the performance of respectable femininity, and loves Daisy with an intensity that the novel refuses to reduce to either friendship or rivalry. The book's most discussed revision is the love triangle's reconfiguration: in Vo's telling, Nick and Gatsby fall for each other, and the famous pursuit of Daisy becomes a tangle in which every line of desire crosses another. This is not provocation for its own sake. It is a reading of the original's erotics, developed the way a photograph is developed, by bringing up what was latent. Fitzgerald's Gatsby loves an idea of Daisy that no real woman could embody; Vo's Gatsby loves a man he cannot have and a woman who cannot save him, and the doubleness makes the original's idealization legible as a structure rather than a quirk.

Daisy herself is transformed by the change of narrator, and the transformation is one of the book's sharpest illuminations. In Fitzgerald's novel Daisy is the novel's great absence, the voice full of money, the beautiful fool who chooses the brute and survives. Nick loves her and judges her and never understands her. Through Jordan's eyes, Daisy becomes something more specific and more damning: the white best friend whose affection is real and whose power is lethal, the woman who could afford to be kind because kindness cost her nothing, the girl who once crept into a hidden Vietnamese child's bedroom during a party and made her feel seen, and who then grew up into a woman for whom seeing was a party trick. The novel does not make Daisy a villain. It makes her a case study in the limits of white female solidarity, the friend who loves you and will still let the world devour you, and in doing so it recovers a perspective the original's male narration could not reach: what it feels like to be loved by Daisy Buchanan.

The book's reception confirmed the scale of the achievement. As a debut novel from a fantasy house, it might have been received as a genre curiosity. Instead it drew the kind of attention literary fiction covets: a Goodreads Choice Award nomination in the fantasy category for 2021, wide critical discussion, and the particular praise, voiced in the trade press, for the balance it strikes between invention and fidelity, the sense that Vo had found exactly the seam where the new material could be grafted onto the old without rejecting it. BookPage's review made the comparison that stuck, likening Vo's channeling of Fitzgerald's prose to the way a great pastiche artist inhabits a predecessor's style, and praised the recasting of a story about male overreach as a story about the limits placed on female and non-white agency. The praise matters for this article's argument because it is evidence, not of novelty, but of illumination. Reviewers did not celebrate the book for being different. They celebrated it for making the original newly legible.

Vo's prose deserves its own accounting, because the retelling's claim to illumination rests partly on style. She writes in a diction that recalls Fitzgerald without copying him, a pastiche in the honorable sense: the long balanced sentences, the catalogues of luxury, the similes that arrive like small detonations. BookPage's review reached for the comparison that has stuck, likening her channeling of Fitzgerald to Susanna Clarke's channeling of Austen, and the comparison is exact in one important way. Both writers inhabit a predecessor's style the way an actor inhabits a role, fully committed and fully aware, and the awareness is the point. The reader feels Fitzgerald's music and hears, inside it, a different mind thinking. The effect is uncanny in the strict sense: familiar and strange at once, the home haunted by a guest who knows the rooms better than the owner.

The novel's ending shows what that haunted style can do with Fitzgerald's most famous machinery. The death on the road arrives as it must, because the retelling keeps the skeleton, but it arrives reweighted: the reader has spent the book inside Jordan's consciousness, so the catastrophe reads as her catastrophe, the destruction of the fragile world she had built from paper and nerve. The funeral, famously unattended in the original, becomes in Vo's hands a scene about who mourns and who is permitted to mourn, about the difference between Nick's Midwestern decency and Jordan's harder knowledge. And the paper magic, which might have remained a clever device, converges in the final pages on a single image, a cut-paper heart, that reviewers singled out as the book's emotional peak. The image works because it gathers every thread: the heritage magic, the love story, the theme of fragile things made durable by the writing on them. Fitzgerald ends with boats against the current. Vo ends with paper against the fire. The retelling does not improve the original's close. It answers it, in the same key, from the other side of the glass.

The Jordan of the retelling also belongs to a longer conversation about the character's modernity, a conversation this series follows in its own right. Readers who want the full account of how Jordan Baker has been read, from Fitzgerald's cool athlete to the feminist icon to Vo's narrator, will find it in [the study of Jordan as the novel's modern woman](/2023/08/07/jordan-baker-modern-woman/), which traces the character's afterlife across a century of reading. This article's business is narrower: to show what happens when that afterlife becomes a first life, when the character who was always observed becomes the observer, and the novel's entire moral lighting changes because the lamp has been moved.

## What the Magic Is For

The fantasy in The Chosen and the Beautiful is not decoration, and misunderstanding its function is the fastest way to undervalue the book. Vo sets her retelling in a Jazz Age where magic is real, fashionable, and dangerous: the smart set makes bargains with infernal powers, marking themselves with a single black-painted fingernail; a drug-like drink called demonaic circulates at parties, sipped straight or slipped into liquor; and Jordan herself practices a paper magic rooted in her heritage, cutting shapes from paper and breathing life into them, a power the novel names with the phrase "in all paper is fire." A reader coming from Fitzgerald might take all this as mere genre furniture, the fantasy house's house style draped over a literary property. That reading misses the mechanism. Every element of the magic is a literalization of something the original keeps figurative, and the literalization is the analysis.

Consider the soul bargains. In Fitzgerald's novel, the characters have all made deals, but the deals are social: Gatsby trades his past for a persona, Daisy trades love for security, Tom trades everything for appetite, Nick trades judgment for access. The costs are real but invisible, which is why the novel's moral accounting feels so slippery. Vo makes the dealing visible. A blackened fingernail marks the bargainer the way a brand marks cattle, and suddenly the reader can see what Fitzgerald's prose only implies: that West Egg is a room full of people who have sold something, that the smart set's glamor is purchased, that every glittering surface has a price written on it somewhere. The magic does not add a theme. It develops one, the way a developing bath brings up a photograph, making the pattern legible that the plain paper only suggested.

Demonaic works the same way on the novel's drinking. Fitzgerald's characters drink constantly, and the drinking is both social lubricant and moral solvent, but the novel treats alcohol as background, as the medium in which the action swims. Vo's demonaic takes the solvent and makes it a substance, a fashionable intoxicant with real infernal associations, and the parties become what they always were in the original: rituals of self-dissolution, communions in which the participants trade pieces of themselves for the feeling of transcendence. The reader who returns to Fitzgerald after Vo will find the original's drinking scenes newly sinister, not because Vo changed them but because she showed what they were for.

Jordan's paper magic is the most personal of the literalizations, and the most pointed. In the original, Jordan is associated with surfaces, with the cool manipulation of appearances: the golf, the poise, the lies. Vo gives her a magic of surfaces made real, the power to cut paper into living things, and roots that power in the heritage the Bakers tried to overwrite. The magic is thus both a figure for art, the reteller's own power to cut new shapes from old paper, and a figure for identity, the thing Jordan was told to fold away and that she unfolds instead. There is a scene near the novel's end, involving her magic and a cut-paper heart, that reviewers singled out as the book's emotional climax, and its power comes from the convergence: the magic, the heritage, and the love story arrive at the same point, and the retelling's whole argument about recovery is enacted in a single image. Paper, the most fragile material, becomes the most durable, because it carries what was written on it.

The larger point is about what fantasy permits a retelling to say. Realism, as Fitzgerald practiced it, is a contract with the reader's sense of the probable, and the probable in 1925 did not include a Vietnamese-American lesbian's interiority as a subject of serious literary attention. That is not an accusation against Fitzgerald, who wrote the world he knew; it is a description of the form's limits in his hands. Fantasy breaks the contract and writes a new one, in which the improbable is permitted if it is true. Vo's magic is never an escape from the novel's social world. It is a set of instruments for dissecting it, sharper than realism's because it is not bound by realism's manners. The black fingernail says what Nick's narration cannot say about the Buchanans. The paper lion says what Jordan's coolness cannot say about her heart. The retelling's fantasy is not the opposite of Fitzgerald's realism. It is realism with the politeness removed.

### What does fantasy add to a famously realistic novel?

Fantasy supplies instruments for saying what realism's manners forbid. Fitzgerald could imply West Egg's moral costs but not show them. Vo's bargains, demonaic, and paper magic make those costs visible, turning figurative deals into marks on the skin. The result reads as more realistic, not less: a social world with its prices finally printed on its surfaces.

## The Chol Hong Nguyen Retelling and Others: Retelling Versus Sequel

The keyword that names this article's cluster needs a gloss, because the category it labels has edges, and the edges are where the thinking happens. A retelling, in the sense this article uses the word, keeps Fitzgerald's story and changes its telling: same summer, same parties, same deaths, new consciousness at the center, new elements admitted, new meanings drawn out. A sequel or a prequel does something else: it leaves the story's boundaries and invents beyond them, giving Nick a later life, Daisy a second act, Gatsby a youth the novel only rumored. The distinction is not a pedantry. It determines what question the book asks. The retelling asks what the known story means. The sequel asks what happened next. Those are different curiosities, and they produce different books.

The broader family of continuations, prequels, sequels, and afterlives has its own home in this series, and this article will not poach its territory. Readers who want the full survey of what writers have done beyond Fitzgerald's last page, the invented futures and excavated pasts, will find it in [the overview of sequels and the wider retelling category](/2030/03/04/gatsby-sequels-retellings/), which maps that terrain in full. The boundary is worth stating once, clearly, because the most instructive near-miss sits right on it. Michael Farris Smith's Nick, published in the first weeks of 2021, is a prequel, not a retelling: it invents Nick Carraway's war years and his drift toward Long Island, filling the biography Fitzgerald left blank. It is a strong book and a legitimate heir, but it asks the sequel's question, what happened before, not the retelling's question, what does the known story mean from a new center. This article mentions it once, to place it, and moves on. The retellings are the books that stay inside Fitzgerald's summer and change what the summer contains.

The edge cases test the definition usefully. Is a graphic novel that follows the plot faithfully a retelling or an adaptation? This article counts K. Woodman-Maynard's watercolor Gatsby as a retelling, because the change of medium changes the telling: the images do interpretive work the prose cannot, and the selection of which passages to quote and which to draw is itself an argument about the book. Is a ballet a retelling? The same logic applies, more strongly: a wordless Gatsby cannot be faithful in the adaptation sense, because fidelity to dialogue is impossible, so everything it communicates is reinterpretation. Is a Broadway musical a retelling? The songs add interiority the novel withholds, and addition is the reteller's signature move. The working rule is simple: if the new version's center of interest is what the old story means, it belongs here. If its center of interest is what happened next, it belongs across the border. The table at this article's heart is built on that rule.

## The Remix Generation

If Nghi Vo's novel is the retelling against which the cluster is measured, Anna-Marie McLemore's Self-Made Boys: A Great Gatsby Remix, published in September 2022 as the fifth volume of Feiwel and Friends' Remixed Classics series, is the proof that the measurement travels, that the recovery Vo performed on Jordan can be performed on the story's other silences by other hands. McLemore's book keeps the 1922 setting, the Eggs, the parties, the green light, the roadside death, and rebuilds the cast around the identities Fitzgerald's novel could not name. Nicolás Caraveo, the Nick figure, is a seventeen-year-old transgender Latinx boy from Minnesota, come to New York to establish himself as a young professional and, in the same motion, to establish himself as a man. His cousin Daisy, refashioned as Daisy Fabrega, has erased her Latina heritage and now passes as white under the name Daisy Fay. And Jay Gatsby, the mysterious neighbor whose parties dazzle and terrify, is also transgender, a boy who made himself from nothing in the most literal sense the novel's mythology allows.

The book's title does the first piece of critical work. "Self-made" is the novel's oldest cliché about Gatsby, the bootlegger's cover story, the American Dream's favorite alibi. McLemore takes the phrase at face value. Her boys are self-made in the sense that they have authored their own genders against the resistance of families, communities, and a 1922 America with no language for what they are. The pun is not cute; it is structural. Fitzgerald's Gatsby invents Jay Gatsby from James Gatz as an act of romantic will, and the novel treats that invention as both magnificent and hollow. McLemore's Gatsby invents himself as an act of survival, and the novel treats that invention as both costly and true. The parallel reframes the original's central ambiguity. What Fitzgerald presents as a possibly empty performance, the worship of a self-made man who may be made of nothing, McLemore presents as the most substantial thing in the book: the labor of becoming. The retelling does not refute Fitzgerald. It answers his question about what self-making is worth with evidence he did not have.

Daisy Fabrega, meanwhile, performs the cluster's other great recovery, the recovery of the passing narrative. In Fitzgerald, Daisy's whiteness is the unexamined ground of her power, the thing that lets her be careless while others pay. McLemore makes that ground into a plot: Daisy was born Latina, and her transformation into Daisy Fay, white East Egg wife, is a willed erasure, a bargain as infernal in its way as any of Vo's soul deals. The retelling thus takes the novel's most famous sentence about its heroine, that her voice is full of money, and gives it a history. The money in Daisy's voice is the sound of everything she paid to acquire it: her name, her family, her face as the world was allowed to see it. Nick's shock at discovering his cousin's reinvention mirrors the reader's shock at discovering how much of the original Daisy's power depended on a whiteness the novel never thought to question. The recovery is exact. It finds the silence, names it, and shows the plot running on it like a car running on a road the map omitted.

The book's reception gave the recovery institutional weight. Booklist awarded it a starred review and the judgment that has been quoted ever since, that McLemore captures the spirit of the original while adding nuance and depth, setting a new bar for what a great retelling can be. The National Book Award longlist included it, a recognition that placed a young-adult remix in the company of the year's most serious fiction. These are not the decorations of novelty. A starred review and a major prize longlist are the literary establishment's way of saying that a book has done the harder thing, that it has honored its source by arguing with it. The Remixed Classics series, which commissions diverse authors to reimagine canonical works, supplied the frame, but the book exceeds the frame's expectations, because McLemore does not merely diversify the cast. She finds the structural rhyme between Gatsby's self-invention and her characters' self-creation, and she plays it for the full length of the novel.

The comparison with Vo clarifies what the cluster's best work shares. Both novels keep the period setting, refusing the easy move of updating the story to the present, and the refusal is a discipline. By staying in 1922, both books force their new identities to contend with the historical world's hostility: Vo's Jordan faces the era's racism and its laws, McLemore's boys face a world without words for them. The pastness is not costume. It is pressure, and the pressure is what makes the recoveries feel earned rather than decorative. Both novels also keep the plot's skeleton intact, the parties, the reunion, the death, the empty funeral, trusting that the reader's memory of the original will do half the work. The retelling's art is the art of the palimpsest: the old text showing through the new, the new text commenting on the old, the reader holding both in mind at once. McLemore's achievement is to prove that the layering works a second time, on different silences, with different ink, and that the original is capacious enough to sustain more than one recovery.

The set pieces show the method at work. Take the shirts. In Fitzgerald, Gatsby hurls his gorgeous shirts at Daisy in a scene of almost embarrassing sensual excess, and the scene's charge comes from Daisy's tears, her overwhelmed response to the sheer materiality of his longing. In McLemore, the shirts land differently: Nick, a trans boy who has fought for every inch of his own presentation, watches another boy deploy clothing as self-creation, and the recognition is immediate and physical. The scene becomes a communion between two people who understand what it costs to dress the self you mean. Or take the Plaza confrontation, the novel's great scene of male rivalry. In Fitzgerald it is Tom's brute ownership against Gatsby's romantic claim, with Daisy as the disputed object. In McLemore the geometry shifts: Tom's masculinity, inherited and unexamined, faces Gatsby's masculinity, built and defended, and Nick watches from a third position, a boy still building his. The scene's violence is no longer only about Daisy. It is about what kinds of men the world permits, and what it does to the kinds it does not.

The roadside death, which the retelling must stage because the skeleton demands it, becomes in McLemore's hands a scene about whose bodies the world protects. Fitzgerald's Myrtle dies because she runs toward the wrong car, toward the wrong dream, and the novel treats her death as the mechanism of the plot's turn. McLemore keeps the mechanism and changes its lighting: the death of a working-class woman in a world built for the rich reads, through Nick's newly centered consciousness, as the cost the dream exacts from those it was never meant to include. The retelling does not sentimentalize Myrtle. It accounts for her, which is rarer and harder.

## The Story in Pictures

K. Woodman-Maynard's The Great Gatsby: A Graphic Novel Adaptation, published by Candlewick Press on January 5, 2021, four days after the American copyright expired, is the cluster's proof that retelling can happen without changing a word of plot or a line of identity, purely by changing the medium. The book follows Fitzgerald's story faithfully, scene by scene, and weaves excerpts of the original prose through its panels, so that the reader is never far from Fitzgerald's sentences. What makes it a retelling in this article's sense is the interpretive work the images perform. Every panel is a decision about what the prose means, and two hundred forty pages of such decisions amount to a sustained critical argument conducted in watercolor.

The argument begins with the treatment of Nick's narration. Fitzgerald's novel is famous for the fluidity of Nick's voice, the way it slides between participant and observer, between the summer's immediacy and the retrospective wisdom of the telling. Prose can sustain that fluidity because the reader cannot see the seams. A graphic novel must draw the narrator, must decide in each panel whether Nick is present, how large to make him, whether his captions float above the scene or press into it. Woodman-Maynard's solution, noted by the book's earliest reviewers, is to make the fluidity visible: Nick's figure shifts in scale and solidity, the watercolor washes refuse hard edges, and the reader watches the narration being unreliable in real time, sees the moments where Nick draws himself large and the moments where he tries to disappear. The effect is a retelling of the novel's most argued-over feature, its narrator, in a medium that cannot take that feature for granted. A student who has puzzled over classroom debates about Nick's reliability can see the argument drawn.

The book's most delightful device is its literalization of Fitzgerald's metaphors, and the device is critical, not whimsical. In the original, a beautiful partygoer is described in language that suggests blooming; Woodman-Maynard draws her blooming into an orchid. Daisy speaks of pushing Gatsby toward his dream in figurative language; the artist draws her pushing him across the sky on a cloud. These are jokes, but they are jokes with a thesis: Fitzgerald's prose is so image-dense that its metaphors can survive being rendered as pictures, and the survival tells the reader something about the prose's construction. A metaphor that collapses when drawn was decorative. A metaphor that holds when drawn was structural. The graphic novel is thus a stress test of the novel's imagery, panel by panel, and the imagery passes. The green light, the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg, the valley of ashes, all arrive in the drawings with their symbolic weight intact, which is itself a finding: the novel's symbols were always pictures first, and the watercolor merely returns them to their native medium.

What the visual retelling recovers, finally, is the novel's own visual imagination, the thing readers forget when they treat Gatsby as a book of ideas. Fitzgerald wrote cinematically before cinema had learned to do what he did: the parties are set pieces, the car ride to New York is a tracking shot, the final pages are a montage. The graphic novel restores that priority, reminding the reader that the book's first power was always the power of the seen. It adds no new viewpoint and no new identity, and in the terms of this article's test it might seem the weakest of the cluster's recoveries. But it passes the test on a different axis. It recovers the perspective of the eye itself, the reader's own act of picturing, and shows that act to be an interpretation, partial and revisable, like all the others.

### What makes a retelling illuminate rather than borrow?

Illumination changes what the reader can see in the original; borrowing only replays the plot in new costumes. The test is the return trip. If Fitzgerald reads differently afterward, with the racial economy, the queer charge, or the narrator's evasions now impossible to unsee, the retelling worked. If nothing changed in the reader's vision, it merely borrowed.

## The Story Without Words, the Story in Song

The Northern Ballet's The Great Gatsby, choreographed and directed by David Nixon with co-direction by Patricia Doyle and set to music by Sir Richard Rodney Bennett, premiered at the Leeds Grand Theatre in March 2013, eight years before the American copyright expired, and its presence in this article corrects a possible misunderstanding. Retelling did not begin with the public domain. It began with the licensed stage, with artists negotiating permission to re-see the story, and Nixon's ballet is the proof that the impulse was already mature before the law set it free. The ballet tells Fitzgerald's story without a word of dialogue, in two acts of dance, and the wordlessness is its argument.

What dance recovers is the body's knowledge, the thing the novel's narration, all voice and judgment, cannot carry. Fitzgerald's parties are described as spectacle, but the description is always mediated by Nick's commentary, his approval and disapproval coloring every dance. Nixon's dancers have no commentary. The Charleston sequences, the tango, the ensemble numbers that fill Gatsby's grounds, arrive as pure physical fact: bodies in motion, desire made visible in partnering, violence made visible in lifts that turn combative. The famous confrontation in the Plaza Hotel suite, the novel's great scene of male rivalry, becomes in dance a contest of physical presence, Tom's weight against Gatsby's reach, with Daisy passed between them like a disputed object. The image is not subtle, and it is not meant to be. It states physically what the novel states verbally, that Daisy is fought over rather than loved, and the physical statement lands differently, in the gut rather than the head. A retelling that changes the medium to the body recovers the novel's physical plot, the plot of who touches whom and who is allowed to touch, which the verbal plot both describes and evades.

The ballet also recovers Myrtle Wilson in a way the novel's other retellings have not attempted. Without dialogue, Myrtle cannot be condescended to in Nick's voice; she can only be danced, and the dancing gives her a vitality the novel rations. Her scenes with Tom have an erotic charge that the prose reports at a distance, and her death, the novel's hinge, becomes a purely visual catastrophe, a body broken on a road. Nixon's production, which earned him a nomination for Best Classical Choreography at the 2014 National Dance Awards and has toured the United Kingdom repeatedly since its premiere, demonstrates the retelling test in its purest form: nothing about the plot is changed, everything about its weight is redistributed, and the audience leaves seeing the story's physical cruelty with new clarity.

Eleven years later, the story found its voice again, this time in song. The Great Gatsby: A New Musical, with a book by Kait Kerrigan and music and lyrics by Jason Howland and Nathan Tysen, directed by Marc Bruni, premiered at New Jersey's Paper Mill Playhouse in the autumn of 2023 and opened on Broadway at the Broadway Theatre in April 2024, the first Broadway musical staging the novel had ever received. The production won the Tony Award for Best Costume Design of a Musical in its opening year, and its jazz and pop inflected score did for the novel's emotions what Nixon's choreography had done for its bodies: made them immediate, unmediated by Nick's retrospective voice.

What song adds to Gatsby is interiority made audible. Fitzgerald's novel withholds its characters' inner lives as a matter of principle; Nick speculates, the characters perform, and the reader infers. A musical cannot sustain that withholding, because the form's contract is emotional directness: when a character sings, the audience understands that the song is the truth the dialogue cannot carry. Gatsby's longing, which the novel renders as behavior, as parties thrown and shirts displayed, becomes in the musical a sung ache, and the transformation changes the character's moral lighting. A Gatsby who sings his desire is harder to dismiss as a hollow striver than a Gatsby who only performs it. Daisy, similarly, gains in song a self-awareness the novel denies her; her numbers give her the chance to articulate the calculations Nick only guesses at. The musical does not recover a silenced identity in the manner of Vo or McLemore. It recovers the novel's buried emotional explicitness, the feelings the prose circles but never states, and in doing so it passes the retelling test on its own terms: the reader who has heard Gatsby sing returns to the novel's parties hearing the music underneath.

The two stage versions diverge most instructively at the ending, where Fitzgerald's machinery is least forgiving. The novel kills Gatsby in his pool and buries him in an empty room, and the emptiness is the point: the dream's hollowness made literal in the attendance. Nixon's ballet stages the death as dance, a body falling and not rising, and the wordlessness strips the scene of Nick's commentary, leaving only the physical fact of a young man dead in water. The effect is colder than the novel's, because there is no narrator to supply meaning, and the coldness is the interpretation: the ballet suggests that the death means nothing beyond itself, that the search for significance is the mourners' vanity. The musical, by contrast, cannot resist giving Gatsby a final song, and the song supplies exactly what the novel withholds, a statement of what the longing was for. The two choices are opposite readings of the same silence, and both are defensible, which is why the cluster needs both. The retelling test does not prescribe an answer. It rewards the versions that find the question.

### Can a retelling change the facts and still be Gatsby?

Yes, when the changes serve the story's meaning rather than its trivia. Vo gives Jordan magic and a Vietnamese childhood; McLemore makes Nick and Gatsby transgender; neither touches the plot's skeleton. Fidelity belongs to the wound, not the bandage: keep the green light's ache and the dream's cost, and the surfaces may change.

## The Gatsby in the Retellings

The retellings agree about the margins, but they diverge about the center, and their disagreements about Jay Gatsby himself are the cluster's most revealing argument. Fitzgerald's Gatsby is a deliberate blank, a rumor with a smile, and each retelling fills the blank according to its own needs, producing a small gallery of Gatsbys that, set side by side, amount to a composite portrait no single version could supply.

Vo's Gatsby is the most altered and the most faithful. He keeps the parties, the shirts, the obsessive love, but his self-invention is literalized by the novel's magic: in a world where souls can be bargained and paper can be brought to life, a man who remade himself from James Gatz is not a metaphor but a practitioner. The magic does not explain Gatsby. It dignifies the explanation he gave of himself, treating his reinvention as a real art rather than a pathetic fraud. And his desire is redistributed: he loves Daisy, as he must, but he also loves Nick, and the doubleness makes his famous idealization legible as a structure, a man who loves what he cannot have because having would end the dreaming. Vo's Gatsby is the most tragic of the gallery, because he understands the price of his bargains and pays it anyway.

McLemore's Gatsby is the most tender. A transgender boy who built himself from nothing, he carries the novel's mythology of self-creation without its hollowness, because his creation was necessary rather than theatrical. Where Fitzgerald's Gatsby performs wealth to win Daisy, McLemore's performs a self to survive the world, and the difference reframes every party, every shirt, every desperate gesture. His love for Daisy is real, but it is shadowed by his recognition of Nick, the fellow self-maker, and the triangle becomes a study in the kinds of love available to people the world did not make room for. This Gatsby is the least mysterious of the gallery and the most moving, because his mystery was never the point. His labor was.

The ballet's Gatsby is the most physical. Danced, not spoken, he exists as presence: the lift, the reach, the fall. Nixon's choreography gives him no interior monologue and no backstory, only a body moving through space with an intentness the other bodies cannot match. The reduction is the reading. Stripped of Nick's narration, Gatsby becomes what the novel's other characters always saw, a force without a history, and the ballet suggests that the force was the truth and the history was the decoration. His death, a body in water, is the gallery's starkest image, because it refuses consolation.

The musical's Gatsby is the most audible. Given songs, he states what the novel's Gatsby only performs, and the stating changes him from enigma to confessor. The audience hears the longing directly, without Nick's mediation, and the directness makes him harder to judge and easier to pity. This is the Gatsby the novel's moralists would dislike, because he explains himself, and explanation is the enemy of mystique. But the musical's contract is emotional explicitness, and within that contract the sung Gatsby is the honest one, the man who finally says what the parties were for.

Set side by side, the four Gatsbys describe the original's blank from four directions, and the composite is richer than any single filling. The magician, the self-maker, the body, the voice: each is a reading of what Fitzgerald left out, and each is supported by something in the text. The gallery is the cluster's answer to the question of who Gatsby was, and its refusal to give a single answer is the most Fitzgeraldian thing about it.

## The Women the Novel Left at the Edge

The retellings' most sustained collective project is the recovery of the novel's women, and the project deserves its own section because it runs through every version this article has examined, gathering force as it goes. Fitzgerald's novel is narrated by a man, centered on a man's longing, and judged by a man's morality, and its women exist inside that frame as objects of desire, symbols of corruption, or cautionary examples. Daisy is the dream and the disappointment. Jordan is the cool observer who observes nothing on the page. Myrtle is the vitality that must be sacrificed. The novel's feminism, such as it is, consists of showing the cage clearly, not of opening it. The retellings open it, each in its own way, and the differences among their methods are as instructive as their shared direction.

Vo's method is the most radical because it is the most complete: she hands the narration to a woman and lets the novel's events be reweighed in a female consciousness. The reweighing changes the moral of the story. In Fitzgerald, the tragedy is Gatsby's, the dreamer destroyed by the dream's inadequacy. In Vo, the tragedy is distributed: it is also Jordan's, the woman who watched the dream destroy people she loved and who learned, too late, the price of her own survival; and it is Daisy's, the woman whose choices the original presents as carelessness and the retelling presents as the constrained moves of a person with no good options. The shift does not exonerate anyone. Daisy remains the woman who let Gatsby take the blame and who retreated into her money. But the retelling insists on the difference between a judgment and an understanding, and it places the reader inside the understanding. That is the feminist recovery in its strongest form: not the acquittal of the women, but the granting of their interiority, the same interiority the novel grants its men as a matter of course.

McLemore's method is different and complementary. Her Daisy Fabrega is not given a narrator's authority; she is given a history, the Latina past she erased to become Daisy Fay, and the history does the feminist work. Fitzgerald's Daisy is powerful because she is white, rich, and beautiful, and the novel never examines the foundations of that power. McLemore excavates them, showing the labor and the loss underneath the poise, and the excavation changes the character from symbol to person. Her Nick, meanwhile, is a trans boy whose masculinity is achieved rather than assumed, which throws the original's unexamined masculinities, Tom's brutishness, Gatsby's performance, Nick's quiet authority, into sharp relief. The retelling's feminism operates by comparison: put a character who had to build his manhood next to characters who inherited theirs, and the inheritance starts to look like the artifice.

The stage versions contribute their own recoveries, less theoretical but no less real. Nixon's ballet gives Myrtle a body that the novel's narration never quite grants her, a physical presence unmediated by Nick's condescension. The Broadway musical gives Daisy songs, and a song, in the musical's contract with its audience, is a claim to interiority: the character who sings is a character with a self. These are not the systematic revisions of the novels, but they move in the same direction, from object to subject, from the woman as seen to the woman as seeing.

One absence haunts the cluster, and honesty requires naming it. The Wilsons, the working-class couple the novel uses as its tragic machinery, have not yet received their retelling. No major modern version centers George's grief or Myrtle's hunger; she appears in the retellings as she appears in the original, as the vitality that must be spent for the plot to turn. The ballet gives her a body, the musical gives her a song, but neither gives her a consciousness. The omission is a measure of the culture's readiness, a sign of which margins are audible and which are still waiting for their key. It is also, paradoxically, a tribute to the difficulty of the task: a Wilson-centered Gatsby would have to invent nearly everything, because Fitzgerald supplied so little, and invention without the original's scaffolding is a different art from retelling. The cluster's unfinished business is thus also its proof of concept. The rooms Fitzgerald locked are many, and the retellers have opened the ones whose keys the present moment could cut. Others wait.

The fuller history of the novel's women on the page, from Fitzgerald's creation through a century of feminist reading to the retellings' revisions, is told in [the study of the novel's women and their recovery](/2027/08/16/gatsby-feminist-women/), which this article will not duplicate. What belongs here is the pattern: the retellings agree, across media and across identities, that the original's treatment of its women was a silence, and they agree that the silence was structural, not accidental. They disagree, productively, about what to do with it, narration or history or body or song, and the disagreement is the sign of a living tradition. A dead classic gets one authorized reading. A living one gets argued over, and the argument is the afterlife.

## The Business of the Retelling Wave

Retellings are art, but they are also products, and the wave's economics explain its shape as surely as its aesthetics do. The public-domain expiry did not merely permit new versions; it created a market for them, and markets have appetites. Understanding what the market wanted clarifies why the cluster looks the way it does, and why some kinds of retellings arrived early while others are still waiting.

The first appetite was speed. Woodman-Maynard's graphic novel, published four days after the expiry, was the product of a publisher that had timed production to the legal calendar, betting that the first authorized-by-nobody Gatsby would command attention precisely for its timing. The bet was sound: the book's January 2021 publication made it news, and the news made it sales. Vo's novel, arriving in June from a fantasy house, fed a second appetite, the genre market's hunger for literary properties it could claim. Tordotcom did not buy a Gatsby homage; it bought a fantasy novel with a built-in audience of Gatsby readers, and the built-in audience is the economic logic of the retelling. Every retelling inherits the original's readership as a floor. The marketing problem that kills most debut novels, finding readers who care, is solved in advance, because millions of people were assigned the book in school.

The Remixed Classics series industrialized that logic. By commissioning diverse authors to reimagine canonical works on a regular schedule, Feiwel and Friends turned retelling from an event into a line, with all the efficiencies of a line: a recognizable format, a built-in young-adult audience, and a pitch, the classic you know through the eyes it excluded, that booksellers can shelve and teachers can assign. McLemore's volume was the fifth in the series, which means the Gatsby remix arrived with four predecessors' worth of institutional knowledge about how to frame, package, and sell a revision. The series is the clearest evidence that retelling had become, by 2022, not a daring anomaly but a publishing category, with its own conventions and its own economics.

The stage versions show the economics at their largest scale. A Broadway musical is among the most expensive artworks a culture produces, and the 2024 Gatsby musical's capitalization, its road from Paper Mill Playhouse to the Broadway Theatre, its Tony-winning costume design, its cast recording, represents a bet that the novel's brand could fill a thousand-seat house eight times a week. The bet's logic is the same as the publishers': the audience arrives pre-sold, and the production's job is to give them the story they know in the form they did not expect. The Northern Ballet's version, touring Britain repeatedly since 2013, works the same logic at a regional scale, using the novel's name to draw audiences who might not otherwise attend ballet. In both cases the retelling is a translation of cultural capital from one market to another, from the classroom and the bookshelf to the theatre and the stage door.

The international afterlife confirms the pattern. The Broadway musical's transfer to London's West End and to Seoul, and the ballet's repeated British tours, show the retelling economics working across borders: the novel's global classroom fame makes it exportable cultural capital, and each territory's production reframes the American story for local appetites. The mechanism is the same as the domestic one, a known property reducing the risk of an unknown form, but the repetition proves the model's durability. A retelling wave that can fill houses in three countries is not a fad. It is an industry.

What the money reveals, finally, is why the cluster's recoveries took the forms they did. Markets reward the legible: a queer Jordan, a trans Gatsby, a sung Daisy are pitches that can be summarized in a sentence, and the sentence sells the book. The Wilsons' unfinished business, noted elsewhere in this article, is legible too, in principle, but no publisher has yet bet on it, which suggests that the market's appetite for marginal perspectives has limits, or at least a sequence. The economics do not diminish the art. They explain its timing, its packaging, and its gaps, and any honest account of the retelling wave must keep both ledgers open, the aesthetic and the commercial, because the wave was both.

## The Verdict

The claim with which this article opened has now been tested against the full weight of the evidence, and it stands, sharpened by the testing. The most acclaimed modern retelling of The Great Gatsby, Nghi Vo's The Chosen and the Beautiful, recasts the story through a queer Vietnamese-American Jordan Baker, recovering the perspectives the original left silent, and the recovery is not a novelty but an illumination, a strong light moved into a familiar room. The book's distinction, confirmed by its award recognition and by the critical consensus around its balance of invention and fidelity, is that it changes what the reader can see in Fitzgerald without asking the reader to love Fitzgerald less. That is the standard. The other retellings in the cluster meet it in their own ways: McLemore by finding the structural rhyme between Gatsby's self-invention and her characters' self-creation, Woodman-Maynard by drawing the narration's unreliability where prose could only imply it, the ballet by dancing the physical plot the novel's voice obscures, the musical by singing the interiority the novel withholds. Each passes the retelling test. Each returns the reader to the source with sharpened attention.

The gallery of Gatsbys assembled here, the magician, the self-maker, the body, the voice, is itself the argument's final form. No single retelling could have supplied it, because each version's insight is partial by design, a strong reading rather than a complete one. Together they describe Fitzgerald's deliberate blank from four directions, and the composite portrait is larger than any of its contributors. That is what a living classic looks like: not a monument with one authorized meaning, but a house with many rooms, each opened by a reader who brought her own key.

The complication has been honored rather than evaded. These retellings do not replace the original, and the strongest of them do not try. They depend on it, lean on the reader's memory of it, and derive their charge from the stereoscopic reading the form requires. A reader who knew nothing of Gatsby could enjoy Vo's fantasy or McLemore's romance or Nixon's dance on their own terms, but the full charge of each requires the original showing through like an underdrawing beneath paint. That dependence is not a weakness. It is the form's honesty. The retelling admits what the adaptation sometimes conceals: that it is an act of reading, partial and interested, and that its authority comes from the quality of its attention rather than from any claim to supersede.

The vandalism objection deserves its answer, because it is the serious form of the resistance. The objection holds that the classic is a finished achievement, that rewriting it from the margins is an act of cultural vandalism, importing present-day pieties into a work that earned its permanence by being itself. The retellings' answer is in their method. None of the versions examined here claims Fitzgerald should have written differently; each claims that his book contains more than his telling released. The vandal breaks what he touches. The reteller, at her best, is closer to the restorer who cleans a painting and finds colors the varnish had hidden. The charge of presentism misunderstands the direction of the work: Vo does not drag 1922 into the present but sends a present-day consciousness back into 1922 to report what it finds there, and what it finds, the racism, the queer charge, the bargains, was in the original all along. A retelling that invented its discoveries would be propaganda. A retelling that uncovers them is criticism, and criticism is not vandalism but attention.

The series thread holds. Reception as illumination is not a slogan but a description of what these books do. Each retelling is a record of a strong reader's encounter with Fitzgerald, made public and made durable, and the record shows the novel to be larger than its first telling. The silences this article catalogued, Jordan's interior, the Wilsons' class, the racial noise, the queer charge, Gatsby's blank past, are not flaws the retellings correct. They are capacities the retellings activate, rooms in Fitzgerald's mansion that he built and left locked, waiting for other hands with other keys. The chol hong nguyen retelling and others, as this series files them, are those other hands, and the keys they carry are the perspectives the twentieth century would not publish and the twenty-first finally could. The mansion stands. It is simply larger, now, than anyone who lived in it first had imagined.

What comes next is already legible in the pattern. The cluster's unfinished business, the Wilsons' unopened room, the working-class Gatsby no major retelling has yet attempted, marks the frontier the next wave will have to cross, because the logic of recovery does not stop at the margins the present moment finds comfortable. Each generation of retellers opens the rooms its own moment can imagine entering, and each leaves the rest for its successors. The test will not change: not novelty, not replacement, but illumination, the strong light carried into the next locked room, and the return trip afterward, when Fitzgerald's familiar house looks, once again, like somewhere the reader has never quite been.

## Studying the Retellings

A retelling rewards the comparative reader, the reader willing to hold two versions in mind at once and watch them argue. The study method this article recommends is simple and demanding: read a passage of Fitzgerald, then read the corresponding passage of the retelling, and ask what changed, what the change costs, and what it buys. Take the Plaza Hotel confrontation, the novel's great scene of male rivalry, and set it beside Vo's version, where the triangle's geometry has shifted and every line of desire crosses another. The comparison teaches more about both books than either book teaches alone, because the difference between them is a critical argument made flesh. The student who can say exactly what Vo kept, what she altered, and why the alteration matters has understood something about Fitzgerald that no summary could deliver.

A worked example shows the method's yield. Take the green light, the novel's most famous symbol. In Fitzgerald it is Daisy's dock light, the orgastic future, the dream's visible edge. In Vo it persists, but Jordan's narration reframes it: the light is also the mark of everything the dream excludes, the green of money and envy seen by someone the money was never meant to include. In McLemore it persists too, but Nick's trans consciousness makes it a figure for the self he is building, the future receding before him in a different sense. In Woodman-Maynard it is drawn, a literal green smudge across the bay, and the drawing asks whether the symbol survives literal depiction. Three retellings, three readings, one light. The student who can articulate all three has not merely compared versions. She has done criticism.

The same method works across the cluster. Read Nick's first description of Gatsby's parties beside Woodman-Maynard's watercolor rendering of the same scene, and ask which details the artist chose to draw large and which she let dissolve into wash; the choices are a reading of Fitzgerald's descriptive priorities. Watch Nixon's dancers perform the confrontation that the novel stages in dialogue, and ask what the bodies say that the words do not. Listen, in memory or in recording, to the Broadway score's treatment of Gatsby's longing, and ask whether the sung version makes the character more or less sympathetic than the novel's performed version. Each comparison is a small seminar, and the seminar's subject is always the same: what the original contains that a single reading cannot exhaust.

For readers who want to work at the level of the sentence, the most useful instrument is a text that shows its workings. The [annotated edition of the novel](https://vaultbook.net/tools/great-gatsby-annotated-text.html), with its notes on sources, allusions, and textual variants, lets the comparative reader check exactly what Fitzgerald wrote before asking what the reteller changed. The habit of verification matters because retellings trade on the reader's memory, and memory is a careless scholar. The retelling that seems to invent may be remembering; the passage that seems remembered may be invented. The annotated text settles the question, and the settled question sharpens the next one, which is always why. Why did Vo keep this scene whole and cut that one to a glance. Why did McLemore preserve the green light and relocate its meaning. The why is where the illumination lives, and the illumination is the point of the whole enterprise. Read the retellings against the source, keep the source open on the desk, and let the two books interrogate each other. That is the study this article's cluster was built to support.

The method can be formalized into three questions to carry to any retelling. First, what did the new version keep, and what does the keeping reveal about the original's load-bearing walls. Second, what did it change, and whether the change serves the story's meaning or merely its novelty. Third, what does the original look like on the return trip, after the retelling has done its work. A reader who can answer all three for Vo, for McLemore, and for one stage version has not just consumed the cluster. She has learned to read the way retellers read, which is to say, with the whole tradition at her back and her own moment in her hands.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What are the notable modern retellings of The Great Gatsby?

The notable modern retellings begin with Nghi Vo's The Chosen and the Beautiful (2021), the queer Vietnamese-American Jordan Baker retelling that anchors the post-public-domain wave, and Anna-Marie McLemore's Self-Made Boys: A Great Gatsby Remix (2022), the trans Latinx remix in the Remixed Classics series. K. Woodman-Maynard's watercolor graphic novel adaptation (2021) retells the story visually, emphasizing the fluidity of Nick's narration. On stage, the Northern Ballet's full-length Gatsby (2013), choreographed by David Nixon, retells the story without words, while the Broadway musical (2024), with a book by Kait Kerrigan and a score by Jason Howland and Nathan Tysen, retells it in song. Each keeps Fitzgerald's plot skeleton and changes its center of gravity. The list is not closed: the public-domain era keeps producing new entries, but these are the versions that have earned critical standing and that this article measures against its test.

### Q: What is The Chosen and the Beautiful?

The Chosen and the Beautiful is Nghi Vo's debut novel, published in June 2021, and the most acclaimed modern retelling of The Great Gatsby. It retells Fitzgerald's story through the narration of Jordan Baker, reimagined as a queer Vietnamese-American woman adopted as a child by a wealthy Louisville family after being found in wartime Tonkin. Set in a Jazz Age where magic is real, the novel gives Jordan a paper-cutting magic rooted in her heritage, populates the smart set with infernal bargainers marked by blackened fingernails, and reconfigures the famous love triangle so that Nick and Gatsby fall for each other. It earned a Goodreads Choice Award nomination in fantasy for 2021 and wide critical praise for balancing invention with fidelity. Its influence now extends beyond its own pages: later retellings are routinely measured against its standard, and its method, the marginal consciousness moved to the center, has become the template for the serious literary retelling.

### Q: How does the retelling recast Jordan Baker?

The retelling recasts Jordan Baker from Fitzgerald's cool, opaque athlete into the novel's narrating consciousness, a queer Vietnamese-American adoptee suspended between white Louisville wealth and the Vietnamese heritage she cannot remember. Vo keeps the original's facts, the golf, the fast driving, the small dishonesties, the affair with Nick, and supplies the interior Fitzgerald withheld: the dishonesty becomes a survival skill learned by a child whose origins were overwritten by her adoptive family, the coolness becomes the discipline of someone for whom any lapse would read as foreignness. Through Jordan's eyes Daisy becomes a case study in the limits of white female solidarity, and the novel's tragedy redistributes from Gatsby's dream to the costs paid by everyone the dream touched.

### Q: How do modern retellings recover silenced perspectives?

Modern retellings recover silenced perspectives by moving the narrative camera to positions Fitzgerald's novel never occupied. Vo centers Jordan Baker, giving voice, history, and magic to the character the original kept as a surface. McLemore centers trans and Latinx identities, making literal the self-invention Fitzgerald treated as Gatsby's ambiguous performance and giving Daisy a passing narrative that exposes the racial foundations of her power. The graphic novel recovers the reader's act of picturing as interpretation, the ballet recovers the physical plot of bodies and violence, and the musical recovers the characters' buried interiority in song. Each recovery targets a specific silence, race, queerness, class, gender, or the narrator's evasions, and fills it without falsifying the source. The method's discipline is what separates recovery from invention: the retelling may supply the interior, but it may not contradict the facts, and the tension between those two obligations is where the illumination happens.

### Q: Are retellings mere novelty or replacements?

Retellings are neither mere novelty nor replacements, and the strongest of them refuse both roles. A novelty retelling would dress the old plot in new costumes for the surprise of recognition; a replacement would ask the reader to forget Fitzgerald. The acclaimed retellings do something harder: they assume the reader knows the original, lean on that knowledge, and rearrange it so the familiar events carry unfamiliar weight. Their full charge depends on the reader holding both versions at once, the source showing through the new like an underdrawing. They function as acts of reading made public, and their test is the return trip: if Fitzgerald reads differently afterward, with previously invisible machinery now impossible to unsee, the retelling has illuminated rather than merely borrowed or replaced.

### Q: How do retellings test the story against new identities and forms?

Retellings test the story by holding its skeleton fixed while changing the consciousness or medium that carries it, then observing what survives and what transforms. Vo keeps the 1922 plot and filters it through a queer Vietnamese-American Jordan, testing whether the novel's tragedy holds when its center is marginal rather than male and Midwestern. McLemore keeps the setting and makes Nick and Gatsby transgender, testing the novel's mythology of self-invention against the literal labor of self-creation. The graphic novel, ballet, and musical keep the events and change the medium, testing which meanings live in prose and which migrate to image, body, or song. What survives every test, the green light's ache, the cost of the dream, is the story's durable core. The failures are instructive too: a retelling that changes the surfaces without touching the meanings reveals, by its thinness, exactly where the original's power lives.

### Q: Why did so many Gatsby retellings appear after 2021?

The cluster follows the expiry of the novel's United States copyright on the first day of 2021, which removed the permission gate that had governed Gatsby adaptation for ninety-five years. Before the expiry, any retelling needed the rights holders' blessing, and blessings come with conditions about what the property may decently become. After the expiry, the only remaining gate was the writer's nerve. K. Woodman-Maynard's graphic novel appeared on January 5, 2021, four days after the opening, and Nghi Vo's novel followed in June, with McLemore's remix in 2022. The timing is not coincidental: the flood is the sound a classic makes when it stops being property and starts being material, and the new freedom favored exactly the irreverent, marginal-centered versions the old gate would have kept out.

### Q: Who is Nghi Vo?

Nghi Vo is the author of The Chosen and the Beautiful, her debut novel, published in June 2021. Born in Illinois and living on the shores of Lake Michigan, she had previously published the acclaimed novellas The Empress of Salt and Fortune and When the Tiger Came Down the Mountain before taking on Fitzgerald. Her publisher for the Gatsby retelling was Tordotcom, a science fiction and fantasy house, which signals how the book was positioned: as a fantasy novel that happened to be Gatsby rather than a literary homage with magical garnish. The novel's success established her as a major new literary voice, and its method, grafting new material onto a canonical plot at exactly the seam where invention illuminates rather than overwrites, has become the reference point for the retelling wave it helped begin.

### Q: What fantasy elements appear in The Chosen and the Beautiful?

The novel's Jazz Age runs on three interlocking magical systems. The smart set makes bargains with infernal powers, marking themselves with a single black-painted fingernail, a visible brand for the invisible deals Fitzgerald's characters make socially. A drug-like drink called demonaic circulates at parties, sipped straight or slipped into liquor, literalizing the moral solvent of the original's constant drinking. Jordan herself practices paper magic rooted in her Vietnamese heritage, cutting shapes from paper and breathing life into them, a power the novel names with the phrase "in all paper is fire," culminating in an emotional late scene involving a cut-paper heart. None of this is decoration: each element literalizes something the original keeps figurative, turning the novel's moral economy into visible marks the reader can finally see. The systems interlock: the bargains price the soul, the demonaic dissolves the will, and the paper magic offers the one power that creates rather than consumes, which is why Jordan's art becomes the novel's moral center.

### Q: How does the novel portray Jordan Baker's queerness?

The novel portrays Jordan's queerness as a frank, lived fact rather than a revelation or a problem. She moves through gay speakeasies, takes lovers of both sexes with the ease of a woman whose outsider status has freed her from the performance of respectable femininity, and loves Daisy with an intensity the book refuses to reduce to friendship or rivalry. The most discussed revision reconfigures the love triangle: Nick and Gatsby fall for each other, so the famous pursuit of Daisy becomes a tangle in which every line of desire crosses another. This is not provocation but development, bringing up what was latent in Fitzgerald's erotics, Nick's lingering gaze at Gatsby's body, the suggestive McKee afternoon, Gatsby's self-invented masculinity, and giving it the explicitness the original's manners forbade.

### Q: What is Self-Made Boys: A Great Gatsby Remix?

Self-Made Boys is Anna-Marie McLemore's 2022 young-adult retelling, the fifth volume of Feiwel and Friends' Remixed Classics series, and the cluster's proof that Vo's recovery could be repeated on different silences. Nicolás Caraveo, the Nick figure, is a seventeen-year-old transgender Latinx boy from Minnesota; his cousin Daisy Fabrega has erased her Latina heritage to pass as white under the name Daisy Fay; and Jay Gatsby is also transgender, a boy who made himself from nothing. The title takes the novel's cliché about Gatsby at face value: her boys are self-made in the sense of having authored their own genders. It earned a Booklist starred review and a National Book Award longlist place, the establishment's signal that the book argues with its source rather than merely borrowing it.

### Q: How does the Remixed Classics series approach canonical novels?

The Remixed Classics series, published by Feiwel and Friends, commissions diverse authors to reimagine canonical works through marginalized identities, pairing each classic with a writer whose perspective the original excluded. The Gatsby volume went to Anna-Marie McLemore, a Stonewall Honor recipient, who rebuilt the novel around trans and Latinx characters. The series method is closer to Vo's than to a simple diversification: the commissioned author is expected to find the structural rhyme between the classic's concerns and the new perspective, so that the retelling reads as an argument about the source rather than a costume change. Self-Made Boys exceeds the frame by locating that rhyme precisely, in the parallel between Gatsby's self-invention and a trans boy's self-creation, and playing it for the novel's full length. The series now spans multiple classics, but the Gatsby volume remains its most argued-over, because the source is the most mythologized and therefore the hardest to re-see.

### Q: Which graphic novel adaptations of Gatsby are notable?

The notable graphic novel is K. Woodman-Maynard's The Great Gatsby: A Graphic Novel Adaptation, published by Candlewick Press in January 2021, among the first adaptations of the book in the genre. Painted in lush watercolors across 240 pages, it follows Fitzgerald's plot faithfully while weaving excerpts of the original prose through its panels, and its interpretive signature is the literalization of metaphor: a partygoer blooms into an orchid, Daisy pushes Gatsby across the sky on a cloud. The device is critical rather than whimsical, a stress test proving which of Fitzgerald's images are structural enough to survive being drawn. The book also makes Nick's unreliable narration visible, shifting his figure's scale and solidity, and it has found a second life as a teaching tool for engaging students with the classic.

### Q: Has Gatsby been retold on stage?

Gatsby has been retold on stage in at least two major versions that belong to this article's cluster. The Northern Ballet's full-length The Great Gatsby, choreographed and directed by David Nixon with music by Sir Richard Rodney Bennett, premiered at Leeds Grand Theatre in March 2013 and has toured repeatedly since, telling the story entirely without words and recovering its physical plot of bodies, desire, and violence. The Great Gatsby: A New Musical, with a book by Kait Kerrigan and music and lyrics by Jason Howland and Nathan Tysen, premiered at Paper Mill Playhouse in 2023 and opened on Broadway in April 2024, the novel's first Broadway musical staging, winning the Tony Award for Best Costume Design of a Musical. The ballet dances what the novel withholds physically; the musical sings what it withholds emotionally. Both premiered to the skepticism that greets any classic in a new medium, and both answered it the same way, by making the audience feel the story's weight in the body or the ear rather than merely remember it.

### Q: What does the Northern Ballet's Gatsby add to the story?

The ballet adds the body's knowledge, the dimension Fitzgerald's voice-driven narration cannot carry. Without dialogue, Nixon's dancers communicate the parties as pure physical fact, the Charleston and tango sequences arriving unmediated by Nick's commentary, and the Plaza Hotel confrontation becomes a contest of physical presence, Tom's weight against Gatsby's reach, with Daisy passed between them like a disputed object. The wordlessness also rescues Myrtle Wilson from Nick's condescension: she cannot be talked down to when no one talks, so she is danced instead, with an erotic vitality the prose rations. The production earned Nixon a 2014 National Dance Awards nomination for Best Classical Choreography. Its addition is a redistribution of weight rather than a change of plot, proving that retelling can happen entirely through the medium.

### Q: Do retellings keep the 1922 setting or modernize it?

The strongest retellings in the cluster keep the 1922 setting, and the discipline is deliberate. Vo, McLemore, and Woodman-Maynard all stage their revisions in Fitzgerald's Jazz Age rather than updating the story to the present, forcing their new identities to contend with the historical world's hostility: Vo's Jordan faces the era's racism and exclusion laws, McLemore's boys inhabit a world with no language for what they are. The pastness functions as pressure, and the pressure makes the recoveries feel earned rather than decorative. A modernized Gatsby would let its marginal characters breathe easily; a 1922 Gatsby makes them fight for air, and the fight is the point. The stage versions keep the period for the same reason, in costume, score, and choreography. The setting is not nostalgia. It is the obstacle course.

### Q: How do retellings handle Nick Carraway's narration?

Retellings handle Nick by demoting, redistributing, or making visible the authority his narration claims. Vo demotes him outright: Jordan takes the pen, and Nick becomes a character observed from outside, his judgments and blind spots newly legible. McLemore redistributes the authority by making her Nick a seventeen-year-old trans boy whose perspective is partial in a different way, young, desiring, and unversed in the world's codes. Woodman-Maynard makes the authority visible, drawing Nick's figure shifting in scale and solidity so the reader watches unreliability happen. The musical bypasses him, giving the characters songs that state what Nick's narration only circles. Each strategy targets the same silence, the gap between what Nick sees and what the reader suspects, and each fills it by refusing to let one consciousness own the story. The cumulative effect is democratic: after the cluster, no reader can mistake Nick's version for the whole truth, and the novel's famous unreliability becomes a shared premise rather than a critical discovery.

### Q: What do retellings reveal about Daisy Buchanan?

Retellings reveal Daisy as the novel's great unexamined power, and they examine her from complementary angles. Through Vo's Jordan, Daisy becomes a case study in the limits of white female solidarity: the best friend whose affection is real and whose power is lethal, who could afford kindness because it cost her nothing and who retreated into money when the bill came due. Through McLemore's Daisy Fabrega, her whiteness becomes a plot rather than a ground: the Latina past she erased to become Daisy Fay exposes the labor underneath the poise and gives the famous voice full of money a purchase history. The musical gives her songs, which in the form's contract is a claim to interiority, letting her articulate calculations Nick only guesses at. Together the versions convert the novel's symbol into a person.

### Q: What is the difference between a retelling and a sequel?

A retelling keeps Fitzgerald's story and changes its telling: the same summer of 1922, the same parties, the same deaths, replayed from a new center of gravity so the familiar events carry unfamiliar weight. A sequel or prequel leaves the story's boundaries and invents beyond them, giving the characters futures or pasts the novel never staged. The distinction determines the book's question. The retelling asks what the known story means; the sequel asks what happened next. Michael Farris Smith's Nick (2021), which invents Nick Carraway's war years, is a prequel and belongs to the sequels inquiry, while Vo's and McLemore's novels, which stay inside Fitzgerald's summer, belong here. The working rule is simple: if the new version's interest is the old story's meaning, it is a retelling. The border has its smugglers, works that do both, but the question sorts them: ask what the book wants you to wonder, what it means or what happened next, and the answer places it.

### Q: Where should a reader begin with Gatsby retellings?

Begin with Nghi Vo's The Chosen and the Beautiful, because it sets the standard the others are measured against and because it assumes the reader's knowledge of Fitzgerald most productively. Read it against the original with the comparative method: note what Vo keeps whole, what she alters, and what the alteration reveals about the source. Then take McLemore's Self-Made Boys for the proof that the recovery travels to different silences, Woodman-Maynard's graphic novel for the visual argument about narration and metaphor, and the stage versions for the recoveries of body and song. Keep Fitzgerald open on the desk throughout. The retellings' full charge depends on the reader's double exposure, the original showing through the new like a pentimento, and the reader who holds both in mind is the one the whole cluster was written for.
