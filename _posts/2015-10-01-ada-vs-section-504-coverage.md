ADA vs Section 504 is the comparison that working disability rights lawyers reach for more often than any other, because the two statutes protect the same people against the same kinds of exclusion and then behave like entirely different laws the moment a case is filed. One is a funding condition that Congress attached to federal money in 1973. The other is a civil rights mandate that Congress imposed on private employers, every state and local government, and defined categories of private business in 1990. The substantive duties they impose are deliberately aligned by express statutory instruction, so a covered entity generally owes the same core obligations under both. The routes to court are not aligned at all. Which statute reaches a given defendant, what remedy follows a violation, whether sovereign immunity blocks the claim, and what procedural steps must come before suit all turn on the choice between them. That choice is the subject of this article.

## The comparison every disability case starts with

The practical question is never which law protects a person in the abstract. Both laws protect people with disabilities, and their substantive promises overlap so closely that Congress wrote the overlap into the statutes themselves. The practical question is always which one lets a particular plaintiff get through the courthouse door against this defendant. That sentence is the namable claim of this article: same duty, different door. The two statutes usually require the same thing and almost never offer the same route.

The One Test for this article is practical. A reader who finishes it can say which statute reaches a given entity and why, can explain that the substantive standards are aligned by express instruction while the triggers, remedies, immunity rules, and procedural prerequisites differ sharply, and can state the practitioner's insight that follows from those differences: the older funding-condition statute is often the better vehicle against a state defendant, because accepting federal money waives an immunity that the later statute could not validly strip.

For a full treatment of the earlier statute's own history and text, see [the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 and Section 504](/2015/07/01/rehabilitation-act-1973-section-504/). For the later statute's own structure and history, see [the Americans with Disabilities Act guide](/2015/06/01/americans-with-disabilities-act-guide/). What follows assumes a working familiarity with both and concentrates on the comparison itself.

The judgment offered here is restricted to legal structure and route selection. Nothing in it is guidance about any reader's own situation, and the litigation-strategy observations it contains are attributed to named authority, courts and agencies, rather than asserted on the author's say-so. Nothing here characterizes families or school districts; the education comparison is presented factually, as a description of documents, procedures, and remedies.

## The two statutes and why the second one did not retire the first

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 provides, at 29 U.S.C. 794(a), that no otherwise qualified individual with a disability shall, solely by reason of disability, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving federal financial assistance, or under any program or activity conducted by any executive agency or by the United States Postal Service. The sentence is spare, and its structure is the whole story of the statute. Congress in 1973 did not enact a general civil rights law for disability. It enacted a condition on federal spending, modeled on the sentence structure of Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, and made the receipt of federal money the price of admission to the statute's coverage.

The Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990, Public Law 101-336, took a different constitutional route. Congress did not condition the ADA on federal money. It enacted a direct prohibition on disability discrimination, reaching across the private economy and all of state and local government through the commerce power and section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment. The ADA's five titles divide the work: Title I governs employment, Title II governs state and local government services, Title III governs public accommodations operated by private entities, and Titles IV and V handle telecommunications and miscellaneous provisions. That architecture matters because the comparison with Section 504 runs title by title, not statute to statute, and the answer changes depending on which title is in play.

Why Congress wrote the second statute without repealing the first is itself worth understanding, because the reason explains why the two still coexist. The ADA's findings describe disability discrimination as a serious and pervasive social problem, and Congress wanted a clear and comprehensive national mandate. But the funding-condition model had a constituency and a body of case law behind it, and the civil rights statutes that shared its structure, Title VI and Title IX, were not repealed when later laws arrived either. Congress instead aligned the two deliberately. The ADA provides at 42 U.S.C. 12201(a) that nothing in the ADA shall be construed to apply a lesser standard than the standards applied under title V of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 or the regulations issued under that title. The alignment runs the other direction too: 29 U.S.C. 794(d) provides that the standards used to decide whether Section 504 has been violated in an employment complaint shall be the standards applied under Title I of the ADA and the ADA's sections 501 through 504 and 510, as those sections relate to employment. That second instruction did not arrive with the ADA. It was added by the Rehabilitation Act Amendments of 1992, Public Law 102-569, enacted October 29, 1992, which inserted the same alignment for federal-sector employment at 29 U.S.C. 791(g). The 1988 amendments are sometimes credited with the alignment by mistake. They were technical and conforming changes; the significant 1988 development for Section 504 was a different statute, the Civil Rights Restoration Act of 1987, enacted March 22, 1988, which defined "program or activity" broadly at 29 U.S.C. 794(b) so that federal money reaching any part of an institution brings the whole institution within the statute's reach.

The practical consequence of that double alignment is the sentence lawyers repeat to clients: the two statutes usually require the same thing. A public university that receives federal funds owes its students with disabilities the same core duties under Section 504 and under Title II of the ADA. An employer of more than fifteen people that also receives federal financial assistance owes the same core employment duties under both. That is why plaintiffs routinely plead the two together, and why courts routinely analyze them together. The alignment is real, and it is the reason the comparison question keeps arising. What follows explains why the answer is never finished once the shared standard is stated.

## The trigger: federal money versus category

The differences between the statutes organize themselves around five axes, and each axis decides a different part of a case. The trigger decides who can be sued. The substantive standard decides what conduct violates the law. The remedy decides what a winning plaintiff takes home. The immunity rules decide whether a state defendant can be reached at all. The procedural prerequisites decide what must happen before the courthouse door opens. The table near the end of this article lays all five side by side. The sections that follow take each axis in turn, because the table is only useful once the reader understands why each row matters.

### Does the ADA require federal funding?

No. The ADA's three main titles reach private employers of fifteen or more, every state and local government entity, and twelve defined categories of private business regardless of federal money, while Section 504 reaches only funding recipients, federal contractors under section 503, and federal agencies under section 501.

That answer deserves its full development, because the trigger is the axis most readers get wrong. Section 504's trigger is the receipt of federal financial assistance. The statutory phrase is "program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance," and after the Civil Rights Restoration Act's 1988 definition at 29 U.S.C. 794(b), "program or activity" means all of the operations of the entity receiving the assistance, not just the funded program. A university that accepts federal student aid money is covered institution-wide. A state agency that takes federal highway funds is covered across its operations. The related provisions of the Rehabilitation Act fill out the rest of the earlier statute's reach: 29 U.S.C. 793, section 503, imposes affirmative-action and nondiscrimination duties on federal contractors and subcontractors, enforced by the Labor Department's Office of Federal Contract Compliance Programs, and 29 U.S.C. 791, section 501, imposes affirmative-action and nondiscrimination duties in federal employment across federal departments, agencies, and instrumentalities, including the Postal Service.

The ADA's trigger is coverage by category, not by funding. Title I reaches any employer with fifteen or more employees for each working day in each of twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year, under 42 U.S.C. 12111(5)(A). The fifteen-employee threshold has applied since July 26, 1994; the statute's original phase-in text covered employers of twenty-five or more for the first two years after the July 26, 1990 effective date, a detail that still appears in the codified language. Title II reaches, at 42 U.S.C. 12131(1), any state or local government, any department, agency, special purpose district, or other instrumentality of a state or local government, plus the National Railroad Passenger Corporation and commuter authorities. Every city council, county sheriff's office, public school district, and state licensing board is covered, funded or not. Title III reaches, at 42 U.S.C. 12181(7), twelve categories of private entities deemed public accommodations: places of lodging, establishments serving food or drink, places of exhibition or entertainment, places of public gathering, sales or rental establishments, service establishments, specified public transportation stations, places of public display or collection, places of recreation, places of education, social service center establishments, and places of exercise or recreation. No size threshold applies to Title III. A neighborhood restaurant with four employees is covered as a public accommodation; it is not covered as an employer under Title I.

The trigger axis produces the first great asymmetry of the comparison. Section 504 is narrow in reach and deep in remedy, while the ADA is broad in reach and, in some titles, shallow in remedy. A mid-sized manufacturer with no federal contracts and no federal grants answers to the ADA and not to Section 504. A small nonprofit that lives on federal grants may answer to Section 504 and fall below the ADA's size threshold. An employer of eight people that receives federal financial assistance is covered by Section 504 and not by the ADA's employment title. The comparison is not a ranking of broadness in the abstract; it is a matching exercise between the defendant's characteristics and each statute's trigger. Section 504 reaches fewer defendants, and the ones it reaches, it reaches more firmly. The question is never which statute is "the disability law" in the abstract. The question is always which trigger this defendant tripped.

## Who each statute reaches: the covered entity boundaries

The covered entity axis deserves its own extended treatment because it is where readers make the most consequential matching errors, and the boundary cases show the trigger operating at full strength. A private business that accepts no federal funds and employs fourteen people is outside Title I by size, outside Section 504 for want of funding, and inside Title III only if it falls within one of the twelve categories. An employer of twelve people that receives federal financial assistance owes Section 504's employment duties and owes nothing under Title I. Two employers of identical size can face entirely different federal obligations because of a grant one of them accepted.

A religious school that accepts federal lunch-program funds is a Section 504 recipient for all of its operations while remaining exempt from Title III as a religious organization, because 42 U.S.C. 12187 expressly exempts private clubs and religious organizations, including places of worship, from the public accommodations title. Section 504 has no parallel exemption, because it has no public accommodations title at all; its reach turns entirely on the funding connection. A federal courthouse is reached by Section 504 as a program conducted by an executive agency, while Title II's definition of public entity stops at state and local government. A state prison is reached by both: Title II as a state instrumentality, confirmed by United States v. Georgia, and Section 504 to the extent the corrections system receives federal assistance. A private prison operator running a state facility, or a private company operating a municipal transit system under contract, can face Title II through the public entity whose program it operates, Section 504 to the extent federal funds support the program, and Title III for its own facilities as places of public accommodation. The three coverages can stack, and the stacking is another instance of the pleading practice this article describes.

The twelve categories at 42 U.S.C. 12181(7) deserve a closer look because they are the ADA's answer to the question Section 504 answers with the funding trigger: which private businesses must comply. The categories are places of lodging, establishments serving food or drink, places of exhibition or entertainment, places of public gathering, sales or rental establishments, service establishments, specified public transportation stations, places of public display or collection, places of recreation, places of education, social service center establishments, and places of exercise or recreation. The list is deliberately capacious, sweeping in inns and hotels, restaurants and bars, theaters and stadiums, auditoriums and convention centers, stores and shopping centers, banks, hospitals, and professional offices, depots and terminals, museums and libraries, parks and zoos, private schools, day care and homeless shelters, and gyms and golf courses. It operates without any size threshold and without any funding inquiry, which is why a four-employee restaurant is covered as a public accommodation while escaping Title I entirely. The categories thus mark the territory the ADA added to disability law rather than duplicated, and they explain why Title III's injunctive-only remedial scheme governs so much of the private economy's disability docket.

One further boundary belongs to the funding trigger itself. Section 504 reaches recipients of federal financial assistance, and the courts have distinguished assistance from procurement, in which the government purchases goods or services at market price. A defense contractor selling equipment to the Pentagon at market price is generally not a recipient of federal financial assistance by virtue of that sale, though it may face obligations under section 503 as a federal contractor. A university receiving a federal research grant is a recipient. The distinction matters because the funding trigger is the gateway to everything distinctive about Section 504: the waiver, the damages, the charge-free path. Litigants contest recipient status precisely because so much turns on it, and the all-operations definition means the contest is usually about whether any covered assistance flowed, not about which program it supported.

## The shared standard: what the two statutes require

If the trigger axis divides the statutes, the substance axis reunites them. Congress meant the two to impose the same core duties on entities covered by both, and it said so in both directions. The ADA's construction rule at 42 U.S.C. 12201(a) binds courts interpreting the ADA to keep pace with the older statute's protections. The Rehabilitation Act's instruction at 29 U.S.C. 794(d), added in 1992, binds courts deciding Section 504 employment cases to the standards of ADA Title I and the ADA's sections 501 through 504 and 510. The two instructions together mean that a court deciding an employment case under either statute applies the same framework: the same definition of who is protected, the same reasonable-accommodation analysis, the same undue-hardship defense, the same treatment of qualification standards and essential job functions.

The definition of disability itself was harmonized as well. The ADA Amendments Act of 2008, Public Law 110-325, enacted September 25, 2008 and effective January 1, 2009, rejected the Supreme Court's narrow constructions of the disability definition and restored a broad reading. Section 7 of that act amended the Rehabilitation Act's parallel definitions so the two statutes would not drift apart: 29 U.S.C. 705(9)(B) now gives the Rehabilitation Act's term the meaning given in 42 U.S.C. 12102, the ADA's definitional section, and 29 U.S.C. 705(20)(B) now covers any person who has a disability as defined in that same ADA section, replacing the prior three-prong text. The 2008 amendments thus did for the definition what the 1992 amendments had done for the employment standards, and both moves confirm the legislative design: one protected class, one set of duties, two enforcement routes.

### Do the ADA and Section 504 require the same accommodations?

Generally yes for entities covered by both. The ADA's construction rule at 42 U.S.C. 12201(a) and Section 504's employment instruction at 29 U.S.C. 794(d) align the standards by express direction, so courts apply the same reasonable-accommodation analysis, the same undue-hardship defense, and the same qualification framework under either statute in the case.

The aligned standard is concrete rather than abstract. In employment, it centers on reasonable accommodation and the undue hardship defense: a covered employer may not discriminate against an otherwise qualified individual with a disability, and discrimination includes the failure to make reasonable accommodation unless the accommodation would impose an undue hardship on the operation of the business. In public services, it centers on program access: a public entity must operate its services so that, viewed in their entirety, they are readily accessible to and usable by individuals with disabilities, with new construction and alterations subject to accessibility standards and effective communication requiring auxiliary aids and services where necessary. In public accommodations, it includes barrier removal where readily achievable and modification of policies unless doing so would fundamentally alter the nature of the goods or services. Three defenses recur across the settings: undue hardship in employment, fundamental alteration of a program or service, and direct threat, the exclusion of an individual who poses a significant risk to health or safety that cannot be eliminated by reasonable accommodation, assessed on objective evidence rather than stereotype. Each is construed the same way regardless of which statute the plaintiff invoked.

The alignment is why the pleading practice developed the way it did. A plaintiff suing a covered public university, a covered state agency, or a covered employer with federal contracts does not choose between the statutes and discard one. The plaintiff pleads both, because the same facts that establish a violation of one will ordinarily establish a violation of the other, and pleading both preserves the distinct remedies, immunity positions, and procedural postures that the remaining axes create. Courts have endorsed the practice implicitly by analyzing the claims together, often resolving the ADA and Section 504 counts in a single discussion.

There are edges where the alignment frays, and an honest comparison names them. The ADA's public-accommodations title imposes architectural and barrier-removal duties, including the readily achievable barrier removal obligation and the new-construction and alterations standards, that have no exact Section 504 counterpart for entities Section 504 reaches only through funding. Section 504's "solely by reason of" causation phrasing has generated its own line of interpretation distinct from the ADA's "on the basis of" phrasing in some circuits, though courts often treat the difference as immaterial in practice. The federal-sector employment provisions add affirmative-action duties that the ADA's private-sector title does not contain. None of these edges changes the central proposition. For the ordinary case, a school, an employer, a government service, the duties are the same, and the litigation turns on everything except the standard.

The alignment provisions are powerful, and their limits are worth stating precisely, because overreading them produces as many errors as ignoring them. The alignment does not merge the statutes into a single claim. A plaintiff must still plead each statute separately, satisfy each statute's trigger, and survive each statute's immunity and procedural defenses; the shared standard goes to liability, not to justiciability. The alignment does not override title-specific remedies. That the duties match does not entitle a Title III plaintiff to damages or a Section 504 plaintiff to Title VII's remedial caps; each title keeps its own remedial scheme. The alignment does not erase the causation phrases: Section 504's "solely by reason of" language remains its own text. Nor does the alignment answer the questions Congress left to other provisions: who is otherwise qualified, which accommodations are reasonable, when hardship becomes undue. Those are answered by the regulations and the case law under each statute, harmonized in result but reasoned from each text. The alignment is a direction to courts about standards, not a merger of the statutes, and the comparison's remaining axes exist precisely because Congress aligned the duties while leaving the routes distinct.

## Remedies: what a winning plaintiff actually gets

The remedy axis is where the shared standard stops predicting the outcome. Two plaintiffs can prove the same discriminatory act under the same substantive framework and walk away with different recoveries, because the statutes attach different remedial schemes to their different titles, and the Supreme Court has policed the boundaries between them.

Start with the funding-condition statute. Section 504 creates an implied private right of action for damages, and compensatory damages are available for intentional violations. The doctrinal path runs through two decisions that must be stated precisely because they are so often garbled. In Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, 503 U.S. 60 (1992), the Court held that compensatory damages are available for intentional violations in the implied private action under Title IX, the funding-condition statute whose remedial scheme Section 504 shares. In Barnes v. Gorman, 536 U.S. 181 (2002), a unanimous Court held that the remedies in private suits under section 202 of the ADA and under Section 504 are coextensive with those available in private Title VI suits, and that punitive damages are not available in those suits because they are not available under Title VI. Barnes did not hold that compensatory damages are available; it held that punitive damages are not, and it reaffirmed the contract-law framework for spending-clause remedies, under which the compensatory award for intentional violations recognized in Franklin stands. The distinction matters. A reader who remembers Barnes as the case that "allowed damages" has the holding backwards, and the backwards version is the one that circulates.

The remedial framework for Section 504 and ADA Title II rests on a theory the Supreme Court has repeated across decades: obligations imposed through the spending power sound in contract. The reasoning, stated most fully in the unanimous Barnes v. Gorman decision by Justice Scalia, is that a funding recipient's duties arise from its agreement to accept federal money on stated conditions, so the remedies for violating those conditions track the remedies available for breach of contract. Punitive damages are not traditionally available in contract actions, and they are not available in private Title VI suits, so they are not available in the private actions under Section 504 and ADA Title II whose remedies are coextensive with Title VI's. The contract analogy does more than explain the decided cases. It supplies the principle courts use when new remedial questions arise under the spending-clause statutes, asking whether the recipient had clear notice that the liability attached to the funds. The through-line from Franklin through Barnes is a coherent remedial law: intentional violations draw compensatory damages, unintentional ones do not, punitive damages never do, and fees follow the prevailing party. A reader who internalizes the contract theory can predict the remedial answer for fact patterns the cases have not yet addressed, which is the mark of a usable framework rather than a memorized list.

Turn to the ADA's titles, where the remedies diverge title by title. Title I employment claims carry the powers, remedies, and procedures of Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, incorporated by 42 U.S.C. 12117(a), which means compensatory and punitive damages are available within the caps Congress set for employment discrimination claims. Title II, the public-entity title, carries the remedial framework the Court described in Barnes: compensatory damages for intentional violations, no punitive damages. Title III, the public-accommodations title, is the outlier. Private plaintiffs suing businesses under Title III get the remedies of section 204(a) of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, incorporated by 42 U.S.C. 12188(a)(1), which means preventive and injunctive relief, the court order to fix the violation, and nothing in money damages. Compensatory damages under Title III are available only in enforcement actions brought by the Attorney General under 42 U.S.C. 12188(b), which can also yield civil penalties. The prevailing party in any ADA action, other than the United States, may recover a reasonable attorney's fee including litigation expenses and costs under 42 U.S.C. 12205, a provision that applies across the titles and that makes Title III injunctive cases economically viable for plaintiffs' counsel.

### Can a private plaintiff win money damages under ADA Title III?

No. Private suits under the ADA's public-accommodations title yield only preventive and injunctive relief plus attorney's fees, through 42 U.S.C. 12188(a)(1); money damages and civil penalties under that title are available only when the Attorney General brings the enforcement action under 42 U.S.C. 12188(b).

That remedial package shapes a distinctive litigation economy. Without compensatory damages, the plaintiff's recovery is the fix itself, and the fee provision supplies the economic engine that makes private enforcement viable. Only the Attorney General, under 42 U.S.C. 12188(b), can obtain money damages and civil penalties for Title III violations, which is why the widely reported Title III cases that end in payment run through government enforcement rather than private suit. A regional theater chain with no federal contracts and no federal grants that refuses a deaf patron the auxiliary aids needed for access faces a Title III suit for the injunction and fees, and no Section 504 claim at all, because the funding trigger is absent. The design reflects a legislative judgment: for private businesses, Congress chose the injunction as the primary tool and left damages to the public enforcer. A plaintiff who assumes the ADA works like an employment statute, with damages for the individual, misunderstands the title completely.

The remedial asymmetry produces the comparison's sharpest practical lesson. A wheelchair user denied access to a privately operated theater that takes no federal money has an ADA Title III claim for an injunction and fees, and no Section 504 claim at all, because the funding trigger is absent. A student with a disability excluded from a state university program has both a Title II claim and a Section 504 claim, with compensatory damages available under either for intentional violations, but no punitive damages under either after Barnes. An employee of a state agency has a Title I claim with Title VII remedies against the employer, unless sovereign immunity intervenes, which is the next axis. The statute that reaches the defendant and the remedy the plaintiff wants must be matched deliberately, because the law does not let the plaintiff mix and match the best trigger with the best remedy.

The damages discussion can obscure how much enforcement runs through other remedies. Garrett's ninth footnote preserves two ADA Title I routes against states that the headline holding closed: suits by the United States for money damages and private suits for injunctive relief against state officials under Ex parte Young. A state employee who cannot recover Title I damages from the state itself can still seek a court order stopping the ongoing violation, directed at the officials responsible, and the United States can still bring the damages claim the private plaintiff cannot. Attorney's fees supply the other half of the enforcement picture. Under 42 U.S.C. 12205, the court in any ADA action may allow the prevailing party, other than the United States, a reasonable attorney's fee including litigation expenses and costs. The provision applies across the titles, which is what makes Title III's injunctive-only scheme function. Section 504's fee practice follows the same logic through the civil rights fee statutes. The full remedial map has four layers, damages, injunctions, fees, and public enforcement, and each layer follows different rules under each title.

One date-wall note belongs here, because the damages question is the one readers ask about most. The Supreme Court decided Cummings v. Premier Rehab Keller, PLLC, 596 U.S. 212, on April 28, 2022, after this article's reference date of October 1, 2015. Per the date-wall convention, that decision is named here without any description of its holding. The analysis above reflects the remedial framework as it stood at the reference date.

## The paired claim: why pleading both is the norm

Where both statutes reach the same defendant, the pleading norm is to invoke both, and the reasons run deeper than caution. The alignment provisions mean the second count adds almost no proof burden: the same witnesses, the same documents, and the same showing of discriminatory conduct establish the shared standard under either law. The unaligned axes mean the second count adds real option value. If the ADA Title I damages count falls to immunity, the Section 504 count stands on the waiver. If a limitations or exhaustion technicality threatens one count, the other count's different procedural prerequisites may avoid it. If the court reads one statute's language narrowly on a novel question, the other statute's text may supply the broader reading. Courts have ratified the practice by deciding the paired counts together, often in a single analysis that treats the standards as interchangeable while preserving each count's distinct remedial and immunity posture.

The paired pleading also serves a forum function that matters in close cases. Section 504's spending-power waiver keeps the damages claim in federal court against state defendants where the ADA Title I claim would be dismissed, preserving the federal forum for the whole dispute. The ADA counts keep alive the injunctive claims and the fee claims that round out the relief. The two statutes thus do different work inside a single complaint even when they describe the same wrong, which returns the reader to the namable claim: the practical question was never which law protects the person, but which door admits the claim against this defendant, and the answer is frequently both doors at once.


## Immunity: the axis that decides cases against states

The immunity axis is the single most practically valuable distinction in this article, and it is the reason the older statute survives as a litigation vehicle long after the newer one arrived. The problem is sovereign immunity. States enjoy immunity from private suits for money damages in federal court under the Eleventh Amendment, and Congress can strip that immunity only through a valid exercise of constitutional power. The two statutes took different paths around that immunity, and only one of the paths held.

Section 504's path runs through the spending power. When Congress enacted the Rehabilitation Act Amendments of 1986, Public Law 99-506, it included at section 1003 a provision codified at 42 U.S.C. 2000d-7, sometimes called by its section heading, "civil rights remedies equalization." Subsection (a)(1) provides that a state shall not be immune under the Eleventh Amendment from suit in federal court for a violation of section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, along with Title IX, the Age Discrimination Act of 1975, Title VI, and the provisions of any other federal statute prohibiting discrimination by recipients of federal financial assistance. Subsection (a)(2) makes the remedies available to the same extent as against any other public or private entity. The provision took effect for violations occurring in whole or in part after October 21, 1986. Courts treat the provision as a conditional-spending waiver: the state, by accepting federal funds, consents to suit in federal court for violations of the attached conditions. The Justice Department's Title VI manual describes the mechanism in exactly those terms, citing Sossamon v. Texas, 563 U.S. 277 (2011). The waiver does not extend to the ADA, which is not a spending statute and therefore cannot borrow the spending power's consent mechanism.

The ADA's path ran through section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment, and the Supreme Court rejected it for the employment title in Board of Trustees of the University of Alabama v. Garrett, 531 U.S. 356, decided February 21, 2001. Garrett, a 5 to 4 decision with Chief Justice Rehnquist writing for the majority, held that suits in federal court by state employees to recover money damages for a state's failure to comply with Title I of the ADA are barred by the Eleventh Amendment. Congress, the Court held, had not validly abrogated state sovereign immunity, because the Title I remedy failed the congruence-and-proportionality test the Court applies to section 5 legislation. The holding is limited in ways the opinion's ninth footnote preserves: suits by the United States for money damages remain available, and private suits for injunctive relief against state officials under Ex parte Young remain available. But the core private damages action by a state employee against the state under Title I does not proceed. For the decisions construing each ADA title, see [the ADA Supreme Court cases](/2015/08/15/ada-supreme-court-cases/).

The contrast is stark and it is the whole point. A state employee with a disability who suffers employment discrimination by a state agency that receives federal financial assistance will find the ADA Title I damages claim barred by Garrett and the Section 504 damages claim proceeding under the 2000d-7 waiver. The facts are identical. The standard is aligned. The defendant is the same. The courthouse door opens under one statute and stays shut under the other. That is what "same duty, different door" means in practice, and it is why experienced counsel pleading against a state defendant lead with the funding-condition statute rather than treating it as the ADA's junior partner.

### Why can a state be sued under Section 504 but not for ADA Title I damages?

Because 42 U.S.C. 2000d-7 treats a state's acceptance of federal funds as a waiver of Eleventh Amendment immunity for Section 504 claims, while the Supreme Court held in Garrett that Congress did not validly abrogate that immunity for ADA Title I damages.

Garrett's bar on Title I damages suits against states is the immunity headline, but the two decisions that followed it define the headline's boundaries, and the boundaries matter for route selection. In Tennessee v. Lane, 541 U.S. 509 (2004), a 5 to 4 decision by Justice Stevens, the Court held Title II of the ADA to be valid section 5 legislation as applied to the class of cases implicating the fundamental right of access to the courts, where the remedy was congruent and proportional to the constitutional injury. Lane did not establish blanket Title II abrogation; its holding is expressly as-applied, limited to the courthouse-access class of cases. In United States v. Georgia, 546 U.S. 151 (2006), a unanimous decision by Justice Scalia issued January 10, 2006, the Court held that insofar as Title II creates a private cause of action for damages against the states for conduct that actually violates the Fourteenth Amendment, Title II validly abrogates state sovereign immunity. Georgia also confirmed that Title II applies to state prisons, resolving the claims of Tony Goodman, the inmate plaintiff. Together, Lane and Georgia carve out Title II damages actions where the underlying conduct violates the Constitution itself or implicates the fundamental right of court access, while Garrett's bar on Title I damages actions stands.

Garrett's own ninth footnote deserves emphasis because it is routinely overlooked. The majority preserved suits by the United States for money damages under Title I and private suits for injunctive relief against state officials under Ex parte Young. The state employer is not untouchable under the ADA; it is untouchable only for private damages under Title I. A plaintiff who needs an injunction against a state official's ongoing violation can still plead the ADA, and the United States can still enforce Title I for damages. Route selection is therefore not only about choosing between statutes. It is about choosing between remedies within a statute, because the immunity rules attach to the remedy sought as well as to the title invoked.

The immunity axis also explains a pattern in the case law that puzzles newcomers. Opinions in disability cases against state universities, state hospitals, and state licensing agencies so often discuss Section 504 at length and the ADA briefly, or resolve the case on the funding-condition claim while the ADA claim drops away on immunity grounds. The pattern is not judicial favoritism for the older statute. It is route selection. Counsel who understand the waiver plead the claim that survives a motion to dismiss, and judges who understand the waiver decide the claim that is properly before them. The 1986 waiver provision is, in this sense, the most consequential sentence Congress ever added to disability law's procedural architecture, and it was added to the 1973 statute, not the 1990 one.

## The state as defendant: a procedural walkthrough

Follow a single case from filing to judgment to see the axes working together. A professor with a disability is denied tenure by a state university that receives federal research funding and student aid. Counsel first maps the triggers. The university is a state entity, so ADA Title II covers its programs; the tenure decision is employment, so ADA Title I covers the employment relationship because the university employs far more than fifteen people; and the federal funds make the university a Section 504 program or activity under the Restoration Act's institution-wide definition. Three doors stand open at the start, and the aligned standard means the underlying facts need be developed only once.

The procedure axis then sorts the doors. The Title I claim requires an EEOC charge within the statutory period and a right-to-sue notice before filing. The Section 504 claim requires neither and can be filed immediately. Counsel who values speed, or who discovers the claim late, feels the difference at once. The Title II claim sits between, requiring no charge but offering a narrower damages framework against the state. The immunity axis then closes one of the doors partway. On the state's motion to dismiss, the Title I damages claim falls to Garrett: Congress did not validly abrogate Eleventh Amendment immunity for private Title I damages suits, and the 5 to 4 decision is settled law. The Section 504 damages claim survives the same motion because 42 U.S.C. 2000d-7 treats the university's acceptance of federal funds as a waiver of immunity, effective for violations after October 21, 1986. The Title II damages claim survives only if counsel can fit it through Lane's courthouse-access class or Georgia's actual-constitutional-violation test, which a tenure dispute ordinarily cannot.

The remedy axis finishes the sorting. The surviving Section 504 claim offers compensatory damages for intentional violations and attorney's fees, but no punitive damages after Barnes. The Title I claim, though barred for damages, remains available for injunctive relief against the responsible officials under Ex parte Young, the route Garrett's ninth footnote preserved, and remains enforceable for damages by the United States. The final pleading therefore looks the way experienced disability counsel write it: Section 504 for damages, the ADA titles for injunctive relief and for the record, all counts sharing one factual core. Every step of that walkthrough was decided by the five axes, and none of it turned on which statute protects people with disabilities more.


## Procedure: the steps before the courthouse door

The final axis governs what must happen before a lawsuit can be filed, and here again the statutes diverge. The ADA's employment title builds in an administrative prerequisite. Section 12117(a) of Title 42 incorporates the powers, remedies, and procedures of Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, including the charge-filing requirement of 42 U.S.C. 2000e-5(e)(1). A private plaintiff alleging employment discrimination under ADA Title I must file a charge with the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission and obtain a notice of right to sue before bringing the action in court. The charge requirement serves the familiar purposes of administrative exhaustion: notice to the employer, an opportunity for conciliation, and a screening function before litigation. Miss the charge deadline and the Title I claim is lost regardless of its merits.

Section 504, as a general matter, imposes no comparable prerequisite. The Fifth Circuit held in Camenisch v. University of Texas, 616 F.2d 127, 135 (5th Cir. 1980), that private suits to enforce Section 504 rights can be brought without previous resort to administrative remedies, a proposition the Education Department's Office for Civil Rights has echoed in guidance stating that the Section 504 regulations contain no requirement to file an administrative complaint and exhaust remedies before filing a private lawsuit. A plaintiff with a damages claim against a federal-funds recipient under Section 504 may proceed directly to court. Two caveats keep the generalization honest. First, the federal-sector employment provision, section 501, is different: 29 U.S.C. 794a(a)(1) incorporates Title VII's remedies, procedures, and rights, and courts require exhaustion of the federal-sector administrative process before suit under section 501, as the Third Circuit explained in Freed v. Consolidated Rail Corp. applying Title VII exhaustion rules to section 501 claims. Second, the education context produced its own line of decisions on the interaction between Section 504 claims and the special education statute's administrative procedures, and that line is addressed in the education section below.

### Must you file an EEOC charge before suing under Section 504?

Generally no. Private Section 504 damages suits against federal-funds recipients require no prior EEOC charge and no administrative exhaustion, unlike ADA Title I employment claims under 42 U.S.C. 12117(a), though federal-sector employment claims under section 501 are the exception because that provision incorporates Title VII procedures.

The procedural asymmetry interacts with the immunity asymmetry to produce the characteristic shape of disability litigation against state employers. The state employee's ADA Title I claim requires an EEOC charge, then faces Garrett's immunity bar on damages. The same employee's Section 504 claim requires no charge and proceeds past immunity under the 2000d-7 waiver, provided the employing agency receives federal financial assistance. The two claims arise from the same facts, allege the same discriminatory conduct, and invoke the same substantive standard, yet one must pass through the Commission and then dies on immunity while the other walks straight into court. Understanding that divergence is the difference between competent route selection and malpractice-adjacent pleading.

Section 504 also offers an administrative enforcement path alongside the private lawsuit, and the choice between them is another route-selection decision. The Education Department's Office for Civil Rights investigates complaints of disability discrimination by recipients of federal education funds, and its guidance confirms what the courts have held: nothing in the Section 504 regulations requires a complainant to exhaust the OCR process before filing a private suit. The two routes are independent. The OCR complaint costs nothing, requires no lawyer, and can produce systemic relief across an institution, but it yields no damages for the complainant and moves at the agency's pace. The private suit offers damages for intentional violations and the plaintiff's control over timing and strategy, at the cost and risk of litigation.

Title III adds a final procedural note worth recording. The public-accommodations title imposes no administrative-exhaustion requirement on private plaintiffs; the majority view holds that the conciliation provision of the 1964 act's public-accommodations title was not incorporated into the ADA's enforcement scheme. A plaintiff denied access to a place of public accommodation may sue directly for injunctive relief. That directness, combined with the absence of damages, shapes the distinctive economy of Title III litigation, in which attorney's fees under 42 U.S.C. 12205 supply the incentive that damages supply elsewhere.

## Education: where most readers meet the comparison

Education is where the ADA versus Section 504 comparison leaves the statute books and enters daily life, because schools are the institutions most readers encounter and because three statutes overlap there rather than two. Section 504 reaches every public school and most private schools through federal financial assistance. Title II of the ADA reaches every public school as a state or local government entity, and Title III reaches private schools as places of education within the twelve public-accommodation categories. The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, the special education statute, reaches eligible children with disabilities through its own funding and procedural scheme, offering a free appropriate public education through an individualized education program. For the special education statute's own standards and procedures, see [the IDEA special education law guide](/2014/10/01/idea-special-education-law-guide/).

The documents the three statutes generate are different, and the differences are the source of most of the confusion. A Section 504 plan is the written document a school develops under the Section 504 regulations to provide a free appropriate public education to a qualified student with a disability, setting out the accommodations and related aids and services the student will receive. An individualized education program under the special education statute is a different document, developed through a different process with different procedural safeguards, different evaluation requirements, and different team composition, for the narrower class of children who meet that statute's eligibility categories. An ADA accommodation in the school setting is not a freestanding document at all; it is the reasonable modification or auxiliary aid the ADA's titles require, implemented through the school's existing processes. Treating a 504 plan and an individualized education program as interchangeable is the recurring error, and it is an error with consequences, because the two documents trigger different rights, different timelines, and different dispute-resolution procedures.

The procedural overlap produced its own line of decisions, and the state of that law at this article's reference date must be stated with care. The special education statute requires exhaustion of its administrative procedures, at 20 U.S.C. 1415(l), before certain civil actions. Whether that exhaustion requirement reached ADA and Section 504 claims filed by students and families was, before 2017, unsettled circuit law, with some circuits requiring exhaustion broadly and others limiting it. The Supreme Court addressed the question in Fry v. Napoleon Community Schools, 580 U.S. 154, decided February 22, 2017, after the reference date used here. Per the date-wall convention, that decision is named here without any description of its holding. What can be said, consistent with the date wall, is that a family or practitioner working under the law as it stood at the reference date faced genuine uncertainty about whether the special education hearing process had to be completed before a Section 504 or ADA suit could proceed, and that the uncertainty itself was a reason to plead carefully and to understand all three statutes' procedures before filing.

The immunity and remedies axes apply in education with the same force they carry elsewhere, and they explain why Section 504 dominates school litigation even though the ADA reaches every district. A damages claim against a school district under Title II must navigate the as-applied limits of Lane and Georgia. The parallel Section 504 claim travels on the funding waiver, because the district's acceptance of federal education funds constitutes the consent that 42 U.S.C. 2000d-7 describes. Compensatory damages for intentional violations are available under the Section 504 claim through the Franklin framework. No EEOC charge stands between the plaintiff and the courthouse under Section 504. The district is covered by both statutes, owes aligned duties under both, and faces a meaningfully stronger claim under the older one.

The comparison also reaches private schools, and the result there illustrates the trigger axis in miniature. A private school that receives no federal financial assistance is outside Section 504, but if it falls within the ADA's public accommodations title as a place of education, Title III reaches it with injunctive remedies. A private school that does receive federal assistance answers to both, with the Section 504 claim carrying the damages remedy the Title III claim lacks. A religious private school sits in the narrow gap: exempt from Title III under 42 U.S.C. 12187, yet a Section 504 recipient for all of its operations if it accepts covered federal assistance. The defendant's funding status, a fact established in discovery rather than in the statute books, decides which of these pictures applies.

Study-path guidance belongs here as well, since this article carries the series' study recommendation. A student deciding which statute to learn first should begin with Section 504's funding-trigger model, because the trigger is the concept that organizes everything else, then take up the ADA's categorical coverage, and then study the alignment provisions that bind the two together. For the series-wide study path across all 300 statutes, see [the US legislation study guide](/2022/06/15/us-legislation-study-guide/). Readers who want to test their command of the comparison may use [a legislation study notebook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) alongside [a government and civics study tool](https://reportmedic.org/tools/us-government-civics-study.html) to work through the axes on their own facts.

## Employment: the charge, the forum, and the federal-sector exception

The employment comparison deserves its own extended treatment because it concentrates all five axes in a single setting. An employee with a disability who works for an entity covered by both statutes faces a fork at the very start. The ADA Title I route begins at the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission: 42 U.S.C. 12117(a) incorporates Title VII's procedures, the charge must be filed within the statutory period, and suit follows only after the Commission issues a notice of right to sue. The Section 504 route begins at the courthouse door: no charge, no notice, no administrative waiting period, just the complaint. The Fifth Circuit's Camenisch decision and the Education Department's Office for Civil Rights guidance both confirm the directness of the Section 504 route for private damages suits against funding recipients.

The remedies at the end of the two routes differ as well. Title I carries Title VII's remedial structure, with compensatory and punitive damages available within the caps Congress set. Section 504 carries the spending-clause remedial framework: compensatory damages for intentional violations, no punitive damages after Barnes v. Gorman, and attorney's fees. The immunity posture differs most of all against state employers, where Garrett bars the Title I damages claim and the 2000d-7 waiver preserves the Section 504 claim. Four differences, one set of facts, one aligned standard. The employment setting is the comparison in miniature.

The federal-sector exception complicates the picture in a way the practitioner must know. Section 501 of the Rehabilitation Act, at 29 U.S.C. 791, governs disability discrimination in federal employment, and it does not offer the direct-to-court route. Section 794a(a)(1) incorporates Title VII's remedies, procedures, and rights for federal-sector claims, and courts require exhaustion of the federal administrative process before suit, as the Third Circuit held in Freed v. Consolidated Rail Corp. The 1992 amendments added the parallel alignment provision at 29 U.S.C. 791(g), conforming federal-sector employment standards to the ADA's. So the federal employee's route runs through the agency's EEO process first, then to court, a procedure closer to the ADA Title I charge requirement than to the direct Section 504 route. The lesson is that "Section 504" as a shorthand covers three different procedural worlds: the direct private suit against funding recipients, the administrative-then-judicial route for federal employment, and the compliance-office enforcement for federal contractors under section 503.

Two more defendants complete the coverage map, and both belong to the Rehabilitation Act's side of the comparison. The federal contractor's obligations under section 503, at 29 U.S.C. 793, run through the Labor Department's Office of Federal Contract Compliance Programs, which enforces the affirmative-action and nondiscrimination duties administratively. The contractor's employee who suffers disability discrimination therefore has a split enforcement picture: the private damages action ordinarily runs through ADA Title I if the contractor meets the fifteen-employee threshold, with the EEOC charge that title requires, while the contractor-specific affirmative-action duties run through the compliance office rather than through a private suit. Section 504 enters only if the contractor also receives federal financial assistance in the statutory sense, a separate inquiry from holding a federal contract. The three provisions, 501, 503, and 504, thus divide the federal nexus into employment, contracting, and funding, and the employee's route depends on which nexus the employer occupies.

The federal employee's route is cleaner but stricter. Section 501 at 29 U.S.C. 791 governs disability discrimination in federal employment, the 1992 amendments conformed its standards to the ADA at 29 U.S.C. 791(g), and 29 U.S.C. 794a(a)(1) incorporates Title VII's procedures, which courts read to require exhaustion of the federal-sector administrative process before suit, the rule the Third Circuit applied in Freed v. Consolidated Rail Corp. The federal employee therefore faces the administrative prerequisite that the private-sector Section 504 plaintiff avoids, a reminder that the direct-to-court route belongs to the funding-recipient claim specifically, not to the Rehabilitation Act generally. The ADA's titles, built around private employers, state and local governments, and private businesses, do not supply the federal employee's claim at all.

## How the two statutes divide public life

Beyond the workplace, the two statutes divide the public sphere along the same axes, and the division is best understood through the institutions readers encounter: transit systems, courthouses, hospitals, and universities.

A municipal transit system is a state or local government entity, which brings Title II to bear without any funding question, and it is typically a recipient of federal transit assistance, which brings Section 504 to bear through the funding trigger. The paratransit duty is stated in the ADA's transportation provisions and elaborated in regulation, and the aligned Section 504 standards impose the parallel duty on the recipient. A rider denied paratransit eligibility can invoke both, with the Section 504 count carrying the damages and waiver advantages this article has described. The transit system cannot defeat the Section 504 count by pointing to the ADA, because the statutes are cumulative rather than alternative.

A county courthouse presents the immunity axis in its most sympathetic form. Title II reaches the courthouse as a state or local government program, and Tennessee v. Lane held Title II's abrogation valid as applied to the class of cases implicating the fundamental right of access to the courts. A litigant unable to enter the courtroom has a Title II claim within Lane's as-applied opening. The same litigant has a Section 504 claim if the court system receives federal financial assistance, traveling on the waiver rather than on abrogation. The two claims converge on the same barrier and diverge on the constitutional theory, and the Section 504 claim does not need Lane's fundamental-rights framing to get past immunity.

A private hospital that accepts Medicare and Medicaid is a Section 504 recipient in all of its operations and a Title III public accommodation as a service establishment. A patient denied effective communication, such as a sign language interpreter where one is required, can state claims under both. The Title III count offers injunctive relief and fees. The Section 504 count offers compensatory damages for an intentional violation. The hospital's duty to provide the interpreter is analyzed under the aligned standards, so the liability question is the same under both counts and the remedial question is where they part.

A state university combines every axis in a single defendant. It is a Title II public entity. It is a Section 504 recipient through its federal research and student aid funds, in all of its operations under the Restoration Act definition. It is a Title I employer for its workforce, subject to Garrett on damages. A student denied program access pleads Title II and Section 504 together. An employee denied accommodation pleads Title I and Section 504 together, with the Section 504 count doing the work Garrett prevents the Title I count from doing on damages. The university is the paradigmatic dual-coverage defendant, and it is the reason the pleading practice of naming both statutes is the norm rather than the exception in education and state-employment litigation.

These configurations confirm the article's verdict in concrete terms. The question is never which law protects the person in the abstract. It is which law's trigger the defendant satisfies, which law's procedure the plaintiff can meet, which law's immunity rule the defendant cannot invoke, and which law's remedy matches what the plaintiff needs. The configurations differ, but the decision procedure is the same.

## The regulatory architecture

The two statutes differ not only in their text but in the regulatory systems that implement them, and the difference affects how the comparison plays out in practice. Section 504 is implemented through regulations issued by each federal agency that provides financial assistance, with the Department of Justice exercising coordination authority over the government's enforcement of the funding statutes under Executive Order 12250. The result is a family of agency-specific Section 504 rules that share a common core: the recipient assurance, the program-access standard, the reasonable accommodation duty, and the complaint procedures. The Department of Education's Section 504 regulations govern schools, the Department of Health and Human Services' govern health programs, and so on, each tailored to the programs the agency funds but each recognizably the same statute.

The ADA is implemented through a smaller set of regulations with clearer lines of authority. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission issues the Title I employment regulations. The Department of Justice issues the Title II public services regulations and the Title III public accommodations regulations. The Department of Transportation issues the transportation regulations that apply across the relevant titles. The concentration of authority means the ADA's regulatory interpretation is more uniform across settings than Section 504's, where the interpretation can vary at the margins from one funding agency to another.

The coordination question is where the two architectures meet. Because the substantive standards are aligned by statute, the agencies have worked to keep the regulations consistent on the conduct questions, and courts treat the two bodies of regulation as mutually reinforcing on what the duty requires. The Department of Justice's Title VI Legal Manual, which addresses the spending statutes' shared structure, is consulted on Section 504 questions for the same reason: the contractual foundation, the waiver mechanism, and the remedial framework are common to the funding statutes as a group. A researcher tracing a Section 504 question will therefore move between the funding agency's regulation, the Justice Department's coordination guidance, and the ADA regulation on the parallel duty, reading them as three expressions of one aligned standard.

The enforcement side of the architecture differs more sharply. Section 504 is enforced both by the funding agencies, which can investigate complaints and ultimately suspend or terminate assistance, and by private plaintiffs suing directly. The ADA's titles divide enforcement among the EEOC for employment charges, the Department of Justice for pattern-or-practice and public-entity enforcement, and private plaintiffs under each title's private right of action. The funding agency's power to cut off assistance is a remedy with no ADA parallel, and it gives Section 504 an administrative enforcement dimension the later statute lacks. In practice the fund-termination power is exercised rarely, with agencies preferring voluntary compliance, but its existence shapes negotiations in ways the ADA's enforcement scheme does not replicate.


## The funding model and its 1988 repair

The funding-condition design was not an accident of drafting. Congress in the early 1970s built civil rights enforcement on the spending power because the spending power gave the federal government leverage over state and private institutions that direct regulation might not have reached, and because the model already existed in Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964. Section 504 borrowed that architecture and its sentence structure, extending the funding-condition technique from race, color, and national origin to disability. Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972 had done the same for sex the year before the Rehabilitation Act passed. The three statutes, Title VI, Title IX, and Section 504, thus form a family: different protected classes, identical enforcement theory, and a shared body of law on what the funding trigger means.

The trigger's meaning was the family's central interpretive battle, and Section 504's chapter of that battle ended with the 1988 repair. The question was whether federal money reaching one program of an institution subjected only that program, or the whole institution, to the nondiscrimination condition. A narrow, program-specific reading let large institutions accept federal funds in one corner while discriminating in the rest, which defeated the condition's purpose. Congress answered with the Civil Rights Restoration Act of 1987, enacted March 22, 1988 as Public Law 100-259, which defined "program or activity" broadly at 29 U.S.C. 794(b) to mean all of the operations of the covered entity. The Rehabilitation Act Amendments of 1988, Public Law 100-630, enacted November 7, 1988, made technical and conforming changes around the same time, but the Restoration Act's definition was the substantive event. From 1988 forward, the funding inquiry in a Section 504 case is an entity-level question, and defendants cannot quarantine their federal money in a single department to escape coverage elsewhere.

That history matters for route selection because the breadth of the trigger determines how often Section 504 is available as the alternative door. Federal financial assistance flows widely: to state agencies, public universities, school districts, hospitals, and private universities through student aid and research support. The institution-wide definition means that in practice, most of the state and local government defendants a disability plaintiff would sue under ADA Title II are also Section 504 defendants. The overlap is not coincidental. It is the product of the 1988 definition, and it is what makes the immunity comparison a live question in case after case rather than a theoretical one.

## From 1973 to 2008: the alignment timeline

The comparison reads most clearly as a sequence, because each legislative and judicial event answered the one before it. In 1973, Congress enacted the Rehabilitation Act with Section 504's funding-condition bar on disability discrimination, borrowing the spending-power architecture of Title VI. In 1986, Congress added the immunity waiver at 42 U.S.C. 2000d-7, effective for violations after October 21, 1986, converting the funding condition into a litigation vehicle against states. In 1988, the Civil Rights Restoration Act defined program or activity institution-wide at 29 U.S.C. 794(b), ending the program-specific reading of the trigger. In 1990, Congress enacted the ADA, reaching private employers, all state and local governments, and twelve categories of private business without any funding trigger, and included the construction rule at 42 U.S.C. 12201(a) directing courts not to apply a lesser standard than the Rehabilitation Act's.

The sequence continued after enactment. In 1992, the Rehabilitation Act Amendments added 29 U.S.C. 794(d), conforming Section 504's employment standards to the ADA's, with the parallel federal-sector provision at 29 U.S.C. 791(g). In 2001, Garrett closed private Title I damages suits against states, making the 1986 waiver the surviving damages route. In 2002, Barnes fixed the remedial framework, denying punitive damages and reaffirming the contract-law theory. In 2004 and 2006, Lane and Georgia carved the as-applied and constitutional-violation openings in Title II immunity. In 2008, the ADA Amendments Act harmonized the disability definition across both statutes at 29 U.S.C. 705(9)(B) and 705(20)(B). The arc runs from a funding condition, through a national mandate, to a fully aligned pair of statutes whose differences are all about the route to court. That is the story the five axes tell in chronological order.

## The 1990 mandate and its five titles

The ADA's architecture reflects a different legislative ambition. Where the 1973 statute conditioned federal money, the 1990 statute commanded the covered categories directly, and its five titles divide American institutional life into the domains Congress meant to regulate. Title I governs the employment relationship for private employers of fifteen or more, incorporating Title VII's procedures at 42 U.S.C. 12117(a). Title II governs the services, programs, and activities of state and local government, covering everything from zoning boards to public hospitals to state courts. Title III governs the twelve categories of private public accommodations, from inns and restaurants to private schools and recreation facilities. Title IV addresses telecommunications relay services, and Title V carries the miscellaneous provisions, including the construction rule at 42 U.S.C. 12201(a) that anchors the alignment with the Rehabilitation Act.

The title-by-title design is why the comparison with Section 504 must be run separately for each title. Title I's fifteen-employee threshold means small employers sit outside the ADA's employment coverage entirely, while Section 504's funding trigger means a small nonprofit that takes federal grant money sits inside Section 504's coverage regardless of headcount. Title II's all-governments coverage means every municipality is an ADA defendant whether or not it takes federal funds, while Section 504 reaches only the municipalities that do. Title III's twelve categories reach private businesses the funding condition could never touch, which is why the ADA's arrival genuinely expanded the protected territory rather than merely duplicating the old one. The reader who treats "the ADA" as a single rule will misapply it; the statute is five related regimes, and the Section 504 comparison changes with each.

## The ADA's three doors

The ADA organizes its coverage into titles, and each title is a separate door with its own trigger, its own covered entities, and its own remedies. Title I, the employment title, covers employers with fifteen or more employees, employment agencies, labor organizations, and joint labor-management committees. The fifteen-employee threshold comes from 42 U.S.C. 12111(5)(A), which counts employees for each working day in each of twenty or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year. Title I is the title that requires an administrative charge before suit, and it is the title whose money-damages claims against states the Supreme Court shut down in Board of Trustees of the University of Alabama v. Garrett.

Title II, the public services title, covers every state and local government entity without exception. The statutory definition at 42 U.S.C. 12131(1) sweeps in any state or local government, any department, agency, special purpose district, or other instrumentality of a state or local government, and, by express inclusion, the National Railroad Passenger Corporation and commuter authorities. No size threshold applies. No funding trigger applies. A town of four hundred people is a covered public entity under Title II. The breadth of Title II is what makes the ADA, in the ordinary case, the broader statute: it reaches government action everywhere, whether or not federal money is involved. The immunity limits on Title II are what make Section 504, in the state-defendant case, the stronger one: Tennessee v. Lane upheld Title II's abrogation of state immunity only as applied to the class of cases implicating the fundamental right of access to the courts, and United States v. Georgia upheld it only insofar as Title II creates a damages remedy for conduct that actually violates the Fourteenth Amendment. Those are real but bounded openings. Section 504's funding-condition waiver, by contrast, opens the door to damages claims against a state across the full range of the statute whenever the state accepts federal funds, which nearly every state does.

Title III, the public accommodations title, covers private entities that own, lease, or operate places of public accommodation, defined through twelve categories at 42 U.S.C. 12181(7): places of lodging, establishments serving food or drink, places of exhibition or entertainment, places of public gathering, sales or rental establishments, service establishments, stations used for specified public transportation, places of public display or collection, places of recreation, places of education, social service center establishments, and places of exercise or recreation. Title III is the title whose private remedies are limited to injunctive relief and attorney's fees, with compensatory damages available only in enforcement actions brought by the Department of Justice. That remedial ceiling is one of the sharpest differences in the entire comparison.

## Worked route selection: four defendants

The five axes come alive in application, so consider four defendants and run each through the comparison. First, a state university that denies tenure to a professor with a disability. The university is a state entity covered by ADA Title II and, because it receives federal funds, by Section 504. The professor's Title I employment claim requires an EEOC charge, then confronts Garrett's bar on private damages against the state. The Section 504 claim requires no charge and proceeds under the 2000d-7 waiver. The substantive standard is aligned either way. The competent pleading leads with Section 504 for damages and preserves the ADA counts for injunctive relief under Ex parte Young. This is the fact pattern the immunity axis was built for.

Second, a privately owned restaurant that refuses to serve a customer who uses a wheelchair and takes no federal money. Section 504 has no purchase here: no federal financial assistance, no trigger. ADA Title III reaches the restaurant as a place of public accommodation serving food and drink, one of the twelve categories, with no size threshold. The private plaintiff's remedy is injunctive relief and attorney's fees, not damages. The route is single and clear, and it demonstrates the ADA's independent value: territory the funding condition could never reach.

Third, a federal agency that denies a promotion to an employee with a disability. The ADA's titles do not supply this claim; the Rehabilitation Act does, through section 501's federal-employment provisions at 29 U.S.C. 791, with the 1992 alignment at 791(g) conforming the standards to the ADA's. The employee must exhaust the federal-sector administrative process before suing, per the rule the Third Circuit applied in Freed. Section 504's direct-to-court route is unavailable because the claim is a section 501 employment claim, not a funding-recipient claim. The forum and the procedure come from the Rehabilitation Act's federal-sector machinery.

Fourth, a private university receiving federal student aid that expels a student with a disability. Both statutes reach: ADA Title III as a place of education, Section 504 through the federal funds and the Restoration Act's institution-wide definition. The standards align. The plaintiff pleads both, seeking Title III's injunctive relief and Section 504's compensatory damages for intentional violations, with no EEOC charge required on either count and no immunity barrier against a private defendant. The pleading captures every available remedy because the axes were checked in order: trigger, entity, standard, remedy, immunity, procedure.

One more application tests the immunity axis at its most demanding. A state prison houses an inmate who uses a wheelchair and denies him access to the prison's programs. Title II reaches the prison as a state government program or activity, and United States v. Georgia, 546 U.S. 151 (2006), confirms that Title II applies to state prisons. Section 504 reaches the prison if it receives federal financial assistance, which state corrections systems commonly do. The substantive standard is aligned. The damages question then runs through Georgia's test: insofar as Title II creates a private damages action for conduct that actually violates the Fourteenth Amendment, the abrogation is valid and the Title II damages claim proceeds. Where the conduct violates the statute but not the Constitution itself, the Title II damages claim faces the immunity barrier Lane left standing outside its courthouse-access class, while the Section 504 damages claim proceeds on the 2000d-7 waiver. The inmate's counsel pleads Title II for the constitutional-violation damages Georgia permits and for injunctive relief, and pleads Section 504 for the full compensatory-damages claim the waiver preserves.

The applications confirm the namable claim. In no case did the choice turn on which statute "protects" the person more. In every case it turned on which door the defendant left open.

## The words that decide close cases: "otherwise qualified" and "solely by reason of"

Close cases under Section 504 are so often decided on two phrases in 29 U.S.C. 794(a) that the phrases deserve their own treatment. "Otherwise qualified" does the gatekeeping work: it limits protection to the individual with a disability who satisfies the essential eligibility requirements for the program, benefit, or position apart from the disability. The word "otherwise" carries the meaning. The statute does not require a university to admit an unqualified applicant, an employer to hire a worker who cannot perform the job's essential functions, or a licensing board to credential a candidate who cannot meet the substantive standards. It requires each of them to disregard the disability when judging qualifications the person otherwise meets. Most qualification disputes in the case law are fights about where the essential requirements end and the discriminatory ones begin, and the phrase tells the court exactly where to look.

"Solely by reason of" does the causation work, and it sets a demanding test. The plaintiff must show that the disability alone drove the exclusion, the denial, or the discrimination, not that it was one factor among many. The word "solely" distinguishes Section 504's text from causation standards elsewhere in civil rights law that ask only whether the protected characteristic was a motivating factor. In practice, courts applying the aligned standards often reach the same outcomes under either statute's phrasing, because the same evidence that proves disability was the sole reason will also prove it was the decisive reason under the ADA's formulation. But the textual difference matters at the margins, in mixed-motive cases where a legitimate reason accompanied the discriminatory one, and counsel pleading Section 504 must be ready to meet the stricter verbal formula even when the practical result converges.

The ADA's side of the vocabulary harmonizes through the alignment provisions rather than through identical text. The 1992 instruction at 29 U.S.C. 794(d) directs Section 504 employment cases into the ADA's framework, and the ADA's construction rule at 42 U.S.C. 12201(a) keeps the ADA's standards at least as protective as the Rehabilitation Act's. The two phrases therefore operate inside a harmonized system: "otherwise qualified" and the ADA's qualification concepts are read together, and "solely by reason of" is applied with awareness of the ADA's causation language. The reader who can explain both phrases, their textual difference, and their practical convergence has mastered the substance axis at the level of detail where cases are actually won and lost.

## The decisions that built the comparison: a reading order

The case law rewards sequential study, because each decision answered the question the previous one left open. Begin with Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, 503 U.S. 60 (1992), which established compensatory damages for intentional violations of the spending-clause statutes. Then read Board of Trustees of the University of Alabama v. Garrett, 531 U.S. 356 (2001), the 5 to 4 decision that barred private Title I damages suits against states and made the Section 504 waiver the surviving route. Then read Barnes v. Gorman, 536 U.S. 181 (2002), the unanimous decision that denied punitive damages under Section 504 and ADA Title II while reaffirming the contract-law framework that keeps Franklin's compensatory rule standing. The three together define the remedial and immunity baseline: damages for intentional violations, no punitive damages, and a closed Title I damages door against states.

Continue with Tennessee v. Lane, 541 U.S. 509 (2004), the as-applied validation of Title II for courthouse access, and United States v. Georgia, 546 U.S. 151 (2006), the unanimous constitutional-violation test that opened Title II damages where the underlying conduct violates the Fourteenth Amendment itself. Then study the procedure cases: Camenisch v. University of Texas, 616 F.2d 127 (5th Cir. 1980), for the direct-to-court Section 504 route, and Freed v. Consolidated Rail Corp. (3d Cir.), for the federal-sector exhaustion exception. Two later decisions sit beyond this article's reference date and are named here without description of their holdings, per the date-wall convention: Fry v. Napoleon Community Schools, 580 U.S. 154, decided February 22, 2017, on the education exhaustion question, and Cummings v. Premier Rehab Keller, PLLC, 596 U.S. 212, decided April 28, 2022, on emotional-distress damages under the spending-clause statutes. Read in this order, the decisions tell the comparison's story as the courts lived it: a shared standard, divergent routes, and a practitioner's map drawn one holding at a time.

## The text behind the myths: five misreadings answered

Five misreadings circulate about this comparison, and each has a specific textual answer. First, that the ADA repealed or replaced Section 504. It did not. Congress left the Rehabilitation Act in place, aligned the standards in both directions, and in 1992 and 2008 amended the older statute to keep pace with the newer one. A repealed statute needs no alignment provisions. Second, that Barnes v. Gorman held compensatory damages available under Section 504. It held the opposite on the point it decided: punitive damages are unavailable, because the remedies are coextensive with Title VI. The compensatory-damages rule for intentional violations comes from Franklin v. Gwinnett, working through the contract-law framework Barnes reaffirmed. Readers who cite Barnes for the compensatory rule have the right result for the wrong reason, and the wrong reason fails when the next remedial question arises.

Third, that the 1988 amendments aligned Section 504's standards with the ADA's. Chronology alone refutes it: the ADA did not exist until 1990. The alignment instruction at 29 U.S.C. 794(d) arrived with the Rehabilitation Act Amendments of 1992, Public Law 102-569. What 1988 did change was the trigger's breadth, through the Civil Rights Restoration Act's institution-wide definition of program or activity at 29 U.S.C. 794(b). Fourth, that Section 504 always requires exhausting administrative remedies or never does. The truth is provision-specific: private damages suits against funding recipients require no prior administrative resort, per Camenisch and the Education Department's guidance, while federal-sector employment claims under section 501 do require exhaustion, per Freed. Fifth, that Garrett ended disability suits against states. It ended private Title I damages suits against states, while preserving government enforcement, Ex parte Young injunctions, the Lane and Georgia carve-outs for Title II, and the entire Section 504 damages action under the 2000d-7 waiver. Each myth dissolves on contact with the text.

## The reader's checklist: five questions before filing

Every disability dispute can be triaged with five questions asked in order, and the order matters because each answer narrows the field for the next. First, what is the defendant. Name the entity precisely, a state university, a private restaurant, a federal agency, a city zoning board, because the trigger analysis runs on the entity's legal character and not on the wrong it allegedly committed. Second, which triggers did the defendant trip. Ask whether federal financial assistance flows to the entity, whether it employs fifteen or more people, whether it is a state or local government body, and whether it fits one of the twelve public-accommodation categories. Write down every statute and title whose trigger is satisfied; the list is the set of open doors.

Third, what remedy does the plaintiff need. Damages for an intentional violation point toward Section 504, ADA Title I, or ADA Title II, with Barnes foreclosing punitive damages under the spending-clause titles. An injunction against a private business points toward ADA Title III, with fees under 42 U.S.C. 12205 supplying the economics. Forward-looking relief against a state official points toward Ex parte Young under the ADA titles even where Garrett bars damages. The remedy sought must match a remedy the chosen title actually offers, because courts do not transplant the best remedy from one title onto another's trigger.

Fourth, does immunity block the chosen route. If the defendant is a state and the claim seeks damages, ask whether the state accepted federal funds, which opens the 2000d-7 waiver for Section 504, and whether the claim fits Lane's courthouse-access class or Georgia's actual-constitutional-violation test, which open Title II. If the claim seeks only injunctive relief against state officials, Garrett's bar does not apply. If the defendant is private, immunity is not in the case. Fifth, what must happen before suit. Title I needs the EEOC charge and the right-to-sue notice. Section 504 damages suits against funding recipients need no administrative step, while section 501 federal-employment claims need exhaustion of the federal-sector process. Education disputes need the additional question whether the special education hearing process must come first, a question the pre-2017 law left unsettled.

Run the checklist on paper before drafting the complaint. The statute that survives all five questions is the claim to file, and where more than one survives, pleading them together preserves every advantage the axes create. The checklist is the article's method compressed to a single page, and it works because the law itself is organized the same way.

## Studying the comparison: a working method

The comparison rewards a particular order of study, and the order follows the axes. Begin with the two trigger sentences: Section 504's funding condition at 29 U.S.C. 794(a) and the ADA's three definitional sections at 42 U.S.C. 12111(5)(A), 12131(1), and 12181(7). Memorize what each reaches and be able to sort any defendant, a city, a contractor, a private college, a federal agency, into the right boxes. Then study the two alignment provisions, 42 U.S.C. 12201(a) and 29 U.S.C. 794(d), with their dates, 1990 and 1992, and the 2008 definitional harmonization at 29 U.S.C. 705(9)(B) and 705(20)(B), until the shared standard feels automatic. Only then take up the three axes where the statutes diverge: the remedial framework from Franklin through Barnes, the immunity line from the 1986 waiver through Garrett, Lane, and Georgia, and the procedural prerequisites from the EEOC charge to the education exhaustion question.

Test the method against new defendants until the sorting becomes reflex. A county jail: Title II as a local government entity, Section 504 if it takes federal funds, Georgia's prison holding in reserve. A private religious school with no federal money: Title III as a place of education, no Section 504 trigger. A state licensing board that takes federal grants: Title II plus Section 504, with the waiver doing the immunity work Garrett denies the ADA. Each answer should name the trigger, the standard, the remedy, the immunity posture, and the procedure, in that order, because that order is the order in which a court will ask the questions. The five axes are not only the article's organizing device. They are the checklist the courthouse uses.

## Same duty, different door: choosing the route

The namable claim of this article can now be stated as a working rule. Same duty, different door: the two statutes usually require the same thing and almost never offer the same route, so the practical question is never which law protects a person but which one lets them get through the courthouse door against this defendant. The rule organizes every decision the comparison demands. Identify the defendant. Ask which triggers the defendant tripped. Ask what remedy the plaintiff needs. Ask whether immunity blocks one route. Ask what procedures must precede suit. The statute that survives all five questions is the claim to file, and the aligned standard means the facts need be proved only once.

The differences between the two statutes are not drafting accidents. They follow from the different constitutional powers Congress invoked, and understanding the powers explains why the remedies, the immunity rules, and the procedures diverge the way they do. Section 504 is an exercise of the spending power. Congress may attach conditions to federal funds, and recipients accept those conditions voluntarily when they take the money. The Supreme Court has treated the resulting relationship as contractual: the recipient's obligation sounds in contract, the remedies track those traditionally available for breach of contract, and the state's acceptance of funds constitutes a waiver of its Eleventh Amendment immunity for suits enforcing the condition. Every distinctive feature of Section 504's route flows from that foundation. The ADA is an exercise of the commerce power and of section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment, invoked precisely to reach beyond the spending trigger: to regulate private employers and private businesses directly, and to subject the states to federal antidiscrimination law as a matter of command rather than condition. The broader reach follows directly. So do the difficulties. Abrogating a state's sovereign immunity under section 5 requires legislation that is congruent and proportional to the constitutional violations Congress identified, and the Court held in Garrett that Title I's abrogation failed that test for private money-damages suits. The two designs are complements, not substitutes, and Congress's decision to align their standards while preserving their distinct triggers, remedies, immunity rules, and procedures was a deliberate division of labor.

The complication that opened this article deserves its explicit refutation here. The ADA did not make Section 504 obsolete, and treating the earlier statute as superseded costs plaintiffs real remedies. Against state defendants, Section 504's spending-power waiver succeeds where the ADA's Fourteenth Amendment abrogation failed. In education, Section 504's plan process, damages availability, and procedural directness give it independent force. In employment against federal contractors and funding recipients, the 1992 alignment means Section 504 adds a route without subtracting a standard. The later statute broadened the protected territory enormously, reaching millions of private employers and businesses the funding condition could never touch. It did not shrink the older statute's territory by an inch. The two now overlap across much of American institutional life, and the overlap is the reason the comparison matters: where both reach, the plaintiff chooses, and the choice decides the remedy, the immunity posture, and the procedure.

A final word on method. Every litigation-strategy observation in this article is attributed to the courts and agencies that made it: the Supreme Court in Garrett, Lane, Georgia, Barnes, and Franklin; the Fifth Circuit in Camenisch and the Third Circuit in Freed; the Education Department's Office for Civil Rights on exhaustion; the Justice Department's Title VI manual on the spending-power waiver. The structure described here is the law as it stood at the reference date, stated at the confidence the sources support. Where the law was unsettled, as in the pre-2017 exhaustion question, the article says so rather than smoothing the uncertainty away. That discipline is what makes the comparison usable, because a route-selection guide that pretends to certainty where none existed would mislead exactly when the stakes are highest. The five axes repay the effort they demand, because each one answers a question the courthouse will ask before reaching the merits. Trigger, standard, remedy, immunity, and procedure decide every disability case in that order, and the lawyer who works through them in sequence will rarely be surprised by the outcome.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between the Americans with Disabilities Act and Section 504?

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 is a funding condition: it bars disability discrimination only by programs or activities receiving federal financial assistance, plus programs conducted by federal agencies and the Postal Service, at 29 U.S.C. 794(a). The Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 is a direct civil rights mandate that needs no federal money: Title I reaches employers with fifteen or more employees, Title II reaches all state and local government entities, and Title III reaches twelve defined categories of private businesses. Their substantive standards are deliberately aligned by express statutory instruction, so a covered entity usually owes the same core duties under both. The differences that decide cases are the trigger, the remedies, the immunity rules, and the procedural steps before suit, which is why lawyers plead the two together and choose between them based on the defendant.

### Q: Which is broader, the Americans with Disabilities Act or Section 504?

The ADA is broader in reach and Section 504 is deeper in remedy, so neither is simply broader. The ADA covers millions of private employers and businesses that take no federal money and therefore sit entirely outside Section 504, which makes it the wider net across the private economy. Section 504 reaches only funding recipients, federal contractors, and federal agencies, a narrower class of defendants. But against the defendants both statutes reach, Section 504 often gives the stronger claim: compensatory damages for intentional violations, no EEOC charge requirement, and, against states, the 2000d-7 spending-power waiver that succeeds where ADA Title I abrogation failed in Garrett. Broader coverage and stronger remedies are different virtues, and the comparison turns on which one the plaintiff needs.

### Q: Do you need federal funding for the Americans with Disabilities Act or Section 504?

Only for Section 504. The ADA's three main titles operate without any funding trigger: an employer of fifteen or more, a city government, or a private restaurant is covered because of what it is, not because of what it receives. Section 504's private damages action requires the defendant to be a program or activity receiving federal financial assistance, which is why the first question in any Section 504 case is whether federal money flows to the defendant. After the Civil Rights Restoration Act's 1988 definition at 29 U.S.C. 794(b), money reaching any part of an institution brings all of its operations within the statute. The related Rehabilitation Act provisions cover federal contractors under section 503 and federal employment under section 501 without a separate funding inquiry.

### Q: Why do Section 504 claims survive when Americans with Disabilities Act claims fail?

The usual reason is sovereign immunity. In Board of Trustees of the University of Alabama v. Garrett, 531 U.S. 356 (2001), the Supreme Court held that Congress did not validly abrogate state sovereign immunity for private money-damages suits under ADA Title I, so a state employee's Title I damages claim against the state is barred. Section 504 claims against the same state proceed because 42 U.S.C. 2000d-7, enacted in 1986, provides that a state accepting federal funds waives Eleventh Amendment immunity for Section 504 suits. The same facts, the same aligned standard, and the same defendant produce opposite outcomes at the motion-to-dismiss stage. Counsel who understand the waiver plead the funding-condition claim against state defendants rather than relying on the ADA title that Garrett closed.

### Q: Is a 504 plan the same as an Americans with Disabilities Act accommodation?

No. A 504 plan is the written document a school develops under the Section 504 regulations to deliver a free appropriate public education to a qualified student with a disability, listing the accommodations, related aids, and services the student will receive. An ADA accommodation is not a document at all; it is the reasonable modification, auxiliary aid, or employment accommodation the ADA's titles require, delivered through whatever process the covered entity uses. The two often contain the same practical measures, such as extended test time or an accessible workspace, because the substantive standards are aligned. But the plan carries Section 504's procedural framework and the accommodation carries the ADA title's framework, so the label determines which procedures, remedies, and exhaustion rules apply when a dispute arises.

### Q: Do the Americans with Disabilities Act and Section 504 use the same standards?

For entities covered by both, generally yes, by express congressional instruction in both directions. The ADA provides at 42 U.S.C. 12201(a) that it shall not be construed to apply a lesser standard than the standards applied under title V of the Rehabilitation Act. Section 504 provides at 29 U.S.C. 794(d), added by the Rehabilitation Act Amendments of 1992, that employment-discrimination standards under Section 504 shall be the standards applied under ADA Title I and the ADA's sections 501 through 504 and 510. The ADA Amendments Act of 2008 further harmonized the definition of disability across both statutes. That is why courts routinely analyze ADA and Section 504 claims together and why plaintiffs plead them in the same complaint. Narrow edges remain, such as Title III's barrier-removal duties, but the core duties match.

### Q: Can you recover emotional distress damages under Section 504 or the Americans with Disabilities Act?

At this article's reference date of October 1, 2015, the framework was as follows. Compensatory damages were available for intentional violations under Section 504 and under ADA Title II, through the contract-law remedial framework the Supreme Court reaffirmed in Barnes v. Gorman, 536 U.S. 181 (2002), building on Franklin v. Gwinnett County Public Schools, 503 U.S. 60 (1992); punitive damages were not available under either, which is what Barnes actually held. Title I employment claims carried Title VII remedies including damages within statutory caps, while private Title III suits against businesses yielded only injunctive relief and attorney's fees. The Supreme Court decided Cummings v. Premier Rehab Keller, PLLC, 596 U.S. 212, on April 28, 2022, after the reference date. Per the date-wall convention, that decision is named here without any description of its holding.

### Q: Which should a student study first, the Americans with Disabilities Act or Section 504?

Study Section 504's funding-trigger model first, because the trigger is the concept that organizes the entire comparison. Once the student can explain why a funding condition reaches some defendants and not others, the ADA's categorical coverage falls into place as the contrasting design: Congress reaching the rest of the economy directly instead of through the spending power. Then study the two alignment provisions, 42 U.S.C. 12201(a) and 29 U.S.C. 794(d), which explain why the standards match while the routes differ. After that, take up the remedies, the Garrett immunity line, and the EEOC charge requirement as the three axes where route selection is decided. The series study guide linked in the education section lays out the wider reading order across all 300 statutes.

### Q: Why does an ADA Title I employment claim start at the EEOC while a Section 504 claim usually goes straight to court?

The difference comes from what each statute incorporates. ADA Title I provides at 42 U.S.C. 12117(a) that the powers, remedies, and procedures of Title VII apply, which includes the requirement to file a charge with the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission and receive a right-to-sue notice before filing suit. Section 504 incorporates no such administrative scheme for private damages actions against funding recipients; the Fifth Circuit held in Camenisch v. University of Texas that private Section 504 suits may proceed without prior resort to administrative remedies, and the Education Department's Office for Civil Rights guidance agrees. The exception is federal-sector employment under section 501, where courts do require exhaustion of the federal administrative process. The practical effect is a timing trap: the same worker may face a short charge deadline under one statute and none under the other.

### Q: Why do lawyers plead Section 504 and ADA claims in the same lawsuit?

Because the alignment provisions make the facts do double duty while the unaligned axes preserve distinct advantages. The same evidence that proves a violation of the shared substantive standard ordinarily proves a violation of both statutes, so pleading both costs little and protects against the possibility that one claim fails on a technical ground the other avoids. Against a state defendant, the Section 504 count survives the immunity challenge that Garrett poses to the ADA Title I damages count. In employment, the Title I count carries Title VII's remedial structure while the Section 504 count avoids the EEOC charge step. Courts facilitate the practice by analyzing the paired claims together, often in a single discussion that treats the standards as interchangeable while preserving each count's distinct remedial and immunity posture.

### Q: What did the 1992 amendments change about Section 504 employment standards?

The Rehabilitation Act Amendments of 1992, Public Law 102-569, enacted October 29, 1992, added 29 U.S.C. 794(d), which provides that the standards for deciding whether Section 504 was violated in an employment complaint shall be the standards applied under Title I of the ADA and under the ADA's sections 501 through 504 and 510, as those sections relate to employment. Before 1992, courts construing Section 504 employment claims worked from the Rehabilitation Act's own text and regulations; after 1992, they apply the ADA's employment framework, including its reasonable-accommodation analysis, undue-hardship defense, and treatment of qualification standards. The same act added the parallel provision for federal-sector employment at 29 U.S.C. 791(g). The change is sometimes misattributed to the 1988 amendments, which were technical; the alignment instruction dates to 1992.

### Q: Why does the ADA tell courts not to apply a lesser standard than the Rehabilitation Act?

Congress included the instruction at 42 U.S.C. 12201(a) to guarantee that the new, broader statute would not become a vehicle for narrowing the protections the older statute had already established. By 1990, Section 504 had nearly two decades of regulations and case law defining the duties of funding recipients, and disability advocates feared that courts interpreting the ADA's fresh language might read those duties down. The construction rule answers that fear directly: nothing in the ADA shall be construed to apply a lesser standard than the standards applied under title V of the Rehabilitation Act or the regulations issued under it. In practice the rule functions as a one-way ratchet, letting ADA protections grow beyond the Rehabilitation Act baseline while preventing them from falling below it, and courts cite it when harmonizing the two bodies of law.

### Q: After the 2008 amendments, does Section 504 use the ADA's definition of disability?

Yes. The ADA Amendments Act of 2008, Public Law 110-325, enacted September 25, 2008 and effective January 1, 2009, rejected narrow judicial constructions of the ADA's disability definition and restored a broad reading. Section 7 of that act amended the Rehabilitation Act's parallel definitions so the two statutes would keep the same protected class: 29 U.S.C. 705(9)(B) now gives the term the meaning stated in 42 U.S.C. 12102, the ADA's definitional section, and 29 U.S.C. 705(20)(B) now covers any person who has a disability as defined in that ADA section, replacing the earlier three-prong text. A person who meets the ADA's definition therefore meets the Rehabilitation Act's definition as well. The amendment closed a potential gap in which the two statutes could have protected different populations despite their aligned standards.

### Q: If one department of a university takes federal money, does Section 504 reach the whole school?

Yes. The Civil Rights Restoration Act of 1987, enacted March 22, 1988, defined "program or activity" broadly at 29 U.S.C. 794(b) so that federal financial assistance to any part of an institution brings all of the institution's operations within Section 504's reach. A university whose financial-aid office processes federal student aid is covered across its academic departments, housing, athletics, and employment, not only in the office that handles the aid. The same institution-wide reading applies to state agencies, school districts, and private entities that accept the funds. Before the 1988 definition, the trigger could be read program by program, which let large institutions wall off their unfunded operations; Congress closed that reading deliberately. The practical lesson is that the funding inquiry is usually answered at the entity level, not the department level.

### Q: What is the difference between a 504 plan and an IEP, and why does it decide which statute a parent invokes?

A 504 plan is the document a school develops under the Section 504 regulations to provide a free appropriate public education to a qualified student with a disability, recording accommodations and related services. An individualized education program is the document developed under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act for the narrower class of children who meet that statute's eligibility categories, through a process with different evaluations, team composition, timelines, and procedural safeguards. Confusing the two matters because each document activates a different dispute-resolution system: the 504 plan leads to Section 504's complaint and hearing procedures, while the individualized program leads to the special education statute's due-process machinery. A parent who invokes the wrong document's procedures can lose time and leverage, which is why the distinction decides strategy.

### Q: Must a family finish the special education hearing before suing a school under Section 504 or the ADA?

At this article's reference date, the answer was genuinely unsettled. The special education statute requires exhaustion of its administrative procedures at 20 U.S.C. 1415(l) before certain civil actions, and before 2017 the federal circuits divided over whether that requirement reached ADA and Section 504 claims brought by students and families, with some circuits requiring exhaustion broadly and others limiting it. The Supreme Court took up the question in Fry v. Napoleon Community Schools, 580 U.S. 154, decided February 22, 2017, after the reference date. Per the date-wall convention, that decision is named here without any description of its holding. The uncertainty itself was the practical point: families and counsel working under the pre-2017 law could not assume that a direct Section 504 or ADA suit would avoid the hearing process, and careful pleading accounted for that risk.

### Q: Which disability law covers the federal government itself, and why is the ADA not the answer?

The Rehabilitation Act carries the federal government's own disability duties, not the ADA. Section 501, at 29 U.S.C. 791, imposes affirmative-action and nondiscrimination obligations in federal employment across federal departments, agencies, and instrumentalities, including the Postal Service, with the federal-sector administrative process that courts require claimants to exhaust before suing. Section 504 reaches programs and activities conducted by executive agencies and the Postal Service directly. The ADA's titles, by contrast, are built around private employers of fifteen or more, state and local government entities, and private businesses in the twelve public-accommodation categories; the federal government as employer and program operator is the Rehabilitation Act's domain. A federal employee's disability claim therefore runs through section 501's procedures rather than through an ADA charge.

### Q: If a disabled employee works for a federal contractor, does Section 504, the contractor provision, or the ADA apply?

All three can matter, for different duties. Section 503 of the Rehabilitation Act, at 29 U.S.C. 793, imposes affirmative-action and nondiscrimination obligations on federal contractors and subcontractors, enforced by the Labor Department's Office of Federal Contract Compliance Programs rather than through a private damages lawsuit. The ADA's Title I gives the contractor's employee a private employment-discrimination claim if the contractor meets the fifteen-employee threshold, with the EEOC charge requirement that title carries. Section 504 reaches the contractor only if it also receives federal financial assistance beyond the contract itself, in which case the private damages action opens as well. The employee's private suit therefore ordinarily runs through ADA Title I, while the contractor-specific affirmative-action duties run through the Labor Department's compliance process.

### Q: Does Section 504 list the same kinds of private businesses that the ADA's public-accommodations title lists?

No. Section 504 contains no list of business categories at all; it reaches a private business only through the funding trigger, when the business is a program or activity receiving federal financial assistance. The twelve categories at 42 U.S.C. 12181(7), from lodging and restaurants to service establishments and places of education, are the ADA's own device for reaching private enterprise, and they operate without any funding inquiry or size threshold. The structural contrast is deliberate: Congress used the spending power for the 1973 statute, so coverage follows the money, and used direct regulation for the 1990 statute, so coverage follows the category. A private business with no federal funds answers only to the ADA's titles; a private business with federal funds answers to both.

### Q: Can a private school be covered by Section 504 but not the ADA?

The configuration is possible but narrow. A private school that receives federal financial assistance is a Section 504 recipient, while a private school that falls within Title III's education category is covered by the ADA as a place of public accommodation, so most private schools with federal funding face both. The gap opens through Title III's express exemption for religious organizations at 42 U.S.C. 12187: a religious private school is exempt from Title III yet remains a Section 504 recipient for all of its operations if it accepts covered federal assistance. The reverse gap is wider: a private school with no federal funding is outside Section 504 entirely but inside Title III as a place of education, unless the religious exemption applies. The two gaps show the trigger axis operating at full strength, with funding status and religious character combining to sort defendants into four possible coverage patterns across the two statutes.
