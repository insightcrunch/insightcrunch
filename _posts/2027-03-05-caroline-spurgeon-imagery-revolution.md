Caroline Spurgeon changed what a critic of Romeo and Juliet is allowed to claim, and she changed it with a card catalogue. Before her book appeared in 1935, a reader could praise the play's glowing language and stop there, safe in the loftiest vagueness. After Shakespeare's Imagery and What It Tells Us, praise had to survive a census. Spurgeon had collected every image in Shakespeare, sorted the harvest into classes, counted what she found, and announced that this play is governed by one running pattern: light in all its forms, blazing against darkness. Sun, moon, stars, fire, lightning, the flash of gunpowder, the reflected light of beauty and of love on one side; night, clouds, rain, mist and smoke on the other. That finding became the standard account of the play's atmosphere, repeated in classrooms and edition introductions until it reads like a fact of nature rather than one scholar's construction. This article argues a split verdict, and argues it from her own pages. The light finding stands: it survives her critics, her own appendix, and every serious rereading of the play since. The instrument that produced it stands too, but demoted: counting is a way of finding patterns, not a way of proving them, and Spurgeon's book is at its weakest exactly where it forgets the difference, in the confident biography it builds out of the tallies. The revolution she started was real. Its first triumph was this play. Its limits were printed, by Spurgeon herself, in her own appendix.

The argument keeps her appendix open beside her chapter throughout, because the two documents correct each other. The chapter supplies the famous sentence about sunlight and starlight in a dark world; the appendix supplies the filing system in which that sentence's evidence is scattered, the caveats its author attached to her own figures, and the one play, Hamlet, whose numbers behave as a slogan-reader expects numbers to behave. Critics who quote the chapter without the appendix inherit a confidence the book does not finally endorse; critics who quote the appendix against the chapter inherit a pedantry the book does not endorse either. Between the two documents stands the real Spurgeon, the most consequential reader this play had in the twentieth century, and the one whose reputation has suffered most from being summarised. What follows restores the whole instrument, finding, filing system, concessions and afterlife, and then delivers the verdict the restored record supports.

## Who Was Caroline Spurgeon, and Why Did Criticism Need Her Census?

Caroline Frances Eleanor Spurgeon (1869 to 1942) came to Shakespeare late and from the top of her profession. Born in India in 1869, educated at Cheltenham Ladies' College and in London, she had made her name first as a Chaucer scholar, compiling the vast record of Chaucer criticism and allusion that appeared in parts across the first quarter of the twentieth century. In 1913 she became professor of English literature at Bedford College in the University of London, the first woman to hold a professorial chair in London and among the first in England. She served on the Newbolt Committee, whose 1921 report reshaped the teaching of English in England, and she worked across the Atlantic as readily as across London, helping to build the international federation of university women with her friend Virginia Gildersleeve of Barnard College. This was not an outsider throwing stones at the academy. The imagery census was designed by a scholar who had already run one of the largest evidence-gathering operations in literary studies, the Chaucer allusion project, and who knew exactly how an archive changes what can be said.

The chair crowned a career already made twice over. Her first book, Mysticism in English Literature, appeared in 1913, the year of the Bedford appointment, and ranged across centuries of writers with a comparatist's appetite. The Chaucer record, issued in parts by the Chaucer Society and complete by 1925, made her the unavoidable starting point for anyone studying that poet's afterlife, less a monograph than a piece of scholarly infrastructure on which other people's monographs would stand. In 1918 she crossed the Atlantic with the British Educational Mission, the visit on which the Gildersleeve friendship and the American network that later financed the census were formed. By the time she turned to Shakespeare in earnest, in her late fifties, she had run a learned society's publication programme, advised a government committee on the national teaching of English, and built an international federation's machinery. The census was not a retirement pastime. It was the third infrastructure project of a scholar who built infrastructure, and it inherited the virtues of the first two: exhaustiveness as a moral quality, classification as a form of hospitality to future workers, and a stated intention, printed in her preface, to publish the material itself one day so that other students could check and extend it. The Geneva digitisers are, eighty years on, executing her stated intention with her own archive.

The criticism she inherited ran on other fuels. The nineteenth century had left Shakespeare studies a rich inheritance of character talk, source hunting and appreciative eloquence. William Hazlitt, writing in 1817, had given the play its most quoted character verdicts, brilliant, quotable and answerable to nothing beyond his own responsiveness; the separate study of [Hazlitt on the Characters of the Play](/2026/10/23/hazlitt-characters-romeo-and-juliet/) shows how much of that tradition is performance by other means. Editors collated texts, antiquarians chased allusions, and the imagery of a play, the thousands of small pictures its language keeps painting, was treated as decoration: noticed in passing, praised in general, never gathered. Nobody had assembled the whole stock of any dramatist's images and asked what the stock, taken entire, might show. Spurgeon's wager was that the whole stock would show two things no selection could: the furniture of the author's mind, betrayed in the comparisons he reached for without thinking, and the undertone of each play, the picture that recurs beneath its action and tunes its emotional key.

The wager nearly stayed a private hobby. Her acknowledgment pages tell the story with unusual candour: a group of American friends, moved by the late Mrs Henry Fairfield Osborn's agency, gave her money when she had little more than a draft and one or two studies; the gift bought a year's leave from Bedford and the secretarial help the counting required. Around 1929 she stepped back from regular teaching and gave the census her full working life. The public unveiling came in lectures, each one a stress test of the method before a specialist audience: a Shakespeare Association lecture in 1930 on the leading motives in the imagery of the tragedies, the British Academy's Annual Shakespeare Lecture in May 1931 on iterative imagery, and an article in the Review of English Studies in October 1933 comparing the imagery of Shakespeare and Bacon. The book followed from Cambridge University Press in 1935, its preface dated from Alciston in Sussex in July 1934, and it announced itself as the first of three planned studies of the image archive. The other two never appeared. Spurgeon moved to Tucson, Arizona, in 1936, where the climate eased her arthritis, and died there in 1942, leaving the single volume to do the work of a trilogy.

### How did a Chaucer scholar end up counting Shakespeare's pictures?

The Chaucer project trained her in the discipline the census required. Recording five hundred years of allusion is filing work before it is interpretation: thousands of discrete items, each captured, dated and classed, from which patterns are allowed to emerge only after the gathering is complete.

Spurgeon brought that order of patience to Shakespeare's metaphors, and she brought something else from the Chaucer years, a conviction that evidence collected without a thesis to serve will embarrass tidy theories and reward patient ones. Her preface insists that the images were assembled with an open mind, the good with the bad, the coarse with the refined, precisely so that the results could surprise the investigator. Whatever else the counting critique later established, the surprise was genuine: nobody who set out to illustrate a prior reading of Romeo and Juliet would have begun by counting herrings, gyves and tennis balls.

Her road to that catalogue ran through an American library. Scholarship on her career records that an American friend's circle drew her attention to John Keats's own copy of Shakespeare, preserved in a private library at Princeton, and that the encounter produced her first substantial Shakespeare publication in 1928, an edition study of the poet's annotated volumes. The episode matters beyond its charm. Spurgeon arrived at Shakespeare's imagery through a material book, through marginalia and the physical traces of a great reader reading, and the census that followed never lost that material temper: images, for her, were things a mind had handled, to be collected as a librarian collects, before they were interpreted as a critic interprets. The international work ran in parallel. With Gildersleeve she had helped to found the International Federation of University Women in the aftermath of the First World War, an organisation built on the premise that scholarship is a countable, connectable, cross-border enterprise, its members registered and its exchanges audited. The woman who federated university women across nations was unlikely to read a play's pictures as isolated beauties. She read them as a population, and populations are counted.

### Why did the lectures matter before the book existed?

The lectures mattered because they let the profession inspect the instrument before it was asked to trust the results. A lecture audience in 1930 or 1931 could watch the method at work on the tragedies and on the idea of iteration itself, and could object to a definition or a filing decision in the room.

Listeners carried the ideas back to seminars before any book existed to be reviewed. By the time Shakespeare's Imagery and What It Tells Us reached print, its central notions, the iterative image, the leading motive, the undertone, were already in circulation as live hypotheses rather than as one author's property. The revolution, when it came, had been rehearsed in public for five years.

## The Method: Every Image in the Canon, Sorted on Cards

The first chapter of the 1935 book states the aim without ornament. Spurgeon proposes to find the author by indirection, borrowing her epigraph and her governing metaphor from Polonius in Hamlet: by indirections find directions out. A poet, she argues, gives himself away in his images, to some extent unconsciously, as a man under stress betrays feeling not in his face but in a muscular tension he does not notice. The comparisons a dramatist reaches for in the heat of composition, drawn from the objects he observes and remembers, and just as revealingly from the objects he never observes at all, lay bare the furniture of his mind. Drama serves the method better than lyric poetry, on her account, because the Elizabethan playwright wrote fast and hot, and his images tumbled out of his characters' mouths before calculation could tidy them. The richer the work, the more the unguarded stock reveals. Hence the census: assemble all of it, classify it, and let the proportions speak.

Her definition of the unit is deliberately enormous. An image, for the census, is every simile and every metaphor, every imaginative picture or experience drawn from any sense, or from mind and emotion, and used by way of analogy. At one extreme an image can fill half a scene, like the untended garden of Richard II; at the other it can hide inside a single word, as ripeness does in King Lear, or flash out of one vivid verb, as murder does when Glamis is said to have murdered sleep in Macbeth. She declines, openly, to fence the category further. People who study metaphor for a living never agree on its boundaries, she observes, and for practical purposes readers know a word-picture when they meet one. The refusal is usually quoted against her, and Section 6 of this article gives the objection its full weight. What should be registered first is the candour: the elasticity of the unit is confessed on page five of the book, not discovered by its enemies on page five of a review.

The definition is pragmatic to the point of defiance, and the pragmatism is itself a position worth naming. Spurgeon's first chapter declines the philosophy of metaphor even while acknowledging its depth, pointing readers to Middleton Murry's essay on metaphor and to the existing literature of definition, and pleading the claims of content over form: her business is with what the images are of, not with the taxonomy of simile, personification, metonymy and synecdoche, whose formal study she cheerfully abandons to a library she does not intend to write. Posterity has usually read the refusal as innocence, the amateur's luck. It reads better as strategy. A census that waited for a settled theory of metaphor would have waited forever, since the theory remains unsettled; a census that fixed its unit by a working rule, one person's consistent judgment applied to every case, could begin on Monday. The cost of the strategy is the filing problem this article examines in due course. The gain was the archive itself, and the archive has outlasted every theory of metaphor proposed in its century.

The labour behind the definition was industrial. Across seven or eight years Spurgeon and her helpers collected the images of the entire Shakespeare canon and of a shelf of contemporaries, Marlowe, Bacon, Jonson, Dekker, Kyd, Greene, Peele, Lyly, Heywood, Chapman, Beaumont and Fletcher, Massinger among them, and entered each item in a card catalogue. Her acknowledgments name the workforce: two successive secretaries, M. A. Cullis and Agnes Latham, whose patient work in sorting, checking, counting, classifying and cross-referencing the cards is, she says, the reason the statistics can be relied on. The quotations throughout the book are taken from one text, the Larger Temple Shakespeare edited by Israel Gollancz and published by Dent in 1899, so that the counted play is a specified play, fixed between specified covers. The classification sorts each image by its subject matter, the field it is drawn from: nature, animals, the body, sickness and medicine, fire and light, daily life, war, the sea, the heavens, and dozens more, with cross-references where one picture belongs to two fields at once. Folded charts at the end of the volume then display the proportions at a glance: the range of subjects in five of Shakespeare's plays set against the whole of Marlowe and practically the whole of Bacon; the daily-life images of five plays set against five contemporary dramatists; the dominating images of individual plays picked out in isolation.

The comparisons were designed as controls, and one of them shows the design at its most elegant. For the daily-life group alone, sport, classes and types, trades, war, substances, money, travel, buildings, royalty and town life, she set the proportions in five of Shakespeare's plays, this play among them, against the output of five contemporary dramatists, and her notes on the chart record what the eye catches in the columns: Ben Jonson towers in topical reference, Dekker and Shakespeare come next, and four of the dramatists register no topical images at all. The point of such a table is not the ranking. It is the demonstration that a habit of imagination can be isolated, held steady across six writers, and compared like a fingerprint, and that Shakespeare's fingerprint, the density of his ordinary world, differs measurably from the fingerprints of men writing for the same stages in the same streets. Whatever the counting critique later did to the biography, this comparative engine was a permanent acquisition: criticism had never before been able to say, with numbers attached, that one dramatist's imagination lived in the daily world more fully than another's.

The book's architecture follows its double claim. Part One, The Revelation of the Man, spends eleven chapters on Shakespeare himself: his senses, his tastes indoors and out, his thought, his habits of association, the man reconstructed from his stock of pictures, down to a pencil sketch of Clopton Bridge at Stratford offered as the original of a passage in The Rape of Lucrece. Part Two, The Function of the Imagery as Background and Undertone in Shakespeare's Art, turns from the man to the plays, marching through histories, comedies, romances and tragedies to isolate each play's leading motive, the running image that forms its pictorial background and emotional undertone. Romeo and Juliet receives its hearing in the tragedies chapter, and the finding announced there is the one the brief for this article, and a century of teaching, call the light-and-dark pattern. What exactly the finding claims, and what the census numbers do and do not have to do with it, are the questions the next two sections separate, because conflating them is the original sin of most summaries of Spurgeon, friendly and hostile alike.

### What exactly did Spurgeon count as one image?

One image, in her practice, is one act of pictorial analogy, whether it takes a clause or a single word to perform it. A simile counts, a metaphor counts, a personification counts, and an image that divides into two subjects is entered once under its main heading and cross-referenced under the other.

The play's total is therefore a count of pictures rather than a count of filings. The definition is generous by design: her quarry was the poet's habits of imagination, and a habit shows as plainly in a buried verb as in a set-piece comparison mounted for applause.

## The Gardener and the Senses: What Part One Built From the Stock

Half of the 1935 book is not about plays at all, and the article's verdict depends on keeping the halves distinct. Part One reads the census as a portrait of the census-taker's subject: Shakespeare's senses first, then his tastes and interests outdoors and in, then the evidence of the images for his thought, then the man himself. Its most quoted finding is sensory. Shakespeare, Spurgeon argues, apprehends the world supremely through sight, and the stock of his comparisons is a countryman's stock, drawn from weather, gardens, animals, the sea and the sports of the field by a mind that had watched those things at first hand. The gardener of her Bacon chapter, the imagination that visualises human beings as plants and trees, choked with weeds or well pruned and bearing ripe fruit, is Part One's presiding figure, and the Clopton Bridge sketch is its most venturesome exhibit: seven lines in The Rape of Lucrece matched, current by current, to the eddies under the eighteenth arch of the bridge at Stratford, and offered as a boyhood memory surfacing in the verse.

Romeo and Juliet feeds this portrait at exactly the points where its census is strongest. Nature is the play's largest class at 39, gardening its largest single field at 11, and the items she files there are a working gardener's calendar rather than a poet's commonplace book: the canker that eats the bud, the bud nipped by an envious worm, fresh female buds and the bud of love, ripeness, medlars, the thorn, the mandrake. The Friar's dawn gathering of baleful weeds and precious-juiced flowers belongs to the same stock, and so does the play's instinct for growth mistimed, sweets ripening too fast and weeds making haste. Part One's inference from such material runs: a dramatist whose metaphors reach, unbidden, for cankers and pruning and the behaviour of sap is reporting a life spent watching growing things. The inference delighted the book's first readers and armed its later critics, and both reactions were rational. As natural history of a vocabulary, the gardening chapters are superb: nobody has ever described the horticultural grain of Shakespeare's imagination better. As biography, they are a chain of plausible steps presented as a path, and each step, from frequency to familiarity, from familiarity to formation, from formation to a named bridge on a named river, is a step the census cannot certify. Part One is where Spurgeon's method meets its limit inside her own book, and the tragedies chapter of Part Two, where this play's light is established by exhibition rather than by inference, is where the method shows what it can do when it stays home. The two halves of one volume are the whole subsequent argument about her in miniature, which is why this article has insisted on reading the appendices against both.

The countryman inference has enjoyed a popular afterlife out of all proportion to its scholarly standing, and the disproportion is itself evidence about how findings travel. In the long quarrel over the authorship of the plays, her demonstration that the imagery is a countryman's imagery, saturated with first-hand knowledge of gardens, rivers, birds and weather, is still brandished as though it closed the case for the Stratford man, quoted by partisans who have never opened the appendix in which she prices her own figures as approximate. The scholarly verdict and the popular verdict on Part One have thus travelled in opposite directions for decades: the academy discounted the biography while the public canonised it. Spurgeon bears a share of responsibility for both journeys, since the confident narrative of Part One invites the quotation and the candid appendix invites the discount, but the divergence belongs to the general law of her legacy. Everything in her book exists in two states, a claim and its qualification, and posterity has a persistent habit of keeping the claim and mislaying the qualification.

The concept that made the census more than a card index arrived in the 1931 British Academy lecture, and the book's twelfth chapter states it most fully. A poet handling a theme, Spurgeon argues, has some picture or symbol raised in his imagination by the theme itself, and the picture recurs in simile and metaphor all through the play that handles it. He may be conscious of the master picture; he is unlikely to be aware of how completely and repeatedly the imagery it generates reveals his symbolic vision. This recurring picture, extended across a whole work, is the iterative image: an extension, over a far larger area, of the same creative impulse that binds a single passage, and one that acts on the audience's imagination with proportionately greater cumulative force. In the histories such undertone symbolism is comparatively elementary; in the comedies it contributes atmosphere and background; in the romances it grows subtle and illustrates an idea rather than a concrete picture; in the tragedies it locks onto the central theme, supplementing and illuminating it, sometimes with extraordinary force, as in Hamlet and King Lear, and sometimes with rare beauty, as in Romeo and Juliet and Antony and Cleopatra.

Three features of the doctrine deserve emphasis, because later criticism kept the word and dropped the doctrine. First, iteration is a claim about recurrence with variation in the service of one governing sensation, not a claim about the frequency of a word. A concordance can count the word light; only a reader sorting pictures can decide that a torch at a feast, a window at dawn, a lamp shamed by daylight and a vault lit by a dead girl's beauty are one image returning in new dress. Second, the doctrine is about effect at least as much as about composition. The undertone works cumulatively and largely below awareness: audiences leave the theatre drenched in a sensation they could not itemise, and the iterative image is Spurgeon's explanation of where the soaking came from. Third, the doctrine carries a built-in modesty that its popularisers shed. The picture is something the theme raises in the poet; it is evidence of how he saw the story, a clue to his imaginative angle, offered as background and undertone rather than as a paraphrase of the play's meaning. The census, on this account, is a detection device for something felt. The count does not create the undertone; it certifies that the undertone is really there, distributed through the text, and not a critic's hallucination projected onto three famous lines.

That certification function is the revolution's durable core, and it explains why the method mattered most for plays whose atmosphere everyone felt and nobody could document. Generations of readers had sensed that Romeo and Juliet glows. Before 1935 the glow was a compliment; after 1935 it was a distribution, traceable scene by scene, and the difference between those two states of knowledge is the difference between appreciation and criticism. The study of [Imagery Clusters: Caroline Spurgeon's Reading](/2025/02/07/spurgeon-imagery-clusters-analysis/) takes up the cluster mechanics in their own right; the concern here is the historical point, that the mechanics were invented to convert a sensation into evidence.

### How is an iterative image different from a frequent word?

A frequent word is a tally of identical tokens; an iterative image is a family of different pictures recognised as one returning sensation. The word night may recur in a shopping list of stage directions, while the iterative image of light runs through suns, stars, torches, lightning, gunpowder flashes and a corpse lighting a tomb, no two instances verbally identical.

Frequency can support the recognition, since a sensation cannot dominate a play it seldom visits, but the recognition itself is an act of reading. Spurgeon's census exists to give that act of reading a documented body to answer to.

## What Spurgeon Found in Romeo and Juliet: Light Against Darkness

The tragedies chapter opens with an analogy to music and to Blake: the recurrent images of a tragedy work as a motif works in a fugue, or as the running accompaniment of Blake's illustrations works beside his text, sometimes interpreting, sometimes decorating, always colouring. Then comes this play, first among the tragedies, and the finding is stated in a single sentence of total confidence. In Romeo and Juliet, she writes, the beauty and ardour of young love are seen by Shakespeare as the irradiating glory of sunlight and starlight in a dark world; the dominating image is light, in every form and manifestation of it: the sun, moon, stars, fire, lightning, the flash of gunpowder, and the reflected light of beauty and of love; set against it, night, darkness, clouds, rain, mist and smoke. Each of the lovers, she observes, thinks of the other as light. The chapter then does what the census licensed it to do: it walks the whole play, gathering instance to instance, until the pattern is not asserted but exhibited.

The exhibition deserves following in her order, because the order is the argument. Romeo's first sight of Juliet at the Capulet ball arrives as a light event: "O, she doth teach the torches to burn bright! / It seems she hangs upon the cheek of night / Like a rich jewel in an Ethiope's ear" (1.5.44-46). In the orchard the compliment becomes cosmology. Juliet is the sun rising in the east (2.2.2-3), her eyes are stars that have traded places with heaven's fairest, and "The brightness of her cheek would shame those stars, / As daylight doth a lamp" (2.2.19-20); her eyes in heaven would stream so bright through the airy region "That birds would sing and think it were not night" (2.2.20-21). Juliet returns the currency at a higher denomination: Romeo, cut out in little stars when he dies, will make the face of heaven so fine "That all the world will be in love with night, / And pay no worship to the garish sun" (3.2.22-25). Romeo's definition of love, offered in Act 1 before he knows what the word will cost, already runs on light's physics: a smoke made with the fume of sighs, and, purged, "a fire sparkling in lovers' eyes" (1.1.186-190). Juliet waiting for the Nurse imagines love's messengers as thoughts "Which ten times faster glide than the sun's beams, / Driving back shadows over louring hills" (2.5.4-5). The lovers, Spurgeon notes, even credit their own beauties as sufficient light to see by in the orchard dark. And at the end Romeo finds the supposedly dead Juliet in the tomb and reads her face in the pattern's last transformation: "her beauty makes / This vault a feasting presence full of light" (5.3.85-86). The corpse irradiates the grave. A pattern that can survive its heroine's death without changing its terms has earned the word dominating.

Between those peaks her chapter gathers the pattern's middle register, the pictorial background that no anthology of famous lines would assemble: the Prince's opening image of rage quenched, or failing to be quenched, "with purple fountains issuing from your veins" (1.1.83-84); Benvolio and Montague talking under the rising sun; the torches, candles, lights and lamps of the talk before the ball, and the ball's flashing torches four times accentuated; the sunrise the Friar watches from his cell; the light and shadow chasing over Juliet's words in the orchard; the black fate of the day Mercutio dies; the fight to which the duelists go like lightning; the lovers' parting song built on the quivering of light from darkness through dawn, "jocund day / Stands tiptoe on the misty mountain tops" (3.5.9-10); Romeo's anguished summary of the whole design, "More light and light; more dark and dark our woes" (3.5.36); and the close, the churchyard dark, Paris's torch quenched, Romeo's torch, the lantern vault, and the overcast morning when "The sun, for sorrow, will not show his head" (5.3.305). Even Capulet, whom nobody casts as a poet, speaks the pattern when it serves him: Paris shall see at the ball "Earth-treading stars that make dark heaven light" (1.2.24-25), and finding Juliet weeping he reaches for a sunset: "When the sun sets, the air doth drizzle dew; / But for the sunset of my brother's son, / It rains downright" (3.5.126-128). The pattern is not the lovers' private idiolect. It is the weather of the whole city, which is what dominating means.

Three counter-currents, which the tragedies chapter's catalogue implies more than it states, complete the finding and protect it from the charge of simplicity. The first is darkness chosen. Romeo enters the play as a manufacturer of gloom, and his father describes the manufacture in the pattern's own terms: the young man steals home from light, shuts up his windows, locks fair daylight out, "And make himself an artificial night" (1.1.139-140). Before love irradiates him, melancholy counterfeits the night the lovers will later sanctify, and the play's light economy begins with a false currency in circulation, against which the true radiance of the feast and the orchard will be measured. The second counter-current is light distrusted. Juliet, at the very summit of the orchard scene, refuses the moon as a witness: "O, swear not by the moon, th' inconstant moon, / That monthly changes in her circled orb, / Lest that thy love prove likewise variable" (2.2.109-111). The dominating image contains its own heresy, a member of the light family cashiered for unreliability, and the pattern is thereby refined rather than merely repeated: the lovers' light must be solar and stellar, fixed and self-luminous, while the borrowed, waxing light is struck from the liturgy. The third is the pattern litigated. At the dawn parting the lovers argue the imagery itself, Juliet inventing a nightingale to keep the night in session, Romeo conceding the lark and the severing day, until the dispute resolves into the line Spurgeon quotes as the design's summary: more light and light, more dark and dark our woes. A pattern that the characters themselves conduct, contest and construe is not a critic's overlay. It is the play's working medium, and the census's service was to show that the medium saturates every register of the text, from the Prince's jurisprudence to a father's small talk, and not only the anthology speeches where the lovers shine.

Two further claims complete the finding, and both are easy to miss in summaries. The first is about time. Shakespeare, Spurgeon argues, saw the story in its swift and tragic beauty as an almost blinding flash of light, suddenly ignited and as swiftly quenched, and the form obeys the image: he compresses the action from the nine months and more of his source to five days, Sunday's meeting to Thursday night's reunion in death, so that swiftness, brilliance, danger and destruction are accentuated together. Juliet's own figure for the betrothal, "too like the lightning, which doth cease to be / Ere one can say 'It lightens'" (2.2.118-120), and the Friar's warning that violent delights die "like fire and powder, / Which, as they kiss, consume" (2.6.9-11), are on this reading not decorations on the pattern but the pattern stating its own physics: the brightest things in the play are the fastest to burn out. The second claim is about origin. The running image, she shows, has a germ in the last place a snob would look, the wooden doggerel of Arthur Brooke's Romeus and Juliet, where the lovers are already, each to the other, as the sun is to the world, and where the feud burns as a mighty fire whose sparks the Prince hopes to quench. Shakespeare took a handful of conventional similes of little poetic worth and transmuted them into a continuous and consistent running image of brilliance swiftly quenched. The finding, in other words, is genetic as well as descriptive: it names not only what the play's light does but where the young dramatist found the seed of it and what his imagination did to the seed.

One more passage locates the finding inside the book's larger argument, and it is the passage where the brief's verification flag, the Spurgeon attribution, can be checked at its most delicate. In the second chapter, comparing Shakespeare's imagery with Bacon's, Spurgeon grants Bacon a passionate association of light with intellect, light as truth, virtue, knowledge, the Father of illumination himself, and states flatly that Shakespeare shows no sign of this interest. The single exception she allows is this play, where the running image shows Shakespeare imaginatively conceiving love as light in a dark world. Bacon's light is the mind's; the light of Romeo and Juliet is the heart's, and the contrast is her Exhibit A for the difference between the two writers' imaginations, Shakespeare being, in her famous summary of the whole comparison, a man who thinks most easily and naturally in the terms of a gardener. The attribution, then, is exact: the dominance of light in this play is Spurgeon's claim, made twice, in the comparison chapter and in the tragedies chapter, and framed both times as a pattern of recurrence, a running image, rather than as the spoils of any single class in her census. Hold that framing in mind. The numbers come next, and they tell a subtler story than the slogan.

One tributary of the comparison chapter feeds this play so directly that it earns a paragraph of its own: her pages on the astronomical imagination. Shakespeare, she argues there, never once mentions the primum mobile that fascinated Bacon and Milton, but the conception of the stars moving in their fixed spheres, and straying from them only as a sign or result of great disturbance or disaster, is the most constant astronomical idea in his mind. The myth of Phaeton driving his horses across the sky, she suggests, helped keep the old Ptolemaic heavens alive in the poetic imagination long after the new astronomy had displaced them. Transpose that argument into Verona and the play's machinery lights up: star-crossed lovers are spheres disturbed, a cosmos whose fixed order has been knocked from its track; Juliet calling for Phaeton's whip in the epithalamium is borrowing the solar chariot for an errand of love; and the Prince's city, in which private violence keeps breaking the public peace, is the earthly register of a heaven whose bodies will not stay in their courses. Spurgeon does not develop the transposition at length, and this article does not pretend she did. What the astronomy pages show is the comparative method generating, almost as by-product, the best available account of why this play's light is never merely decorative: in the imagination she reconstructs, light is what order looks like, and darkness is what its disturbance feels like, from the spheres down to a street fight at dawn.

## Her Own Numbers: The Appendix, the 204 Images, and What They Actually Prove

Appendix II of the 1935 book prints the census totals play by play: Romeo and Juliet contains 204 images in a text of 2,989 lines. Appendix IV then does something no summary of Spurgeon bothers to mention: it prints the detailed analysis of this play's 204 images as a sample of the method, class by class, with the items named. Read straight, the table behind the finding is this. Nature is the largest class at 39, within which gardening alone supplies 11, the sea 9, weather 7, celestial bodies 7. Personification comes next at 23, and Spurgeon annotates the class herself with the parenthesis that gives the whole game away: note how many of them are of light and darkness. Animals supply 21, birds 10 of them. Fire and light, the class a slogan-reader expects to top the poll, supplies 12. Food and cooking supply 11, classes and types of people 11, war and weapons 10. Sickness and medicine supply 8, and her prose note on the sample is explicit that the corresponding class in Hamlet furnishes 20. Domestic images supply 7, sport and games 7, the body 5, proverbial sayings 5, religion and superstition 5; classical allusion, human relations and books and reading supply 4 each; law, buildings, substances and clothes 3 each; jewels, music, theatre and indoor games 2 each; and eight single strays, one each for birth, death, royalty, science, topical reference, facts from books, money and emblem, bring the classes to exactly 204. Even her denominators were borrowed with acknowledgment: the line counts printed beside the image totals are taken, her footnote records, from a count by Mr Hart published in the Review of English Studies in January 1932 and made from the Cambridge edition of 1863 to 1866. The census, for all its solitary heroism, was a network product, secretaries, a draughtswoman, borrowed line counts and an endowed leave of absence, and its front matter says so at every step; the lone-counter legend is the imitators' embroidery, not the author's claim.

The lead finding, in short, does not sit at the head of her own table, and this article counts that fact as the most instructive page in the book rather than as an embarrassment to be managed. Light dominates the play, on Spurgeon's own showing, by running across the filing system: 12 images filed directly under fire and light; 7 more filed as celestial bodies inside nature, the stars and sunrises and meteors; the lightning filed under weather; the personified suns and moons and mornings that make up so much of the class of 23; the jewel in the Ethiope's ear filed, unavoidably, under jewels; the lantern and the feasting presence filed nowhere tidy at all. The dominance is real precisely because it cannot be contained by one drawer of the cabinet. What the census proves is the distribution: that these pictures occur all through the play, in all mouths, at every hinge of the action, gathering as no other sensation gathers. What the census cannot do, and was never honestly asked to do by its maker at her best, is perform the recognition that they are one pattern. That recognition is criticism, and the tragedies chapter performs it in prose, instance by instance, as Section 4 of this article followed. The counting objection, properly aimed, therefore hits not the finding but a lazy theory of the finding, the theory that a tally somewhere contains the number that means light. Spurgeon at her most careful never held that theory. Her first appendix says so, in terms her detractors rarely quote: probably no two people would entirely agree on the number of images in any one play, any count of this kind must to some extent be approximate, and the safeguard of her figures is only that they were all made by the same person on the same method, so that the results and general proportions may be assumed sound. Proportions, not verdicts. The appendix concedes in advance almost everything the later critique would say about precision, while quietly preserving the one thing the critique could not touch, the pattern's existence.

Two smaller yields of the numbers deserve record before the objection is heard in full. The sickness figure disciplines a lazy assumption about the play: plague and poison decide the plot, yet sickness and medicine supply only 8 of the 204 images, against Hamlet's 20, because in this play disease is machinery rather than atmosphere, a letter stopped by a quarantine and a vial bought in Mantua, while in Hamlet sickness is the air the court breathes. A count that can correct an impression that confident has earned its keep. And the density figure, plain arithmetic on her printed totals, places the play honestly in the canon: 204 images in 2,989 lines is a thinner imagistic weave than Macbeth's 208 in 2,084, which suits a play whose effects run through a single sustained pattern rather than through saturation. Neither yield requires the numbers to be exact. Both require them to be roughly right, which is all their maker ever claimed.

The summary below gathers the method and the finding in the form the series keeps for reference.

| Element of the study | What Spurgeon did or found |
|---|---|
| The instrument | A card catalogue of every image in Shakespeare and in a shelf of contemporaries, collected over seven or eight years and classified by subject |
| The unit | One image: any simile, metaphor, personification or word-picture used by way of analogy, from a whole scene's symbol to a single vivid verb |
| The text counted | The Larger Temple Shakespeare, edited by Israel Gollancz (Dent, 1899) |
| Total for this play | 204 images in 2,989 lines (Appendix II of the 1935 book) |

Read as a whole, the InsightCrunch Spurgeon summary records a study whose headline and whose arithmetic point in different directions, and whose greatness lies in the headline being right anyway. That tension, not the slogan, is what the rest of this article adjudicates.

### Why does her largest class for this play say Nature rather than Light?

Because the classes file images by the field they are drawn from, not by the sensation they build together. A sunrise belongs to nature, a personified morning to personification, a torch to fire and light, a star to the celestial bodies, and the pattern that those four filings jointly sustain has no drawer of its own.

Nature tops the table because Shakespeare drew his comparisons from the outdoor world more than from any other warehouse, a fact about the man's habits that Part One of the book exists to establish. Light rules the play across the drawers, which is a fact about the play. The two facts coexist in her appendix without contradiction, and the confusion of the two is the commonest error in the whole Spurgeon literature.

### What did Spurgeon herself admit could not be counted exactly?

Almost everything about the edges of the exercise. Her appendix warns that no two people would agree on the number of images in any play, that deciding whether a phrase is an image at all, whether it is one image or two, and which heading owns it, are judgments on which honest counters divide, and that her figures are approximate.

Their safeguard, she insists, is consistency: one counter, one method, throughout, so that the proportions between classes and between plays can be trusted even where any single figure could be challenged. It is a remarkable concession to find printed inside the book that founded a counting school, and the school's later troubles began when its imitators quoted the figures and skipped the appendix.

## The Charts: Seeing a Mind at a Glance

Bound into the back of the 1935 volume, folded to be opened flat, are seven charts, and they deserve treatment as arguments in their own right, because they embody the census's boldest claim in its purest form: that proportion can be seen. Charts I to III place the range and subjects of images in five of Shakespeare's plays beside the whole of Marlowe's work and practically the whole of Bacon's, three imaginations rendered as three profiles. Chart IV performs the daily-life comparison across five contemporary dramatists. Chart V displays the range of Shakespeare's images entire. Charts VI and VII isolate the dominating images of individual plays, among them Hamlet. Her acknowledgments thank Lois Latham for expert copying and drafting of some of the charts, a small sentence that records a large fact: this criticism was drawn before it was typeset, plotted by hand like an engineer's diagram or an actuary's table, in an era when literary argument lived exclusively in paragraphs.

The chart form argues three things no paragraph can argue as fast. It argues scale, since a class that fills half a column has its dominance in the eye before the mind has parsed a single figure. It argues absence, since the empty cell, the subject a writer never touches, shows on a chart as a visible blank, and Spurgeon's whole theory of revelation depends on absences, on the objects a mind does not observe or remember. And it argues comparability, since one profile laid beside another converts the vague language of influence and affinity into differences of degree that can be pointed at. The risks of the form are the reciprocal of its powers. A chart launders judgment into geometry: the filing decisions vanish into the columns, the reader's eye receives proportions as if they were measurements of rainfall, and the approximate hardens into the exact by the mere act of being drawn. Her notes on the charts show her half-aware of the strain: the grouping, she writes of the great range chart, on the whole explains itself, though certain points may strike the reader as strange, war under daily life among them, and she defends the strange groupings case by case rather than by principle. The defence is characteristic, local, practical, unillusioned, and it concedes in passing what the charts' clean columns deny, that the architecture of the evidence is a series of judgments wearing a uniform. The history of the book's reception is partly the history of that laundering, as handbooks reproduced her findings with the confidence of her charts and without the diffidence of her appendix. Yet the form's priority in her century should not be missed. Before the database, before the concordance printout, before the screen, a professor of English at Bedford College folded seven hand-drawn data displays into a work of literary criticism and dared her colleagues to think in distributions. The Geneva project's instinct to file her under the prehistory of computational literary studies is vindicated nowhere more plainly than in those folded sheets.

One absence in the charts is worth recording, because it locates this play precisely inside her system. The two dominating-image charts are given to the histories and to Hamlet with Troilus and Cressida; Romeo and Juliet receives no dominating-image chart of its own. Its evidence appears instead as the prose analysis of Appendix IV, items named one by one under their classes, and as the exhibited catalogue of the tragedies chapter. The difference in treatment matches the difference in the findings. Hamlet's dominance can be drawn, because it gathers inside classes a chart can stack; the light of Romeo and Juliet must be narrated, because it lives between classes, and a column of figures for fire and light, standing at twelve, would have argued against the very pattern the chapter proves. Her formats, in short, knew their limits before her critics did, and the play that made her famous is the play her charts decline to summarise.

## The Revolution: What Criticism Could Claim After 1935

The measure of a methodological revolution is the set of sentences that become unsayable after it, and by that measure 1935 divides the century. Before the census, the atmosphere of a Shakespeare play was a matter of testimony: a critic reported a sensation and the report stood or fell by the critic's general credit. After the census, atmosphere was a matter of distribution, and a claim about a play's emotional weather had either to reckon with the sorted evidence or to confess itself impressionism. Spurgeon's chapters supplied the new sentence-forms that the profession has used ever since. To say that Hamlet is saturated with disease, that Macbeth thinks in darkness and unnatural deeds and ill-fitting garments, that King Lear's imagination runs on the tortured body, is to speak her language whether or not her name is attached; the imagery chapter of every subsequent handbook, and the play-by-play atmosphere studies that fill the journals of the middle decades of the century, work in the space her census cleared. The detailed anatomy of [Light and Dark Imagery in Romeo and Juliet](/2024/01/26/light-and-dark-imagery-analysis/) is one late flower of that clearance: an entire study built on a pattern whose existence had first to be certified before it could be analysed.

For this play the revolution's local effect was to fix the default description. The light-against-dark account moved from her tragedies chapter into the common stock of teaching, where it hardened into the first thing a student is told about the play's language, and into the introductions of editions, where it frames the reading before the first scene begins. A teacher assembling the census into a seminar presentation, the classes on one slide and the running pattern across the next, can check the deck in the [ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) before facing the room; the finding itself needs no software, because it had already become the room's furniture. Print tells the same story of absorption. The Geneva scholars who study her legacy record six printings of the book within thirty-five years, and the volume's own imprint page runs to a nineteenth printing in 2005, seventy years after first publication: a working life for a methods book that few systematic studies in any field match. Una Ellis-Fermor is reported to have carried elements of the unfinished programme forward in The Frontiers of Drama, and Edward A. Armstrong's Shakespeare's Imagination of 1946 extended her iteration into chains of associated images, pictures that travel in companies and betray the mind's habits of linkage. Whether or not every thread of that descent can be documented, the direction of travel is plain in the record that can be documented, the reprintings, the handbooks, the schoolrooms. The imagery revolution did not merely influence criticism of this play. It set the terms on which the play's language is still introduced to nearly every new reader.

The schoolroom tells the absorption most plainly, because the schoolroom keeps only what it can use. Study materials for this play across the English-speaking world introduce light and dark as the first fact about its language, and the lineage is openly acknowledged in the tradition's own reference summaries: the light theme, as one widely used study text puts it, began as an idea in Caroline Spurgeon's work, before branching in several directions in later hands. That branching is the normal fate of a founding finding, and its directions are instructive. In one direction the pattern became moralised, light for love and dark for hate, a tidier scheme than anything in her chapter, where the night is the lovers' element and the daylight belongs to the brawling streets. In another it became temporal, the play's lights read as clocks in a tragedy of haste, which is her flash argument developed. In a third it became theatrical, a matter of torches and windows and the practical lighting of an afternoon stage. Each branch is a simplification of the census chapter and each preserves its root claim, that the play's language is organised by a single returning sensation. A finding that can survive being simplified by three generations of teachers has passed a test no review can administer.

Two qualifications keep the triumph honest. The revolution was methodological before it was interpretive: what conquered the profession was the idea that verbal patterning is evidence, collectable and checkable, and that idea survived even where particular findings were disputed. And the revolution had a shadow, which the next section examines: a method that certifies patterns can also manufacture them, and the same decades that absorbed Spurgeon's findings absorbed a generation of cluster-hunting in which any play could be shown to be dominated by any sufficiently elastic category. The founder had foreseen the danger in her own appendix and built the safeguard of the single consistent counter; the danger arrived anyway, wearing her vocabulary.

## The Counting Objection: Where the Census Flattens the Play

The objection, stated at full strength, runs as follows. A count is only as good as its categories, and the categories are the counter's inventions: the census files each image once, under a main heading chosen, by Spurgeon's own confession in her first appendix, arbitrarily where the picture divides, so the celebrated proportions are artefacts of filing decisions made at a desk in Sussex. A count is blind to weight: the sun speech in the orchard and a throwaway lamp in a servant's mouth each register once, though one bends the whole play around it and the other is spent in a breath. A count is blind to voice and moment: it cannot tell that the same torch means welcome at the feast and doom in the churchyard, because meaning in drama is an event, not a property of phrases. A count sweeps the living together with the dead: conventional comparisons inherited from the love-poetry stock, and the very similes Spurgeon traces to Brooke's doggerel, swell the classes alongside the images Shakespeare forged. And the whole enterprise, the objection concludes, commits the sin its founder's first appendix half-confesses, mistaking an approximate instrument for a measuring rod, and reading designs into distributions that a different counter, with different drawers, would have distributed otherwise.

Every clause of that indictment lands somewhere, and this article's verdict assigns the blows carefully. Against the lazy use of her numbers, the objection is fatal, and it is just that the laziest use was made by her imitators rather than by her text, which prints its own caveats. Against the filing system, the objection is half-fatal: the classes are indeed judgments, and Appendix IV's own evidence, light scattered across five headings, shows the drawers failing to hold the book's most famous pattern, which is either a reductio of the system or a demonstration that the system was a prospecting tool whose results always required a reader to finish them. Against the finding itself, the objection fails, for a reason the objectors rarely face: the light pattern does not rest on the census at all in the way a theorem rests on a proof. It rests on the tragedies chapter's exhibited instances, line after line, scene after scene, which any reader can verify in an armchair without a single card. Burn the card catalogue and the chapter still stands; the count was the scaffolding, and scaffolding can be condemned without touching the building. What the objection does destroy, permanently, is the biography built on the same numbers in Part One of the book, where proportions are asked to testify about a man's inner life: that Shakespeare loved gardening, watched rivers as a boy at Clopton Bridge, and thought like a countryman may be true, but the route from image frequencies to a boyhood is paved with exactly the filing judgments the objection impeaches, and on that road the counts carry more weight than their maker, in her appendix mood, said they could bear. L. C. Knights had fired the period's warning shot at documentary biography two years before the book appeared, in his 1933 essay asking how many children Lady Macbeth had; the imagery census gave the warning a second, larger target, and the profession's slow turn against biographical inference in the middle of the century is unintelligible without it.

The filing problem deserves a worked example, because in the abstract it sounds pedantic and in the concrete it is decisive. Take the play's most famous light image, the rich jewel in an Ethiope's ear (1.5.45-46). In Spurgeon's Appendix IV the line sits under jewels, a class of two, alongside an agate-stone on a vizard. As an entry in the light pattern it is Exhibit A, the moment the feast's torches are taught their business; as an entry in the census it is jewellery. Or take the meteor of the dawn parting, filed among the celestial bodies, or the lightning of the betrothal speech, filed under weather, or Romeo's sighs figured as smoke, filed under fire and light beside the ashes. No filing is wrong; every filing is a choice; and the pattern that the tragedies chapter celebrates exists only in the cross-references between the choices. Her safeguard, the single consistent counter, answers the charge of randomness and immediately invites the deeper charge: a consistent counter is a consistent bias, one reader's habits of seeing, replicated two hundred times and then cited as objectivity. The fair conclusion is narrower than either party's slogan. The census is best understood as the record of one superb reader's attention, kept with a discipline no other reader of her generation imposed on attention, and its authority is the authority of that attention, not of arithmetic. So understood, the numbers lose their false majesty and keep their real use, which is to show where a great reader looked, and how the looking distributed itself when it was not allowed to cherry-pick.

The weight objection has a limiting case in this play, and it deserves stating because no critic of Spurgeon, to this article's knowledge, has put it at the centre. Her census files money in Romeo and Juliet at exactly one image, a stray under one each: tribute. Substances add wax and lead. Yet the play's catastrophe is transacted in literal gold: gold changes hands for the Nurse's errand, gold buys the apothecary's poison against the law of Mantua, and gold, cast into statues, is the fathers' final settlement of accounts. None of that gold is an image, because none of it is figurative; it is the thing itself, weighed and paid. A method that counts analogies will therefore register the play's least lethal substance, light, two hundred ways, and its most lethal substance, money, almost not at all, and a reader who trusted the census as a map of the play's causal world would invert the tragedy's economics entirely. Spurgeon's own chapter is innocent of the inversion, since it claims to map the imagination and not the plot. The limiting case simply fixes, once and for all, what her instrument measures: the weather of a mind, not the machinery of a world. Criticism that keeps the two apart can use her for a century more; criticism that confuses them will keep rediscovering the confusion and calling it her fault.

## Clemen's Correction: Images Develop; They Do Not Merely Accumulate

The most consequential answer to Spurgeon came not from an enemy of imagery study but from its other founder. Wolfgang Clemen's The Development of Shakespeare's Imagery, published in English in 1951 and reissued in a second edition in 1977, accepted her premise that the images are where the plays think, and rejected her procedure root and branch. For Clemen an image has a career: it enters a play in one function and is transformed by the action, gathering meaning from speaker, situation and sequence, until in the mature tragedies the imagery and the drama are a single organism. A census that lifts every instance out of its moment and files it under its subject therefore destroys the very thing it hopes to study, the way an image behaves in time. His book marches through the canon chronologically to show the art developing, from the iterative and comparatively external imagery of the early plays toward the fully integrated symbolism of the great tragedies, and it gives Romeo and Juliet a chapter of its own, placing this play early in the development, its patterns still working largely as atmosphere and accompaniment rather than as the fused structural symbolism of the later work.

The adjudication this article owes its readers can now be given, and it splits, though not evenly. Clemen wins the theoretical point outright: frequency is not function, an image's tenth occurrence may matter more than its first nine combined, and any method that cannot register development will misread a developing artist. But the victory is parasitic on the defeat it claims, because the developmental history Clemen writes presupposes exactly the mapped distributions Spurgeon's census supplied; a critic cannot chart how a pattern grows through a play, or through a career, until somebody has gathered the pattern's instances, and in 1951 the gathering existed because she had done it. Nor does Clemen's placement of this play refute her finding about it: to call the light pattern iterative and atmospheric in the early manner is to redescribe her dominating image in his vocabulary, at a lower temperature, not to produce a rival pattern that fits the text better. Where the two genuinely part company is over what a pattern proves. For Spurgeon in her Part One mood, patterns testify about the man; for Clemen, they testify about the work's growth; the subsequent century has sided with Clemen on the destination of the evidence while continuing to use Spurgeon's maps to get there. The counting objection and the developmental correction together leave the census standing as the indispensable first operation of imagery study and the insufficient last one, which is a more useful legacy than either party's partisans advertised.

### What does an image's development mean that its frequency cannot show?

Development means that an image's meaning is the sum of its history inside the play, not a constant it carries from scene to scene. The torches of the Capulet feast signify welcome, display and erotic promise; the torch Paris carries into the churchyard signifies intrusion and extinction, and Romeo's torch in the vault burns beside a beauty that outshines it.

A frequency count registers three torches. A developmental reading registers a single instrument played in three keys, the last of them the pattern's own extinction, which is the difference between knowing that a play contains an image and knowing what the play does with it.

The sun itself supplies the cleanest demonstration, and Clemen's framework earns its fee on it. The play's solar career opens in borrowed plumage: Romeo, still Rosaline's Petrarchan tenant, awards her the stock superlative, "The all-seeing sun / Ne'er saw her match since first the world begun" (1.2.92-93), a compliment any sonneteer could sign. At the orchard window the same body is promoted from witness to identity: Juliet does not rival the sun, she is the sun, and the compliment's grammar mutinies into cosmology. Then the beloved herself demotes the star she was made of, promising a heaven so fine with Romeo's fragments that the world will pay no worship to the garish sun (3.2.25), and the word garish, gaudy and overbright, files the first complaint against light from inside the pattern. The career closes under a sky that withdraws the symbol entirely: a glooming peace, a morning in which the sun for sorrow will not show his head (5.3.305). Witness, identity, rival, mourner: four stations, one body, and the whole tragedy of the lovers' reputation among the elements enacted in the changes. No column of Spurgeon's charts can hold that story, because the story is the column's contents set in motion; yet no reader could be sure the story was the play's, and not a selection effect, until a census had established that the sun's appearances are the pattern's spine and not its decoration. The two methods are successive operations on the same evidence, and the profession's habit of staging them as rivals is the least interesting thing about either.

## The Editor's Test: What an Edition Can Certify

The third corner of the disagreement is occupied by the editor, and the brief for this article rightly insists on one, because editing is where the census meets its unexamined premise. Spurgeon counted a specified text, the Larger Temple Shakespeare edited by Israel Gollancz, and said so in her prefatory note; yet the counted play of her appendix is silently treated, by her and by nearly everyone since, as the play itself. An editor knows better. The play that reaches a counter has already been decided, line by line, by editorial labour: which early text to follow where the 1597 and 1599 quartos diverge, how to lineate, where to place the images' very words. Change the base text and the census changes with it, not at the margins of interpretation but in the raw material. Brian Gibbons, whose Arden edition of 1980 closed the era of the great text-first introductions, presents the play's poetic design, its imagery included, as settled descriptive furniture for the reader he is guiding; Marjorie Garber's later reading of the play, surveyed in [Marjorie Garber Reads Romeo and Juliet](/2026/12/25/marjorie-garber-romeo-and-juliet/), shows a post-census critic working the same image-fields with psychoanalytic and theatrical instruments Spurgeon never held. The editorial tradition's relation to her finding is thus double. Editions ratify the finding, printing introductions in which light against darkness is the play's recognised weather and notes that gloss its instances one by one; and editions quietly expose the finding's foundation, since the instances glossed are instances an editor first chose to print.

The adjudication here is brief because the editor's role is clarifying rather than competing. An introduction can certify that the profession believes the pattern; only the text can certify that the pattern is there, and the text an edition prints is itself an argument. Spurgeon's instinct to name her edition was the soundest methodological sentence in her front matter, and the correct lesson for anyone citing her numbers is to cite them as she earned them: the proportions of the play as Gollancz printed it, robust enough that no plausible re-editing overturns the pattern, and not one image more precise than that.

The sequence of standard editions across the century makes the ratification visible as a relay. John Dover Wilson's Cambridge text of 1955, George Walton Williams's edition of 1964, Gibbons's Arden of 1980, G. Blakemore Evans's New Cambridge of 1984 and Jill Levenson's Oxford of 2000 differ from one another in textual policy, in performance interest and in critical temper, sometimes sharply. What none of them does is introduce the play without its weather. A comparative study of how these editions handle a single crowded scene has been able to treat the imagery framework as the common ground from which their real disagreements, about staging, about copy-text, about the first quarto's authority, proceed. That is the deepest form of a finding's victory: to become the premise other arguments stand on. Spurgeon's name is not always spoken in the introductions that transmit her pattern, and the anonymity is itself diagnostic. Discoveries keep their discoverers' names; furniture does not need them.

## The Statistical Afterlife: Vindication in a Different Key

The strangest chapter of the story is the one in which the counting, dismissed by the middle of the century as a positivist embarrassment, returns wearing the prestige of computation. The University of Geneva's project Caroline Spurgeon in the 21st Century states its aims plainly: to recover, update, digitise and recontextualise her work on Chaucer allusion and on Shakespeare's imagery, and to situate her methods within a history of computational literary studies that has not yet been properly told. The framing is exact. A scholar who, before computers, charted and classified thousands of images in order to assess a playwright's distinctiveness against his contemporaries was practising, by hand and at heroic cost, the operations that corpus methods now perform by default: exhaust the evidence, classify consistently, compare against a control set, and let proportions argue. Her comparisons of Shakespeare with Marlowe and Bacon were prototype attribution studies, attempts to fingerprint an imagination by its habits, and the modern stylometry that tests disputed plays by counted habits of language descends from that prototype in spirit even where its units differ from hers.

The archive itself has become part of the afterlife. Her papers, presented after her death to the Bedford College archives and now held at Royal Holloway in the University of London, run to thirty-two boxes, and Geneva's scholars treat the surviving materials, including the drafted companion studies that print never reached, as evidence to be worked rather than relics to be shelved. There is a fittingness in the arrangement that Spurgeon, who made a career of arguing that a mind is known by what it files, would have appreciated with some wariness. The card catalogue has become the document; the filing system is being read as the thought.

The vindication is real and it is partial, and the partiality is the same seam this article has traced throughout. Computation vindicates the pattern: digital counts confirm that the distributions she reported are broadly there to be found, and her proportions survive re-counting far better than her critics predicted, because a consistent counter's consistency is precisely what a machine reproduces. Computation does not vindicate the biography: no concordance can certify that a boy watched eddies under Clopton Bridge, and the inferential leap from proportion to person remains a leap however the proportion is computed. Frank Kermode's later turn from imagery to language, examined in its own right elsewhere in this series, supplies the afterlife's necessary counterweight in a single reminder: a cluster is an abstraction from sentences, and the sentences still have to be read one by one, in their scenes, by a mind. Between the machine that counts and the reader who reads, Spurgeon's census turns out to have been an early treaty, negotiated with index cards.

## What the Census Cannot See in This Play

A fair audit ends by walking the instrument around its own blind spots, and three of them are specific to this play. The first is the stage. Spurgeon's images are read from a page, by a scholar, in a study, with a card box; the play's first audiences met its light in a yard open to the afternoon sky, where torches burned visibly in daylight and the radiant night of the orchard was an act of verbal scene-painting performed at two o'clock. The census cannot register that transaction, in which the imagery does not describe the lighting but replaces it, building a darkness the building could not supply and then populating the darkness with suns. Her pattern is, if anything, more remarkable for the conditions it overcame, but the instrument that found it has no column for the finding's theatrical cause. The second blind spot is register. The census files the prose of the servants, the Nurse's anecdotes and Mercutio's flying matches on the same terms as the lovers' verse, and the filing conceals a distribution inside the distribution: the light pattern lives overwhelmingly in the verse of the principals, while the play's prose keeps its feet in kitchens, streets and errands. A count by speaker and by medium, which hers is not, would show the pattern as an aristocracy of the imagination, the high style's weather, with the low style admitted only when it borrows the masters' sky, as Capulet does. The third is sound. Her definition of the image honours every sense, and her practice, like her subject's, is dominated by sight; yet some of this play's patterning is aural, the chiming of light-words against dark ones in the ear, the musicians' debate about silver sound in the house of mourning, the lark and nightingale settled by hearing. A census of pictures can point at these things and cannot weigh them. None of the three blind spots overturns the finding. Together they mark the boundary at which her instrument hands the play back to the kinds of attention, theatrical, sociological, musical, that no card catalogue can perform, which is where Clemen and the editors and the performers were waiting all along.

## Why This Play Was Her Perfect Subject

A method is lucky or unlucky in its first great exhibit, and Spurgeon's luck in Romeo and Juliet deserves a section of its own, because the fit between instrument and play explains the finding's durability better than any defence of the instrument in general. Consider what the census needs in order to shine. It needs a play whose meaning travels substantially in its atmosphere, so that mapping the atmosphere maps the meaning; the Verona tragedy, in which the plot is a feud's mechanics and the experience is a climate, qualifies exactly. It needs a play written fast by a young author whose symbolic vision had not yet learned to hide, so that the governing picture recurs undisguised; the five-day flash, composed in the middle 1590s by a dramatist still in his early thirties, qualifies again. It needs a pattern built from common stock, suns and torches and weather, rather than from recondite learning, so that a subject classification can catch it; and it needs the pattern to matter, to be the very thing readers had always felt and never documented, so that the documentation lands as revelation. On every count the play obliges. The same instrument applied to a late romance, where the imagery serves ideas too subtle for subject drawers, or to a history, where the undertone is comparatively elementary by her own account, yields findings that are true and inert. Applied here, it yielded the sentence with which this article began, the sentence the play's readers had been waiting, without knowing it, for three centuries to be able to say with evidence.

Her own book stages the fit as a diptych. Appendix IV prints the full sample analysis for exactly two plays, this one and Hamlet, and the tragedies chapter passes from the first to the second with a sentence of deliberate contrast: in Hamlet, naturally, an entirely different atmosphere. The two exhibits are the method's thesis and antithesis in its inventor's own arrangement. For Hamlet the census and the chapter agree without friction, since sickness leads its classes in spirit and the chapter's disease reading can lean on the arithmetic; for Romeo and Juliet the chapter must read across the classes, gathering its light from five drawers, and the finding is the stronger for needing the reader's act. A method that contains both cases, one where the tally crowns the pattern and one where the pattern crowns the tally, is a method that understands its own instrument. The much-quoted sentence that opens the Hamlet discussion, with its casual naturally, gives the game away: by the time her reader reaches it, the atmosphere of a play has become a thing that can be natural to state, because the census has taught the profession how such statements are earned.

The luck cuts the other way as well, and fairness requires the observation. A method proven on its perfect subject is a method whose general warrant remains unproven, and the profession's error, in the cluster-hunting decades, was to promote a local triumph into a universal licence. Spurgeon's own book contains the corrective in its structure: the tragedies chapter gives each play its own motive, found not deduced, and the histories and comedies are allowed their thinner undertones without inflation. The discipline she modelled, and her imitators discarded, was to let the census report what a given play's stock actually holds, a sickness count of twenty where the sickness is the air and of eight where it is only the machinery, and to write the chapter the proportions permit. This play permitted a great chapter. The greatness is hers; the permission was the play's; and the finding's ninety-year survival is what happens when a strong method meets the subject it was, unknowingly, designed for.

## The Verdict: What Stands, What Falls, and Why She Still Matters

The verdict can now be stated in its parts, as the evidence has arranged them. The light finding stands, and stands at the highest level of confidence criticism affords: stated twice in her book, exhibited instance by instance in her tragedies chapter, consistent with her own appendix once the filing system is understood, confirmed by the play's whole subsequent critical history, and robust to the change of base text that the editorial objection raises. Any future account of this play's language that ignores the dominance of light against darkness will be wrong in a way that can be demonstrated from the text, and the demonstration will follow her route. The finding also survives the fairest test a pattern claim can face, the challenge of the rival sensation. Propose haste as the play's governing feeling, and the proposal is strong: five days, a friar's warning, a lover's rash tongue, a letter too slow and a potion too fast. Yet haste in this play is consistently pictured as light: as lightning that ceases to be before a witness can name it, as thoughts gliding ten times faster than the sun's beams, as fire and powder kissing into extinction, as a flash ignited and quenched. The rival sensation turns out to be the dominant image's tempo, a property of the light rather than a competitor with it. Propose gold, or names, or the garden, and the distributions fail sooner, the instances thinning into single scenes where light's instances thicken across all five acts. A pattern that absorbs its strongest rival has earned its title in the only court that matters, the text read whole.

The method stands in a demoted but honourable rank: the census is the indispensable prospecting instrument of imagery study, the operation that converts sensation into checkable distribution, and it is not, and in her first appendix she said it was not, a proof machine. Clemen's correction stands beside it as the necessary second operation: patterns must be read in their development, in voice and moment and sequence, before they testify. The biography falls, in its confident form: the man reconstructed from the tallies, the gardener, the countryman, the boy at the bridge, survives as a gallery of plausible suggestions whose epistemic status her own concessions forbid us to upgrade. The sequels never came, the trilogy stands at one volume, and the archive she left has become, in Geneva's hands, an object of study in itself, which is perhaps the fate she would have chosen for it: evidence, gathered to be checked.

Measured against the play she served best, the balance is not close. Romeo and Juliet is the tragedy in which her instrument and her subject were made for each other: a young man's play, written at speed, whose meaning genuinely travels in its atmosphere, whose atmosphere genuinely is a pattern, and whose pattern she was the first to gather whole. The cliché quotes single images, a sun here, a torch there, and calls the quotation criticism. Spurgeon restored the system the single images belong to, and every study of this play's imagery since, the cluster analysis, the light catalogue, the conceit anatomies that fill this series, works downstream of that restoration. She miscounted nothing that matters, she overclaimed one thing that does, and she left the most useful sentence any of her critics could have asked for printed in her own first appendix, where the proportions are sound and the certainty is not. That is what a landmark looks like after ninety years of audit: not a monument, a method, still earning its keep.

For the reader who comes to her fresh, the order of reading matters, and the audit this article has performed suggests one. Begin with the tragedies chapter's pages on this play, and let the light gather as she gathers it, torches to stars to the lit vault. Turn then to Appendix IV and watch the same evidence refuse to sit still in its drawers, nature at thirty-nine and the pattern dispersed through five classes. Finish with Appendix I, and hear the founder price her own instrument with a candour her school forgot. Read in that order, the book teaches its own correct use: exhibition first, distribution second, humility throughout. No other single volume in the play's critical history contains its thesis, its evidence and its refutation in such compact and honest adjacency, and none has altered so permanently what a reader is able to see in the most familiar love tragedy in the language.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Spurgeon summary, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch Spurgeon summary is a ten-row digest of Caroline Spurgeon's method and of her finding for this play, drawn from her 1935 book and its appendices. It exposes a productive tension at the heart of her work. Her headline declares light the dominating image of Romeo and Juliet, yet her own census files nature as the largest class, personification second, and fire and light only fourth, because the light pattern runs across her filing classes rather than inside one of them. The summary preserves both halves of that record, the verdict and the arithmetic, so that readers meet the finding as Spurgeon actually left it, a pattern recognised by reading and certified, not created, by counting.

### Q: How many images did Spurgeon count in Romeo and Juliet, and across how many lines?

Spurgeon's Appendix II gives the play 204 images in a text of 2,989 lines. The figure comes from her complete hand census of the canon, in which every simile, metaphor, personification and word-picture in the Larger Temple Shakespeare was entered on a card and classified by subject. For scale, her table gives Hamlet 279 images in 3,762 lines and Macbeth 208 in 2,084, so this play's weave is comparatively spare. She warns in her first appendix that any such count is approximate and that no two counters would agree exactly; the safeguard of her figures is that one person applied one method throughout, so the proportions between plays and between classes can be trusted even where a single total might be challenged.

### Q: Why does her census give this play only eight sickness and medicine images when plague and poison decide its plot?

Because the census counts images, not plot devices, and in this play disease works as machinery rather than as atmosphere. The plague enters as a sealed door in Friar John's errand and the poison as a purchased vial; both are events, briefly pictured, not a climate the language keeps breathing. Her class of sickness and medicine therefore holds only eight of the 204 images, among them love as a madness, infection and poison, and the jest at scars. Hamlet, by her count, holds twenty, because there sickness is the court's very air. The contrast is one of the census's most useful corrections: it separates what a plot uses from what an imagination dwells on.

### Q: What did Spurgeon say Shakespeare took from Arthur Brooke's poem to build the play's light?

She said he took the germ of the whole running image from Brooke's Romeus and Juliet, the last place, in her phrase, a reader would expect to find it. Brooke's poem already describes each lover as being to the other what the sun is to the world, already burns with the feud as a mighty fire, and already hopes the Prince may quench the sparks within the citizens' breasts. Shakespeare's originality, on her account, lies in the transformation rather than the invention: a few conventional similes of little poetic worth became in his hands a continuous and consistent running image of brilliance swiftly quenched. The finding matters because it anchors the play's most famous pattern in a documentable act of reading and remaking.

### Q: How did Spurgeon contrast this play's light with Bacon's light, and what did she claim the contrast proved?

She contrasted them as two different imaginations using one element. Bacon's light, in her second chapter, is intellectual: truth, virtue, knowledge, clear and unbiased judgment, even the essence of God, the Father of illumination. Shakespeare, she states flatly, shows no sign of that interest, and the single great exception is Romeo and Juliet, where a running image conceives love as light in a dark world. The contrast proved, for her, that the two writers' minds furnished themselves differently at the deepest level: Bacon reflects on human nature in terms of light and shade, while Shakespeare thinks most easily and naturally in the terms of a gardener, picturing people as plants and trees. The play's light is therefore evidence about its author's imagination, not a decorative habit.

### Q: What did Spurgeon mean by calling the story a flash of light suddenly ignited and as swiftly quenched?

She meant that the play's form obeys its dominating image. Shakespeare, she argues, saw the story in its swift and tragic beauty as an almost blinding flash, and he compressed the action from the nine months and more of his source into five days, meeting on Sunday, marriage on Monday, parting at dawn on Tuesday, reunion in death on Thursday night. Swiftness, brilliance, danger and destruction are thus accentuated together, and the lovers' own figures confess the physics: the betrothal is too like the lightning, and violent delights die like fire and powder as they kiss. The flash is her name for the identity of pattern and plot, the sensation the iterative imagery produces and the structure the narrative takes.

### Q: Why did the imagery revolution begin in lectures before it became a book?

Because Spurgeon tested the instrument in public for five years before asking the profession to trust its results in print. A Shakespeare Association lecture in 1930 presented the leading motives in the imagery of the tragedies; the British Academy's Annual Shakespeare Lecture in May 1931 introduced iterative imagery; an article in the Review of English Studies in October 1933 set Shakespeare's imagery beside Bacon's. Each occasion let specialists object to a definition or a filing decision while the method was still corrigible. When Shakespeare's Imagery and What It Tells Us appeared in 1935, its central concepts were therefore live hypotheses in circulation rather than novelties sprung from a single volume, and the book's reception had been rehearsed in the rooms where its claims were first heard.

### Q: What does the printing history of Shakespeare's Imagery and What It Tells Us show about its standing?

It shows a methods book with the working life of a classic. Scholars at the University of Geneva who study her legacy record six printings within thirty-five years of publication, and the volume's own imprint page runs on to a nineteenth printing in 2005, seventy years after Cambridge University Press first issued it in 1935. Methods books ordinarily date faster than any other kind, because each generation rebuilds its instruments; a census compiled by hand, on cards, before computers existed, that publishers kept reprinting into the digital century had plainly crossed from being a tool of its moment to being part of the discipline's permanent furniture. The print record is the reception history in its least disputable form.

### Q: What happened to the two further studies Spurgeon planned to follow the 1935 book?

They never appeared, and the 1935 volume remains the sole instalment of an announced trilogy. Her preface is explicit that the book is the first only of three planned studies, the later volumes to treat authorship in the light of the image evidence and the background and origins of Shakespeare's imagery. Health, the move to Tucson in 1936, and her death in 1942 closed the programme; Geneva's scholars note that she drafted follow-up studies which stayed unpublished and almost unremarked. The consequence for readers is structural: the parts of her system that the sequels would have defended at length, above all the authorship inferences, stand in the single volume with their boldest claims unguarded by the fuller argument their author intended.

### Q: Where did Wolfgang Clemen place this play in Shakespeare's development as an image-maker?

Clemen placed it early, among the works in which imagery still operates largely as iterative atmosphere and accompaniment rather than as fully integrated dramatic symbolism. His The Development of Shakespeare's Imagery, published in English in 1951 and reissued in 1977, reads the canon as a growth: images begin by recurring around a theme, as Spurgeon had shown, and end, in the great tragedies, fused with action, character and structure. Romeo and Juliet receives a chapter of its own in that history, and its light pattern is, in his terms, a magnificent instance of the early manner rather than an exception to it. The placement corrects Spurgeon's statics without displacing her finding, since an iterative pattern is still the pattern she said it was.

### Q: How do modern editions of the play carry Spurgeon's light finding to new readers?

Editions carry it in their framing before a single line is read. The standard modern introductions, Brian Gibbons's Arden edition of 1980 among them, present light against darkness as the play's recognised poetic weather, and their notes then gloss the pattern's instances scene by scene, torch, sun, star, lightning, lantern, so that a first-time reader meets the pattern as part of the play's given description. That editorial ratification is itself evidence in the finding's history: a hypothesis printed in 1935 had become, within two generations, the furniture an editor supplies by default. The dependence runs both ways, since the instances an editor prints, from a chosen early text, are the same raw material from which any census of the pattern must be built.

### Q: Why did Spurgeon's light finding outlive her claims about Shakespeare the man?

Because the two claims rest on different strengths of evidence. The light finding rests on exhibited instances, hundreds of lines any reader can check in an armchair, and on a distribution her appendix documents; it survives re-counting, re-editing and changes of critical fashion. The claims about the man, that the imagery betrays a gardener's mind, a countryman's boyhood, a watcher of eddies under Clopton Bridge, rest on proportions asked to testify about an inner life, and proportions are artefacts of filing decisions she herself conceded were judgment calls. When the profession turned against biographical inference in the middle of the century, the weaker structure fell and the stronger stood, and the book's reputation sorted itself along exactly the seam its first appendix had drawn.

### Q: What is the University of Geneva project on Spurgeon setting out to recover?

The project, titled Caroline Spurgeon in the 21st Century, sets out to recover, update, digitise and recontextualise her pioneering work across both her fields, the Chaucer allusion record and the Shakespeare imagery census, including the unpublished companion studies almost no scholarship had remarked. Its third aim is the one that reframes her for the discipline: to situate her methods inside an untold history of computational literary studies. A scholar who charted and classified thousands of images by hand, before computers, in order to measure a playwright's distinctiveness against his contemporaries was performing the founding operations of corpus analysis with index cards. The project treats her archive accordingly, as data to be preserved and re-run rather than as a monument to be admired.

### Q: Which five plays supply the Shakespeare side of Spurgeon's comparison of daily-life images with five contemporary dramatists?

The five are Romeo and Juliet, Macbeth, As You Like It, A Winter's Tale and Richard III, named in her own notes on Chart IV in Appendix V. That chart isolates the small group of images she files under daily life, sport and games, classes and types of people, trades and crafts, war and weapons, substances, money, travel, buildings, royalty and town life, and sets the proportions in those five plays against the work of five contemporary dramatists drawn from the shelf her census had processed. The choice shows the method's comparative engine at its clearest: a single category of comparison, a fixed sample on each side, and proportions allowed to argue. That this play anchors the sample says much about the evidential weight she gave its ordinary world.

### Q: What is the second-largest group in Spurgeon's census of this play, and why does it matter to the light finding?

The second-largest group is personification, with 23 of the 204 images, and Spurgeon's own note on the class supplies the reason it matters: many of them are of light and darkness. Here the sun peers from the window of the east, the grey-eyed morn smiles on the frowning night, darkness reels like a drunkard, night stands as a sober-suited matron, jocund day stands tiptoe on the misty mountain tops, and death keeps house as a lover. These are not stray ornaments; they are the light pattern wearing grammatical personhood, proof that the pattern governs not only what the play pictures but how its abstractions behave. A census class her headline never names turns out to be one of the headline's strongest witnesses.

### Q: Why did it matter to the book's reception that its author held a London professorial chair?

Because the census asked the profession to accept a new kind of evidence, and new evidence travels on the credit of its presenter. Spurgeon had held the chair of English literature at Bedford College since 1913, the first woman to hold a professorial chair in London, had compiled the standard record of Chaucer allusion, and had sat on the Newbolt Committee that reshaped English teaching. A card catalogue of metaphors from an unknown amateur could have been dismissed as a hobby; from the Bedford professor it arrived as an institutional act, a claim that the discipline's own methods were incomplete. Her standing did not make the findings true, but it made them answerable, reviewed, argued with and absorbed rather than politely ignored, which is the fate that decides whether a method becomes a revolution.

### Q: What part did American friends play in making the 1935 book possible?

A decisive part, recorded in the book's own front matter. The volume is dedicated to the memory of Lucretia Perry Osborn and to Spurgeon's other kind friends in America, whose practical and generous help first made it possible for her to begin writing it. Her acknowledgments specify that a group of American friends, through the agency of the late Mrs Henry Fairfield Osborn, gave substantial financial aid when she had little more than a preliminary draft and one or two studies; the gift bought a year's leave from Bedford College and the secretarial help the counting required. Dean Virginia Gildersleeve of Barnard College assisted from the book's inception to the final proof sheets. The imagery revolution, an English professor's project, was financed across the Atlantic.

### Q: What did Spurgeon find in Capulet's speeches that carried the light pattern beyond the lovers?

She found the pattern in the mouth of the play's least poetical speaker, and treated the discovery as proof of the pattern's dominion. Promising Paris the ball, Capulet undertakes that his guest shall see earth-treading stars that make dark heaven light, the dancing girls of his household already recruited into the celestial register. Finding Juliet weeping, as he supposes, for Tybalt, he clothes his comment in the same weather: when the sun sets the air drizzles dew, but for the sunset of his brother's son it rains downright. A pattern that a bluff old man reaches for, unthinking, at his two most practical moments, a party and a funeral arrangement, is not the lovers' private language. It is the idiom of the whole city, which is what her word dominating was chosen to claim.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Spurgeon summary to argue that counting changed what criticism could claim about this play?

An essay should build the argument on the summary's two registers, the finding and the filing. Start from the exhibited pattern, light against darkness, and show that before 1935 the pattern could be praised but not demonstrated, while after the census it could be cited as a distribution with classes, totals and proportions. Then use the summary's own tension, nature largest at 39 while fire and light files only 12, to state the change precisely: counting did not replace reading, it gave reading a body of evidence to answer to, caveats included, since Spurgeon's appendix concedes the counts are approximate. The strongest form of the claim is also the most accurate one, that criticism after Spurgeon argues about patterns in a way that can be checked.

### Q: What is the strongest surviving objection to treating light as this play's dominating image?

The strongest surviving objection is that dominance is a construction across categories, not a result any tally delivers. Filed strictly, light's own class holds twelve images, nature holds thirty-nine, and a counter with different drawers would distribute the pattern differently, so the word dominating names an act of recognition performed on the census rather than a figure inside it. The objection is fatal to the slogan form of the finding and harmless to the finding itself, because the tragedies chapter never asked the tally to do the recognising: it exhibited the pattern line by line through the whole play, and the exhibition remains checkable by any reader. What does not survive the objection is the claim that the numbers, alone, prove the pattern.
