## Cold Open

The room is not being vacated, and Sema Koll packs it anyway.

She packs it in the morning, before the minister arrives, because the order said the work should be done while the corridor is quiet and because she has learned, in three years of keeping this office, that a task done early makes less noise than a task done under watching eyes. The crates stand in a row along the wall beneath the window, five of them, open, their lids leaned against the skirting like doors taken off hinges. They are good crates, the kind the delegation stores use for archive moves, with rope handles and stencilled sides, and nothing stencilled on them says where they are going. That is the first thing she hates. A crate without a destination is not a crate. It is a suggestion.

She begins with the outer desk, her own desk, because it is the one she can bear to empty first. Into the first crate go the day files, stacked in their order, tied with the blue cord the registry insists on, each bundle squared at the corners the way she was taught when she was new and frightened of doing it wrong. The files are not dead files. Two of them were open yesterday. One of them has a note in her own hand on its cover, in pencil, reminding herself to ask the registry about a missing return. She ties the cord anyway. She lays the bundle flat. She tells herself that tying is not finishing, that a knot can be undone, that paper taken out of a drawer is still paper and can be put back into a drawer by whoever is told to put it back. The thought does not help. Her hands know what packing means even if her mouth is careful not to say it.

The seals go into the second crate, nested in their cloth bags, the heavy brass of the ministerial seal settling with a soft knock against the lighter seal of the delegation, and she wraps each bag in a sheet of packing paper so the metal will not mark the wood of the crate or be marked by it. Nobody has told her to take this care. Nobody has told her not to. The order, when it came, was short and clean and signed by a hand she recognised, and it said only that the outer office was to be packed and ready, and that the work was to be complete before the end of the week. Ready for what, the order did not say. She asked once, standing in the doorway of the inner office with the order still in her hand, and the answer she received was a look and a sentence about tidiness, about delegations that travel light, about the virtue of an office that could move at an hour of notice. She has served the minister long enough to know when a sentence is a door being closed in her face. She did not ask again.

She is angry, and the anger is not a flare. It has settled in her the way weather settles over the Barrow road in the wet months, low and continuous and impossible to argue with. She is angry at the order, which pretends to be about tidiness. She is angry at the hand that signed it, which is not the minister's hand. She is angry at herself for the steadiness of her own hands, for the squared corners and the tied cord, for being good at this. Most of all she is angry at what the packing does in the minds of everyone who passes the open door. The clerks from the registry pass and slow down. The messenger from the treasury passes and does not slow down, which is worse. A junior from the Valtessa desk comes to borrow the hole punch, sees the crates, and leaves without borrowing it, and by the middle of the morning she can feel the story moving through the building ahead of any fact, the way a draught moves ahead of a closing door. The Teshkar office is packing. The Teshkar office is going. Nobody has voted. Nobody has decided. But a room half boxed is a room half gone, and everyone who sees it understands it that way, and she understands that they understand, and the understanding is the point of the work, and she is the one doing the work.

The minister's working papers go into the third crate, and these she packs more slowly, because they are the papers he actually uses, not the papers the office keeps for show. There is the bound return with the worn corner, which he opens at the same page every sitting. There is the folder of draft answers, half of them in her handwriting where she has taken his dictation and half in his, which is worse than hers and which he defends with a joke she has heard so often she can hear it before he says it. There is the small tin of clips. There is the cup he drinks from, washed, dried, wrapped in paper like something fragile, though it is made of thick stoneware and has survived three years of his desk and would survive the crate and the road and the crate again. She puts the cup in last and stands looking at it, nested among the papers, and the sight of it does something to her chest that she refuses to name. A man's cup in a crate is a man gone from his desk. She has not been ordered to pack the cup. She packs it because the order said the office, and the cup is in the office, and because leaving it alone on the emptied desk would look worse, would look like a thing forgotten by people in a hurry, and whatever else this packing is, she will not let it look hurried. If it is theatre, and some cold part of her mind has been saying since the first crate that it is theatre, then it will at least be theatre done properly, by someone who knows where everything goes.

Nothing is being thrown away. She wants that understood, though there is no one in the room to understand it. The waste basket stands empty because she emptied it herself the evening before, in the ordinary way, and nothing since has gone into it. The broken nibs, the spoiled sheets, the drafts that failed, all the ordinary rubbish of a working office, none of it is in the crates. The crates hold only what the office would need if the office were somewhere else, which is another way of saying they hold the office entire, minus the furniture, minus the walls. Nothing is labelled for a destination. She has labels. The labels are in the drawer of the desk she has already emptied, in a paper packet, blank, and she has taken them out twice and put them back twice, because to write a destination she would have to know one, and to leave the labels blank on the lids would be to write the truth too plainly, which is that these boxes are going nowhere that anyone will name. So the lids stay unlabelled, leaned against the skirting, waiting.

The furniture stays, and she is grateful for the furniture in a way that embarrasses her. The desks stay. The chairs stay, hers and the two for visitors, with the wicker seat that complains under weight. The tall cabinet stays, emptied, its doors open, its shelves showing the pale rectangles where bound volumes stood in rows and kept the dust off in straight lines. The minister's door stays shut, because the inner office is not in the order, or not yet in the order, and she has read the order four times looking for the inner office in it and has not found it, and she holds to that absence the way a person crossing ice holds to the tested part. Beyond that door he will sit when he comes, at a desk that still has its papers, and he will call for a file, and she will have to say that the file is in a crate, and he will look at her, and she will look at the crate, and the whole pretence will be there in the room between them, packed and tied and squared at the corners.

She thinks about who will unpack it. That is the thought that returns, crate after crate, bundle after bundle. Someone will unpack this room. Either the vote goes one way and the boxes are carried out by porters she will never see, to a building she has never entered, in a Teshkar that has walked out of the Accord and will keep its own counsel behind its own doors, or the vote goes the other way and the boxes stay here, and some morning not unlike this one a person will cut the blue cord and lift the files out and put them back in their drawers, bundle by bundle, in their order. She hopes it will be her. She is afraid it will be her, because unpacking would mean looking at every object twice, once going in and once coming out, and understanding both times what she was being made to do. She is afraid it will be a stranger, some clerk from a pool, who will put the seals in the wrong drawer and the cup on the wrong shelf and feel nothing at all, because the room means nothing to the stranger, and a room that means nothing can be packed and unpacked like a stage set, struck and raised and struck again.

By the middle of the morning the fourth crate is full and she is tying the fifth bundle when the anger changes its shape, the way it has changed every morning this week, from anger at the order to anger at the reason underneath it, which nobody has spoken in her hearing and which she has assembled herself from the order's timing and the order's signature and the way certain visitors have stopped coming to the outer office and started sending notes instead. The packing was ordered before anyone in this office spoke of leaving. She knows the sequence because keeping the sequence is her work. Letters come to her desk. Orders pass across her desk. Dates live in her head the way other people hold the names of their cousins. The order to pack came across her desk on a morning when the word departure had not yet been said aloud in this room by anyone with the standing to say it, and she remembers the morning precisely because she read the order twice and thought it must belong to some other office, some reorganisation, some move between floors. It did not. It belonged here. Someone wanted this room ready to vanish before vanishing had been proposed, and wanted it done by her hands, so that when the proposal came the room would already look like the answer.

She finishes the bundle. She squares its corners. She lays it flat, and she does not stop working, because stopping would mean standing in the middle of a half packed room with her hands empty, and empty hands would have to do something, and the only thing left for them to do is shake. So she works. She folds the packing paper along its old creases. She counts the crates again, five, though she knows the number, though counting is only a way of passing her eyes along the wall and seeing, each time, what the room has become. A room with its life in boxes. A room rehearsing an ending that five days of talk have not yet agreed to. She hates every minute of it, and she does every minute of it well, and both of those things are true at once, and she suspects, tying the last knot of the morning, that whoever arranged this work counted on exactly that.

## Tasking

Leyden sent for her in the middle of the afternoon, and he did it himself, which told Mira most of what the hour would hold before she reached his door. A tasking that came through the registry came with paper. A tasking that came by Leyden's own note, in his own hand, carried on the internal tray and marked with nothing but her callsign, came with something that would not be written down, and she had learned to eat before such meetings when she could and to sleep after them when she could, and on this occasion she had done neither, because the note reached her desk as she was finishing the returns from the crossing and there had been no interval worth the name. She went up the half flight and along the corridor with the notebook in her coat pocket where it always was, and she felt its small weight against her side with every step, the way she had felt it for months now, as a fact of her dress, like the keys, like the pencil she kept though the Office issued pens.

He had the motion on his desk. That was the first thing she saw, because Leyden arranged his desk the way other men arranged a board, and whatever lay squared at its centre was the matter. The copy was the Office's copy of the Teshkar departure motion, bound in the grey boards the registry used for assembly papers, and beside it, unbound, lay a single covering note and a watcher's report on a sheet still warm from the copying frame. He did not ask her to sit. She sat anyway, in the chair that faced him, and he turned the bound copy toward her with two fingers and let her read the head of it while he watched the window, which was his way of giving a person privacy with a document.

The motion was plainly drawn. It proposed that Teshkar withdraw from the Meridian Accord by the stated process, that the withdrawal be debated in Teshkar's own assembly, and that the vote be taken at the close of the present Session. The drafting date stood at the head of the second page, in the clerk's hand, in the form the assembly used, a day within the Twenty Ninth Session, counted from the Session's opening, and Leyden put his finger beside it without touching the ink and said that the date was the first thing she was to hold, because everything else in the case would try to make her forget it. The motion had been drafted on that day. Not tabled, not spoken, not published. Drafted. Written, by a hand in Teshkar, on that day, before the rest of the sequence existed. He let the page lie. He said the vote was five days away, that five days was the whole of her allowance, and that the Office had not been asked to stop the vote or to win it, but to find the pressure that kept Teshkar in the Accord, if such a pressure existed, and to find it in a form that could be used by people who did not read weight and never would.

Then he told her about the file, and he did it before he told her anything else about the case, and the order of the telling was itself a piece of the telling.

Two floors above the room they sat in, in Commissioner Harrand's office, there was a file open on a desk, and the file had Mira's name on it, and the file was not about a case. It was about the register. [the private register of four empty rooms she agreed to stop keeping and did not](/2026/11/10/nightjar-private-register-010/) was the shape of it, he said, though Harrand's clerks would have given it a longer and drier title, and the notebook in Mira's coat pocket, the private register of absences she had kept against instruction and against her own word given to Leyden's face in this same room, was its subject. He had not been shown the file. He had been told of it, by a person who had seen it open, and the telling had cost him something, and he named the cost plainly and without decoration, because he had decided, he said, that she would hear of the file from him rather than find it for herself on some worse afternoon, and that the price of her hearing it from him was that she should know it had a price. A favour was gone. A credit with oversight, built over years and held against a need he had expected to be greater than this one, had been spent entire on a single sight of an open file and a single permission to carry the knowledge down two floors. He did not itemise it further. He did not name the person. He said that the file existed, that it was open rather than closed and shelved, which in oversight's practice meant that it was being added to, and that its earliest page was older than she would expect, and then he stopped, and the stopping told her that the age of that earliest page was a thing she would have to learn in the case and not at this desk.

She sat with that for a moment, and he let her sit, and the room held the ordinary sounds of the building going about its afternoon, a cart in the corridor, a door, the registry bell far below. She thought about the reprimand first, because the reprimand was the smaller thing and her mind went to it the way a tongue goes to a sore tooth. Harrand had written it before Mira crossed into the Scour, had held it ready through the days of the crossing, and had handed it to her on her return with the air of a woman returning a borrowed tool. It had stung then and it stung in the remembering, not for its language, which had been exact, but for its timing, which had been exact as well, and which had said that oversight's judgment of her did not wait upon her results. Under the reprimand, deeper and duller, lay the other thing she carried into the tasking, the ten years of Teodor Bask's carefulness that she had taken on at the foundry list and had not yet put down, ten years of one man's patient, unglamorous, day by day care, the dullest thing she had ever carried and the hardest to set aside, because it did not flare or fade but simply accompanied her, a second set of habits laid over her own, making her check twice and fold once and distrust any conclusion that arrived too quickly. Leyden, who knew what she carried the way he knew the state of every officer he ran, glanced at her hands where they lay still on her knees and said that she was entering the case full, rested by her standards, which were not the standards he would have chosen for her but were the ones she kept. The Bask years were a cost she was already paying and would go on paying. The reprimand was a cost paid and receipted. Neither excused anything. Both were noted, in his ledger manner, and set aside.

The covering note named the aide. Sema Koll, aide to the Teshkar minister, the keeper of his outer office, the person through whose hands the office's paper passed and in whose head its sequence lived. Leyden slid the note across and Mira read the name and the two lines beneath it, which said only that the aide was cooperative by position rather than by inclination, that she had not sought contact, and that any approach to the minister's office would in practice be an approach to her desk first. The watcher's report said the rest of what the tasking needed. The minister's outer office was being packed. Crates stood along the wall. Files were being tied and seals wrapped and the work was being done by the aide herself, in the mornings, ahead of any decision the assembly had taken or the minister had announced. The watcher, who was a delegation clerk with a legitimate errand and no reason to linger, had seen five crates and an emptied cabinet and a room that looked, in the clerk's phrase, which Leyden read aloud without emphasis, already half gone.

Mira turned the bound motion back to its drafting page and looked at the date again, and then at the watcher's sheet, and then at the covering note with the aide's name on it, and she held the three papers in her mind in the order Leyden had given them to her, which was the order he intended. A motion drafted on a day that mattered, for a reason she did not yet know. An aide packing a room that was not being vacated. A file open two floors up, older than it should be, kept by a commissioner who believed the Ninth Office should not exist and who was, on the evidence Mira had met, sometimes right in ways that were uncomfortable to stand near. Leyden gathered the papers into their order and squared them, and he told her the shape of her allowance once more, because he believed in saying a task twice and writing it never. Five days. The delegation would visit openly, as delegations did, and she would go with it in her ordinary capacity. The minister's office was a place she might be shown. Teshkar's assembly returns were open to the Office and she was to work the motion as paper before she worked anything as weight. And if she found the pressure, she was to bring it to him in words a minister could use, not in sensations a Weigher could feel.

She asked one question, which was whether Harrand knew that Mira knew. Leyden said that Harrand had not been told, that the spent favour bought silence as well as sight, and that what Mira did with the knowledge was hers, as the register had been hers, and the keeping of it, and the lie she had told him about stopping, which he named once, without heat, because it belonged in the room and because a tasking that left it unnamed would have been a tasking built on a politeness neither of them practised. She had agreed to his face to stop keeping the register. She had not stopped. The notebook was in her coat pocket as they spoke, and both of them knew it, and the file two floors up existed because someone in oversight had known it longer than she had known they knew. She stood. The motion stayed on his desk. The date stayed in her head, counted in Session days, fixed at the head of the sequence like the first stone of an arch, and everything else she would build in the five days would have to stand against it.


## Ground Truth

She worked the motion as paper first, because Leyden had ordered it in that sequence and because paper, unlike weight, could be shown to another person and made to hold still while they looked at it. The Office copy of the departure motion lay in the registry reading room under the lamp she preferred, the one whose shade had been mended with wire, and she went through it the way she had been taught to go through any instrument that proposed to change the shape of the Accord, which was slowly, from the head, with a pencil resting unused across her fingers and her notebook, the other notebook, the clean one the Office issued and oversight was welcome to read, open at her elbow for the sequence. The motion proposed withdrawal by the stated process. Its language was neither wild nor careless. Whoever had drafted it knew the Accord's terms the way a clerk knows a staircase in the dark, by use, and the clauses followed one another in the order the terms required, notice, debate, vote, and then, if the vote carried, the setting of terms for the departure itself, each step given its proper interval, each interval counted in Session days. Nothing in the drafting reached for effect. There was no rhetoric in it at all, and Mira, who had read denunciations drafted as motions and ultimatums drafted as enquiries, marked the plainness of it as the first true fact of the case. This was a document made by someone who expected it to be used.

The tabling record came next, in the assembly returns, which the Office received by right and which arrived bound by the week with the returns of every member state in the same grey boards. Teshkar's returns were neither fuller nor thinner than the others. The motion appeared in them on its tabling day, entered by the assembly clerk in the ordinary list, between a water compact amendment and a question on road tolls along the Barrow border, and the ordinariness of its company did it the same service the plainness of its language had done. A thing tabled between tolls and water is a thing the machinery has accepted as business. She copied the tabling date into her clean notebook beneath the drafting date and looked at the interval between them, a week and a little more, drafting to tabling, which was neither haste nor delay but the ordinary pace of a paper being carried through hands that each had to read it. Then she went looking for the rest of the sequence, because a motion alone is only an intention, and intentions in assembly politics are as common as rain in the wet months and as lightly held. She needed the dates that surrounded it, the dates of the world the motion claimed to answer.

The treasury date took her the longest, not because it was hidden but because it was not the Office's paper and had to be assembled from the edges of other people's. Teshkar's deficit was real. She established that early and she established it firmly, because a case built on a doubted fact collapses the first time an opponent leans on it. The returns showed it, the treasury's own published abstract admitted it in the abstract's guarded language, and a delegation clerk who had served a season in Teshkar's finance ministry confirmed the shape of it over a working lunch she arranged for exactly that purpose, confirming nothing she could not already read but confirming it in the tone of a man describing weather he had stood in. The deficit existed. What she needed was the day it had become known to Teshkar's own treasury, not the day it had become true, since deficits become true gradually, in the manner of a river silting, and not the day it had become public, since treasuries sit on knowledge the way hens sit, with intent. The day of knowledge had a paper trail of its own, a minute of a treasury sitting, circulated to a named list, and the abstract's drafting history, and a request for revised figures that only made sense as the act of men who had just seen the unrevised ones. She triangulated it the way she had been taught, three sources, none of them sufficient, all of them pointing at the same day within the Session, and she wrote that day into her notebook beneath the other two and sat back and looked at the column of three dates with the feeling, familiar and unwelcome, of a pattern declining to be the pattern she had expected.

The threat's public date was the easiest, because public things keep their own records. The minister's statement, the assembly's notice, the delegation's circular to the other member states, all of them carried the same day, the day the threat to leave the Accord had been made in the open, in words that could be quoted back. And the vote's date was fixed by the assembly's own calendar, five days from the morning of the tasking, at the close of the Session's business on that day, unmoved and, as far as the returns showed, unmoveable except by the assembly itself. Five dates. She had all five by the end of her second day, and she wrote them out fair, in her clean notebook, in the order of their happening, and then she copied them onto a single sheet in the form the Office used for such sequences, because Leyden would want the artifact itself and not her account of it, and because she had begun to understand that the sheet, once made, would be the thing the whole case turned on, for her and for everyone she would eventually have to persuade.

The table settled the order of the case and nothing else, and she was careful, in the note she wrote beneath her own copy of it, to claim no more for it than that. It settled that drafting came first, well first, before tabling, before knowledge, before publicity, before the vote. It settled the intervals, a week from drafting to tabling, six days from tabling to the treasury's knowledge, three days from knowledge to the public threat, five days from the threat's publicity to the vote that would decide it, the last interval being the one she was living inside as she wrote. It settled that the sequence had a shape, that the shape could be pointed at, and that anyone who wished to argue about the case would now have to argue about these five days and not about impressions. What it did not settle, on its face, was why. A table of dates is a fence, not a house. It marks the ground. It does not say who built on the ground or what they intended by building, and she wrote that caution into her note as well, because she had seen officers fall in love with an artifact and begin to hear it say things it had never said, and because the drafting date, sitting plainly at the head of her column, was already beginning to exert the quiet pull that first things exert, and she did not yet trust the pull. She trusted the date. The pull she would test.

The delegation visited openly on the third day, as delegations did, with its errand declared and its members named and its route through the Teshkar mission's rooms agreed in advance down to the order of the doors. Mira went with it in her ordinary capacity, which was to say in no declared capacity at all, listed as an Office observer, dressed as she always dressed, with her issued notebook in one pocket and her private register in the other, against instruction and against her word, carried the way it was always carried, a clause of fact she noted to herself on the stairs because the case had made the register its second subject and she refused to pretend otherwise even in the privacy of her own head. The delegation was received by the minister's staff in the outer rooms, was offered the courtesies, was shown the returns it had asked to see and two it had not, and was conducted, in the latter part of the visit, through the minister's outer office, on the stated ground that the delegation wished to pay its respects to the office itself, which was the kind of graceful fiction the Accord's diplomacy ran on and which nobody in the corridor believed and everybody in the corridor honoured.

She looked at the outer office as a place she would later read, and she looked at it first with her ordinary eyes, deliberately, thoroughly, the way a surveyor walks a field before any instrument comes out of its case. She named its anchors to herself as she passed, fixing them in order so that the later reading would have its stations. The aide's desk stood first, broad and ordered even in its emptied state, its drawers shut, its surface bare except for a blotter and a folded sheet of packing paper, positioned so that whoever sat at it commanded both the corridor door and the minister's door and could rise to meet either. The packing crates stood second, along the wall beneath the window, five of them, lidded now, the lids unlabelled, the rope handles tucked, presenting to the room the finished and patient aspect of luggage in a hall. And the minister's door stood third, shut, of dark wood, with a brass plate worn brighter at its centre where hands had steadied themselves against it over years of entries, a door that had been opened and closed by the same few people in the same few ways for long enough that its brass had learned them. Desk, crates, door. She fixed the three, and she fixed the distances between them, and she noted the tall cabinet standing emptied with its doors closed now, and the pale rectangles she could not see but knew were on its shelves, and the chairs, and the window with its view of a courtyard where a porter was sweeping with the unhurried competence of a man who has swept the same stones through three administrations. She did not read the room. That was not the visit's business and she would not have done it under the delegation's flag even if it had been, because a read taken under false courtesies would have poisoned whatever it found. She looked, and she memorised, and she let the delegation's talk flow past her, the minister's deputy speaking of cooperation and continuity in the practised phrases, and she watched Sema Koll instead.

The aide stood by her own emptied desk and answered the delegation's small questions with a correctness that cost her visibly. She was younger than Mira had pictured from the covering note, or perhaps tiredness was doing the picturing, and she kept one hand flat on the desk's bare surface as she spoke, as if steadying the desk or herself. When the deputy, in an excess of the graceful fiction, remarked that the office looked ready for anything, the aide's hand pressed down once, hard, and her answer, that the office endeavoured to be tidy, came out level and exact and utterly without conviction, and Mira understood that she was looking at a woman performing a part in a play she had not written, in a room she had packed with her own hands, for an audience of visitors who were meant to carry the sight of the crates out of the building with them. Which they were doing. She could see the crates registering, delegate by delegate, in the small pauses and the lengthened glances, and she thought that whoever had ordered the packing understood audiences the way the motion's drafter understood clauses. The visit ended with the courtesies reversed. The corridor received them again. Sema Koll closed the corridor door behind the delegation with both hands, gently, like someone closing the door of a room in which something is sleeping, and Mira, last in the file, saw the gentleness and filed it with the pressed hand and the level voice, as facts about the aide, not yet as facts about the case.


Back in her own rooms, with the delegation's report filed and the visit's courtesies discharged, she set out the case as three live possibilities, because Leyden's method, which had become her method, required that every reading she might favour be made to stand in line beside the readings she did not, and be stated as fairly as their own advocates would state them. She wrote them in the clean notebook, numbered, in her plainest hand.

The first possibility was money. Teshkar's deficit was real, its treasury knew it, its assembly feared it, and a member state in want of money holds one great asset in an accord of this kind, which is its own continued membership, and that asset can be priced. On this reading the threat to leave was a price being named by other means. The motion was leverage. The vote was a deadline in a negotiation. The expected end was not departure but a settlement, money moved, terms eased, the deficit answered by the Accord's collective purse in exchange for the threat's withdrawal, and everyone retreating from the brink with the satisfied exhaustion of people who had never intended to step over it. The reading had the great virtue of fitting the public timing. The threat had been made public three days after the treasury's knowledge, which was exactly the interval of a government discovering a hole in its accounts and reaching for its loudest instrument. It fit the deficit. It fit the publicity. It was the reading the other delegations were already making in their corridors, and it was the reading Teshkar's own deputy had done nothing, during the visit, to discourage.

The second possibility was the inspections. Under the Accord's terms a member state submitted to inspection, of its stores, its bindings, its practice, by officers answerable to the Accord rather than to the inspected state, and Teshkar had chafed under that submission longer and louder than any member except Dravin, which chafed as a matter of national character. On this reading the threat was a shield. The deficit was the occasion rather than the cause, a respectable public reason held up in front of a private one, and the true object was to bargain the inspections down, to thin them, to delay them, to make them survivable for a government that had things in its stores it would rather describe than show. The reading was plausible and she stated it fairly, as she had been required to do, because it deserved fairness. Teshkar's objections to inspection were on record across three Sessions. Its ministers had argued them in committee with a consistency that suggested genuine grievance rather than convenience. A state might well threaten the largest thing it could threaten in order to protect a smaller thing it cared about more, and the departure motion, on this reading, was a wall built to cast a shadow, and the inspections were what stood in the shadow. She had seen the pattern before, in other rooms and other Sessions, and so had [the one seller protected for nine years by a warning that arrived before its cause](/2026/11/15/nightjar-foundry-list-015/) taught her, in its way, that the loudest grievance in a file is not always the grievance the file is about, and that a warning, a threat, a motion, any of these might be standing in front of the thing that mattered, facing outward, doing the work of a door.

The third possibility was the faction. On this reading Teshkar was not threatening at all, in the sense that mattered. A faction inside Teshkar, placed in its ministries and its assembly and its delegation, wanted the departure for a reason that had nothing to do with money or inspections, and the reason was witnesses. A member state is watched. Its stores are inspected, its returns are read, its officers are known by name in foreign registries, its rooms are visited by delegations who look at its crates and its cabinets with ordinary eyes. A departed state has none of that. A departed state keeps its own counsel behind its own doors, and what is done in its rooms is done unobserved, and for people whose work requires unobserved rooms, departure is not a policy but a condition of survival. On this reading the motion was somebody else's work, drafted by hands that served the faction, carried through the assembly's machinery by members who owed the faction their places, and dressed in the deficit's respectable clothing once the deficit conveniently became known. The minister himself, on this reading, was not the author but the instrument, a man being pushed toward a vote by the arrangement of his own office, his own papers, his own packed and waiting rooms. The reading was the darkest of the three and she distrusted it for exactly that reason, because dark readings flatter the reader, making her feel she has seen further than the corridor has. She wrote it third and she wrote it as carefully as the other two, and she noted against it, honestly, that it rested, as matters stood, on arrangement and atmosphere, on a signature that was not the minister's and a packing order that preceded any public word, and that arrangement and atmosphere were not evidence a minister could use.

She tested each reading against the sheet of dates, because that was what the sheet was for. Money fitted the public dates and stumbled, though she did not yet let herself say it fell, on the first date in the column. The inspections fitted the record of three Sessions and fitted no date in particular, which was its own kind of weakness, a reading that no fact could embarrass being a reading no fact supported. The faction fitted the first date and the packing order and the aide's pressed hand, and fitted nothing that could be carried into an assembly and laid on a table. She copied the table onto a second sheet, for the file, and referred to it again in her working note, the drafting day against the treasury day, the forty first against the fifty fourth, thirteen days of interval in which a motion had existed before its most respectable reason had been known to the people who would supposedly benefit from it, and she wrote beneath the referral, in words she chose with care, that the table settled the order and that the order was now the case's spine, whatever flesh the remaining days put on it. Thirteen days. A motion in search of its pretext, or a motion whose pretext had simply not been shared with the treasury's minute takers, or a coincidence of drafting, a contingency paper prepared against a rainy season and pulled from a drawer when the rain came. Contingency was the money reading's best answer to the drafting date and she wrote that down too, fairly, in the margin, because a government might draft a departure motion the way a household keeps a packed bag by the door in a flood month, against a need it hopes will not arrive. She would have to kill the contingency answer or be killed by it, and she knew it, and knowing it she closed the notebook and went to the window and stood looking at the courtyard of her own building, where no porter swept, and thought about paper, and about how paper remembers sequence perfectly and motive not at all, which was the precise shape of the gap she would have to fill with weight.

Two floors above her, Commissioner Ilse Harrand sat alone in her office in the late part of the afternoon, when the oversight floor emptied earlier than the floors below it, because oversight kept the hours of a court rather than the hours of a workshop. The office was spare in the manner of a person who has decided that furniture is a form of argument and intends to make few arguments. A desk. A chair for the commissioner and one for a visitor, placed at an angle that discouraged settling. Shelves of bound returns, arranged by Session, with the current Session's volume lying flat on the desk's left side, where it lived during working hours. And on the desk, open, squared with the desk's edge in the manner of a thing that is returned to its position after every handling, the file.

The file had Mira Kovac's name on it, and beneath the name, in the registry hand of oversight, a subject line that described the matter without heat and without mercy, as oversight described all its matters. It concerned a private register of absences, kept by a field officer of the Ninth Office, against instruction, over a period that the file's contents were still in the course of establishing. Harrand turned a page. The page beneath was not new. She had read it before and she read it again now with the attention of a woman checking a sum she already believes, because the page's date was the reason the file lay open on this desk in this Session and not in some earlier one, closed and shelved and forgotten. The earliest page in the file was dated within the Session, but early within it, earlier than the foundry list, earlier than the crossing of the Scour, earlier than any occasion on which Mira Kovac had reported an absence to anyone in any room. The page recorded, in a clerk's summary of a watcher's note, that the officer in question appeared to be keeping a record of her own, in her own hand, in a notebook carried on her person, of certain null results encountered in the course of her duties. Appeared to be. The watcher had not seen the notebook's pages. The watcher had seen the notebook, and the officer's habit with it, and the occasions of its use, which had a pattern, and the pattern had been enough for oversight, doing exactly what oversight exists to do, to open a file.

Harrand took up her pen and added a page to the file in her own hand, which she did rarely, preferring dictation, because a page in the commissioner's own hand carried a weight in oversight's internal economy that dictated pages did not, and this addition warranted it. She wrote the date. She wrote that the officer had been tasked, that morning, in the Teshkar matter, and that the tasking had been done by the handler personally, and that the file should note the handler's personal involvement as a fact without comment. She wrote that the register, if it existed as described, constituted a record of a pattern of null results across multiple cases and multiple Sessions, assembled by one officer, held by one officer, and known, until oversight's own watching brought it to light, to one officer alone. She wrote the next sentence slowly, because it was the sentence the whole file existed to hold. A pattern of that kind, known to one person and recorded nowhere else, was a power that nobody had voted on, granted, or reviewed, and it remained such a power however honest the person holding it might be, and indeed the honesty of the holder made the arrangement harder to correct rather than easier, since honest holders are trusted, and trusted holders are not checked. She stopped. She read the sentence back. She did not soften it, because it was, in her judgment, simply true, and she set a particular store by sentences that were simply true, having built a career and a reputation and a not inconsiderable collection of enemies on the practice of writing them down. An unrecorded pattern known to one officer was exactly the kind of record oversight existed to hold. Not to punish. To hold. To take out of one pair of hands and place in the keeping of an institution that could be asked, by people with the standing to ask, what it knew and when it had known it. That the officer had kept the register against instruction aggravated the matter in form. In substance it changed nothing. Even a register kept with permission would have had to come to this desk eventually, because the absences, whatever they were, had been watched by oversight before the officer had chosen to report them to anyone, and a thing watched by two parties and recorded by one is a thing already halfway into the light, whether the one recording it wishes that or not.

She added a final line, an instruction to the file's keeper, that the Teshkar tasking was to be followed in the ordinary way and that no approach was to be made to the officer while the tasking ran, because oversight's interest was in the record and not in the disruption of live work, and because she had no wish, she wrote, and then crossed out the wish and wrote instead that there was no advantage, in unsettling an officer mid case. The crossing out was initialled. She blotted the page. She set it in the file at its proper place, which was at the back, the file growing the way files grow, newest at the rear, the earliest page still at the front where it had been placed on the day the file was opened, with its early date and its careful appeared to be, patient as a foundation stone. Then she closed the file, not because the matter was finished but because the hour was, and she squared it with the desk's edge, and she sat for a moment with her hand flat upon its cover, in a gesture she would have denied resembled anything but tidiness, while below her, two floors below, unheard and unregarded, the building carried on with its evening, its carts and doors and registry bells, and the officer whose name was on the cover carried the subject of the file in her coat pocket up the stairs to her own small office, past [the customs post that stamped convoys which never passed, toward a country made of paper](/2026/11/16/nightjar-scour-crossing-016/) still fresh in her working memory as the last place the pattern had shown itself, and sat down to her dates again, unaware that the page Harrand had just written existed, and that its earliest fellow, at the front of the file, had been written before she had told a living soul.

Mira, at her own desk, with the lamps lit against the Session's early dark, returned to the sheet of dates for the last work of the day, because the sheet was the ground truth of the case in the only sense the Office fully trusted, which was the sense that could survive her. She checked the five days against the returns one final time, drafting, tabling, knowledge, publicity, vote, and she confirmed each against its source, the clerk's hand, the assembly list, the treasury minute and its companions, the public circular, the assembly calendar. She thought about the aide's desk and the crates and the minister's door, the three anchors, waiting for her in a room across the mission quarter, holding whatever they held in metal and wood and brass, which hold best. She thought about the three readings standing in their line in her notebook, money and inspections and the faction, each stated fairly, each awaiting the tests the remaining days would bring. And she thought, because the tasking had put it in the room and the room had kept it there, about the file two floors up, open on its desk, and about what Leyden had spent to know of it, and about the age of its earliest page, which he had not given her and which she would have to earn from the case itself. The vote was four days away now, by the count of her own evening. The sheet lay squared before her. The order of the case was settled and the reasons were not, and that, she reflected, closing the clean notebook and leaving the other in her pocket where it lived, was precisely the shape of ground truth as she had ever known it. The ground held. What stood on it remained to be seen.

She did not sleep on the sheet so much as she carried it, the way she carried everything the Office gave her, into the ordinary business of the following morning, when the procedure resumed its patient work of surrounding the case with verified fact. The assembly returns for the Sessions before this one were brought up from the registry vault at her request, and she read Teshkar's part in them with the same slowness she had given the motion, looking for the inspections grievance in its native habitat, and she found it, sitting after sitting, in the committee lists and the questions and the formal objections, a grievance with a history and a vocabulary of its own, which had grown more formal as the Sessions passed and had never once, in all that record, been attached to a threat of departure. States attached departure threats to grievances they intended to settle by departure's price. Grievances they intended to live with they argued, as Teshkar had argued this one, in committee, in the ordinary voice, year upon Session, and the absence of any earlier coupling between the inspections and the leaving strengthened nothing and weakened nothing conclusively, but it thinned the second reading in her hands, the way a fabric thins, not tearing, only letting more light through than it had. The money reading she tested against the treasury's behaviour rather than its figures, because figures are the part of a treasury that can be arranged for visitors. A treasury that has discovered a deficit and intends to bargain with it behaves in recognisable ways. It slows its payments in particular quarters. It recalls its clerks from leave. It writes to its counterparts in a new tone, warmer or colder depending on its school, but changed. She enquired, through the delegation clerk and through the Office's own watcher, after each of these signs, and the answers came back mixed and human and inconclusive, some payments slowed and some not, one clerk recalled and two sent on leave in the ordinary rotation, letters in the ordinary tone. A treasury might behave so if it were bargaining. A treasury might behave so if it were merely a treasury, in a hard season, doing its sums. She recorded the inconclusiveness as inconclusiveness, in the notebook, in words that would still be true when read back to her by an unfriendly reader, because ground truth that has been polished for a conclusion is no longer ground and no longer truth, and the case, she was coming to see, would be decided by the few facts hard enough to bear weight, the drafting date chief among them, and not by the accumulation of soft ones.

In the afternoon she walked the mission quarter without errand, which was her habit when a case had a room in it that she would later have to read, because a place approached on foot, at street level, in the weather it was actually having, keeps a different and humbler truth than a place approached by carriage with a delegation's flag. She passed the Teshkar mission's gates and noted the porters and the comings and goings, the Valtessa courier leaving at a run, the Melora delegation arriving at a walk, the ordinary traffic of a member state conducting its membership. Nothing in the street spoke of a state packing itself. The crates were indoors, and their meaning was indoors, addressed to those who entered, and she turned that over as she walked, the addressed quality of the packing, its selectivity as a message. A departure genuinely prepared is prepared at the docks and the treasuries and the border posts, in manifests and orders and quiet words to garrison clerks. A departure rehearsed in one outer office, where delegations are received, is prepared for an audience, and the audience, she thought, watching the Melora party pass through the gates and imagining the crates registering behind the aide's desk in pause after lengthened pause, was not Teshkar's own people at all. It was the Accord. It was everyone who would carry the sight home and report that Teshkar looked already half gone, and lower their price for keeping it, or raise their alarm at losing it, according to their natures. Theatre, the aide had not called it. Theatre, Mira did not yet call it either, in the notebook. But she wrote the walk down, and the gates, and the ordinary traffic, and the selectivity, and she referred once more to the sheet of dates lying back in her office, the drafting day and the treasury day standing thirteen days apart across the head of the case, and she let the two observations stand side by side in her mind without forcing them to embrace, because forcing was the error the remaining days existed to prevent.

## The Collapse

The money reading was the kindest reading, and Mira had liked it for as long as it could be liked. It gave everyone a shape that could be held without shame. A member state in deficit, a treasury that had counted and found itself short, a threat spoken aloud in the assembly so that the price of staying might be named in a room where prices could be answered. That story asked for no hidden hands. It asked for no theatre. It allowed the minister to be a man bargaining for his people, allowed the Accord to be an elder brother asked to pay more than its share, allowed the vote itself to be a lever in the ordinary sense, a thing any state might lift when the accounts would not close. She had carried that reading through the procedure, through the paper, through the visit to the delegation, and she had tested it the way Leyden had taught her to test any first reading, by asking what would have to be true for it to hold and whether the record, laid flat, would bear the weight of it.

The record would not bear it.

She laid the departure clock out again in the quiet of the small room the delegation had given her, not as a table now but as a sequence held in her head in the order in which the days had actually fallen, and she walked it slowly because haste at this point would have been a kind of dishonesty. The motion had been drafted. Then the motion had been tabled. Then, after both of those things were already done, after the words had been chosen and the clauses numbered in the clerkly hand that Teshkar used for its assembly business, the deficit had become known to Teshkar's own treasury. Then the threat had been made public. Then, still ahead of them all, the vote. The order was not a matter of interpretation. The Office copy carried the drafting date within the Session in its heading. The tabling record carried its own date. The treasury minute, shown to her without flourish by a man who plainly thought it helped his state's case, carried the date on which the figures had first been laid before those entitled to see them, and that date sat after the drafting date by a margin no clerical slip could explain. A deficit cannot write a motion that already exists. A shortfall cannot reach backwards and put words in a clerk's hand before the counting that revealed it. The money was real. She did not doubt the money. The ledgers she had been permitted to glance at had the tired honesty of figures that nobody had bothered to dress, and the treasury man's embarrassment had been the embarrassment of a servant who knows the household accounts are thin and would rather the neighbours did not discuss them at table. The timing of the threat fitted the money too, and that was what had made the reading so reasonable. A threat made public after a deficit is known looks like a price being named, because in most assemblies, in most sessions, that is exactly what such a threat would be. But the motion was older than its excuse. Whatever hand had wanted the departure had wanted it before there was a respectable reason to offer for it, and had then been handed, by chance or by patience, a reason that would serve.

She sat with that for a long while, because killing the reasonable reading is not the same as finding the true one, and she knew the difference in her bones from cases where she had mistaken the death of one story for the birth of another. Money dead meant only that money had not written the motion. It did not yet say who had. It did not yet say whether the inspections reading might still hold, whether a minister who disliked being watched might have drafted early out of a longer resentment that predated any single account. It did not yet clear the minister himself. For that she needed the rooms.

The aide's room first, because she had been given leave to enter it in the aide's absence and because its anchors had been named to her plainly and she could work without invention. The aide's desk stood by the window, a working desk and not a show desk, its surface scored where seals had been pressed and lifted over many sessions. The packing crates stood along the wall where the filing cabinets had been, open, half full, their lids leaning. The minister's door closed the far end of the room, a heavy door with a handle worn bright where hands had taken it in the same place for years. Metal and stone hold best, and the desk had metal fittings and the door had a metal handle and the crates, though wooden, had iron corners that would keep what the wood let go. She did not touch anything at first. She stood in the doorway and let the room be ordinary, because a room read too eagerly gives back only the reader.

When she did work, she worked at the anchors in the order she had named them. The desk first. She laid her palm flat near the seal press, not on it, close enough that the fitting could speak. What came was not a scene. Weight never gives a scene. It gives intensity held in a place where feeling has been repeated until the place has learned it, and what the desk had learned was anger sustained over weeks, not the flash of a single afternoon. It came as a pressure behind her own ribs, a tightening that did not rise and fall but stayed, the way a jaw stays clenched through a long meeting. It had layers. Underneath, order. Someone had worked at this desk properly even while furious, had kept the papers straight, had set the seals in their row. Over the order, haste, a wish to be done, hands moving faster than the task required because slowness would have left room for thought. Over the haste, a return, again and again, to the same objects, the same files lifted and set down and lifted again, as if the fury kept circling back to the things that proved the errand was real. She felt her own breathing shorten and she made herself breathe out slowly, the way she had been trained, and the cold began in her fingers and moved up her wrists in the familiar way, so that by the time she lifted her hand the desk edge looked very clear and very far away at once.

She moved to the crates. The iron corners were colder than the room and held more. Here the anger sharpened. Boxing is a particular kind of work. It asks the hands to treat things as if they were already gone while the things are still present, to wrap and stack and close lids on objects that will be needed again on the following morning. The crates had learned resentment at that doubleness. She could feel the repeated effort of making a room look emptied without emptying it, the care taken not to damage anything, the care taken not to throw anything away, and under the care a fury that returned each time a lid was pressed down on papers that had no destination written on them. Nothing in the crates spoke of arrival. Places that are really being left carry, even in anger, some forward pull, some imagining of the next room, the next desk, the next city. These crates carried none of that. They carried rehearsal. They carried the weight of a departure practised so that anyone entering would see the practice and mistake it for the fact.

At the minister's door she stopped short of the handle and read from the frame, where stone met metal in the hinge. The door gave her less, and the less mattered. Through it, faintly, came the ordinary pressure of the room beyond, which she would read properly in its turn. From the outer side she got only crossings, many crossings, brief and businesslike, and the aide's anger again as a thinner layer where the aide had stood with boxes in her arms and waited to be admitted or dismissed. What she did not get, at desk or crate or door, was any name. She could prove, if proof is the word for what a Weigher can lay before a sceptical commissioner, that someone had been furious in that room for weeks, furious in a sustained and ordered way, furious while packing things that were not being thrown away and not being sent anywhere. She could never prove it was the minister. She could never prove it was the aide. Weight records intensity and affect. It does not record identity. It does not record who. The limit bit exactly there, in the middle of the strongest reading she had taken in the whole case, and she honoured it by stopping. Another officer might have been tempted to let the known facts lean on the reading until the reading seemed to say more than it did. The aide had been seen packing. The aide was the person who kept the office. Therefore the anger must be the aide's anger. That chain feels like logic and is not. It is biography pasted over affect. The room had told her what had been felt in it, over weeks, at length. It had not told her whose heart had done the feeling, and any report that claimed otherwise would be a lie dressed as a gift.

She wrote nothing down in the room. She stood in the cold that now reached her elbows and looked once more at the crates, at their open lids and their careful contents, and she understood the packing better for having felt its temper. Whoever had ordered it had chosen well. Packing is visible from a corridor. Packing can be reported by a watcher without the watcher needing to invent anything. Packing makes clerks lower their voices and makes visiting delegations go home and say that the office looked already half gone. If you wished to push a minister toward a vote he had not chosen, you could do worse than to make his own outer office rehearse his leaving for him, day after day, until leaving began to look like the settled direction and staying began to look like the effort that needed explaining.

The minister's office was the second demonstration, and it demonstrated by refusing to perform.

She was permitted to approach the desk, not to sit at it, not to open it, and the permission was given by the minister himself with a courtesy so plain that she watched it closely for theatre and found none. He was a man in the later part of middle age, tired in the way that office tiredness differs from bodily tiredness, and he spoke of the vote as a man speaks of weather that will inconvenience a journey he has not yet decided to take. She listened while she was meant to listen. Then, when the formal part was done and he had withdrawn to the inner room to take a call from his assembly, she worked, briefly and within the leave she had been given, at the anchors the door and the desk afforded her.

The door anchors from inside gave her crossings again, but slower, fewer, with none of the aide's room's layered fury burned into the metal. The desk, approached as permitted, gave her the ordinary weight of a working office and almost nothing else. Paper holds worst and most of the desk was paper, but the seal and the heavy pen and the stone weight that kept the papers from the draught of the inner door all held enough to speak, and what they said was routine. Meetings held. Letters dictated and disliked and sent anyway. Boredom in the long afternoons when the assembly was in recess. Irritation at subordinates, swiftly spent. Concern about the deficit, real concern, but recent in the layering of it, sitting on top of older and duller matters the way fresh dust sits on a shelf. No departure fury. No triumph at a threat well made. No rehearsed leaving, no repeated handling of objects as if they were already relics. No sustained intent to be gone. A man driving a departure does not have to rage about it. She knew that. Cold intent also leaves weight, sometimes the heaviest weight of all, the weight of a decision returned to daily and fed. There was none. There was the weight of a man who had not yet made the decision that everyone outside his door was busy making on his behalf.

She took her hand away and the cold this time was less, because the read had been less, and the less was the answer. She thanked the empty desk in her head, absurdly, the way one thanks a witness who has helped by having nothing to hide. When the minister returned he asked whether she had found what she needed, and she said that she had found an office, which made him smile for the first time, a brief and unguarded smile that did more to clear him in her own private accounting than any reading could have done, though she would never put a smile in a report.

With money dead and the minister clear, the shape that remained was the shape that had been third on the list and least comfortable for everyone, because it accused no single villain who could be removed and thereby save the whole. A faction inside Teshkar wanted the departure. Not the state as a whole, not the treasury with its honest embarrassment, not the minister at his ordinary desk, but a grouping within the state's own house that understood one plain thing about membership. A member state is watched. Its assembly is reported. Its offices receive delegations. Its motions are copied and read in Carrowmere by clerks who note the dates. Its rooms can be entered by leave and read by a Weigher whose report, however limited, will be filed. A departed state has no witnesses. No copies. No delegations in the corridor remarking on the crates. No oversight, however resented, standing between a faction and the quiet rearrangement of a country once the door is closed from inside. That was the driver. Not money, which had arrived too late to write the motion. Not inspections alone, though the faction no doubt found inspections irksome and said so loudly whenever a respectable reason was needed. The inspections reading had been argued fairly and she kept what was fair in it. Many in Teshkar disliked being inspected and would vote to leave for that reason alone if asked in the wrong week. But dislike is not drafting. The motion had been drafted early, by hands that wanted it before the public reasons existed, and the packing had been arranged in advance, by the same logic, so that the office would look already half gone before any vote had made it so.

The pressure that keeps such a state in is therefore not a bribe and not a threat of the Accord's own. It is the one thing the faction cannot survive, which is witnesses. If the vote happens in full view, with the drafting date on the record, with the sequence laid out where the assembly and the Accord's observers can see it together, then leaving means being seen to leave for reasons nobody will state aloud. A faction that needs darkness cannot afford a well lit vote. It can push in corridors. It can pack rooms. It can lend its minister a deficit and a grievance and hope the respectable reasons carry the motion over the line while nobody asks why the line was drawn before the reasons arrived. What it cannot easily do is stand up in a watched chamber and say plainly that it wishes to leave so that nobody will watch what happens next. Mira stood in the corridor outside the aide's room with the cold still in her arms and felt the case settle, not with triumph, which she distrusted in herself and in others, but with the quieter feeling of a lock finding its key. The lever existed. It had existed since Ground Truth, sitting plainly in the clock. It only needed to be pulled in public.

She thought then, unbidden, of the file two floors up, and of her own register in her coat pocket, the notebook she carried against instruction, the private register of absences she had agreed to stop keeping and had not stopped keeping, and she understood that the same principle she meant to use for Teshkar was already being used, fairly or not, upon her. Witnesses change what can be done quietly. She had kept the only copy of a pattern. That had given her freedom and it had given her power of a kind nobody had voted on, and Harrand, who believed the Ninth Office should not exist and was sometimes right, had opened a file because oversight, too, understands that an unwitnessed pattern is a temptation. The thought did not make the file welcome. It made it legible. She put her hand in her pocket and felt the notebook's edge through the cloth, and she knew, before any order came and before any summons was sent, what the rest of the case was going to cost her and that she would rather pay it by her own hand than have it taken from her by someone else's.


She went back over the clock once more before she left the delegation, because a finding that kills the public reason for a vote is a serious thing to carry into another office, and she wanted no softness in it. She asked for the treasury minute again and was brought it without argument, and she set it, in her mind, against the Office copy of the motion, page by page. The motion spoke of departure as a settled intention, with clauses about notice and property and the recall of Teshkar's own officers from joint service, clauses that take time to draft well and longer to draft badly, because badly drafted departure clauses invite dispute about every desk and seal the state has ever shared. The care in those clauses was itself a kind of date. Haste leaves a different mark on paper than patience does. Whoever had prepared the motion had not prepared it in the panic of a newly discovered deficit. They had prepared it as a builder prepares a bridge before the river is in flood, quietly, while the weather still allowed denial. The treasury minute, by contrast, had the marks of genuine surprise in its own house style, the figures called for twice, the second calling noted in a different hand, the margin carrying the small, shocked arithmetic of men recalculating what they thought they knew. She had seen enough genuine surprise in offices to recognise its clerical shape, and enough performed surprise to recognise that too, and this was the former. The two documents, read together, did not merely sit in the wrong order for the money story. They sat in the right order for a different story, in which a prepared departure waits for a public grief to arrive and then steps forward wearing it.

She considered, fairly, whether the inspections reading could still rescue a simpler account, because she owed every live possibility its full strength before she let it fall. It could explain a great deal. It could explain resentment in the assembly, speeches that needed no coaching, a minister willing to let a threat stand because the threat itself sometimes softened an inspector's report. What it could not explain was the packing arranged before the vote, in a room whose occupant had not chosen it, by people who needed the appearance of departure more than they needed any particular clause in the motion. Inspections are argued about. They are not usually rehearsed with crates. Nor could the inspections reading explain why the drafting had been kept so quiet for so long if its purpose had been bargaining, because a bargain requires the other side to know the price early enough to pay it. A motion drafted in quiet, tabled without flourish, and only made public when a deficit gave it a respectable companion begins to look less like a price and more like a plan that had been waiting for weather. She let the inspections reading keep its fair share of the assembly's temper and denied it the authorship of the motion, and that distinction, temper against authorship, was the distinction she would later put on the record without naming it, because assemblies are entitled to their tempers and are not always entitled to be believed about authorship.

In the aide's room she had also learned, in the way weight teaches a reader through her own body, what weeks of ordered anger do to a place that is used daily by other people who feel nothing of the kind. The clerks who passed through that room would have felt it only as a thinness in their own patience, a wish to finish errands quickly, a reluctance to linger by the crates. Places teach the people in them without permission. That, too, was part of the arrangement's cleverness, and its cruelty. Even those who had not been told to rehearse the departure were being rehearsed by the room itself, their voices lowering, their expectations settling, so that when the vote came the idea of leaving would arrive already furnished, like a room one has visited often in a dream. Mira, standing among the crates with the cold climbing her arms, had felt a professional respect for the design that she would have preferred not to feel. It was the respect one owes a well made lock even while picking it. Someone in Teshkar understood that facts are not only argued. They are staged, and then the staging is forgotten, and then the fact is quoted as if it had grown there by itself.

The minister's office, read afterwards, had given her the necessary counterweight to that respect. A staged room can be answered by an unstaged one. His desk had not been arranged for her visit in any way her gift could detect, and her ordinary eyes, which she trusted almost as much, had seen the small disorder of real work, the cup left where a left handed man would leave it though the minister was not left handed and must therefore have been interrupted mid gesture, the papers weighted against a draught that did indeed come, while she stood there, from the inner door. Reality is untidy in ways theatre rarely permits itself. Theatre tidies toward a meaning. His office tidied toward nothing. It merely continued. That continuation, more than any protestation he might have made, told her the departure was not his child. It was an animal that had been led into his outer room and tethered there, where visitors would see it and report that the minister kept such an animal, and she resolved, while thanking him for his leave to approach the desk, that her report would untether it in public and let the assembly see whose hands had held the rope.


Before she reported, she made herself state the finding in the fewest words that would hold it, because a finding that cannot survive brevity will not survive a chamber. Money did not write the motion. The minister did not drive the departure. The packing was arranged to be seen. The departure was wanted by hands that needed unwitnessed room. Each sentence rested on a different kind of evidence, paper for the first, weight honestly limited for the second and third, and the shape of the whole for the last, and she marked, in her own mind, which sentence she would defend with documents, which she would defend with her gift and its stated limits, and which she offered as the reading that remained when the others had been fairly tried and fairly set aside. That discipline, learned over cases that had punished its absence, steadied her more than confidence would have done. She did not need to be certain of everything. She needed to know, exactly, what she was certain of, and to carry only that weight into the rooms where the lever would be pulled.

## Pressure

She decided on the stairs.

Not at the top. Not in the corridor. On the stairs themselves, between the delegation floor and the floor above it, with her hand on the rail and the cold from the two rooms still sitting in her forearms like water in a cloth. She stopped. She looked at the notebook in her pocket without taking it out. She thought it through once, plainly, the way she had been taught to think through any surrender of material.

If she waited, it would be taken. A file was open. A file that is open does not close itself out of politeness. Sooner or later a written instruction would come down the two floors and a clerk would stand where she was standing and ask for the notebook by its description, and she would hand it over because refusal after a lawful instruction is a different case and a worse one. Taken, it would be read as a thing seized. Every entry in it would be read against her. The gaps would be read as concealment. The care in it would be read as obsession. The fact that she had kept it against instruction would be the first fact and the last fact and it would colour every absence she had recorded until the pattern itself was lost inside the story of the officer who had hidden it.

If she gave it, it would be read differently. A volunteered document is read differently. Not kindly. Harrand did not do kindly as a method. But differently in the way that matters in oversight, which is the order in which questions are asked. A given document is first read for what it contains. A taken document is first read for why it was kept. She wanted the absences read for what they contained. She wanted the pattern out of her pocket and into a room where other eyes would have to reckon with it, even if reckoning began with her own reprimand deepened and her own name written more firmly on the file. She wanted, she admitted on the stairs, to stop being the only witness. That was Harrand's fair point and she was tired enough to grant it without bargaining.

She took the notebook out. She held it a moment. The cover was worn at the corners from her coat. The pages had swollen slightly where her hand, cold from reads, had held it too long in earlier cases. She put it back. No. She took it out again and carried it in her hand, openly, the rest of the way up, because carrying it hidden to the moment of giving would have spoiled the giving.

The two floors took longer than two floors should. Her legs felt narrow. That was the reads. Two rooms in succession, one heavy and one light, and the heavy one had taken more than she had allowed for while she was inside it, because anger sustained over weeks does not let go of a reader cleanly. It clings. It makes the shoulders ache. It makes plain tasks require counting. She counted the doors. She counted the turns. She got one turn wrong and had to come back along a corridor where a delegation clerk looked up and then looked down again with the practised blindness of a man who has learned that officers carrying notebooks in their hands are not to be greeted. She did not resent the error. She noted it as depletion, not failure of care. She had not misread a room. She had not mistaken a date. She had taken a wrong turn in a building she knew because her body was paying for two reads and a decision, and the payment had to come from somewhere, and it had come from the small change of attention that keeps a person oriented among familiar doors.

Harrand's outer office was quiet. The desk there was unoccupied. The inner door was open by the width of a hand. Mira knocked on the frame, once, and waited to be seen.

Harrand saw her. Harrand saw the notebook. Something in her face changed by a very small amount, and Mira, who had learned to read commissioners the way other people read weather over water, understood that surprise had occurred and had been set aside in the same breath. Harrand did not rise. She moved a paper from the centre of her desk to the side of her desk. The file was there. Mira could see it. Open. Her own name on the tab in a hand she did not know. Pages inside it, more pages than she had expected, the earliest of them dated within the Session at a point that made her stomach go still, because the date sat before the day she had first spoken aloud about the absences to anyone in the Office. She did not stare. Staring would have been a confession that the date was news, and it was news, and she gave herself the small discipline of looking at Harrand instead.

She said she had come to give a document to oversight.

Harrand said nothing.

She said the document was a private register. She said it recorded absences. She said she kept it herself, in her coat, against instruction. She said she had agreed to stop keeping it and had not stopped. She said she was giving it now, voluntarily, before any instruction to surrender it had been issued to her, and she asked that the giving be recorded as a giving, with the day and the manner of it noted on the file.

Harrand said, is that the whole of it.

Mira said it was the whole notebook. She said there was no second copy. She said the entries were in her hand and in order and that she would answer questions about any entry that oversight wished to put to her, in writing if writing was preferred.

Harrand held out no hand. That was correct, Mira thought, with a clarity that cut through the narrowing in her head. A commissioner who reaches for a volunteered document too quickly makes the volunteer feel seized after all. Harrand waited. The waiting was the procedure. Mira stepped forward to lay the notebook on the desk herself.

The clerk came in behind her without knocking.

He was young. He had a paper in his hand, folded once, with a seal showing at the fold. He said Commissioner, and then he saw Mira, and then he saw the notebook in her hand moving toward the desk, and he made the error that young clerks make when a written instruction and a live scene disagree. He obeyed the paper. He stepped past the threshold too fast. He said the register was to be secured. He put his hand out. Not toward the desk. Toward her hand. His fingers closed on the edge of the notebook while her own fingers were still on it, and he pulled, not hard, but with the authority of a man who believes a seal on a fold entitles him to pull.

Mira did not let go.

It was brief. It was close. It was unshowy in the way that real struggles in offices are unshowy, because there is furniture everywhere and no room to be grand. The clerk pulled again and stepped in as he pulled, and his shoulder struck the door frame and the folded paper fell from his other hand to the floor. Mira turned her wrist rather than wrench, because wrenching would have torn pages, and pages were the whole point, and the turn brought her elbow against the edge of Harrand's desk with a knock that sent a pen rolling. The clerk, startled by the knock or by her refusal or by the sound of his own breathing, tightened his grip and tried to lift the notebook up and away as if it were a thing being rescued from a table edge. She felt the cover bend. She felt a stitch in the binding give a fraction. She said, quite quietly, no. She said it once. She set her other hand flat on the desk to steady herself, because the room had narrowed and the floor seemed to be considering movement, and she held the notebook down toward the desk with both her weight and her intent, not striking, not pushing the man, only refusing the taking and completing the path she had chosen for the thing in her hand.

Harrand stood up.

She said the clerk's name. She said it in the tone that stops lifts in mid ascent and meetings in mid sentence. She said, stand down. She said, that instruction is mine and I am countermanding it in this room. She said, pick up your paper.

The clerk let go as if the notebook had become hot. He stepped back. He bent for his paper with a face that had gone a deep and miserable red, and Mira felt, even through the narrowing, a pity for him that she filed away for later because pity in the moment would have looked like triumph and she had no triumph to offer anyone. Her hand was shaking. She made it finish the journey. She laid the notebook on the desk, squarely, in the cleared space Harrand had made at the start of the scene, and she took her hand off it and stepped back one step and put both her hands where they could be seen, empty.

Harrand looked at the notebook. Harrand looked at the clerk. She told him to wait outside and to write down exactly what had happened, including his own part, before he spoke to anyone about it. She told him the written instruction he carried would be attached to the file with her countermand noted on it in her own hand. He went. The door did not quite close behind him. Neither of them moved to close it.

Harrand said, you came to give it.

Mira said she had.

Harrand said, then it is given. She did not touch it yet. She asked whether Mira understood that a volunteered document became an Accord document from the moment of giving, that it would be copied, listed, and held by oversight, that access to it would no longer be Mira's to grant or refuse, and that questions arising from it would be put formally. Mira said she understood each part as it was spoken. Her voice sounded thinner than she wanted. She let it be thin. Depletion is not improved by performance.

It was then that word reached her, and it reached her inside the same unbroken standing in that office, with her hands still empty and the notebook still untouched between them, because the clerk outside, doing at last the useful thing, took the telephone call that had been ringing in the outer office through the whole scene in short, ignored bursts, and came to the half open door without entering and spoke to Harrand and to the room together. The boxes in the aide's room in the Teshkar delegation. People had come for them. Not porters. Men with a delegation authorisation that the watcher at the door had not felt able to refuse. The boxes were being carried out. The aide was there. The aide had not been harmed. The aide had stood in the doorway of the room she had packed and had watched her own rehearsal being taken away as if it were the real departure arriving to collect its props.

Mira understood at once what was wanted of her by her own instincts, which was to go. To go down the two floors and across to the delegation and to stand in that doorway herself and read the men and the crates and perhaps to say the drafting date aloud in a corridor where it would do the most good. She felt the wish as a physical pull, stronger than the shaking in her hand. And she knew, in the same breath, that she could not. Not as a matter of permission. As a matter of capacity. The two reads and the giving had narrowed her to a few plain things. She could stand. She could speak clearly if she spoke slowly. She could answer questions. She could not cross a building and enter a contested room and read it well and intervene wisely. If she went, she would go depleted into the one room where a mistake would be used, and any failure there would be caused by depletion rather than by error of judgment, which did not make it less of a failure for the people in the room. She said so. She said it to Harrand, plainly, because Harrand was the officer present and because hiding a limit from oversight in the very act of surrendering a register would have been a poor beginning. She said she could not also go herself to stop the boxes being taken. She said the watcher should be told to observe and to record times and names if names were offered, and not to obstruct, because the boxes were theatre and theatre taken by force in a corridor would become a different and better story for the faction than the one they had written for themselves.

Harrand listened. Harrand did not say whether she agreed. She reached at last for the telephone herself and gave the instruction about the watcher in words very close to Mira's own, which Mira chose not to remark upon. Then Harrand put the telephone down. Then, for the first time, Harrand touched the notebook. She drew it toward her by one corner, the way a person draws a document that has become theirs to hold and not theirs to love. She did not open it. She looked at Mira over it.

She said, this will be read differently because you gave it.

Mira said she knew.

She said, it will still be read.

Mira said she knew that too.

The clerk's paper lay where Harrand had set it, by the file. The file lay open. The notebook lay closed. Mira stood in the narrowing and waited to be dismissed, because a giving is not finished when the hand lets go. It is finished when the receiver accepts the terms of the gift aloud, and Harrand, who understood procedure as a form of honesty, kept her standing there a moment longer while she wrote, in her own hand, on the topmost page of the file, a note that Mira could not read upside down and did not try to read. The pen scratched. The room was very quiet. Somewhere below them, two floors and a building away, boxes were leaving a room that had never been vacated, and she let them go, because letting them go was the cost of the thing she had chosen to do with her own hand, and she would not pretend otherwise to herself on the way down.


Harrand asked one question while the pen was still moving, and she asked it without looking up, in the voice of a woman reading a form aloud to herself. She asked when the register had last been added to. Mira told her. She asked whether any entry had been altered after it was first written. Mira said that crossings out existed where a word had been chosen badly on a tired night, and that the crossings out had been left visible, and that nothing had been removed. Harrand wrote that down too, or wrote something, and the scratching of the pen became, for Mira, the sound of the document changing houses. In her pocket it had been a secret. On the desk it was becoming a record. The difference was not comfort. The difference was jurisdiction. She felt it in her shoulders, which ached from the crates, and in her jaw, which she had to unclench by an act of will, and she understood that she had been clenching it since the clerk's hand had closed on the cover, as if her body had wished to bite down on the moment and hold it still. She let her jaw soften. She let her hands hang. She waited for the dismissal as a soldier waits, not because she was a soldier, but because some postures are older than any office and the body reaches for them when ceremony, however small, is under way.

## The Silence

Afterwards there was a room she was allowed to sit in, and she sat.

It was not a cell and it was not a kindness. It was a small waiting room on the oversight floor with a chair that had been made for shorter sittings than the one her body now required, and a window that gave her the tops of other roofs and a sky that had gone the colour of paper left in light. Someone had brought her water. She drank it in sips because drinking it quickly made the cold in her stomach announce itself. She did not sleep. She could not sleep after heavy reads, and the giving had been its own kind of read, a reading of herself performed in front of a witness, and her mind, denied sleep, did the work it always did in the stillness before she had to speak formally, which was to lay out, fairly, everything she actually had.

She gave the case its fair chance in the silence, as she would have wanted an opponent to give it, with no thumb on the scale for her own cleverness.

First, the drafting date. The motion to leave had been drafted before the deficit was known to Teshkar's own treasury. That was paper. That was checkable by any clerk with leave to see the two records side by side. It did not depend on her gift. It did not depend on weight. It would still be true if she had never entered a single room.

Second, the boxes. A minister's outer office had been packed, in advance, by arrangement, with nothing thrown away and nothing labelled for a destination, so that the office would look already half gone to anyone who came to look. That was visible. Watchers had seen it. The aide had lived it. The crates had held the anger of weeks and the care of someone handling things that were meant to be unboxed again by someone else.

Third, the notebook, and in the notebook, four entries, which she named to herself plainly in the stillness because they were the reader's evidence as much as hers, and together with the drafting date and the packed room they were everything she was about to act on. The first entry was the earliest absence she had dared to write down, a room where a negotiation had plainly happened, cups still warm in the weight of the stone table, and no weight at all where the deciding words should have settled, as if the decision had been taken by a binding that left nothing behind. The second entry was the empty store by the delegation quarter, used once and cleaned, where fear should have lingered for days and had left no trace on the iron shelving, so that she had checked her own hands for failure and found them cold and working. The third entry was the customs house side room from an earlier case, where repeated crossings had worn the threshold bright with intent and yet the inner bench, where a bribe or a threat must surely have been rehearsed, held a smoothness she had no honest word for except absence. The fourth entry was the most recent, the one she had written with Bask's ten years still heavy in her and her handwriting gone smaller to fit the page, an absence in a place of transit where violence had been done nearby and the place itself remembered the violence and remembered nothing of the quiet minute that must have preceded it, the minute in which someone had prepared the ground and left it unnaturally clean.

Four entries. A date. A room full of boxes. That was all. It was enough to act on and it was not enough to be comfortable, and she sat in the chair and let both truths stand without forcing either of them to leave. No new action came to her in the stillness that changed the outcome. Nothing needed to. The lever was already shaped. What the silence gave her was not a plan but a steadiness, the knowledge that she could say the small set of true things aloud, in order, without inflating any of them, and that if she was to be judged, by Harrand or by the assembly or by Leyden when he heard what she had done with her pocket's secret, she would rather be judged on the small set than defended by a larger invention. The water was finished. The light had not changed. She stood, slowly, and the chair scraped, and she went to speak.

## Close

The lever was pulled in public, which is the only way such a lever works.

The drafting date was placed on the record before the assembly, not whispered in a corridor and not leaked to a friendly clerk for the evening papers that Teshkar did not have, but entered, by leave and by procedure, where motions are entered and where dates, once entered, cannot be unentered without a second and more embarrassing procedure. With it went the sequence, laid out in the plain order of the Session. The motion drafted. The motion tabled. The deficit known to the treasury. The threat made public. The vote. No speech accused any man or any faction by name. No Weigher stood up and described anger in a room, because anger in a room, honestly reported, proves feeling and not identity, and a lever that depends on overstating a gift will snap in the hand that pulls it. The paper did the work. Paper, which holds weight worst of all materials, holds dates best of all, because dates on paper are not feelings but undertakings, witnessed and copied and filed in more than one house.

And the vote was held in full view. The Accord's witnesses were present by right, not by favour, seated where witnesses sit, with their notebooks open and their presence noted in the same record that noted the date. Delegations that might have stayed away out of delicacy were reminded, delicately, that delicacy at a departure vote is a gift to those who prefer unwitnessed rooms. They came. The chamber was full in the way that matters, which is not numbers but attention. Every member who rose to speak for leaving had to speak knowing that the drafting date sat on the record beside the motion, and that any reason he gave would be heard against the knowledge that the motion had existed before the reason. Some spoke for inspections, and were heard fairly, because the dislike of inspections was real and had its honourable holders. Some spoke for money, and were heard fairly too, until a treasury man, to his credit, confirmed the treasury date himself and sat down with the air of a man who has refused to carry a parcel that is not his. Nobody rose to say that Teshkar should leave so that nobody would watch what happened next. Nobody ever does. That is the point of witnesses. The reason that drives a thing in private is often the one reason that cannot survive being required in public, and a faction that has arranged crates and corridors finds, in a lit chamber, that its hands are empty of any speech it can actually make.

Teshkar stayed.

It stayed for two years, on terms nobody likes, and the terms deserve to be stated plainly enough to be believed, because a settlement that is described only in the language of relief will be doubted by everyone who has to live under it. Inspections continue. They continue on the existing cycle, with the existing rights of entry and report, and the delegation that dislikes them most has signed the continuation with the others. Money is not paid as a price. No sum was offered for staying and no sum was accepted for staying, and the deficit, which remains real, is to be dealt with through the ordinary channels that existed before any threat was made, so that no future assembly can point to this session and claim that a drafted motion is a way to open a treasury. The two year term runs from the vote. At the end of two years Teshkar may raise departure again by the stated process, with a motion drafted in its own time and for its own stated reasons, and the Accord may answer as it answers then. Nobody in the chamber called the terms a victory. The minister, when he spoke at the end, called them endurable, which Mira, listening from the witnesses' gallery where she had been placed without comment by a clerk who did not know her, thought was the most honest word used all day. Sema Koll was not in the chamber. Sema Koll was, Mira learned afterwards, in the outer office that was no longer half gone, unboxing files with the help of a porter who kept apologising for the disorder of the crates, and putting the seals back in their row. The boxes that had been taken were returned two days later without explanation, and were emptied, and were stacked empty in a store room, where they would no doubt be used again for something innocent. Mira hoped the aide's anger, when next she felt it at that desk, would be her own anger, about her own business, and not a rehearsal imposed on her hands by people who needed a room to lie for them.

In the Office, the quieter half of the close proceeded without a chamber.

The register was now an Accord document. It had been taken into oversight's keeping as a volunteered paper, listed and copied under oversight's rules, and it was read differently because it was given. That difference was not a pardon. Mira's reprimand stood. A further note was added to her record, in Harrand's hand, stating that the register had been kept against instruction and surrendered voluntarily before any instruction to surrender it had been issued, and that both halves of that sentence were to be read together whenever the document was consulted. Leyden, when he spoke to her at last, did not reproach her for the giving. He reproached her, mildly and with the weariness of a man spending the last of a credit he had already spent once, for making him learn of it from the oversight floor's daily list rather than from her own mouth on the stairs. She accepted that. It was fair. She had chosen the receiver of the giving with care and the messenger of the news with none, and handlers, like ministers, are entitled to be told by their own people first.

Her position on the register, when she was required to state it without triumph and without self pity, was this. She no longer holds the only copy of the pattern. That is the cost. The absences she had found, in rooms and stores and side rooms and places of transit, are no longer a private burden she can choose when to share, and the freedom to wait for a better moment, a fuller pattern, a safer season, has gone with the notebook out of her coat. She will be asked questions at times not of her choosing. Entries she wrote in haste after heavy reads will be read by rested eyes and found wanting in places, and she will have to answer for the wanting. And that is also, in Harrand's one fair point, the correction. A pattern known to one officer is a power nobody voted on. A pattern held by oversight can be checked, challenged, added to by other officers in other cases, and refused the dignity of a private oracle. She had wanted witnesses for Teshkar. She could not honestly refuse witnesses for herself. The notebook's going did not make her lighter in the way that putting down Bask's ten years would one day make her lighter. It made her more ordinary in her own office, which is a different and perhaps a healthier thing. Her coat hung differently without it. She kept putting her hand in the pocket for two days, and finding only the lining, and a receipt she did not remember keeping, and once, absurdly, a seal she must have borrowed and never returned, which she returned.

There was paperwork after the vote, as there is paperwork after every rescue, and Mira read the settlement summary in the witnesses' gallery while the chamber emptied in the slow, anticlimactic way of rooms that have decided not to do the dramatic thing. Clerks carried the record away in parts. Members who had prepared speeches for a departure found themselves folding them into cases with the particular embarrassment of actors whose play has been cancelled after the costumes were pressed. She felt no wish to savour that embarrassment. Several of those members had believed, honestly, that money or inspections were the whole of it, and being shown a date on a record is a humbling education for an honest believer. She hoped, without evidence, that the humbling would make some of them ask harder questions in private about who had drafted what and when, because a faction that has failed once in a lit chamber may try again in a darker one, and the best guard against a second attempt is a chamber full of members who remember being used. The Accord's witnesses wrote their reports that evening. Hers was the shortest, by design. It stated the date, the sequence, the leave she had been given to enter two rooms, what weight had shown her in the first room in terms of affect sustained over weeks, what its absence of drama had shown her in the second, and the limit on both, plainly, so that no reader could mistake feeling for identity. She signed it with a hand that had stopped shaking an hour earlier and had begun, instead, to feel the honest tiredness that follows the shaking, the tiredness that asks for food and a chair and no more decisions until morning.

Leyden read her report standing up, in his own doorway, as was his habit with documents he did not wish to sit down to, because sitting implied leisure to object. He read it twice. He asked whether the faction would be named anywhere. She said not by her, and not on this evidence, because naming required the kind of proof weight cannot give and paper had not given either, and a named accusation without that proof would hand the faction the injury it needed to survive the vote. He nodded at that, slowly, as a man nods when a junior officer has refused the tempting error he was waiting to correct. He asked whether she was content with the terms. She said nobody was content with the terms, which was the definition of the terms, and that Teshkar staying for two years with its inspections intact and its deficit unpaid as a price was a better outcome than any contentment on offer, because contentment would have meant someone had won the thing that should not be winnable. He folded the report into his case and told her to eat, in the tone handlers use when they mean several other things and have decided, kindly, to say only the one that can be obeyed at once.

In the days that followed, the settlement showed its texture in small things rather than in speeches. An inspection party that had expected refusal was admitted with stiff courtesy and completed its work a day early. A treasury clerk who had confirmed the date in the chamber was not punished, as far as anyone in Carrowmere could learn, and was seen a week later carrying the same files to the same room, which Mira took, perhaps optimistically, as a sign that honesty about a date had not been entered in Teshkar as a crime. The delegation's outer office resumed its ordinary untidiness. Reports reaching the Office spoke of a minister working longer hours than before the vote, as men sometimes do when a danger they did not choose has passed and left them with the bill for its passing. None of it amounted to gratitude, and none of it was owed. States do not thank the witnesses who keep them in a room they wished to leave. They resume, and resumption, after a rehearsed departure, is its own plain evidence that the rehearsal is over.

## The Notch

One notch only, and she was careful, in her own accounting, not to let the settlement in the chamber persuade her that more than one notch had moved.

Rung seventeen stands as this. Harrand has a file on the register, which means oversight was watching the absences before Mira told anyone about them. Not suspecting in the general way that oversight suspects all private notebooks on principle, though it does, but watching in the particular way that leaves a dated page. The earliest page in that file sat within the Session at a point before her own first report, before any conversation in which she had chosen her words and decided how much of the pattern to admit. Someone in oversight had seen enough, early enough, to open a file with her name on the tab and to keep adding to it in a commissioner's own hand. Leyden had spent something to learn that the file existed, and had told her himself rather than let her find it, and she had spent the register itself to answer it, and still the file's earliest date stood where it stood, unmoved by either expenditure, asking its plain question of anyone who laid the dates side by side as she had laid the departure clock side by side. How did oversight know to watch a pattern that had not yet been reported. Who had spoken, or written, or been read, before she had. The question is concrete and it has, for now, a single object to hold it, small enough to be carried in the mind without a notebook. The carried question is the numbered Accord label on the cover of her own notebook.

She signed the statement of giving below the place where Harrand had already signed, and the clerk witnessed both signatures with a care that suggested he was rebuilding, in small acts of correctness, the credit he had spent in the doorway. She bore him no ill will. He had obeyed a paper in a room where the paper's author sat countermanding it, which is a trap any young clerk might fall into once, and his written account had not spared himself, which is rarer than obedience and worth more. If oversight kept him, he would be better for the day, and she said as much, briefly, when he handed her the pen, and watched the misery in his face ease by a fraction that mattered to them both. Harrand herself said nothing at the signing beyond the necessary words. She did not thank Mira. Thanks would have made the giving a favour, and neither of them wanted it entered as a favour. It was a surrender and a correction and a piece of evidence changing custody, and it was allowed to remain all three of those things without decoration. Mira came down the two floors afterwards with her hands empty and her coat light in the wrong place, and she thought about labels, and how a pattern she had carried in secret had acquired, in a single afternoon, a number that other people would use to ask for it by, in rooms she would never enter, for reasons she would not always be told. That is what it means for a private thing to become an Accord thing. It does not become public. It becomes requestable. It acquires a handle by which the institution can lift it without her.


She did not ask to hold it again. Holding it would have made the confirmation a farewell, and farewells invite the very sentiment the procedure had been designed, by both of them, to refuse. She looked, she confirmed, she signed, and she left her hand where it was, at her side, empty and cooling in the ordinary air of the office. She saw it on the last visit she was permitted to make to the oversight floor before the file went to its shelf and the daily list moved on to other names. She had gone up to sign the statement of giving, a short document that recorded the day and the manner of it, and Harrand's clerk, the same clerk, who did not meet her eyes and whose written account of the doorway she had later been shown and had found honest, including his own part, brought the notebook itself to the desk so that she could confirm it was the document she had given and no other. It was. The wear at the corners was hers. The swollen pages were hers. What was not hers was already on it, neat and official and impossible to mistake for anything she would have chosen. The notebook on Harrand's desk, closed, with a numbered Accord label already stuck to the cover.

## Case File

### Q: What instrument must a member state lodge to begin a formal departure from the Accord?

A departure begins only when a member lodges a written instrument with the Accord secretariat in the form prescribed for that purpose. The instrument must identify the member, state the intention to leave in plain terms, and bear the seals and signatures required under the member own procedure. A speech, a press notice, or a resolution debated without lodging has no procedural effect and does not start any clock. Upon receipt the secretariat enters the instrument in the departure register, notifies every other member through the usual channels, and issues a certified copy to the lodging member. From that entry the stated process governs, and no later statement by the member can withdraw the entry except by a further instrument lodged in the same form.

### Q: At what point does a departure motion acquire procedural standing before the assembly?

A motion acquires standing when it has been drafted, tabled, and entered in the assembly order paper in accordance with the rules of the member own house and the Accord terms that recognise such motions. Drafting alone creates a paper but confers no standing, and discussion outside the order paper does not advance the matter. Once entered, the motion may be debated, amended within the limits allowed for departure business, and set down for a vote on a named day within the session. The entry is recorded by the clerk of the house, and that record is the evidence other members and Accord officers rely upon. Any dispute about standing is settled by reference to the order paper and the clerk entry, not by recollection of debate.

### Q: Which notices must a departing member serve on the remaining members before a vote is taken?

Before a vote is taken the departing member must serve notice of the motion, notice of the day fixed for the vote, and notice of the terms it proposes should govern its leaving. Service is made through the secretariat to each member delegation at its recorded address for Accord business, and proof of service is filed with the departure register. A member that has not been served may apply to have the vote deferred until service is complete, and the assembly is expected to grant such an application as a matter of course. The purpose of the rule is plain. No member should learn of a departure from rumour or from public report when a formal channel exists for telling it directly and in writing.

### Q: What obligations continue to bind a member during the interval between notice and vote?

Notice does not suspend membership. During the interval the member remains bound by every obligation it held before notice was given, including payment duties, inspection access, witness attendance, and the keeping of records in the ordinary form. Its officers continue to sit, its delegations continue to be received, and its premises remain subject to the same rights of entry as before. Acts done in the interval to remove papers, empty offices, or place property beyond inspection are recorded as acts of a member still bound, and they may be examined on that footing. If the vote fails or the instrument is withdrawn, no gap in obligation is recognised. If the vote succeeds, the interval is still counted as full membership for every purpose of account.

### Q: How is Teshkar recorded in the Accord roll in respect of its vote and its obligations?

Teshkar is entered in the roll as a member in full standing, with a single vote in matters reserved to members and with the full set of obligations that attach to that standing. The roll records the date of its admission in session terms, the delegations it has accredited, and the instruments it has lodged or received. No annotation of doubt, arrears, or threatened departure alters the entry while membership continues. Officers consulting the roll are directed to treat the entry at face value and to look elsewhere for any question about intention or internal division. The roll is a record of status, not of mood, and it is corrected only when status itself changes by a completed process recognised under the Accord terms.

### Q: What exposure follows for Teshkar from the openness of its assembly record?

The assembly record of a member is open to other members by right, and Teshkar record is no exception. Motions, tabling entries, and the dates attached to them may be inspected, copied, and cited in Accord proceedings without leave from the member concerned. That openness creates a particular exposure. A sequence that is visible on the face of the record, such as a motion drafted before the reason later offered for it was known, cannot be explained away by private assurance. Officers are instructed to rely on the dated record rather than on accounts of what was intended at the time. The exposure is not a penalty. It is the ordinary consequence of conducting departure business in a house whose papers every other member may read.

### Q: Which Accord rights does Teshkar exercise most often, and what does that use disclose?

In ordinary session business Teshkar exercises inspection attendance, witness presence, and access to shared records more often than it exercises any right of refusal or deferral. The pattern of use is kept by the secretariat as part of the routine account of member activity, and it may be consulted by officers with a proper interest in a live matter. Frequent use of witness and inspection rights discloses a member whose administration depends on being present when matters are examined, and whose officers expect to be heard on the record. That dependence is relevant when a departure is proposed, because departure would end the very attendance the member relies upon. The account of use proves habit and reliance. It does not prove intention in any single case.

### Q: How does a packed outer office alter the reading of a ministerial intention to depart?

An outer office found in a state of packing is treated as an administrative fact, not as evidence of a minister decision. Boxes, crates, and removed furnishings show that an order to pack was given and carried out, but they do not show by whom the order was given, for what purpose, or with what expectation about a vote. Officers are directed to record the state of the room, the absence of destination labels, and whether working papers remain in use, and to leave intention to be proved by instruments and dated records. A room packed in advance of any decision may create an appearance of departure that the formal record does not support. That gap between appearance and record is itself a matter for report, stated plainly and without inference.

### Q: On what grounds may Commissioner oversight open a file concerning an officer private keeping?

Oversight may open a file when information in its possession suggests that an officer is keeping a record of official matters outside the authorised filing system. The ground must be stated on the opening page by reference to the source of the information and the nature of the keeping alleged, though names of informants may be withheld under the usual protections. Suspicion arising from a single incidental note is not sufficient. The keeping must appear to be sustained, to concern matters within the officer duties, and to be withheld from the ordinary record. Once those elements are shown, oversight is not required to wait for the officer to report the keeping. The file is opened to preserve the question for examination, not to decide it in advance.

### Q: What must the opening page of an oversight file show before further pages may be added?

The opening page must show the date of opening in session terms, the officer or office concerned, the ground relied upon, and the signature of the Commissioner or of an officer acting with written authority in that behalf. It must also show whether the subject of the file has been informed, and if not, the reason recorded for proceeding without notice. No further page may be added until the opening page is complete, and any page added before completion is struck from the file and noted as irregular. The rule protects both sides. Oversight gains a file whose origin can be tested, and the officer gains a fixed point from which the growth of the file can be measured. Examiners always begin with the opening page.

### Q: How is an oversight file held while the officer concerned remains on active tasking?

While the officer remains on active tasking the file is held in oversight custody, under seal as to its contents, and is not circulated to the officer chain of tasking except by direction of the Commissioner. Tasking officers are informed only that a file exists where that knowledge is necessary to a decision they must take, and they are not given its contents for that purpose. The existence of a file does not suspend tasking, reduce authority, or alter the officer standing in any proceeding. If the file later becomes material to a formal inquiry, its custody and every access to it are proved from the oversight register. Casual inspection by any person outside oversight is forbidden and, if discovered, is itself entered on the file.

### Q: What limits govern oversight in adding material to a file without the officer knowledge?

Material may be added without the officer knowledge only where the material was obtained in the ordinary course of oversight business and where giving notice would defeat the purpose for which the file was opened. Each addition must be dated, sourced, and initialled by the officer who adds it, and no addition may rest on anonymous assertion alone. Oversight may not commission new inquiries into an officer private conduct under cover of an existing file unless the Commissioner records a fresh ground for that inquiry on the file itself. When the officer is later informed, the full sequence of additions must be disclosed in order, so that the officer can see what was known and when it came to be known. Undated additions carry no weight.

### Q: How is a renewed term of membership reckoned when a member elects to remain?

When a member elects to remain after a departure has been proposed and not carried, a renewed term is reckoned from the day of the vote or, where no vote is taken, from the day the instrument is formally withdrawn. The term runs for the full cycle prescribed by the Accord, and no deduction is made for the period during which departure was under discussion. The secretariat enters the reckoning in the roll, notifies all members, and issues a statement of the new term dates in session terms. Obligations that were continuous during the interval continue without interruption into the new term. The reckoning is administrative, not negotiable, and a member may not propose a shorter term as part of the price of remaining.

### Q: What matters are settled for the duration of a term and may not be reopened within it?

A term settles the member standing, its vote, the schedule of payments, the rights of inspection, and the attendance of witnesses for the whole of the cycle. Those matters may not be reopened within the term at the instance of the member or of any other member, except where the Accord expressly provides a procedure for variation and that procedure is followed in full. Dissatisfaction with the terms, however strongly felt, is recorded for consideration at the next renewal and has no effect in the present term. The purpose of the rule is certainty. Administrations on every side plan staff, money, and inspection rounds around a settlement that will hold, and the Accord treats a term as a promise by all parties to let settled matters rest.

### Q: How are terms of remaining recorded so that inspection and payment duties remain certain?

Terms of remaining are recorded in a single instrument held by the secretariat, with certified copies issued to the member concerned and to every office charged with inspection or payment business. The instrument states the term dates, the inspection rights preserved, the payment schedule, and any particular condition agreed at the renewal, in plain language and without annexes that contradict the main text. Offices act on the certified copy in their possession and refer any conflict between copies back to the secretariat for correction. No oral assurance given during negotiation forms part of the terms unless it is written into the instrument before signature. After signature the instrument is the sole authority, and officers are directed to cite it by its register entry in all later papers.

### Q: What happens to unsettled proposals when a new term begins after a departure vote fails?

Proposals that were under discussion but not agreed before the vote do not carry forward into the new term unless the terms instrument expressly preserves them. A proposal for payment, variation of inspection, or change in attendance that failed to secure agreement lapses with the old term, and any party wishing to revive it must lodge it afresh under the ordinary procedure. Officers are warned against treating a lapsed proposal as an understanding. The record of negotiation may be consulted to show what was offered and refused, but it creates no entitlement. This rule prevents a failed departure from leaving behind a residue of half agreed concessions that no assembly approved and no instrument records, and it keeps the new term clean at its start.

### Q: What changes in custody when an officer hands a document to oversight without a taking order?

When a document is handed over voluntarily, custody passes at the moment of handing and is recorded as a giving, not as a taking. No taking order is issued, none is recited in the receipt, and the register entry states that the document came by the officer own act. From that moment oversight holds the document on the same terms as any other paper in its custody, with the same duties of preservation and the same restrictions on circulation. The distinction matters in later proceedings. A document that was given may be characterised as offered for examination, while a document that was taken may be characterised as withheld until compelled. The register entry is the conclusive record of which of those things happened.

### Q: How does voluntary surrender affect the order in which oversight may read a document?

A volunteered document is read in the order in which it presents itself, from its first page to its last, and oversight undertakes to complete a first reading before drawing any conclusion from a part of it. Extracts may be made after that reading for working purposes, but no extract may be circulated as if it were the whole. Where a document was taken under order, oversight may begin with the passages that prompted the order. The difference is deliberate. A giving is met with a complete reading, so that context is preserved and an officer who offers a full record is not prejudiced by selective attention to its hardest pages. The reading officer certifies completion of the first reading on the file before any minute is written.

### Q: What label and entry are applied to a volunteered document on receipt by oversight?

On receipt the document is given a numbered Accord label, fixed to its cover or, where there is no cover, to its first page in a position that does not obscure any writing. The number is taken from the oversight sequence and is entered in the register with the date of receipt in session terms, a short description of the document, and the name of the officer who gave it. A receipt bearing the same number is issued to that officer and retained by her as proof of the giving. No other label, mark, or annotation may be added to the document itself. Working comments belong on separate minutes filed alongside it, so that the document as given can always be distinguished from what oversight later thought about it.

### Q: What rights of access does an officer retain to a document after voluntary surrender to oversight?

After surrender the officer retains a right to inspect the document in oversight custody, at reasonable times and in the presence of an oversight clerk, and to take notes from it for the purpose of any answer she is required to make. She does not retain a right to remove it, to copy it in full, or to lend her access to any other person. Requests for inspection are entered in the register, and refusal of a request must be reasoned in writing by the Commissioner. The original remains an oversight paper for as long as the file to which it belongs remains open. When that file is closed, its disposition is decided under the ordinary rules for closed oversight papers and recorded in the same register.
