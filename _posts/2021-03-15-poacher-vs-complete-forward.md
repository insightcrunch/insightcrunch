The argument about the poacher and the complete forward has been running for as long as people have written about football, and it keeps running because almost nobody involved defines the terms first. One person means a forward who does not track back. Another means a forward who scores tap-ins. A third means a specialist whose value is concentrated in the penalty area. A fourth is using the word as an insult. Four people using one word for four things will argue indefinitely and settle nothing, which is exactly what has happened.

This article does something narrower and more useful. It defines both archetypes precisely, resolves the comparison on four axes that a decision-maker can actually act on, and then gives four separate verdicts, because the honest answer differs by level, by style, and by the quality of service a side can generate. A coach at an elite club and a coach running a Sunday side are asking the same question and need opposite answers, and any article that gives them one answer has failed both.

![Poacher or complete forward, the archetype decision grid for coaches and scouts - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-93.webp)

Before anything else, a statement that the rest of the article depends on. Describing a forward as a poacher is a description of role concentration. It is not a judgment about his ability, his effort, his intelligence, or his worth, and the word is used pejoratively in ordinary football conversation in a way this article does not import. Every forward used as an illustration here is a real person and is treated as a subject of study rather than as a label.

## Defining the two archetypes properly

A poacher is a forward whose value is concentrated inside the penalty area and whose contribution outside it is minimal by design. The phrase by design matters: this is a role choice and a positional habit, not an inability. His output is finishing. His movement is oriented around arrival rather than involvement. His touch count is low and his touch location is extreme.

A complete forward is one whose value is distributed across finishing, link play, and chance creation. He receives under pressure and retains the ball, he brings others into play, he contributes to the side's progression up the pitch, and he finishes. His output is spread across several columns rather than concentrated in one.

Both of those are ends of a spectrum rather than categories, and this is the single most important sentence in the article. Almost no real forward sits at either extreme. Real forwards are distributed along a line between concentration and distribution, and the useful question about any individual is where on that line he sits and whether his position on it suits the side he plays in.

The reason the spectrum framing matters practically is that it changes the question from which type is better, which is unanswerable, to how much concentration can my side support, which has an answer that a coach can work out in an afternoon.

There is a second axis hiding inside the first that is worth separating out, because conflating the two causes most of the confusion in scouting language. Concentration of value and quality of finishing are independent. A forward can be highly concentrated and a poor finisher, which makes him nearly useless. He can be highly distributed and an excellent finisher, which is the profile everybody wants and almost nobody can afford. The archetype tells you where the value sits, not how much of it there is.

### Is a poacher or a complete forward harder to defend?

The poacher, in the specific moment of the chance; the complete forward, across the whole match. A concentrated forward creates a duel in the one area where defensive tools do not exist. A distributed forward creates more problems in more places, none of them individually as difficult, and forces a defence to solve several questions rather than one.

## The four axes that actually decide it

Most versions of this argument compare the two archetypes on quality, which is not a comparison at all. The four axes below are the ones that change a decision.

The first axis is how much of the side's chance creation must come from elsewhere. A concentrated forward creates almost nothing, so every chance he finishes was manufactured by somebody else. That is a demand placed on the other ten players, and it is a large one. A distributed forward generates a share of his own supply and a share of other people's, which reduces the creation burden on the rest of the side.

The second axis is how much the archetype constrains the rest of the eleven. A concentrated forward is not available as an outlet under pressure, does not participate in build-up, and does not press the first line effectively in most versions of the role. Those absences have to be covered, which shapes the selection of the midfield, the wide players, and sometimes the defensive line's willingness to play out. A distributed forward constrains the side far less and in some systems actively enables it.

The third axis is how the archetype's value changes as the quality of service falls. This is the axis that decides everything below elite level and the one that almost no discussion includes. A concentrated forward's output is a function of deliveries into the penalty area: reduce the quality and volume of those deliveries and his output falls sharply, because he has no alternative route to a chance. A distributed forward's output falls too, but less sharply, because he can drop, receive, carry, and manufacture something from a possession that would otherwise have produced nothing.

The fourth axis is how each ages. A concentrated forward's game depends on anticipation, positioning, and short-range movement, all of which decline slowly. A distributed forward's game depends on ball retention under pressure, carrying, and repeated involvement across the pitch, several of which depend on physical qualities that decline faster. This axis runs in the concentrated forward's favour and it is usually left out of the argument entirely.

## The service-quality threshold

Here is the claim this article advances. Below a certain standard of delivery into the penalty area, the poacher's concentration bet stops paying and the complete forward becomes the better choice. Call it the service-quality threshold. Its consequence is uncomfortable and is the reason the article exists: the archetype that dominates elite football is the wrong recommendation for almost every amateur side, and the recommendation gets copied downward anyway.

The mechanism is straightforward once stated. A concentrated forward converts a share of the deliveries that reach him. His output is therefore the product of delivery volume, delivery quality, and his conversion rate. Two of those three belong to other people. If delivery volume and quality are high, his conversion rate is applied to a large and favourable base and produces a lot of goals, which is why the profile thrives in strong sides. If delivery volume and quality are low, the same conversion rate is applied to almost nothing, and a forward who is genuinely excellent at his job produces very little, because his job is not available to him.

A distributed forward's output has a different structure. Some of it comes from the same base of deliveries, and some of it comes from possessions he creates himself by dropping, receiving, retaining, and carrying. That second component is not dependent on the quality of the side around him in the same way, which means it survives a fall in service quality that would eliminate the first component entirely.

The threshold is the point at which the second component becomes larger than the difference in finishing concentration. Above it, take the concentration. Below it, take the distribution.

Where is the threshold in practice? It cannot be stated as a number, and any article claiming otherwise is inventing one. It can be assessed directly, and the assessment is the most useful thing a coach can take from this piece. Count, over several matches, how many times your side delivers a ball into the opposition penalty area in a state that a forward could finish first time. Not crosses attempted. Deliveries that arrive at a finishable height in a finishable place. Most amateur sides, counted honestly, produce a handful per match, and many produce fewer.

A side producing a handful of finishable deliveries per match cannot support a concentrated forward, because he will touch the ball four times and the side will have spent ninety minutes playing with ten players. The same side playing a distributed forward gets a player who receives thirty times, keeps the ball, brings midfielders into the game, and manufactures chances that would not otherwise exist.

### Which forward type needs more service?

The poacher, by a wide margin and by design. His output is the product of delivery volume, delivery quality, and his own conversion rate, and two of those three belong to his teammates. A complete forward generates part of his own supply by dropping, receiving, and carrying, so his output survives a fall in service quality far better.

## Which is harder to defend, properly answered

The defensive question is the one most people actually mean when they start this argument, so it deserves a full answer rather than a slogan.

The concentrated forward is harder in the moment. Everything in the article on defending the six-yard box applies: inside that space a defender has no jockeying, no delay, no showing him wide, and no cover, and a forward who lives there has restricted the contest to the ground where defending does not work. A defence facing him will lose more of the individual duels that matter, because those duels are close to unwinnable by defending.

The distributed forward is harder across a match. He appears in more places, each of which requires a different defensive response, and a defence that solves one has not solved the others. He drops and a centre-back has to decide whether to follow. He receives wide and a full-back has to decide whether to engage. He carries and a midfielder has to decide whether to press. Each decision is individually manageable and the accumulation is not, because a defence that gets three of five right still concedes.

Those two forms of difficulty are not on the same scale and cannot be added. Which one is worse depends entirely on the defending side.

A well-organised side with a good structure and poor individual box defending finds the concentrated forward far harder, because its structure will do its job, deliver the ball into a crowded area, and then fail in the one place its organisation does not reach.

A side with excellent individual defenders and a poor collective structure finds the distributed forward far harder, because he will find the seams the structure cannot close and its good defenders will spend the match arriving after the decision has already been made.

The practical instruction for a coach is therefore to answer the question about his own side rather than about the forwards. Audit where your goals are conceded. If they cluster inside twelve metres from crosses, rebounds, and scrambles, the concentrated forward is your problem and the last-square protocol is your fix. If they cluster from build-up breakdowns, from balls into the pocket, and from carries, the distributed forward is your problem and your structure needs the work.

### Which forward type causes a back four more problems?

The concentrated forward causes a back four more problems inside the penalty area, where its tools do not function. The distributed forward causes it more problems everywhere else, by forcing a chain of decisions about following, engaging, and pressing. Which is worse depends on whether the back four's weakness is individual or structural.

## Is a poacher easier to mark inside a low block?

This question is asked constantly and the intuition behind it is exactly backward, which makes it worth answering carefully.

The intuition says that a low block puts many bodies in a small space, that a forward who only operates in that space is therefore surrounded, and that he must be easier to mark than a forward who roams.

The reality is that a low block manufactures the conditions a concentrated forward exists to exploit. The block forces play wide, which produces deliveries into a crowded area. The crowd removes defenders' ability to see the ball and the man at once, which is an information advantage for the forward, who only has to track the ball. The crowd produces deflections, blocks, and half-clearances, which are the events a rebound specialist converts. And a compressed block clears the ball badly, because every player is facing his own goal in a small space, so the ball stays in the area and the sequence resets.

The forward who is genuinely easier to mark in a low block is the distributed one, because his value depends on receiving in the spaces the block has removed. A block that has closed the pocket, denied the drop, and compressed the pitch has taken away the reception points he needs and has left him operating in front of the shape rather than inside it, which is harmless.

That inversion is one of the more useful practical findings in this whole cluster, and it points at a broader lesson: the defensive shape that reduces one archetype amplifies the other, which means the choice of shape has to be made with reference to the specific forward rather than as a general preference.

### Is a poacher easier to mark inside a low block?

No, and the intuition is backward. A low block forces play wide, produces deliveries into a crowded area, generates deflections and rebounds, and clears badly because everyone is facing their own goal. Those are precisely the conditions a concentrated forward exists to convert. The distributed forward is the one a low block reduces.

## Which type suits a counter-attacking side?

The answer here is more nuanced than the usual one and depends on what kind of counter-attack the side runs.

A side that counters through space, breaking at speed into a disorganised defence with players running beyond the ball, needs a forward who can run, receive on the move, and finish while travelling. Neither archetype describes that directly, though a distributed forward is closer, because carrying and receiving under pressure while moving forward are distributed skills.

A side that counters through directness, playing early into the final third and attacking second balls and knockdowns, is better served by a concentrated forward, because the chances that pattern produces are penalty-area chances arriving from chaos, and chaos in the box is where concentration pays.

A side that counters by absorbing pressure and then delivering long into a lone forward, which is what most amateur sides actually do when they say they counter, needs a distributed forward and specifically one who can hold the ball, because the entire pattern depends on a single player retaining possession alone against defenders while support arrives. A concentrated forward in that role is not a counter-attacking outlet; he is a player waiting for a delivery that his side is not organised to provide.

The general point is that the phrase counter-attacking side describes at least three different patterns with three different forward requirements, and the argument goes in circles partly because people use one phrase for all three.

### Which forward type suits a counter-attacking side?

It depends which kind of counter. A side that breaks at speed into space needs receiving and carrying under pressure, which favours the distributed forward. A side that plays direct and attacks second balls in the area is better served by concentration. A side that counters by holding a long ball alone needs retention, which the concentrated forward does not offer.

## Which type suits a possession side?

The possession answer inverts the common assumption in an interesting way.

The usual claim is that possession sides need complete forwards, because they need a player who can receive under pressure, link with midfielders, and participate in circulation. That is true of the build-up phase and it is only half the story.

A possession side's problem is not building; it is finishing against a compact defence that has been given time to organise. Sustained possession produces a settled low block, and a settled low block produces exactly the delivery pattern described above: wide entries, cutbacks, crowded areas, deflections, and rebounds. Those are penalty-area chances, and converting them is a concentration skill.

The genuinely strong possession sides tend to resolve this by carrying a distributed forward and adding penalty-area occupation from elsewhere, usually from wide players cutting in and midfielders arriving late. That is a way of buying concentration without spending the forward position on it, and it is the most sophisticated answer available.

A possession side that cannot do that, because its wide players and midfielders do not attack the box, needs the concentration in the forward position, and the build-up cost has to be absorbed by a midfielder dropping to provide the outlet instead.

So the correct answer is that a possession side needs distribution in the build-up phase and concentration in the finishing phase, and the question is only where each comes from. Putting both in the same player is the ideal and is rarely available.

### Which forward type suits a possession side?

Both, in different phases, which is why the question is usually answered badly. A possession side needs distribution to build and concentration to finish against the settled block its own possession creates. Strong sides buy the concentration from wide players and late-arriving midfielders and keep distribution in the forward position.

## Is the poacher extinct in modern football?

This is the loudest of the two dominant positions and it is overstated rather than wrong, so the useful work is identifying exactly which conditions make it true.

The claim's strongest support is real. Modern top-level forwards are expected to press, to receive under pressure, and to participate in build-up, and a player who does none of those is a significant tactical cost to a side that presses high and plays out from the back. Recruitment reflects that expectation, and a forward whose profile is extreme concentration will be filtered out by most elite recruitment processes before anyone assesses his finishing.

The claim's weakness is that it describes selection rather than effectiveness, and those get conflated constantly. A profile that is not selected is not a profile that has been shown not to work; it is a profile whose costs have been judged too high for a particular set of systems. Change the system and the judgment changes with it.

There is also a definitional sleight of hand in the claim. The extreme version of the archetype, a forward who does nothing at all outside the box, is rare at elite level and probably always was. The moderate version, a forward whose value is heavily concentrated in the penalty area and who contributes modestly outside it, is extremely common at elite level and is often the highest-scoring player in a major league. Declaring the archetype extinct requires defining it so strictly that almost nobody has ever qualified.

The honest verdict is that extreme concentration is rare at elite level and is a system-fit problem rather than a quality problem, that moderate concentration is thriving, and that the death of the poacher is a claim about a definition rather than about football.

There is a further point that runs the other way and is worth stating. Modern defending has developed enormously outside the penalty area and barely at all inside it, which means the value of penalty-area specialism has arguably risen relative to everything else. A player whose entire skill set operates in the one space defences have not engineered is exploiting a market inefficiency, not a historical curiosity.

### Does a poacher still have a place in modern football?

Yes, though the extreme version is rare and is filtered out by elite recruitment because a forward who does not press or link imposes a real system cost. Moderate concentration is thriving. And since defending has developed far more outside the penalty area than inside it, box specialism arguably matters more now, not less.

## Is a complete forward always more valuable than a poacher?

The mirror position, that the complete forward is simply the superior product and the poacher is a lesser version, deserves the same treatment.

It is wrong for three reasons that are worth separating.

The first is the service threshold already argued. Below a certain level of supply the complete forward is more valuable, and above it the concentrated forward frequently is not less valuable but more, because at high supply volumes finishing concentration compounds and distribution does not add much that the side does not already have.

The second is squad cost. A complete forward is one of the most expensive profiles in football, both to buy and to develop, because he requires a rare combination of finishing and retention. A concentrated forward is cheaper by every measure. A side that spends its entire budget on a distributed forward and cannot afford the service around him has bought the wrong thing, and a side that buys concentration cheaply and invests the difference in creation has often bought better.

The third is that value is contextual rather than absolute. A forward's value is what he adds to a specific eleven, and a side that already has creation, retention, and pressing from its midfield and wide areas adds nothing by having them again in the forward position. In that side, concentration is the missing quality and distribution is duplication.

The legitimate version of the complete forward's advantage is robustness. He is the safer choice when a coach does not know what the side around him will look like, when the service is unpredictable, or when the squad is likely to change. Robustness is a real virtue and it is not the same thing as superiority.

### Is a complete forward always more valuable than a poacher?

No. Above a certain level of service, concentration compounds and distribution duplicates qualities the side already has. A complete forward is also markedly more expensive to buy and develop. His genuine advantage is robustness, which is a different thing from being better and matters most when the side around him is unpredictable.

## Which type ages better?

This axis is the one most consistently left out of the argument and it runs clearly in one direction.

A concentrated forward's game is built on anticipation, positional reading, short-range movement, balance in contact, and finishing technique. Anticipation and reading improve with experience and decline late. Short-range movement declines slowly. Finishing technique is close to permanent. The result is that a forward of this type frequently remains effective long after his physical peak, provided the service continues, and the qualifier matters because he is dependent on the side around him at every stage.

A distributed forward's game is built on retention under pressure, carrying, repeated involvement across large areas, and pressing volume. Retention under pressure depends on strength and balance, which hold reasonably well. Carrying and repeated involvement depend on repeated acceleration and total distance, which decline earlier and more visibly. The result is that this profile tends to change shape with age, usually by concentrating, which is worth noticing: the ageing complete forward frequently becomes something closer to the other archetype.

That last observation is the most interesting thing on this axis and it undercuts the hierarchy framing completely. If distributed forwards routinely become concentrated ones as they age, and remain valuable while doing so, then concentration cannot be a lesser form of forward play. It is the form that survives.

The practical consequence for recruitment is that a concentrated forward is a longer asset at the same age, and the practical consequence for a coach is that the plan around an ageing distributed forward has to change, because the outlet and retention functions he used to provide have to be relocated to somebody else.

### Which forward type ages better?

The concentrated forward, and the margin is not small. Anticipation, positional reading, short-range movement, and finishing technique all decline late. Retention, carrying, and repeated high-volume involvement decline sooner, which is why distributed forwards frequently become more concentrated with age rather than less.

## Which type puts more defenders inside the penalty area?

This is a tactical question rather than a preference question, and the answer has consequences for the rest of the attacking side.

A concentrated forward pulls defenders into the area and keeps them there. A marker cannot leave him, the covering defender cannot step up while he is occupying the space in front of the goalkeeper, and a side defending against him ends up with more bodies deep than it wants. That is a cost to the defending side, and it is also a benefit to the attacking side that is frequently overlooked: defenders who are pinned deep are defenders who are not stepping into midfield, which gives the attacking side's midfielders time on the ball.

A distributed forward does the opposite. By dropping and moving wide he draws defenders out of the area, which creates space inside it for others but reduces the pinning effect. A side built around a distributed forward has more space in the box and fewer players naturally positioned to use it, which is why such sides need midfielders and wide players who attack the area.

Neither of those is better. They are different mechanisms with different requirements, and the requirement is the thing to check. A side with midfielders who arrive in the box benefits from a forward who draws defenders out. A side whose midfielders do not arrive needs a forward who occupies the box himself, because otherwise nobody is there.

### Which forward type puts more defenders inside the penalty area?

The concentrated forward, and it is one of his underrated contributions. He pins a marker and a covering defender deep, which keeps them out of midfield and gives his own midfielders time. A distributed forward draws defenders out of the box instead, which creates space that only works if other players attack it.

## The two illustrations that define the ends of the spectrum

Rather than re-analysing forwards who have their own articles, it is worth naming the two clearest illustrations of each end and pointing at what they demonstrate.

At the concentrated end sits the forward whose entire game happened inside the penalty area and especially inside the six-yard box, whose tools were a low base, a turn requiring no room, anticipation of the second ball, and finishing with any surface. The defensive difficulty he created is documented at length in [the article on defending the six-yard box against a pure box forward](/2021/03/01/stopping-gerd-muller/), and the relevant point for this argument is that the difficulty was real, structural, and unanswerable by better defending. That is the strongest available evidence that concentration is a genuine tactical weapon rather than a lesser form of participation.

At the distributed end sits the forward who ran the channels, dropped wide to receive, carried the ball at speed from deep positions, created for others, and finished. The defensive difficulty he created is documented in [the article on how defenders planned against a wide-drifting complete forward](/2020/12/01/stopping-thierry-henry/), and the relevant point here is different: the difficulty was spread across the pitch, and a defence could reduce it substantially by making a series of correct structural decisions. It was a harder overall problem and a more solvable one.

That contrast is the argument in miniature. Concentration produces a smaller number of nearly unsolvable moments. Distribution produces a larger number of solvable ones. Which is worse for a defence depends on whether the defence is better at moments or at structures, and which is better for an attacking side depends on whether it can manufacture the moments.

The broader era context, including how defensive cultures across different periods adapted to both kinds of forward, is set out in [the cluster's overview of how defenders coped with the greatest strikers](/2020/11/01/greatest-strikers-how-defenders-coped/), and the modern category-level version of the defensive question belongs to [the complete guide to stopping elite strikers](/2020/01/01/stopping-elite-strikers-complete-guide/) rather than to this article.

## Verdict one: the elite side with elite service

For a side that generates a high volume of high-quality deliveries into the penalty area, the recommendation is concentration, and the deciding factor is that at high service volume finishing concentration converts better than distribution adds.

The reasoning is arithmetic rather than aesthetic. A side producing a large number of finishable deliveries per match has already solved creation. Adding a forward who also creates adds a quality the side already possesses in surplus, and the marginal return on the eleventh creator is low. Adding a forward who converts the existing supply at a higher rate applies an improvement to a large base, and the marginal return is high.

The costs are real and are affordable at this level. The pressing cost can be absorbed by a side whose midfield and wide players press well. The build-up cost can be absorbed by a side whose defenders and midfielders are comfortable playing out without a forward outlet. The constraint on the other ten is a constraint an elite squad has the players to satisfy.

There is one condition that overturns this recommendation, and a coach should check it before accepting the verdict. If the side's pressing scheme depends on the forward specifically, because it presses from the front in a way that requires the first defender to set the trap, then a concentrated forward breaks the scheme and the calculation changes entirely. That is not a small exception; it describes a large share of modern elite football.

So the fuller version of the verdict is this: concentration for an elite side with elite service and a pressing scheme that does not depend on the forward. Distribution for an elite side whose pressing scheme does. The service quality is not the only variable at this level, because at this level the side can afford to have other requirements.

## Verdict two: the mid-table side with inconsistent service

For a side whose delivery volume and quality fluctuate, the recommendation splits, and the deciding factor is where the side's creation actually comes from.

If the side creates primarily from wide areas, through crosses, cutbacks, and overloads, concentration is viable even with inconsistent service, because wide creation produces penalty-area chances by its nature and a forward who occupies the box will get opportunities in the matches where the pattern works. The variance is high, the profile will disappear in some fixtures, and across a season the return is acceptable.

If the side creates primarily through central possession, working the ball through midfield and looking for combinations around the area, distribution is the better choice, because that pattern requires a forward who participates in the combinations. A concentrated forward inside a central-creation side is a spectator for most of the build-up and a target for a pattern the side is not running.

The second consideration at this level is squad robustness. A mid-table side loses players to injury, suspension, and transfer more disruptively than an elite one, and a concentrated forward's output collapses when the specific creators who supply him are unavailable. A distributed forward is less dependent on particular teammates, which is worth a great deal to a side that cannot guarantee its best eleven.

The practical recommendation for most mid-table sides is therefore distribution as the first-choice profile and concentration as the second, because the variance reduction is worth more than the conversion premium at a level where consistency is the constraint.

## Verdict three: the amateur side with poor service

For a side playing at amateur level, with limited training time and inconsistent delivery, the recommendation is distribution and it is not close. The deciding factor is that below a certain service threshold, a concentrated forward starves.

This is the verdict that matters most, because it is the one most often ignored. Amateur coaches watch elite football, observe that the highest scorers in strong leagues are frequently penalty-area specialists, and conclude that the way to score more goals is to find a player who stays in the box. Then they play him in a side that delivers three finishable balls into the area per match, and he touches the ball four times and is substituted for not working hard enough.

The arithmetic that made concentration correct at elite level runs in reverse here. A high conversion rate applied to almost no supply produces almost nothing. Meanwhile the side has spent its most advanced position on a player who cannot receive the long ball under pressure, cannot hold it while support arrives, and cannot relieve the defence when it is under sustained pressure, all of which are things an amateur side needs from its forward far more often than it needs a finish from six metres.

The specific profile an amateur side should want is a forward who can receive a difficult ball with his back to goal, keep it, and either win a foul or lay it off. That single capability transforms an amateur side's ability to escape its own half, and escaping its own half is the main problem most amateur sides have. Finishing is the second problem and it cannot be reached until the first is solved.

The counterintuitive corollary is that an amateur side with a genuinely excellent penalty-area finisher should probably not play him as its lone forward. It should play him alongside or behind a retaining forward if it can, or accept that his output will be low and value him for the chances he does convert rather than expecting volume.

A coach working out which side of this threshold his own team sits on can record the count of finishable deliveries alongside his match notes and session plans, and keeping that record in one place, such as the [free opposition and match planning workspace on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.org/tools/football-match-planner.html), makes the assessment a matter of evidence rather than of impression after a couple of months.

### Which forward type is harder for an amateur defence?

The distributed forward, because amateur defences are structurally weak rather than individually weak, and a forward who receives in midfield, carries, and combines exploits structural weakness continuously. A concentrated forward at that level depends on service most amateur attacking sides cannot produce, so he rarely gets the chance to punish anyone.

## Verdict four: the young player deciding what to develop

For a young forward, the recommendation is neither archetype exclusively, and the deciding factor is that box skills are perishable while link skills are cumulative.

The reasoning rests on a distinction that youth coaching almost never makes. Penalty-area skills, which are anticipation, arrival timing, reading the deliverer, finishing with any surface, and holding position under contact, are refined through repetition and they hold their value indefinitely once acquired. A player who develops them at seventeen still has them at thirty-two, and a player who develops them at twenty-four is not meaningfully behind.

Link skills, which are receiving under pressure with the correct body shape, retaining the ball against contact, scanning before receiving, and combining in tight areas, are built cumulatively and are far harder to acquire late. A player who has not developed the habit of scanning before he receives by his late teens will struggle to build it afterwards, because it is a perceptual habit rather than a technique.

The developmental sequence that follows is clear. Build the link skills early, because the window for them is narrower. Build the box skills continuously throughout, because they accumulate and never expire. Do not specialise into concentration before the physical and technical picture has settled, because the player who concentrates at sixteen has closed the door on the profile that ages into concentration anyway.

There is a second reason not to specialise early, which is about opportunity rather than development. A young forward with link skills can play in more sides, more systems, and more positions, which means more football, and more football is the largest single input into development. A young forward who can only occupy a penalty area is dependent on being selected by a coach whose side generates penalty-area chances, which at youth level is uncommon.

The one caveat that youth coaches should hear is that this argument is sometimes used to over-coach the box instinct out of players. A young forward who naturally arrives in the right place should not be dragged into midfield in the name of completeness. The instruction is to add link skills, not to subtract box ones, and coaches who make a naturally concentrated player spend his development years receiving to feet in midfield have taken away his best quality in exchange for a mediocre version of somebody else's.

### Should a young striker develop as a poacher or a complete forward?

Develop link skills first and box skills continuously, because link skills are built cumulatively and are hard to acquire late, while penalty-area skills accumulate and never expire. Do not specialise into concentration early, and do not coach the box instinct out of a player who already has it.

## The label trap in scouting

The scouting-language problem deserves separate treatment, because the word itself causes players to be misjudged in both directions and it is the most avoidable error in this whole area.

The first failure is that the label gets applied on the basis of statistics that measure involvement rather than value. A forward with a low touch count, a low pass count, and a high share of touches in the penalty area gets labelled, and the label then travels ahead of him into every subsequent assessment. Nobody rechecks whether he can receive under pressure, because the label has already answered the question.

The second failure is that the label carries a pejorative charge in ordinary football language. Calling a forward a poacher implies, in most dressing rooms and most media coverage, that he does not work, does not contribute, and scores easy goals. A scout who uses the word is importing that judgment whether or not he intends to, and the player is downgraded for a role description.

The third failure is that the label is applied to output rather than to capability, which are different things. A forward playing in a side that delivers constantly into the area will produce a concentrated output profile regardless of what else he can do, because the chances he receives are penalty-area chances. Judging his archetype from that data is judging his team.

The corrective is to assess capability directly rather than inferring it from output. Watch him receive with his back to goal under pressure and note whether he keeps the ball. Watch him in the phase of play where his side is under sustained pressure and note whether he is available as an outlet. Watch him when his side is losing and having to build, and note whether he participates. Those three observations answer the capability question in a way no statistical profile can, and they take one match rather than a season.

The second corrective is linguistic and it is worth adopting. Describe forwards by where their value sits rather than by archetype nouns. A forward whose value is concentrated in the penalty area is a clearer and more neutral description than a poacher, it invites the follow-up question about why, and it does not travel with a judgment attached.

### Is the poacher label still useful for scouting?

Only as shorthand, and it does more harm than good in practice. The label is applied from involvement statistics, carries a pejorative charge that travels with the player, and describes output rather than capability, which means it frequently describes the team rather than the forward. Assess capability directly instead, in one match.

## Which type is harder to replace in a squad?

This is a practical question for anyone building a squad rather than picking a team, and the answer is not the obvious one.

The intuitive answer is the complete forward, because he is rarer and more expensive. That is true of the market and it is not the same as being harder to replace within a squad, which is a question about what happens on the Saturday after he is unavailable.

A distributed forward's functions can be redistributed. His retention can be covered by a midfielder dropping into the position. His creation can be covered by wide players and by the midfield taking more responsibility. His pressing can be covered by adjusting the trigger so the press starts a line deeper. None of the substitutes is as good, and all of them are available, which means a side missing a distributed forward is a worse version of itself rather than a different one.

A concentrated forward's function cannot be redistributed easily, because occupying the penalty area at the right moment is not something a midfielder or a wide player can simply be asked to do. A side that loses its box occupant and replaces him with a distributed forward has changed the pattern its whole attacking structure was built around, because the wide players are crossing to a player who is no longer there. The delivery pattern has to change, and delivery patterns are rehearsed rather than instructed.

So the answer inverts the market logic. The complete forward is harder to acquire and easier to cover. The concentrated forward is easier to acquire and harder to cover, because his replacement has to be another concentrated forward or the side has to change how it attacks.

The squad-building consequence is that a side committed to concentration should carry a second concentrated forward rather than a backup who plays differently, and a side committed to distribution has more freedom in its second choice.

### Which forward type is harder to replace in a squad?

The concentrated forward, which inverts the market logic. A distributed forward's functions can be redistributed to midfielders and wide players, producing a worse version of the same side. A box occupant's function cannot, because the delivery patterns the whole attack rehearsed are now aimed at a player who is not there.

## Does one archetype win more contests?

This question, phrased as whether one type of forward wins more matches, is the most common form the argument takes on social media and it is close to unanswerable in the form it is asked, so it is worth explaining why and then answering the useful version.

The unanswerable version compares outcomes across different sides, different leagues, different eras, and different systems, and attributes the difference to the forward. Sides win matches for dozens of reasons and the forward is one of them, which means any comparison of win rates between archetypes is measuring squad quality rather than archetype.

The useful version is narrower: within the same side, at the same level, with the same service, which archetype produces more points? That version can be reasoned about even if it cannot be measured cleanly.

The answer is that concentration produces more goals in matches that are already going well and distribution produces more points in matches that are going badly. A side that is dominant, creating chances, and delivering into the area converts more of them with a concentrated forward. A side that is under pressure, unable to build, and struggling to get out of its own half needs an outlet, and an outlet is a distribution function.

Since points are lost more often in difficult matches than they are gained in comfortable ones, and since most sides have more difficult matches than comfortable ones, the practical conclusion for most sides is that distribution produces more points and concentration produces more goals. Those are different objectives and a coach should know which one he is optimising for.

The exception is a side that is genuinely dominant most weeks, which is a small number of sides, and for which the calculation runs the other way. That exception is elite football, which is where the recommendation to prefer concentration comes from, and it is why the recommendation should not be copied by anyone else.

### Does a poacher or a complete forward win more contests?

Within the same side, concentration produces more goals in matches that are already going well and distribution produces more points in matches that are going badly, because a struggling side needs an outlet more than it needs a finisher. Since most sides have more difficult matches than comfortable ones, distribution wins more for most teams.

## Which forward type should a coach build around?

The building question is different from the picking question and deserves a separate answer, because building around a forward means shaping the other ten positions to serve him and that is a commitment rather than a selection.

Building around concentration means committing to a delivery system. It requires wide players who deliver rather than dribble, midfielders who arrive for second balls rather than sitting, a rehearsed pattern for entering the final third, and a willingness to accept that the side will look less impressive in possession because its forward is not part of it. The payoff is that the pattern is simple, repeatable, and coachable, which matters enormously at levels below professional football where complexity does not survive.

Building around distribution means committing to a support system. It requires runners beyond the ball, because a forward who drops leaves the space behind the defence empty and somebody has to attack it. It requires a midfield that can play forward into pressure. It requires wide players who attack the penalty area rather than only crossing into it. The payoff is flexibility and resilience, and the cost is that the pattern is more complex and takes longer to install.

The decision therefore turns on training time as much as on personnel, and this is the consideration most discussions miss entirely. A side that trains four times a week can install a support system. A side that trains once a week probably cannot, and should build around the simpler pattern, which is the delivery system, unless its service quality is too poor to sustain one, in which case it should not build around a forward at all and should instead build around whatever it does well.

That last option is worth stating explicitly because it is frequently the right answer and is rarely offered. Not every side should build around a forward. A side whose strength is its midfield should build around its midfield and select a forward who fits, which usually means a distributed one, because distribution integrates with a good midfield and concentration requires the midfield to serve it.

### Which forward type should a coach build around?

Concentration requires a delivery system, which is simpler and more coachable with limited training time. Distribution requires a support system with runners beyond the ball, which is more flexible and takes longer to install. Training time decides it as much as personnel, and many sides should build around their midfield instead.

## How to measure your own service quality

The service-quality threshold is only useful if a coach can locate his own side relative to it, so the method deserves a full description rather than a mention.

The count is of finishable deliveries, and the definition has to be strict or the exercise is worthless. A finishable delivery is a ball that arrives inside the penalty area, at a height and pace a forward could strike or head first time, in a position from which a first-time contact would trouble a goalkeeper. A cross that is cut out is not one. A cross that clears everybody is not one. A ball into the area that a forward has to control before doing anything is not one. A hopeful punt is not one.

Count them across five matches. Do not count from memory and do not count while managing, because both produce inflated numbers. Count from video if it exists, or have somebody on the touchline whose only job that afternoon is the tally.

The number that comes back is usually far lower than expected, and the surprise is the point of the exercise. A side that produces a genuinely finishable delivery every ten minutes is producing nine per match, which is enough to support a concentrated forward. A side producing three per match is not, and no amount of instruction to its forward will change that, because the constraint is not in the forward position.

Two refinements make the count more useful. Record the origin of each finishable delivery, because a side that produces all of them from one pattern has a fragile supply that disappears when that pattern is denied, and a concentrated forward inside a fragile supply is a high-variance bet. And record whether the delivery arrived when a forward was actually in the box, because a side that produces good deliveries into an empty area has a different problem entirely, which is an occupation problem rather than a supply one, and it is solved by the archetype rather than by the count.

That last case is worth naming because it is common and gets diagnosed backward. A coach whose wide players deliver well into an empty penalty area concludes that the crossing is poor. The crossing may be fine and the box may be empty, and the fix is a forward who occupies rather than a delivery that is better.

The count also answers a question that has nothing to do with archetypes, which is whether the side's problem is creation or conversion. A side with a high finishable-delivery count and a low goal count has a conversion problem and should look at its forward. A side with a low count and a low goal count has a creation problem and should leave its forward alone. Most sides blame the forward without ever establishing which of the two they have.

## Does playing both solve it?

The obvious response to a trade-off is to refuse it, and playing a concentrated forward alongside a distributed one is a genuine option rather than a dodge, so it deserves an honest assessment.

The pairing works, and it works for a specific reason: the two archetypes have complementary rather than overlapping demands. The distributed forward provides the outlet, the retention, and the link, which removes the concentrated forward's main cost to the side. The concentrated forward provides the box occupation, which removes the distributed forward's main gap. Each covers the other's deficiency, which is the definition of a good pairing.

The costs are equally specific. Two forwards means one fewer midfielder or one fewer wide player, and the player lost is usually the one who was providing the delivery or the defensive cover. A side that adds a second forward to solve its box occupation and thereby loses the wide player who was crossing into the box has traded a solution for the problem it was solving.

The second cost is out of possession. Two forwards is a harder defensive shape to organise than one, particularly against a side that builds with three at the back, and the pressing structure has to be genuinely rehearsed rather than improvised. Sides that pair forwards and do not rehearse the press end up defending with eight.

The judgment is that the pairing is the correct answer for sides whose midfield can hold its shape with one fewer body and whose wide delivery does not depend on the player being sacrificed. It is the wrong answer for sides whose midfield is already being overrun, which describes most sides that are tempted by it, because the temptation usually arrives when a side is not scoring and a coach is looking for something to add.

The historical pattern worth noticing is that the pairing was the standard answer for a long period and became less common as midfield control became the dominant organising principle. That is a statement about what football decided to prioritise rather than a statement that the pairing stopped working.

## What each archetype costs the other ten positions

A decision about the forward is a decision about the whole side, and the specific obligations each archetype creates are worth listing so a coach can check whether he can meet them.

Concentration obliges the wide players to deliver rather than to dribble, which means selecting players for a different quality than the one that usually gets wide players selected. It obliges at least two midfielders to arrive in the penalty area for second balls, which is a running demand and a positional discipline. It obliges the defenders and the goalkeeper to have a way of escaping pressure that does not involve the forward, which usually means a midfielder dropping to receive and therefore a midfielder who is comfortable receiving under pressure facing his own goal. And it obliges the pressing scheme to start from the second line rather than the first, which changes where the side wins the ball and therefore how it attacks after winning it.

Distribution obliges somebody to occupy the penalty area, which means either wide players who attack the box rather than crossing into it or midfielders who arrive late and reliably. It obliges somebody to run beyond the forward, because a forward who drops leaves the space behind the last line empty and an empty space behind a defence is an unused asset. It obliges the midfield to play forward into pressure rather than sideways, because the forward's retention is only useful if the ball reaches him. And it obliges the coach to accept fewer bodies in the box at the moment deliveries arrive, which means the deliveries themselves have to be better.

Reading those two lists side by side is a faster route to the decision than any amount of argument about which archetype is superior. A coach who cannot meet the concentration obligations should not select concentration, and a coach who cannot meet the distribution obligations should not select distribution, and most coaches can tell within a minute which list their squad already satisfies.

## The valuation distortion

There is a market effect running underneath this whole argument that shapes how clubs behave and that almost never gets discussed, and it explains a good deal of why the debate feels lopsided.

Transfer valuations reward visible involvement. A player who touches the ball frequently, completes passes, carries into dangerous zones, and appears in highlight compilations doing several different things is easier to value, easier to justify to a board, and easier to sell on. A specialist whose contribution is a small number of decisive actions per match looks cheaper on every visible measure, which means clubs buy him cheaply and sell him cheaply.

That distortion has two consequences worth naming.

The first is that specialists are systematically underpriced relative to what they produce, which is an exploitable inefficiency for any club willing to look. A recruitment process built around involvement metrics will consistently value this profile below its output, and a club that assesses capability directly can buy the same production for less money.

The second consequence runs the other way and is a caution rather than an opportunity. Because specialists are cheap, clubs in difficulty buy them as a quick fix for a scoring problem, install them in teams that cannot supply them, and get nothing. The cheapness is real and the reason for it is partly real too, since the profile carries genuine risk: its output depends on conditions the buying club may not be able to create.

The honest position is that the market underprices the production and correctly prices the risk, and that a club which can guarantee the supply is getting a bargain while a club which cannot is getting exactly what it paid for.

There is a parallel distortion in how coaches are judged, which reinforces the same behaviour. A manager whose attacker holds the ball, links play, and looks busy is credited with a functioning attack even when nothing is being scored. A manager whose attacker is invisible for eighty-five minutes and scores twice is described as lucky. Neither judgment survives contact with the scoreline, and both push coaching toward the profile that looks like work.

## The hybrid, and why he is rarer than people assume

Every argument of this kind ends with somebody saying that the answer is a player who does both, and the observation is true and less useful than it sounds, so it deserves a direct response.

A player who genuinely finishes at an elite rate inside the penalty area and also retains, links, and creates at an elite rate outside it exists, and there are a small number of them in world football at any moment. They are among the most expensive assets in the sport and they are unavailable to almost everyone reading this. Recommending them is not a recommendation; it is a description of a constraint.

There is also a perceptual reason the combination is rare beyond mere scarcity of talent. The two skill sets place different demands on where a player's attention sits during a possession. A box occupant spends the build-up phase watching the deliverer, reading defenders' positions, and preparing to move, which is a scanning pattern oriented toward the moment of arrival. A link player spends the same phase involved in the ball, which is a scanning pattern oriented toward the next pass. Those are not merely different jobs; they are different places for the eyes to be, and doing both means doing each slightly worse.

The players who manage it tend to do so by sequencing rather than by simultaneity, linking in one phase and occupying in another, switching modes as the possession develops. That switching is itself a rare skill and it is why the combination shows up in a handful of careers rather than being coachable at scale.

The practical version of the hybrid available to ordinary teams is not one player who does both but a pair of positions that between them cover both functions, which is the pairing question already addressed, or an attacker at a moderate point on the spectrum who does each adequately. Adequate at both is a genuine option and is what most professional clubs actually select, and it is worth saying plainly that this is a compromise rather than a solution, chosen because the ideal is unavailable rather than because it is best.

## Why the argument shifted, historically

The debate has not always leaned the same way, and understanding why it moved makes it easier to see which parts of the current consensus are about football and which are about fashion.

For a long period the specialist was the default. Teams were organised to deliver into the penalty area, wide players existed primarily to cross, and a number nine who stayed central and finished was doing exactly what the structure required. Nobody thought of this as a limitation because the alternative was not being asked for.

The shift came with the rise of midfield control as the dominant organising principle. Once teams began to prioritise possession, pressing, and building through the thirds, the most advanced player's participation in those phases became valuable in a way it had not been, and a player who could not participate became a cost. The requirement changed, and the archetype that fitted the new requirement was reclassified as superior rather than as better suited.

That reclassification is the fashion element, and it is worth separating from the football. The football says that a possession and pressing structure imposes demands on its most advanced player. The fashion says that a player who does not meet those demands is a lesser player, which does not follow, and which is how a role description became an insult.

The current moment contains a countervailing pressure that may push the balance back. Defending outside the penalty area has improved to the point where chances created through open-play combination are harder to manufacture than they were, and teams increasingly generate their highest-value opportunities from crosses, cutbacks, second phases, and dead balls. All of those are penalty-area events, and penalty-area events reward occupation. If that pressure continues, the specialist's value rises without anybody's opinion needing to change.

## Reading this by role

For a senior coach, the deliverable is the finishable-delivery count and the obligations list. Those two together answer the selection question without any need to resolve the general argument.

For a scout or recruiter, the deliverable is the capability assessment rather than the archetype label: watch him receive under pressure, watch him when his side is pinned back, watch him when his side is building from the back and losing. Three observations, one match, no statistical profile required.

For a youth coach, the sequence is link skills early and box skills continuously, and the warning is against coaching the box instinct out of a player who has it in the name of making him complete.

For a player deciding what to become, the honest answer is that you should not decide yet, that link skills have a narrower window and should come first, and that the concentration end of the spectrum is available later and is where many distributed forwards end up anyway.

For an amateur or Sunday coach, the recommendation is the counterintuitive one: your side needs a forward who can receive a difficult ball with his back to goal and keep it, far more than it needs a finisher, because escaping your own half is your actual problem and finishing is downstream of it.

For an analyst or student, the transferable idea is the threshold question. Any argument that takes the form which of these two is better can usually be improved by asking under what conditions does the answer change, and then finding the condition.

For an enthusiast, the useful thing to take away is why the argument never ends: six specific confusions keep it alive, and most online disagreements about it are two people using one word for two different things and being equally right about their own version.

## Why the argument keeps going in circles

Six specific confusions keep this debate alive past the point where it should have been settled, and naming them is more useful than adding another opinion.

The first is the definitional problem already addressed. Four people use one word for four things.

The second is the conflation of concentration with quality. People argue about whether the poacher is good, which is a question about individuals, when the archetype describes where value sits rather than how much of it there is.

The third is the level problem. Elite conclusions are imported into amateur contexts and amateur experience is generalised upward, and both directions produce nonsense because the service threshold sits between them.

The fourth is the era problem. Comparisons are made across periods in which the tactical requirements of the forward position were genuinely different, and the differences are then attributed to the archetype.

The fifth is the selection versus effectiveness confusion. Statements about what elite recruitment prefers are treated as statements about what works.

The sixth, and the one this article partly concedes, is that the archetypes really are a false binary in the sense that the spectrum framing is more accurate than the category framing. People who argue that the whole distinction is artificial have a genuine point, and the response is not to defend the categories but to say that the spectrum still has ends, that where a forward sits on it has real consequences, and that a coach still has to decide how much concentration his side can support. The categories are a simplification. The decision they simplify is real.

## What this generalises into

The service-quality threshold is a specific instance of a more general principle that runs through the whole of this series, and it is worth stating in its general form because it applies far beyond the forward position.

The principle is that a specialist's value is a function of the system's ability to supply him, and that the same specialist can be the best player in one side and useless in another without changing at all. A ball-playing centre-back in a side that plays long is a liability. A pressing forward in a side that sits deep is a wasted body. A creative number ten in a side that never has the ball is a spectator. In every case the player has not changed and the value has.

The corollary is that recruitment and selection should proceed from the system rather than from the player, and that the question is never who is the best forward but which forward does this specific eleven convert into the most goals. Sides that reverse the order accumulate good players who do not fit and then blame the players.

The generalised method for making decisions of this kind, applied across positions and against opponents rather than just to forward selection, is the subject of [the series' complete counter-planning and decision framework](/2032/06/15/complete-counter-planning-framework/), which takes the threshold logic used here and extends it into a repeatable process.

## The one question that replaces the whole argument

If a reader takes a single thing away, it should be a question to substitute for the debate rather than a position within it.

The question is: how many finishable balls does my team deliver into the penalty area in a typical match, and is anybody in there when they arrive?

That question resolves the selection decision, because the count locates the team relative to the threshold. It resolves the coaching decision, because a low count points at the delivery and a high count with an empty box points at occupation. It resolves the recruitment decision, because it tells a coach which of the two functions is actually missing. And it resolves most of the arguing, because two people who have both counted are usually discussing the same thing.

It takes five matches and a tally sheet, and almost nobody does it. That is the honest reason this argument has lasted as long as it has: not that the question is hard, but that answering it requires somebody to count, and opinions are cheaper than counting.

## A note on how the two archetypes are talked about

One habit is worth abandoning before anybody applies this framework, because it corrupts the decision at the point of use.

The words used for the concentrated end of the spectrum carry a tone that the words for the distributed end do not. Complete sounds like a compliment before any evidence has been offered. Poacher sounds like a shrug. Selfish, greedy, does not work, gets his goals for free: those phrases attach to one archetype in ordinary conversation and not to the other, and none of them describes anything a coach can act on.

The consequence is that a coach weighing the decision is not weighing two neutral options. He is weighing one option that sounds like ambition against another that sounds like settling, and that asymmetry pushes decisions toward the profile with the better public relations rather than the profile the team can support.

The correction is to describe the choice in terms of where value sits and what the team must supply, which is how this article has tried to phrase it throughout. A concentrated attacker and a distributed one are two allocations of the same position, each with obligations attached. Stated that way, the decision becomes an audit of the squad rather than a judgment about a type of player, which is what it should have been from the start.

The same discipline applies inside a dressing room. A player told he is a poacher hears a limitation. A player told his value is concentrated where matches are decided, and that the team is being organised to supply him, hears a role. The information is identical and the second version produces the behaviour a coach wants.

## Closing verdict

The question of whether a poacher or a complete forward is harder to defend has an answer, and it is that the poacher is harder in the moment and the complete forward is harder across a match, which means the answer for any specific defence depends on whether its weakness is individual or structural.

The more useful question, which is which archetype to build around or become, has four answers rather than one.

For an elite side with elite service and a pressing scheme that does not depend on its forward, take concentration, because at high supply volume finishing concentration converts better than distribution adds. For a mid-table side with inconsistent service, take distribution unless your creation runs through wide areas, because variance reduction is worth more than a conversion premium when consistency is the constraint. For an amateur side with poor service, take distribution without hesitation, because below the service threshold a concentrated forward starves and what your side actually needs is a player who can receive a difficult ball and keep it. For a young player, build link skills early because they are cumulative and box skills continuously because they never expire, and do not let anyone coach the box instinct out of a player who has it.

The claim that ties all four together is the service-quality threshold: below a certain standard of delivery, the concentration bet stops paying. Its practical form is a count that any coach can do, which is how many finishable balls his side actually delivers into the penalty area per match. Most coaches have never counted, and most would be surprised.

The last word belongs to the framing rather than the verdict. Concentration and completeness are trade-offs rather than a hierarchy. One profile survives ageing better, pins defenders deeper, and exploits the one part of the pitch defending has never solved. The other integrates more easily, fails more gracefully when the side around it fails, and can be replaced more readily when it is unavailable. A side that understands what it is buying with each will choose correctly. A side that believes one is simply better will pay for a profile it cannot supply, and will then blame the forward.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Is a poacher or a complete forward harder to defend?

The concentrated forward is harder in the moment of the chance, because his work happens inside the penalty area where a defender has no jockeying, no delay, no showing him wide, and no meaningful cover. Those duels are close to unwinnable by defending alone. The distributed forward is harder across a full match, because he appears in several places and each one demands a different defensive decision about whether to follow, engage, or press, and a defence that gets three of five right still concedes. Which form of difficulty is worse depends on the defending side: a well-organised unit with weak individual box defending suffers more against concentration, while a side of good individuals in a poor structure suffers more against distribution.

### Q: Which forward type causes a back four more problems?

Concentration causes more problems inside the area and distribution causes more everywhere else, so the useful version of the question is about the back four rather than the forwards. A back four whose goals are conceded from crosses, rebounds, and scrambles inside twelve metres has an individual and protocol problem, and a concentrated forward will expose it every week. A back four whose goals are conceded from balls into the pocket, from carries through midfield, and from build-up breakdowns has a structural problem, and a distributed forward will exploit it continuously. Auditing where your own goals actually come from answers the question far more reliably than any general argument about archetypes.

### Q: Does a poacher still have a place in modern football?

Yes, though the claim depends heavily on how strictly the term is defined. The extreme version, a forward who does nothing whatever outside the penalty area, is rare at elite level and probably always was, and it is filtered out by recruitment because a forward who neither presses nor links imposes a genuine system cost on sides that press high and build from the back. The moderate version, a forward whose value is heavily concentrated in the box with modest contribution outside it, is common and frequently tops scoring charts. There is also a point running the other way: defending has developed enormously outside the penalty area and barely at all inside it, which arguably raises the value of box specialism rather than lowering it.

### Q: Is a complete forward always more valuable than a poacher?

No, for three separate reasons. Above a certain level of service, finishing concentration compounds while distribution mostly duplicates qualities the side already has in surplus, so the marginal return on another creator is low. A distributed forward is also markedly more expensive to buy and to develop, because he requires a rare combination of finishing and retention, and a side that spends everything on him and cannot afford the creation around him has bought the wrong thing. And value is contextual rather than absolute, since a forward's worth is what he adds to one specific eleven. The complete forward's real advantage is robustness, which is not the same as superiority.

### Q: Which forward type suits a counter-attacking side?

It depends which of the three patterns people mean by counter-attacking. A side that breaks at speed into space, with players running beyond the ball, needs receiving on the move and carrying under pressure, which sits closer to the distributed end. A side that plays direct into the final third and attacks knockdowns and second balls is well served by concentration, because those chances arrive as penalty-area chaos and chaos in the box is where concentration pays. A side that absorbs pressure and then plays long to a lone forward, which is what most amateur sides actually do, needs retention above everything, and a concentrated forward in that role is waiting for a delivery his side is not organised to provide.

### Q: Which forward type suits a possession side?

Both, in different phases, which is why the question is usually answered badly. A possession side needs distribution in the build-up phase, because it needs a forward who can receive under pressure and combine, and it needs concentration in the finishing phase, because sustained possession produces a settled low block and the chances that follow are penalty-area chances arriving as crosses, cutbacks, deflections, and rebounds. The strongest possession sides resolve this by keeping distribution in the forward position and buying the concentration from wide players cutting inside and midfielders arriving late in the box. A side whose wide players and midfielders do not attack the area needs the concentration in the forward instead.

### Q: Is a poacher easier to mark inside a low block?

No, and the intuition behind the question is backward. A low block manufactures exactly the conditions a concentrated forward exists to convert. It forces play into wide areas, which produces deliveries into a crowded box. The crowd removes defenders' ability to watch the ball and their man at once, which is an information advantage for a forward who only has to track the ball. The crowd generates deflections, blocks, and half-clearances, which are the second-ball events a box specialist reads better than anyone. And a compressed block clears badly, because everyone is facing their own goal, so the ball stays in the area. The forward a low block genuinely reduces is the distributed one.

### Q: Which forward type puts more defenders inside the penalty area?

The concentrated forward, and it is one of his least appreciated contributions. A marker cannot leave him, and the covering defender cannot step up while he occupies the space in front of the goalkeeper, so the defending side ends up with more bodies deep than it would choose. Defenders pinned deep are defenders not stepping into midfield, which gives his own midfielders time on the ball. A distributed forward does the opposite: by dropping and drifting wide he draws defenders out of the area, creating space inside it that only produces goals if midfielders and wide players actually attack it. Neither mechanism is better, and the requirement is what a coach should check.

### Q: Which forward type is harder for an amateur defence?

The distributed forward, by a clear margin, because amateur defences are structurally weak far more often than they are individually weak. A forward who receives in midfield, turns, carries, and combines is attacking a structure that cannot hold its shape for ninety minutes, and he will find seams continuously. A concentrated forward at that level is dependent on service that most amateur attacking sides simply cannot produce, so he rarely gets enough opportunities to punish anybody. This is the same threshold logic that determines which forward an amateur side should select, viewed from the defending end, and it points in the same direction.

### Q: Should a young striker develop as a poacher or a complete forward?

Neither exclusively, and the reason rests on a distinction youth coaching rarely makes. Penalty-area skills, meaning anticipation, arrival timing, reading the deliverer, finishing with any surface, and holding position under contact, accumulate through repetition and never expire. Link skills, meaning receiving under pressure with the right body shape, retaining against contact, scanning before receiving, and combining in tight areas, are built cumulatively and are far harder to acquire late, because scanning in particular is a perceptual habit rather than a technique. So build link skills early, build box skills continuously, and do not coach the box instinct out of a player who already has it.

### Q: Which forward type ages better?

The concentrated forward, and the margin is not small. His game rests on anticipation, positional reading, short-range movement, balance in contact, and finishing technique. Reading and anticipation improve with experience and decline late, short-range movement declines slowly, and finishing technique is close to permanent. The distributed forward's game rests on retention under pressure, carrying, repeated involvement across large areas, and pressing volume, several of which depend on repeated acceleration and total distance and decline earlier. The telling observation is that ageing distributed forwards frequently become more concentrated rather than retiring, which undercuts any framing that treats concentration as a lesser form of play.

### Q: Which forward type needs more service?

The concentrated forward, by design rather than by accident. His output is the product of three things: the volume of deliveries into the penalty area, the quality of those deliveries, and his own conversion rate. Two of the three belong to his teammates, which means a forward who is genuinely excellent at his job can produce almost nothing in a side that cannot supply him. A distributed forward's output has a second component that comes from possessions he generates himself by dropping, receiving, retaining, and carrying, and that component survives a fall in service quality that would eliminate the first entirely. This asymmetry is the whole basis of the service-quality threshold.

### Q: Does a poacher or a complete forward win more contests?

Comparing win rates across different sides and leagues measures squad quality rather than archetype, so the answerable version is narrower: within the same side, at the same level, with the same service, which produces more points? Concentration produces more goals in matches that are already going well, because a dominant side delivering into the area converts more of them. Distribution produces more points in matches going badly, because a side that cannot escape its own half needs an outlet more than it needs a finisher. Since points are lost in difficult matches more often than they are gained in comfortable ones, distribution wins more for most sides. Elite sides are the exception.

### Q: Which forward type is harder to replace in a squad?

The concentrated forward, which inverts the market logic and surprises most people. A distributed forward's functions can be redistributed: retention passes to a midfielder dropping in, creation to the wide players and midfield, pressing to a trigger set a line deeper. None of the substitutes is as good and all of them exist, so the side becomes a worse version of itself rather than a different one. A box occupant's function cannot be redistributed, because arriving in the right square of the penalty area at the right moment is not something a midfielder can simply be told to do, and the delivery patterns the whole attack rehearsed are now aimed at nobody.

### Q: Which forward type should a coach build around?

Building around concentration means committing to a delivery system: wide players who deliver rather than dribble, midfielders who arrive for second balls, a rehearsed pattern for entering the final third, and acceptance that the side will look less impressive in possession. Building around distribution means committing to a support system: runners beyond the ball to attack the space he vacates, a midfield that plays forward into pressure, and wide players who attack the box rather than only crossing into it. Training time decides between them as much as personnel, since the delivery system is simpler to install. Many sides should build around their midfield instead and select a forward who fits.

### Q: Is the poacher label still useful for scouting?

Only as rough shorthand, and in practice it causes more misjudgment than it prevents. It gets applied from statistics that measure involvement rather than value, so a low touch count and a high share of touches in the box produce a label that then travels ahead of the player into every later assessment. It carries a pejorative charge in ordinary football language that a scout imports whether or not he intends to. And it describes output rather than capability, which means it frequently describes the team supplying him. Assess capability directly instead: watch him receive under pressure, watch whether he offers an outlet when his side is pinned back.
