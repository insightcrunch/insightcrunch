## What Did Coppelia Kahn Argue in "Coming of Age in Verona"?

Coppelia Kahn's "Coming of Age in Verona," published in Modern Language Studies in the winter of 1977-78, is the essay that relocated the tragedy of Romeo and Juliet from the stars to the street. Its opening sentence refuses the romance outright: the play, Kahn writes, is about a pair of adolescents trying to grow up. Growing up, in her account, means separating from parents by forming a bond with a partner that supersedes the claims of the household, and Verona is arranged to make that ordinary task lethal. The milieu she names is patriarchal in a precise sense, organized around the father: his name, his honor, his house, his power to dispose of children in marriage and to demand sons in his quarrels. Kahn's central claim is that the feud, understood as a social mechanism rather than as an agent of fate, is the primary tragic force in the play, an extreme and peculiar expression of a patriarchal society that Shakespeare shows to be tragically self-destructive. The verdict of this article is that she is right about the mechanism and that the essay remains the founding landmark of the play's modern gender criticism: every serious account of masculinity, patriarchy, or adolescence in Romeo and Juliet written since passes through Kahn's Verona, including the accounts that correct her.

![Verona's fathers and sons in Coppelia Kahn's coming-of-age reading - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-12.webp)

The essay's afterlife confirms the landmark status. Reprinted in The Woman's Part: Feminist Criticism of Shakespeare in 1983, the founding anthology of the field edited by Carolyn Ruth Swift Lenz, Gayle Greene, and Carol Thomas Neely, it reached the generation of feminist scholars then remaking Renaissance studies. Kahn herself carried the argument into her first book, Man's Estate: Masculine Identity in Shakespeare (1981), where the Romeo and Juliet material forms the fourth chapter, "Coming of Age: Marriage and Manhood in Romeo and Juliet and The Taming of the Shrew," and where the play's conflict is stated as a war between two definitions of manhood: manhood as violence on behalf of the fathers, and manhood as separation from the fathers through sexual union with women. Between the journal essay, the anthology, and the book, Kahn built the framework inside which the feud stopped being scenery and became the subject.

What makes the essay durable is that its large claim is cashed out in small, checkable observations. Kahn notices that the play never explains the feud's origin, and treats the omission as the point: a quarrel without a cause is a pure structure of allegiance, a machine for sorting sons from outsiders that needs no grievance to run. She notices that the older generation's investment in the feud is the reverse of the young men's, that the fathers reach for swords their wives mock them for lifting, and that the Prince's law speaks to the city as if its grown men were refractory children. She notices that Juliet is asked to become a wife at an age when the play itself keeps calling her a child, and that no interval of sanctioned experiment is granted to her at all. Each observation is anchored in a line, and the lines accumulate into a system. The sections that follow reconstruct that system piece by piece, test it against the play's hardest scenes, and then weigh the two serious challenges it has attracted: the materialist charge that Kahn's lovers are an ideological mirage, and the psychoanalytic excesses of the essay's own boldest pages.

Kahn's method deserves a word before her conclusions, because the essay's authority rests on its double allegiance. Its psychology is developmental: adolescence, in the psychoanalytic account she adapts, is the labor of separating from the parents and founding an adult identity on a chosen bond, and she reads the play as the record of that labor attempted under sabotage. Its sociology is material: the sabotage is not a mood but an arrangement, households competing for honor and heirs, with law, church, and marriage market aligned in their support. Criticism before Kahn tended to choose between psychologies of character and histories of everything else. Her essay's wager was that a single mechanism, the feud as the patriarchy's rite of male induction, operates at both levels at once, shaping what sons feel by dictating what sons must do. The wager explains the essay's texture, which moves without strain from a servant's pun to a theory of the state, because in her Verona the pun and the state are one system at different altitudes.

The date matters as well. In 1977 feminist Shakespeare criticism was still assembling its first shelf: the essays that would fill The Woman's Part were being written in the same years, and the dominant academic account of the play remained a compound of romance celebration and moralized blame inherited from the nineteenth century. Kahn's intervention was not to add a theme but to change the tragedy's causal address. Where the tradition located the destructive power in the heavens, in haste, or in the lovers' own excess, she located it in the play's most visible and least examined furniture, the quarrel that opens the action and closes it. Novelty of that kind, a familiar object seen from an angle that reorganizes everything around it, is the rarest achievement in criticism of a much-read play, and it is why the essay could found a line of inquiry rather than merely join one.

One structural feature of the argument should be flagged at the outset, because everything in the sections that follow depends on it. Kahn's feud socializes Verona's children on two channels at once. It polices identity, fixing the houses' sons and daughters inside names they did not choose and punishing every attempt to step outside them; and it supplies the criteria by which identity is judged, so that a son's worth, a daughter's value, and a father's standing are all computed in the feud's units of honor, alliance, and revenge. A system that only policed could be evaded; a system that only judged could be ignored. Verona's does both, which is why its children experience its demands as the texture of reality rather than as rules, and why escape requires not disobedience but a whole alternative court of judgment, of the kind the lovers improvise in the orchard and the Friar attempts to institutionalize in his cell. The tragedy, in Kahn's accounting, is that the alternative court never acquires jurisdiction.

## The Feud as a Rite of Passage: How Verona Manufactures Its Men

Kahn's signature formulation calls the feud the deadly rite-de-passage which promotes masculinity at the price of life. The anthropological vocabulary is chosen with care. A rite of passage is not a habit or a taste; it is the ceremony by which a community converts a boy into a recognized man, and its tests are public, dangerous, and witnessed. In Verona the test is always at hand, because the feud supplies a standing enemy, a standing insult, and a standing audience. A son of either house proves what he is by being ready to fight a son of the other, and the proof is worthless unless it can be repeated on demand, in the street, before the friends whose judgment constitutes his standing. Manhood on these terms is not a state but a performance under permanent audit, and the feud is the examination board.

### What does Kahn mean by calling the feud a rite-de-passage?

Kahn means that the feud functions as Verona's only recognized ceremony for turning boys into men: a young man's readiness to fight for his father's house is the public proof that he has arrived at adult male identity, so the quarrel inducts each generation into manhood by rehearsing it in violence.

The play's first scene is her Exhibit A, and she is right to start where Shakespeare starts, below stairs. Sampson and Gregory, Capulet servants, open the tragedy not with love but with a sexualized rehearsal of the feud's logic: Sampson will take the wall of any man or maid of Montague's, will push Montague's men from the wall and thrust his maids to the wall, and will be civil with the maids by cutting off their heads, "the heads of the maids, or their maidenheads, take it in what sense thou wilt" (1.1). Kahn's reading of this banter is exact. The feud's violence and its sexuality are the same gesture at two registers: to dominate the enemy house is to unman its men and to possess its women, and women enter the young men's talk only as the territory on which male standing is registered. The brawl that follows, drawing in Benvolio, Tybalt, the citizens, and finally the two old men themselves, shows the rite operating at every level of the household at once. Old Capulet calls for his long sword and is answered by his wife's contempt: "A crutch, a crutch! Why call you for a sword?" (1.1). Old Montague has to be held back by his wife. The fathers are not directing the machine from above; they are ageing exhibits inside it, still aching to pass the examination their sons now sit.

This is why Kahn insists the feud socializes the young in two directions at once. It binds each child to the paternal household against the other household, polarizing every social relation in the city into friend and foe; and it supplies the criteria by which a son's worth will be measured, so that loyalty to the house and readiness for violence become indistinguishable. Courtship, which in a healthier order would lead a son outward toward a new household of his own, is structurally the feud's enemy, because it proposes a bond that supersedes the father's. The series' full anatomy of this machinery appears in its companion study of [masculinity and violence in Romeo and Juliet](/2024/02/16/masculinity-and-violence-analysis/), which tracks the code scene by scene; Kahn's contribution is to name the code's function. The feud is not what interrupts the coming of age story. The feud is Verona's official version of the coming of age story, and the lovers' version is the heresy.

The Prologue had already supplied Kahn's evidence in its own diction, though centuries of romance reading had trained audiences not to hear it. "From ancient grudge break to new mutiny, / Where civil blood makes civil hands unclean" (Prologue): the feud is civil, a war of the city against itself, and its product is pollution distributed across every hand it touches. Kahn's reading takes the adjective seriously. A civil pathology cannot be cured by removing two young offenders, because the offenders are manufactured by the citizenry's own arrangements; the citizens who rush in with clubs in 1.1, crying down both houses, are themselves part of the theater in which manhood is displayed and judged. The feud is the city's public culture, its sport, its news, and its school, and the school admits every male resident.

The feast in 1.5 gives the rite its second examination, under festive conditions. Tybalt, hearing Romeo's voice, knows the enemy by sound alone and reaches for the examiner's response: a Montague at the feast is an insult the code obliges him to answer on the spot. Capulet's restraint of his nephew is usually played as genial hospitality, and Kahn's framework sharpens it into something more diagnostic. The old man's standing is capital accumulated over a lifetime; he can afford to call the intruder a virtuous and well-governed youth because his own credit is not at risk in the transaction. The nephew's credit is entirely at risk, and his obedience, purchased with the prophecy that the intruder shall be endured for the night, stores the insult as a debt. The challenge letter of 2.4 and the ambush of 3.1 are that debt coming due with interest. Kahn's generational analysis explains what a character reading cannot: why the same system produces a host who forbears and a nephew who cannot, and why the forbearance of the owner merely defers the violence of the apprentice.

## "The Fathers Are the Stars": Fate Translated into Patriarchy

The boldest translation in the essay converts the play's most famous metaphysical machinery into sociology. Generations of readers had taken the Prologue's "star-cross'd lovers" and the lovers' own talk of fortune as the play's explanation of itself: the deaths happen because the heavens decreed them. Kahn does not deny the language; she decodes it. The fathers are the stars, she argues, and the stars are the fathers, because the fathers stand for the accumulated weight of the past, for tradition and authority, the two forces that in fact mold and limit the children's lives. What the characters experience as fate is the pressure of a social order older than themselves, transmitted through the household, and so total that it feels like cosmology. The sense of doom that saturates the play has, in her phrase, its objective correlative in the dynamics of the feud.

The law's performance in 1.1 completes the translation, and Kahn's framework is what exposes it. The Prince's edict is absolute in its wording: further breaches of the peace will be paid with the offenders' lives. It is also, in the event, unenforceable against the men it names, because the Prince's authority is itself a branch of the same patriarchal order, a ruler's power exercised over fellow householders whose credit the city cannot do without. The penalty falls, when it falls at last, on the son, banished for a killing the edict's authors had every instrument to prevent and no instrument to punish in themselves. Kahn's reading draws the conclusion the scene invites: Verona's law is not the feud's opposite but its referee, empowered to set the rite's boundaries, to declare its casualties disturbers of the peace, and to mourn them in the idiom of fate once the rite has run. The closing division of the survivors into the pardoned and the punished is the referee's final act, and the list of the punished contains no one who designed the game.

### Why does Kahn say the fathers are the stars?

Because in her reading the forces that determine the children's lives are the fathers' authority, the inherited quarrel, and the marriage system, so the astrology the characters invoke is the form in which a patriarchal order's pressure is felt from inside: impersonal, ancient, and apparently irresistible.

The translation has real explanatory yield. It explains why the Prologue's fatalism and the play's social density coexist without contradiction: "From ancient grudge break to new mutiny" (Prologue) names a historical mechanism in the same breath that "star-cross'd" names its felt effect. It explains the Prince, whose edict in 1.1 addresses the patriarchs as "rebellious subjects, enemies to peace" and threatens their lives for the city's disturbance, yet whose law never once touches the household arrangements, the marriage market and the honor code, that manufacture the disturbance. Escalus polices eruptions; the system that produces them is beneath his jurisdiction and, Kahn would add, beneath his imagination, since his own house is bound into the feud's losses by the end. Above all, the translation explains the tragedy's distribution of responsibility. A fate reading acquits everyone and indicts the sky. Kahn's reading indicts a specific, describable order that specific men maintain, and it does so without flattening the characters' own experience, since the essay keeps the felt doom and reassigns its cause.

One qualification belongs here, and Kahn's critics have pressed it: the play's astrology is not only a metaphor waiting to be decoded, since dreams, premonitions, and untimely accidents, the plague-quarantined letter above all, do causal work that no sociology exhausts. The adjudication this article defends is that Kahn never claims otherwise. Her argument is about the primary tragic force, and primacy is the right standard: remove the stars and the play's machinery still grinds, because the feud, the forced marriage, and the banishment suffice to produce the tomb; remove the feud and the stars are decoration. The machinery test is hers, and the play passes it.

The decoding faces its hardest exhibit in Romeo's most famous sentence, and Kahn's handling of it completes the translation. "Then I defy you, stars" (5.1) is spoken in Mantua at the news of Juliet's death, and the tradition heard in it the hero's heroic metaphysics, man shaking his fist at heaven. In Kahn's frame the line is sadder and more exact: the son reaches for the idiom of fate at the moment he is most completely the system's product, riding back to Verona to perform the final act the feud always scripted for its defectors, self-destruction in the fathers' monument. The defiance is real as feeling and hollow as analysis, because the stars he defies are not in the sky; they are in the tomb he is riding toward, in the house that made the marriage unspeakable, the market that made the secret necessary, and the rite that left a banished bridegroom no imaginable future except a dramatic one. Kahn's essay is the criticism that teaches a reader to hear both registers of the line at once, the cosmic shout and the social sentence underneath it, and to understand that the play's poetry of fate is the sound a social order makes when its victims describe it from inside.

## The Blame Kahn Moved: From Haste and Stars to the City's System

To measure the essay's intervention, set it against the account it displaced. The inherited reading, descending from Arthur Brooke's preface to his 1562 poem, treated the lovers as a cautionary exhibit: young, disobedient, and punished, their deaths a sermon against haste and lust preached over two beautiful corpses. Academic criticism softened the sermon into psychology, haste as a tragic flaw, precipitancy as the lovers' character defect, but kept the sermon's address: the cause of the catastrophe lived inside the children. Kahn's essay is the first systematic statement of the alternative address. The precipitancy is real, she grants, but it is assigned, trained, and timed by the order around the children, and a criticism that stops at the flaw has mistaken the symptom for the system. Romeo is hasty in the feud's tempo, which allows no interval between insult and answer; Juliet is hasty on the marriage market's schedule, which allows no interval between childhood and the altar. Their shared speed, the quality every moralist had indicted, turns out on inspection to be Verona's own clock, installed in its children by the institutions that raised them.

The redistribution of blame is the essay's most consequential legacy, because it changed what an explanation of the play is allowed to look like. After Kahn, an account that rests in fate, in flaw, or in accident reads as unfinished, a description of the weather that omits the climate. Her scapegoat conclusion completes the redistribution by following the blame to its final resting place: the community that manufactures the catastrophe also manufactures its own absolution, loading the guilt onto the lovers' bodies and calling the transfer justice, or providence, or the stars. When the Prince closes the play by dividing the survivors into the pardoned and the punished, the audience that has read Kahn watches the absolution being administered and notices that the one defendant never summoned is Verona itself. That act of noticing, now habitual among the play's readers, is the essay's permanent alteration of the tragedy's reception: it taught the audience to sit in the jury box of a trial the play's own court never convenes.

## Three Foils Around Romeo: Benvolio, Tybalt, and Mercutio

Among the essay's most quoted analytic moves is its map of the young men. Kahn argues that Shakespeare surrounds Romeo with three foils who divide between them the possible dispositions of male energy inside the feud's economy. Tybalt channels virile energy directly and exclusively into aggression; he is the rite's perfect graduate, a man whose identity has been entirely consumed by the readiness to fight, and whose courtesy is a fencing manual's. Benvolio represents the total sublimation of the same energy into peacemaking, agape instead of eros; he beats down swords in the first scene and spends the play trying, and failing, to talk the city out of its examination. Mercutio attempts the sublimation of virile energy into fancy and wit, converting aggression into verbal display, the Queen Mab speech being the supreme instance of a sword transposed into a tongue. Romeo and Paris, Kahn adds, seek manhood through love rather than through fighting, and are finally impelled by the feud to fight each other over a grave.

### How do Benvolio, Tybalt, and Mercutio divide Romeo's choices among them?

In Kahn's map the three friends embody the three careers the feud offers male energy: Tybalt spends it wholly on aggression, Benvolio wholly on peacemaking, and Mercutio on wit as displaced combat, so Romeo's tragedy is staged as a passage among options that each fail him at the crisis.

The map is schematic, and Kahn knows it; schemas earn their keep by what they let a reader see. This one lets a reader see that Mercutio's bawdy is not decoration but doctrine. Mercutio mocks not merely the enfeebling love Romeo feels for Rosaline but, in Kahn's formulation, all love, because within the peer group's economy a lover is a man withdrawn from the male collective and softened for its purposes. His conjuring of Romeo in 2.1, cataloguing Rosaline's body part by part, is a ritual of recall: the group summons its straying member back by renaming his desire as anatomy. When Mercutio greets the recovered Romeo of 2.4 with relief that he is sociable again, now art thou Romeo, the compliment defines the group's terms of membership. A Romeo who jests is a Romeo restored; a Romeo who sighs is a Romeo lost. The wit that makes Mercutio the play's most loved character is, in this reading, the feud's velvet enforcement arm, policing the boundary between the men's world and the women's with laughter instead of steel.

The Queen Mab speech, on this map, is the wit-sublimation caught in the act of failing. Mercutio's fairy runs her rounds through the dreams of the whole Veronese hierarchy, courtiers, lawyers, ladies, parsons, and the catalogue ends where Kahn's thesis says it must: with the soldier, who dreams of cutting foreign throats, of breaches, ambushes, and Spanish blades, and wakes swearing. Even the sleeping mind of Verona is garrisoned; even fancy, the supposed refuge from the feud, dreams in the feud's vocabulary of wounds and spoils. Mercutio narrates the infection with delight, which is Kahn's point about his function: the attempted sublimation keeps leaking aggression, and the wit who diagnoses everyone's dreams cannot dream outside the system either. When Romeo stops the speech with "Thou talk'st of nothing," the dismissal is gentler than the truth. Mercutio talks of the only thing Verona talks of, displaced into miniature.

The map also clarifies Tybalt, so often played as a mere villain. For Kahn he is the control case: the son in whom socialization has succeeded completely. He has no interest that is not the house's interest, no speech that is not the code's speech, and his fury at the feast in 1.5, when Capulet restrains him, is the fury of a graduate watching the examiner suspend the examination. Capulet's restraint is itself diagnostic for Kahn's larger argument: the older man's honor can afford hospitality because his standing is settled, while the nephew's standing is still on trial and cannot afford the insult of an uninvited Montague dancing in the hall. Generational difference inside the patriarchy, the essay shows, is not softness versus hardness; it is the difference between those who own the system and those still being processed by it.

## From Rosaline to Juliet: An Initiation the Street Keeps Interrupting

Romeo enters the play performing a script. His oxymorons in 1.1, "O brawling love, O loving hate," are not feelings but citations, the Petrarchan lover's part learned from books and rehearsed for an audience of male friends who recognize every posture. Kahn reads the Rosaline phase as continuous with the feud rather than opposed to it: the sighing lover and the street fighter are both boys trying on adult male identities supplied by their culture, one borrowed from the sonnet vogue, the other from the honor code, and both are forms of display before the same jury of peers. Rosaline, who never appears, is the perfect prop for such display precisely because she makes no answering demand. The Friar states the diagnosis in the play's own terms, ruling that young men's love lies not in their hearts but in their eyes (2.3).

Kahn's zoning point completes the Rosaline analysis, and it is easy to miss because the play states it in a servant's errand. Rosaline is Capulet's niece, named on the guest list for the feast that the illiterate servant carries through the streets, so Romeo's first desire was already aimed inside the enemy household's orbit before he ever saw Juliet. Eros in Verona is not a private weather system; it is routed by the feud's map, its objects assigned by proximity, enmity, and the guest lists of rival houses. The sighing over a Capulet kinswoman and the brawling with Capulet servants are the same boyhood at two temperatures, which is why Mercutio's circle can treat the love as a phase of the feud's own calendar, a madness to be cured by the group's counter-magic of bawdy and dancing. When the desire transfers to Juliet, the geography does not change. What changes is that the object answers, and the answer converts a symptom into a conspiracy against the map itself.

Juliet is the interruption of the script because she answers. Kahn follows Harry Levin's celebrated observation that the lovers move from conventional formality toward a plainer, organic expressiveness, and she assigns the initiative to Juliet: in the orchard scene it is Juliet who breaks the vow-swapping ritual, forbids the moon as a witness, and asks the plain question, "Dost thou love me? I know thou wilt say 'Ay'" (2.2). Where Romeo's Rosaline speeches were monologues performed for men, the balcony dialogue is a genuine exchange in which a woman's answering voice educates the lover out of his repertoire. The marriage Juliet insists on, "Thy purpose marriage, send me word tomorrow" (2.2), is the institutional form of the superseding bond Kahn's opening paragraph describes: a new household founded against both fathers, sealed in secret because the public order has no room for it. The full account of that answering initiative belongs to the series' study of [Juliet's agency as the girl who drives the plot](/2022/07/08/juliet-agency-character-analysis/); Kahn's essay is where the critical tradition first framed the agency as a structural threat to the fathers' world rather than a charming precocity.

The feast scene had already shown the new thing being born inside the old forms, and Kahn's use of Levin equips her to say how. The lovers' first exchange is a sonnet, the most inherited, rule-bound artifact in the period's erotic technology, yet it is composed jointly, line answering line, hand answering hand, so that the form's content becomes mutuality enacted in real time. A script performed for peers becomes, without changing its words' genre, a covenant made between principals. Kahn's framework explains why the moment matters structurally: the sonnet is the first adult identity in the play that is co-authored rather than assigned, a household of two founded in fourteen lines inside the enemy's hall, under the eye of the father who owns the room. Tybalt's interrupted challenge crosses the scene at exactly that moment, the rite demanding its examination while the covenant is still wet, and the play's whole tragedy is present in miniature in Capulet's decision to let the dancing continue. The old order can postpone its claim by an evening. It cannot, on Kahn's showing, do anything else.

Juliet's contribution to the escape is theoretical as well as practical, and Kahn's reading gives her the play's most radical speech its full weight. "Deny thy father and refuse thy name" (2.2) is usually heard as a lover's plea; in Kahn's frame it is a constitutional proposal, the suggestion that identity might be unhooked from paternity by an act of will. Her follow-up analysis, "What's Montague? It is nor hand, nor foot, / Nor arm, nor face, nor any other part / Belonging to a man" (2.2), dismantles the name into anatomy and finds no feud in the body. The orchard scene thus stages the exact contest the essay describes: the daughter, granted no adolescence in which to theorize, produces in one night a more searching critique of patriarchal naming than the city around her has managed in generations of grudge. Kahn's Juliet is not merely the plot's engine. She is the play's resident critic of the system that owns her, and her criticism is conducted in the system's own central term, the name.

The interruption, however, is never allowed to complete itself, and Kahn's structural point is that the feud schedules its counter-claims inside the love plot. The wedding in Friar Laurence's cell is scarcely accomplished before Tybalt's challenge, issued earlier and ripening offstage, falls due in the square. Romeo's two initiations, into marriage and into the feud's manhood, are set on a collision course by the play's own plotting: the same afternoon makes him a husband and summons him to prove he is not a coward. Kahn's essay is the first to describe that collision as the tragedy's engine rather than its backdrop, and the description has become so standard that its origin in 1977 is easy to forget.

## The Refusal That Fails: Act 3, Scene 1 and the Price of Re-Entry

The duel scene is the proving ground of Kahn's whole argument, and she reads it as a sequence of forced choices inside the rite. Romeo, newly married to Juliet and therefore secretly Tybalt's kinsman, answers the insult "thou art a villain" with a refusal that the code cannot parse: "I do protest I never injured thee, / But love thee better than thou canst devise" (3.1). The reason he has to love Tybalt is the marriage, which he cannot name. To every witness, including Mercutio, the refusal reads as exactly what Mercutio calls it, "O calm, dishonourable, vile submission" (3.1). Kahn's insight is that the scene is engineered so that the new identity Romeo has chosen cannot be spoken in the square: marriage to a Capulet is the one fact that would explain his peace, and it is the one fact the feud's publicity forbids him to declare. The rite thus protects itself. A son who defects in secret can be shamed back; a son who defects in public might found a precedent.

### Does Kahn read Romeo's killing of Tybalt as murder or as graduation?

Both, in sequence: she calls the killing a murder to mark its moral weight in Romeo's own story, but her structural point is that Verona receives it as a graduation, the moment the defecting son re-earns his standing by shedding the enemy's blood at the required price.

After Mercutio falls, wounded under Romeo's own intervening arm, the defection collapses in the play's most Kahnian speech: "O sweet Juliet, / Thy beauty hath made me effeminate / And in my temper soften'd valour's steel" (3.1). Love is named as a solvent of manhood, softening the metal of which a Veronese man is supposed to be made, and the cure Romeo applies is the rite's own: "Away to heaven, respective lenity, / And fire-eyed fury be my conduct now" (3.1). He kills Tybalt, is banished, and cries "I am fortune's fool" (3.1), a line Kahn's translation of fate handles neatly, since the fortune Romeo blames is the social machinery that offered him no third move between submission and slaughter. The banishment completes the pattern: the law exiles him not for killing, which the code half-excuses, but into the wilderness where the marriage cannot be lived. His re-entry into manhood by the feud's criteria is purchased, exactly as her formula predicts, at the price of the adult life the marriage had begun.

The scene's coda extends Kahn's analysis into the courtroom. Benvolio's narration to the Prince is the system's official version of the event, fluent, ordered, and subtly edited, a young man performing competence before the law in the idiom the law rewards. Lady Capulet answers it with the feud's arithmetic in its female register, demanding a life for a life in the language of justice: Romeo slew Tybalt, Romeo must not live. The Prince's sentence, banishment tempered against the statute's death, is then less a judgment on a killing than a negotiation between the houses' competing claims, conducted in the currency of male lives. Kahn's framework explains why no participant in the inquiry can state the event's actual cause. The cause is the examination system itself, and every speaker in the square is either its officer, its candidate, or its creditor. A transcript of the proceedings would read, in her terms, as the rite reviewing its own casualty list and finding the paperwork in order.

One word of Kahn's in this scene has drawn fire since the first reviews, and adjudicating it sharpens the whole reading. She calls the killing of Tybalt a murder, and the objection writes itself: the fight was fair, the provocation public, the dead man an armed aggressor minutes from killing again. As a legal description the word fails, and Kahn, a precise writer, cannot have chosen it carelessly. Its function in her argument is to mark the act from inside Romeo's own story rather than Verona's: the bridegroom who entered the square refusing violence exits it having done the one thing his new identity existed to renounce, and the word registers his fall in his own currency. Verona keeps the other set of books, in which the act is a credit entry, the defecting son restored. The scandal of the scene, which her provocative word forces a reader to hold in both hands at once, is that both ledgers are accurate, and that a world in which a young man's murder and his graduation are the same event has indicted itself more completely than any critic could.

## Romeo's Two Fathers: Montague's Silence and the Friar's Conspiracy

Kahn's patriarchy has an internal dissenter, and the essay's handling of him illuminates the whole design. Romeo has two fathers in the play. The first, Montague, opens the action unable to know his own son: Romeo has been seen weeping at dawn in the sycamore grove, shutting his windows against the day, and the father commissions Benvolio as an intelligence agent, confessing that he could gladly learn the cause and gladly cure it if he knew it. It is the most sympathetic portrait of a Veronese father in the play, and it is a portrait of total estrangement managed by delegation. The patriarchal house, on Kahn's showing, does not produce intimacy between fathers and sons even when the father wishes for it; the system's channels between them run through kinsmen, reports, and, at need, the street. Montague loves his son the way his station permits, at one remove, and the remove is never closed: at the tomb he arrives to find the son dead and his own wife dead of the same exile, a father informed of his family at every stage by other people's summaries.

The second father is the Friar, and his authority runs in the opposite direction. Where Montague cannot reach Romeo, Laurence reaches him instantly, knows the Rosaline history, hears the Juliet confession within minutes of its formation, and agrees to weaponize the marriage against the feud. In Kahn's framework the distinction is precise: the Friar's fatherhood is elected and confidential, exercised in a cell rather than a hall, and directed against the houses rather than on their behalf. He is the patriarchy's own officer turned saboteur, the one adult male in Verona whose authority over a son is used to extract the son from the system rather than induct him into it. The tragedy's irony, which Kahn's reading states with more precision than any rival, is that the conspiracy fails not because the counter-father lacks love or courage but because his instruments are the system's instruments: secrecy, haste, potions, letters, the whole underground logistics of a world in which legitimate channels belong to the fathers of blood. Romeo escapes his father's house into his other father's cell, and the cell, for all its goodwill, has no jurisdiction in the square where the examination is held.

## No Adolescence for Daughters: Juliet's Compressed Coming of Age

If the feud gives sons a dangerous adolescence, Kahn's most influential observation is that Verona gives daughters none at all. Sons are granted a sanctioned period of experiment with adult identities and activities: they may play the lover, play the wit, play the duellist, and the city reads the play as rehearsal. Daughters pass directly from childhood to wifehood, with the transfer arranged by the father and the girl's consent treated, in Capulet's own chilling formula, as "but a part" of his will: "My will to her consent is but a part" (1.2). The evidence is arranged by the play in a single movement of scenes. Paris sues in 1.2, where Capulet calls Juliet "the hopeful lady of my earth" (1.2), a phrase Kahn lingers on because it prices the daughter as the father's estate and hope of heirs. Capulet first pleads her youth, "Too soon marred are those so early made" (1.2), a caution Kahn treats with suspicion, since its ground is not the girl's unreadiness but the risks of early childbearing to the family's investment. The mother closes the sale in 1.3 with the curriculum stated as demography: "Younger than you, / Here in Verona, ladies of esteem / Are already made mothers" (1.3).

Kahn's framing converts these familiar beats into a system with a name. Juliet's coming of age must be accomplished against the schedule the system sets, which is why it takes the form of seizure rather than growth: she chooses her husband in a night, marries him in a day, defies her father within the week, and contemplates suicide as the remaining instrument of selfhood, "If all else fail, myself have power to die" (4.1). The speed that moralizing critics from Brooke onward blamed on girlish rashness is, in Kahn's account, the rational tempo of a person whose life contains no sanctioned intervals. The marriage market reading of this system, which the series develops independently in its account of [patriarchy and the marriage market in Verona](/2024/02/23/patriarchy-marriage-market-verona/), shares Kahn's premise that the daughter is transacted between men; her distinctive addition is developmental. The market does not merely sell Juliet. It abolishes her adolescence, and the abolition is what makes her story a tragedy of coming of age rather than only a tragedy of exchange.

The wooing scene of 1.2 deserves Kahn's lens at length, because it performs the whole arrangement in polite miniature. Paris presses his suit; Capulet, in his celebrated moderation, pleads his daughter's youth, stipulates that her consent must crown his own, and invites the suitor to the feast to survey the field of Verona's daughters and choose with a comparison shopper's freedom. Every element of the scene is reasonable, and every element, on Kahn's analysis, is a component of the machine. The consent clause costs the father nothing while the preferred buyer is still browsing; the feast invitation converts the courtship into a market exhibition in which Juliet is one lot among many; the delay itself is not protection but pricing, the interval in which the asset matures toward its peak. The clause's fate in 3.4 and 3.5, revoked the moment it is exercised against the father's chosen transaction, reveals what it always was: a courtesy printed on the contract, honored precisely as long as the daughter's will and the father's interest coincide. Kahn's reading of the daughters' schedule predicts the revocation exactly, because a consent that can be withdrawn at the owner's convenience was never a right. It was a feature of the goods, advertised at the viewing and struck from the terms at the sale.

Her defiance scene then reads as the adolescence she was denied, conducted in a single confrontation. In 3.5 Capulet, converting the marriage from offer to ultimatum after Tybalt's death has raised the alliance's value, threatens to drag her to church and to cast her out to "hang, beg, starve, die in the streets" if she refuses. Kahn notes the exact structure of the threat: the father's love is explicitly conditional on the daughter's function, and her famous equivocations to her mother, praising Romeo in phrases that sound like curses, are the first exercises of an adult doubleness the household has forced her to learn in an afternoon. Juliet's growing up is real, the essay grants, and it is accomplished; it is Romeo's that fails. The asymmetry is one of the essay's most durable contributions, because it reverses the conventional distribution of sympathy in which the son's story is the developmental one and the daughter's merely the occasion.

The apology scene of 4.2 completes the portrait of Juliet's compressed education, and Kahn's framework reads it as the final proof of the asymmetry between the children's fates. Sent to the Friar and returned with a script, Juliet kneels to the father she has defied and performs submission so complete that Capulet, delighted, accelerates the wedding by a day, the acceleration that will kill the potion plan's timing. The performance is flawless because it is feigned: the daughter has learned the household's language well enough to counterfeit its central sacrament, repentance, while meaning none of it. In a son, such doubleness would be read as the wit of survival. In Juliet it is the only form of agency the schedule left her, an adolescence of one week's standing producing, under mortal pressure, a diplomat. Kahn's larger point lands here with full force: the system that denied the girl any sanctioned experiment has not prevented her growing up. It has ensured that her growing up takes the form of deception practiced on the system itself, and that the deception, discovered too late, is indistinguishable in its effects from the catastrophe it was meant to prevent.

The potion soliloquy of 4.3 is where Kahn's account of the daughter's education renders its final verdict, and it deserves the weight she implicitly gives it. Alone, having dismissed the Nurse whose curriculum has failed her, Juliet inventories the terrors of the vault with a completeness no adult in the play matches: the fear that the mixture does not work, that it works as poison, that she will wake early among the bones of her ancestors and go mad among the shrieks of mandrakes. "My dismal scene I needs must act alone" (4.3) is the schedule's epitaph. The adolescence Verona denied her is conducted here, in one night, in one room, as a solo examination in mortality that no son is ever required to sit. Sons are examined in public, with seconds, applause, and the law's transcript. The daughter is examined in private, by herself, with a dagger laid by in case the examiner's questions turn out to have no other answer. Kahn's essay, by framing Juliet's story as a coming of age rather than a romance, is the criticism that first made this distribution of ordeals visible as a system rather than a plot convenience, and the visibility has never been revoked.

## The Mothers of Verona: The System's Administrators and Its Casualties

Kahn's patriarchy is not a conspiracy of fathers alone, and her essay's account of the mothers gives the system its full staffing. The wives enter the play restraining their husbands in 1.1, Lady Capulet mocking the call for a sword, Lady Montague physically holding Montague back from the fray, two women performing the peace their men are too processed to want. The gestures are usually read as comic deflation. In Kahn's frame they mark the women's assigned station in the machine: mothers absorb the system's shocks, manage its embarrassments, and transmit its values, while holding none of its formal power. Lady Capulet's own history is the system's credential. Married and a mother at the age Juliet has now reached, she can present early motherhood to her daughter as Verona's norm because it was imposed on her as one; the recruiter for the marriage market is herself its earliest exhibit in the play.

The mothers also register the feud's costs in the currency assigned to them. Barred from the sword, Lady Capulet pursues Tybalt's revenge by petition and by poison, plotting in 3.5 to send to Mantua an agent who will give Romeo such an unaccustomed dram that he shall soon keep Tybalt company. The plan is the feud conducted by the means left to women, and Kahn's framework is what makes its logic visible: the hatred is identical, the weapons are rationed by gender. Lady Montague's end is the quieter entry in the same ledger. She dies offstage of grief for her son's exile, reported by her husband at the tomb as an afterthought of the catastrophe, one more seal upon the feud's sorrows. The system that made her a restrainer in the first scene makes her a statistic in the last, and the play gives her no speech at either end of the transaction. Kahn's essay, by insisting that the patriarchy's costs be counted in the women's column as well as the men's, recovered these figures from the margins of the romance reading, where they had stood for centuries as local color in somebody else's tragedy.

Lady Capulet's conduct in the defiance scene shows the administrator at work under pressure, and Kahn's frame explains its otherwise puzzling shape. Presented with her daughter on her knees, begging delay, the mother does not intercede, bargain, or soften; she withdraws, announcing that she will speak no more and washing her hands of the girl in the idiom of a dismissed petition. Earlier in the same scene she had answered Juliet's grief for Tybalt with the plan to poison Romeo, the feud's business conducted at full commitment; maternal tenderness appears in neither register because the office she holds has no such function. She is not written as a monster, which is Kahn's point about administrators generally: the system's cruelty does not require cruel people, only formed ones, and a mother who was herself a mother at the age her daughter has now reached will process her own daughter's terror as a scheduling problem in the household's honor. The scene's coldest implication, which the essay's framework was the first to state plainly, is that Juliet in 3.5 is not failed by two parents in succession. She is processed by one system wearing two faces, the father's ultimatum and the mother's withdrawal being the same decision at different volumes.

## The Nurse's Curriculum: Womanhood Taught as Nature

The mechanism by which the daughter's role is made to feel natural, rather than imposed, is the subject of Kahn's set-piece reading of the Nurse's great speech in 1.3. The Nurse is, in Kahn's phrase, a surrogate mother within the patriarchal family, but one of little help in assisting Juliet's passage from child to woman, because the only model of womanhood the Nurse has to transmit is the one that formed her. The speech, with its winding chronicle of weaning, the earthquake, the bumped forehead, and the dead husband's joke, is for most audiences the play's warmest comedy. Kahn reads it as curriculum. The husband's jest, repeated with delight, holds that the baby Juliet will "fall backward when thou hast more wit," and the Nurse's own gloss applies the prophecy to the grown girl: the joke's whole content is that a female life is a preparation for the marriage bed, told to the child as a prophecy of her nature.

### What does the Nurse's weaning story teach Juliet about being a woman?

In Kahn's reading it teaches that a woman's biography has exactly four events, birth, suckling, weaning, and marriage, and that sexuality and childbearing are not choices but seasons: the Nurse measures time itself by those turnings, for whom earthquakes are less cataclysmic than the milestones of the female body.

The close of the essay's Nurse analysis generalizes the point into one of its most quoted sentences: in the patriarchal setting, the Nurse's speech epitomizes how a woman's subjugation to her role as wife and mother is made to seem integral with nature itself. Subjugation that arrives as nature cannot be argued with, which is why the Nurse is a more effective agent of the system than the raging Capulet of 3.5. Capulet issues commands that can be refused at a price; the Nurse issues a world in which refusal has no vocabulary. Kahn is careful, though, to keep the Nurse human rather than allegorical: the woman's counsel in 3.5, urging the Paris marriage with Romeo as good as dead, is the curriculum applied under pressure, the surrogate mother falling back on the only wisdom her formation left her, and Juliet's instantaneous recognition of the betrayal is the moment her education outgrows its teacher. The essay thus gives feminist criticism one of its permanent instruments, the distinction between the patriarchy enforced by fathers and the patriarchy administered, lovingly, by women formed inside it.

The Nurse's own ending confirms the curriculum's cost to its teacher. Excluded from the potion plan, the confidence she forfeited in 3.5, she is reduced in Act 4 to wedding bustle and then to the discovery of the body she believes dead, where her lament out-grieves the parents in volume and desolation. Kahn's framework reads the trajectory without cruelty: the surrogate mother is discarded by the plot at the exact point where the daughter's real education begins, because the formation the Nurse embodies has nothing to teach about the ordeals ahead. Her grief is genuine and her formation is genuine, and the play's refusal to reconcile the two is the curriculum's final examination, set for the audience. Readers formed by the romance tradition had always wept for the Nurse without asking what she had taught. Kahn's question, what the teaching cost the taught, is the one the scene now permanently asks.

## The Friar's Cell: The Name Romeo Tries to Cut Out

Kahn's psychoanalytic register is most exposed in her reading of 3.3, and the passage has become the standard exhibit for both her daring and her excess. Romeo, banished, flings himself on the Friar's floor and begs to be told in what vile part of his anatomy his name lodges, "that I may sack / The hateful mansion" (3.3). The Friar answers with the play's own gender policing: "Art thou a man? Thy form cries out thou art; / Thy tears are womanish" (3.3). Kahn reads Romeo's wish to locate and excise his name as a symbolic attempt to cut the patriarchal identity out of his body, the name being the father's property inscribed in the son, and she presses the symbolism to a deliberately shocking conclusion about self-castration. The father's name, on her account, is the feud carried inside the self: Romeo Montague is the feud's soldier by birth certificate, and his fantasy of surgery is the wish to be unborn from the house that owns him.

### Why does Kahn seize on Romeo's wish to cut out his name?

Because the name is the precise point where Verona's public system becomes private flesh: to be Montague is to be enlisted, so Romeo's fantasy of excising the name dramatizes, for Kahn, the son's discovery that patriarchal identity is not a role he plays but a body he inhabits.

The adjudication this scene requires is the brief's complication in miniature: does the feminist-psychoanalytic frame overdetermine the text? The honest verdict is mixed. The name reading is earned, since the play itself obsesses over names from the orchard scene forward, "Deny thy father and refuse thy name" (2.2), and since the Friar's rebuke confirms that the scene's own currency is manhood. The castration flourish goes beyond anything the lines will certify and imports a Freudian algebra the passage does not need. Yet the flourish is detachable in a way that weaker readings' excesses are not: remove it, and the scene still demonstrates Kahn's thesis, a young man discovering that the identity his world certified is the instrument of his ruin, being shamed for unmanly grief by the play's kindest adult. The frame overdetermines a paragraph; it does not overdetermine the play.

The scene's remainder strengthens her hand further. The Friar's consolation, when it comes, is a catalogue of Romeo's fortunes recited like a balance sheet, wife alive, Tybalt dead who would have killed him, the law's death sentence commuted to banishment, adversity's sweet milk, philosophy, offered as nourishment. Kahn's reading notices what the catalogue cannot contain: any acknowledgment that the disaster was socially authored. The Friar, who married the lovers to end the feud and thus understood its mechanics better than any man in Verona, consoles its victim with a philosophy of individual fortune, as if the machinery that produced the banishment were weather. The gap between the Friar's knowledge and his consolation is, for Kahn, the measure of the system's depth. Even its internal critic, the one adult who tried to use the marriage against the feud, retreats at the crisis into the idiom of fate, leaving the son to learn the truth of his situation from its consequences rather than from any elder's analysis.

## The Rite's Staff: Pages, Servants, Musicians, and the Watch

Kahn's system has a workforce, and noticing it extends her argument into corners of the play that landmark readings rarely visit. Below the named combatants, Verona's rite is staffed: Gregory and Sampson open it as a labor dispute of livery and insult; the illiterate servant carries the guest list that routes Romeo's desire; Peter, the Nurse's man, guards her errand with self-important officiousness; Balthasar rides to Mantua with the news of Juliet's death and outruns, by days, the Friar's letter that would have corrected it. The information economy of the tragedy is a servants' economy, and it fails in the servants' channel, not the masters'. Kahn's framework explains the asymmetry without straining: a system organized around the fathers' honor invests in the theater of that honor, swords, funerals, statues, and starves the unglamorous infrastructures, posts, messages, verifications, on which mercy would have depended. Verona can afford gold statues. It cannot afford a reliable courier.

Act 4's household scenes give the staff its most unsettling hour. The wedding machine Capulet sets running, cooks, musicians, logs, and napkins, processes Juliet's apparent death without pausing: the feast becomes a funeral by the father's own conversion formula, the hired musicians negotiate their wages over the supposed corpse, and Peter trades insults with them in the play's most notorious comic trough. In Kahn's terms the scene is not tonal relief but the system photographed at work, converting a daughter's body from one ceremony to the other with the catering unchanged. The Watch of Act 5, arriving at the tomb with lanterns and procedure, completes the staffing picture: the city's lowest officers are the first to see the rite's final product, three young bodies and a friar with a crowbar, and their report sets the last assembly in motion. Kahn wrote about the principals, but her system, once seen, organizes the servants' hall as faithfully as it organizes the square, which is the signature of a reading that has found something load-bearing in its text.

The Apothecary of Mantua is the staff's last and most damning exhibit. Romeo finds him in a shop of empty boxes and beggarly accounts, a tradesman starved by the same civic order that gilds its patriarchs, and buys from him, for forty ducats, the poison the law of Mantua makes a capital sale. The tradesman's consent is the system's economics in one sentence: his poverty, not his will, consents. Kahn's framework, though she does not linger here, completes the scene's meaning without effort. The feud's Verona spends its wealth on the theater of honor, gold for statues and cooks for weddings, and leaves its margins so bare that death itself retails cheaply to any desperate customer with coin; the fathers' monument will be furnished, in the end, out of a poor man's starvation. Between the servant's bawdy that opens the play and the Apothecary's poverty that closes it, Kahn's system describes a whole civic economy, ordered by the fathers' prestige and paid for, at every level, by the young, the poor, and the female. That a single essay's frame can organize evidence from the play's first pun to its last purchase is the practical test of a landmark, and it is the test her successors, even the dissenting ones, keep quietly applying to their own readings.

## Paris at the Tomb: The Rite's Last Examination

Kahn's foils map contains one prediction the plot saves for its final scene, and the essay's readers have not always given the confirmation its due. Romeo and Paris, the two young men who sought manhood through love rather than through fighting, are finally impelled by the feud to fight each other, over Juliet's body, in the dark, neither knowing the whole of what he does. Paris comes to the monument as the marriage market's certified purchaser, strewing flowers on the bridal bed the grave has become, and challenges the intruder as a vandal and a condemned criminal. Romeo, who has come to die, begs him off with a courtesy the square never taught him: tempt not a desperate man. The examination proceeds anyway, because in Verona a challenge before a witness, even a hidden page, cannot be declined by a man who still answers to the code's jury. Paris dies asking to be laid beside Juliet, and Romeo, recognizing the suitor his marriage displaced, grants the request and delivers the system's bleakest compliment, calling him one writ with me in sour misfortune's book.

For Kahn's argument the scene is the controlled experiment that closes the case. Strip away Tybalt's aggression, Mercutio's wit, the fathers' presence, and the street's audience, and the machinery still runs: two gentle suitors, each in Verona to serve a woman's memory, kill and are killed because the only grammar their world gave them for a disputed claim over Juliet is the duel. The feud has so thoroughly become the medium of male relations that it no longer needs the feudists. Romeo's tenderness in the scene, his reluctance, his eulogy, is the new identity asserting itself to the last; the sword work is the old one, and the old one is faster. An essay that predicts its play's final scene from its first principles has earned the right to be argued with seriously, and the challenges Kahn attracted are best understood as the tribute of a field that recognized a complete explanation when it saw one and set about finding its seams.

## The Tomb as the Fathers' Monument: Kahn on the Ending

Kahn's reading of the ending gathers every strand. Romeo enters the Capulet monument and addresses it as a "detestable maw, thou womb of death" (5.3), and Kahn takes the image as the play's own verdict on the system that built the tomb: the patriarchal house, which promised to preserve the family line, finishes as a mouth that eats the family's children. The lovers die inside the monument of the fathers, the scapegoats, in her term, of the order whose reconciliation their deaths purchase. For the settlement that follows is conducted entirely between the men who remain. Capulet offers his hand, "O brother Montague, give me thy hand: / This is my daughter's jointure" (5.3), converting the handshake into a marriage settlement paid over a corpse, and the two fathers bid against each other in golden statues, Montague promising to raise Juliet's statue in pure gold, Capulet answering that Romeo's shall stand as rich beside his lady's. The feud's competition survives its cause, transposed from swords to monuments, and the daughters and sons who were to inherit the houses become their decoration.

This is the reading's final proof, and it is structural rather than symbolic, which is why it has convinced readers unmoved by the cell scene's psychoanalysis. A fate reading of the ending sees heaven's scourge and a chastened city. Kahn's reading sees a transaction: the patriarchal order absorbs the catastrophe, converts the children into capital of memory, and continues, minus the generation it consumed. Even the Prince's closing distribution of blame, some shall be pardoned and some punished, leaves the household system itself unindicted, as the essay's opening analysis of his law predicted. The play's last word on Verona, in Kahn's account, is that Verona survives. What dies is the alternative the lovers had begun to build, an adult bond founded on mutual election rather than paternal assignment, and the monument is its tomb in both senses.

The Friar's explanation at the tomb gives Kahn's fate argument its final test, and she passes it in a way the fate tradition cannot return. His account of the plan's collapse reaches for the idiom his whole career in the play has alternated with his sociology: a greater power than can be contradicted has thwarted the intents of priest and lovers alike. Heard through Kahn's translation, the sentence is the system's last line of defense spoken by its kindest officer. The greater power had a street address. It was the quarantine that stopped a letter because a plague made friars suspect, the haste that Capulet's wedding acceleration imposed on a forty-two-hour drug, the secrecy that a marriage market made necessary, and the honor code that put a sword in the hand of every messenger's potential challenger. Chance dealt the final cards, as chance does in every tragedy worth the name; the deck, Kahn's essay insists, was stacked by Verona. Her reading does not need to deny accident. It needs only to ask who arranged the conditions under which accident would be fatal, and the play answers that question in every scene.

The wedding morning of Act 4 had already given that answer in its most domestic form. Capulet, presented with his daughter's performed repentance, does not merely forgive; he produces. The household becomes a factory on his orders, twenty cooks engaged, the wedding day pulled forward, the master awake all night supervising a celebration whose scale is the family's credit made visible. Kahn's framework reads the bustle as the father's love in its only fluent language, logistics, and therefore as the tragedy's cruelest mechanism: it is Capulet's joy, not his rage, that advances the wedding to Thursday and strands the Friar's plan on a timetable no one can now meet. The rage of 3.5 threatened Juliet with the streets; the delight of 4.2 kills her with efficiency. A reading that cannot tell the difference between the father's moods would miss the point of both. Kahn's can, because hers locates the danger in the office rather than the temperament, in a fatherhood whose tenderest expression is the staging of the daughter's transfer, on schedule, before witnesses, with catering.

## What Kahn's System Cannot Hold: Lyric, Chance, and the Surplus

A verdict for Kahn is worth more if it survives an honest inventory of her reading's limits, and three are structural. The first is the lyric surplus. The lovers' poetry, the shared sonnet at the feast, the orchard dialogue, the aubade, exceeds any function her system assigns it, and readers who come to the play for that surplus will find her Verona accurate and insufficient in the same sitting. Kahn herself registers the excess obliquely, through Levin: the movement from formality to organic expressiveness is her evidence that a new identity is being founded in language. But an identity founded in language is also a poem being written, and the essay's sociological attention keeps sliding past the writing to the founded thing. The reading explains why the lovers must speak as they do better than it explains why their speaking is beautiful, and a complete criticism of the play needs both explanations.

The second limit is chance, already conceded in part: the plague quarantine, the letter's failure, and the minutes at the tomb are not social facts, and Kahn's primacy claim, that the feud is the main tragic force, must be content to govern a play in which the final mechanism is a missed delivery. The third limit is historical specificity. Her adolescent is a universal figure, the separating son of developmental psychology, and Verona is asked to stand for Renaissance England and Italy and, in the essay's own reach, for the reader's world as well. Historicist critics have rightly asked what happens to the reading when the 1590s are restored to it: the London of apprentices and riots, the specific marriage law, the actual fencing fashions. The answer, on the evidence of the decades since, is that the reading survives the restoration, because its mechanisms were drawn from the text's particulars in the first place; but the survival is a tribute to her evidence rather than to her universalism, and the distinction matters for the adjudication that follows.

A fourth limit belongs to tone. Kahn's Verona is a machine, and machines in her account do not joke except functionally: the play's comedy is assigned to sublimation, displacement, and curriculum, each laugh accounted for as the system at work. The accounting is brilliant and it is not the whole experience of the play, whose first two acts are among the funniest Shakespeare wrote and whose laughter builds a world the audience grieves precisely because it was delightful. A criticism that converts all delight into diagnosis risks the puritanism it would elsewhere deplore, and Kahn's essay, to its credit, mostly resists the conversion by keeping its eye on the cost accounting rather than the pleasures. Still, the reader who loves Mercutio's aria for its own verbal glory, or the Nurse's timing for its own sake, is registering something the essay's instruments measure badly. The limit does not overturn the verdict. It defines the neighboring territory, art's surplus over system, that the next generation of the play's critics, Snow among them, set out to reclaim without surrendering her ground.

## The Essay's Vocabulary: Rite, Maw, and Scapegoat

Kahn's analytic terms repay attention as a group, because they reveal a method that landmark essays often share: she builds her theory out of the play's own words and ceremonies rather than importing a finished apparatus. Rite-de-passage comes from anthropology, but she earns it from the text's repeated examinations, challenges, and graduations. The maw is Romeo's own image for the tomb, promoted by Kahn into the emblem of the whole system, the father's house revealed as an appetite. Scapegoat belongs to ritual theory, and she applies it not to the deaths but to the settlement, the community's use of the deaths to absolve itself. Even her governing noun, patriarchy, is defined locally and technically at the outset, rule organized around the father, rather than waved as a slogan; the Verona she describes would be recognizable to its own citizens in everything except the conclusion she draws from it, which is that the arrangement consumes its heirs.

The vocabulary also marks the essay's one systematic temptation. Terms drawn from ritual and psychoanalysis carry a built-in claim to universality: rites, scapegoats, and castration anxieties are presented by their home disciplines as human constants, and an essay armed with them can drift from Verona's particulars toward a timeless machinery of fathers and sons, as Kahn's occasionally does. The critics who corrected her were, at the level of vocabulary, correcting the drift: Callaghan's ideology and Goldberg's ideologized love are terms that insist on dates, markets, and print, on a desire manufactured in a specific decade rather than awakened in a universal boyhood. The mature position the debate produced, and the one this article's verdict endorses, keeps Kahn's words and pays Callaghan's toll on them: the rite is real, the maw is real, the scapegoats are real, and all three are also artifacts of a particular city, a particular stage, and a particular moment in the history of both.

## Does the Feminist Frame Overdetermine the Reading? Callaghan, Goldberg, Snow, and Conaway

The serious challenges to Kahn have come from inside feminist and historicist criticism, not from its enemies, and the essay's landmark standing is partly a function of the debate it organizes. Dympna Callaghan's "The Ideology of Romantic Love: The Case of Romeo and Juliet," published in The Weyward Sisters: Shakespeare and Feminist Politics in 1994, is the weightiest. Callaghan, whose feminist-materialist reading has its own article later in this run and appears here at the length the fence allows, argues that the romantic love Kahn treats as the order's victim is itself an ideological formation, a version of desire manufactured by and serviceable to the emergent social order that produced the play. On this account the lovers' bond is not a clean outside to patriarchy from which the system can be judged; it is one of the system's own products, and the play participates in constructing the myth of transgressive love that later centuries would consume as liberation. Jonathan Goldberg pressed a cognate objection, in the debate Paul A. Kottman later summarized in Shakespeare Quarterly: Kahn, like Janet Adelman, idealizes the lovers' love, treating as natural and oppositional a desire that the play itself has ideologized.

The aubade of 3.5 shows, in miniature, why the counter-witnesses so often extend her. The lovers dispute the dawn in distinguishable grammars: Juliet argues from wish, renaming the lark a nightingale and the light a meteor, while Romeo argues from consequence, pricing the alternatives of life and death in plain terms, before capitulating into her grammar and being recalled by her own reversal. A discourse-centered criticism finds sexual difference encoded in the styles themselves, two speakers sharing a bed and a mortal danger yet reasoning in idioms their formation assigned them. Kahn's structural account explains why the parting must happen; the linguistic accounts describe how the parting sounds from inside. The scene is a small demonstration that the play's feminist criticisms, psychoanalytic and materialist and formalist, are instruments pointed at the same object from ranges that need not compete.

The counter-witnesses extend Kahn rather than refute her. Edward Snow's "Language and Sexual Difference in Romeo and Juliet," published in 1985 in Shakespeare's "Rough Magic," the festschrift for C. L. Barber that Kahn herself co-edited with Peter Erickson, takes Kahn's Levin-derived insight about the lovers' changing language and gives it a sexual politics of its own: Juliet's speech is the more grounded, generous, and clear-sighted, and Romeo's education is an education in mutuality administered by a woman's words. Snow confirms Kahn's asymmetry thesis from inside the verse. Charles Conaway, writing on teaching the play in 2011, disputes a hinge of the argument from the other side: marriage and courtship, he observes, are themselves coming-of-age rituals that in the period guided youths into paternal culture rather than out of it, so Kahn's claim that the lovers' bond leads toward separation from the paternal house needs the qualification that the culture had already annexed marriage to its own purposes.

Taken together, the four witnesses map the debate's present state with unusual completeness. Callaghan corrects the lovers' halo; Goldberg corrects the criticism's halo; Snow extends the linguistic evidence; Conaway complicates the escape route. What none of them does is restore the account Kahn displaced, and the absence is the adjudication's strongest exhibit. No major critic since 1977 has rebuilt the tragedy on the stars, on the lovers' haste, or on a feud read as bad luck with swords; the contest is entirely over how the system Kahn identified should be described, extended, and priced. Fields ratify their landmarks in exactly this way, by ceasing to argue about whether the founder's object exists and arguing only about its properties. The feud as the patriarchy's machinery for manufacturing men is now such an object, with a half-century of scholarship devoted to its properties, and the founder's name is attached to it in every syllabus that teaches the play whole.

Beneath the individual objections runs a disagreement about what feminist criticism is for, and naming it clarifies what is at stake in choosing between the readings. Kahn's feminism is psychoanalytic and diagnostic: it asks how a social order gets inside its children, how the father's name becomes the son's shame and the mother's curriculum becomes the daughter's nature, and it treats the play as evidence in that inquiry. Callaghan's feminism is materialist and suspicious of the evidence itself: it asks what work the play, as a cultural product, performed for the order that produced it, and it treats the lovers' celebrated resistance as part of the product's design. The first method risks finding its theory everywhere; the second risks explaining away the text's evident sympathies. Shakespeare criticism after 1994 has mostly tried to hold both instruments at once, and the effort defines the modern field: read the play as a machine for making gender, with Kahn, and as a machine for selling the making as romance, with Callaghan, and let each suspicion audit the other.

The adjudication defended here is a split verdict with the weight on Kahn. Callaghan and Goldberg win the theoretical caution: Kahn's lovers are not outside ideology, and the essay occasionally writes as if desire were a natural force merely repressed by Verona, when the play's own sonnet machinery shows desire arriving pre-formatted by Petrarch. But Kahn wins the dramatic facts, which are the facts her essay was built to explain. Whatever love's ideological pedigree, the play stages the feud, not the love, as the killing agent at every turn: the brawl, the duel, the forced marriage, the tomb. A reading that dissolves the lovers' bond into ideology still has to explain why the text lavishes its causal machinery on the fathers' system and its final transaction on the fathers' handshake, and on that ground Kahn's account remains the one to beat. Conaway's qualification is absorbed rather than fatal: the lovers' marriage is subversive in Verona not because marriage in the abstract escapes paternal culture, but because this marriage, contracted without consent, knowledge, or settlement, seizes the institution from the fathers who owned it, which is why the household experiences it as theft.

## Editors, Anthologies, and the Making of a Landmark

A reading becomes a landmark when institutions take it up, and Kahn's institutional path is unusually legible. The Woman's Part placed the essay in 1983 between the founding documents of Shakespearean feminism, where it functioned as the Romeo and Juliet chapter of a collective argument that the canon had to be reread from the position of its daughters. Man's Estate gave the argument its theoretical frame, masculine identity as a historical and psychic construction, and founded a subfield in the process: Peter Erickson's Patriarchal Structures in Shakespeare's Drama (1985) and the masculinity studies that followed, down to Mark Breitenberg's Anxious Masculinity in Early Modern England (1996), work in a clearing Kahn opened. The depth of the clearing can be measured in an unlikely place: when Paul A. Kottman opened his 2012 Shakespeare Quarterly essay "Defying the Stars" by mapping the modern criticism of the play, the frame he found organizing the whole field was the dialectic between the lovers' individual desire and the social, familial forms that condition it, and the scholars he named as the frame's feminist architects were Kahn and Janet Adelman, with Goldberg as their critic. A reading that supplies the paradigm inside which even its opponents argue has passed beyond influence into infrastructure, and Kottman's survey, written thirty-five years after the essay, is the nearest thing the field has produced to a certificate of that status.

The book version of the argument deserves its own note, because its design states Kahn's ambition more nakedly than the essay could. The Man's Estate chapter is titled "Coming of Age: Marriage and Manhood in Romeo and Juliet and The Taming of the Shrew," and the pairing is itself a critical act: the tragedy of the feud yoked to the comedy of the taming plot under a single thesis, that marriage in Shakespeare's early work is the gate at which manhood is tested and certified. Read together under her thesis, the two plays answer each other. The comedy shows the gate passed brutally and successfully, a husband manufactured by the subjugation of a wife; the tragedy shows the gate defended by the fathers until the candidates for it are dead. Critics who treat the essay as a local reading of one play underestimate it. From 1981 onward it was a book's load-bearing chapter, a claim about how Shakespeare's drama, across genres, imagines the making of men, and the subsequent masculinity criticism of the early modern stage is largely a footnote to that claim. Editors carried the reading to students. Jill L. Levenson, whose Oxford Shakespeare edition of the play appeared in 2000 and whose essay "'Alla stoccado carries it away': Codes of Violence in Romeo and Juliet" anatomized the play's fencing codes and street honor, treats the feud as a social system with rules a modern reader must be taught to see, which is Kahn's premise translated into editorial practice. The classroom followed the editions: the questions Kahn taught teachers to ask, why the play opens with servants rehearsing rape as banter, why Romeo calls his love effeminate, why Juliet has no time to grow, are now the standard furniture of the syllabus.

The institutional story behind the landmark reinforces the textual one. Kahn built, alongside the argument, part of the infrastructure that carried it: with Murray M. Schwartz she co-edited Representing Shakespeare: New Psychoanalytic Essays (1980), the volume that consolidated the psychoanalytic turn her essay had helped to start, and with Peter Erickson she co-edited the Barber festschrift in which Snow's extension appeared. Her career at Brown University, where she held a chair spanning English and women's studies, placed the reading's author inside the new discipline's founding generation, training students in a method that treated gender as a system to be analyzed rather than a theme to be appreciated. Landmarks in criticism are rarely the work of arguments alone; they are arguments that arrive with editors, anthologies, syllabi, and students attached. Kahn's arrived with all four, which is a material explanation, of the kind her materialist correctors taught the field to value, for why this essay and not another became the foundation stone.

The landmark claim should still be stated precisely. Kahn did not invent the social reading of the feud, and her essay's psychoanalytic vocabulary dates it as surely as its venue does. What she invented was the synthesis that made the feud a gendered institution with a developmental casualty list, and she documented it at the level of the line. Later critics have corrected her from several directions, Callaghan on ideology, Goldberg on idealization, historicists on the universality of her adolescent, and the corrections have stuck where they were earned. But a corrected landmark is not a superseded one. The test of a founding reading is whether its rivals must cite it to disagree, and by that test "Coming of Age in Verona" stands where it stood in 1983, as the argument every subsequent feminism of this play defines itself against or through.

## The Verdict on Kahn: Why This Reading Still Governs

The final judgment, then, is that Kahn's essay deserves its landmark status on evidence, not on piety. It explains the play's first scene, which the romance tradition could only skip; it explains the duel scene's otherwise baffling emotional logic, in which a bridegroom apologizes to his enemy and then apologizes for apologizing; it explains the ending's statues, which it taught readers to see as a settlement rather than a sentiment. Its overreach is real but localized, a psychoanalytic flourish in the cell scene and a universal adolescent who occasionally floats free of the 1590s. Its central perception, that Verona manufactures its men through a deadly rite and its women through a stolen childhood, and that the tragedy is the bill for both, has been absorbed so completely into the way the play is taught, edited, and staged that it now reads as common sense. Making a perception into common sense is what landmarks do. The reading's cultural afterlife tells the same story in the popular register: later screen Veronas of corporate towers and televised vendettas read, whatever their makers intended, as Kahn's system visualized, a feud maintained by media, livery, and brand until its children pay the subscription in blood. An essay whose diagrams keep recognizing themselves in new adaptations has not dated. It has become part of the lens through which the play is seen, and lenses are not refuted; they are, at most, supplemented by critics honest enough to say what the lens cannot show. The remaining question, whether the play is finally a feminist one, is a larger hearing that the series reserves for its own courtroom in the debate over [whether Romeo and Juliet is a feminist play](/2031/04/18/is-romeo-and-juliet-feminist/); Kahn's essay is that debate's founding brief, and the brief still wins on the mechanism even where it loses on the metaphysics.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Kahn summary, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch Kahn summary is a twelve-row table that sets each element of Coppelia Kahn's argument beside the scene that carries it, from the feud's function as a rite of passage to the golden statues of the final settlement. The pattern it exposes is the completeness of her mechanism: every major beat of the play, the opening brawl, the courtship, the duel, the forced marriage, and the tomb, falls into place as a stage in one system, a patriarchy that manufactures its sons through violence and its daughters through a stolen childhood, then bills both for the cost.

### Q: When and where was "Coming of Age in Verona" first published?

"Coming of Age in Verona" was first published in Modern Language Studies, volume 8, number 1, in the winter of 1977-78, at pages 5 to 22. The venue matters to the essay's history. A journal essay of eighteen pages became, within six years, one of the founding documents of feminist Shakespeare criticism, reprinted in The Woman's Part in 1983 and expanded by its author into the fourth chapter of Man's Estate in 1981. Its trajectory, from a specialist journal to anthologies and syllabuses, is the standard biography of a critical landmark, and its argument has outlasted the psychoanalytic vocabulary in which parts of it were first framed.

### Q: Which book carried Kahn's argument into a full study of masculine identity in 1981?

The book is Man's Estate: Masculine Identity in Shakespeare, published by the University of California Press in 1981. Its fourth chapter, "Coming of Age: Marriage and Manhood in Romeo and Juliet and The Taming of the Shrew," pairs the Verona essay's argument with a reading of Petruchio's taming, and states the play's conflict as a war between manhood as violence on behalf of the fathers and manhood as separation from the fathers through union with women. The book widened the essay's local insight into a general theory of Shakespearean manhood, and in doing so founded the masculinity-studies line of Shakespeare criticism that later scholars developed.

### Q: Why does Kahn argue that Verona's daughters get no adolescence?

Kahn argues that daughters get no adolescence because Verona grants girls no sanctioned period of experiment with adult identities or activities, the interval sons are allowed for trying on the lover, the wit, and the duellist. A daughter passes directly from childhood to wifehood on a schedule set by her father, as Juliet does between Capulet's first caution in 1.2 and his ultimatum in 3.5. Her mother states the curriculum as a fact of nature, that ladies of esteem are already made mothers at Juliet's age. The absence of the interval, Kahn shows, dictates the tempo of Juliet's rebellion: a person with no licensed time to grow must seize adulthood in days.

### Q: Why does Kahn begin her account of Verona with the servants' talk of maidenheads?

Kahn begins with the servants because their opening banter states the feud's whole gender economy in its crudest form, before the principals dress it in honor. Sampson's boasts in 1.1, to thrust Montague's maids to the wall and to cut off their maidenheads, fuse the feud's violence with sexual domination: defeating the enemy house means unmanning its men and possessing its women. For Kahn the exchange is not low comic filler but the system's foundation document, proof that the quarrel between the houses is from its first minute a competition among men in which women figure as territory. The gentlemen's honor code later in the play is this banter with better manners.

### Q: How does Kahn explain Romeo's "effeminate" speech after Mercutio's death?

Kahn explains the speech as the moment the feud's definition of manhood repossesses Romeo from inside his own mouth. Blaming Juliet's beauty for softening valour's steel in 3.1, Romeo describes love as a solvent of masculinity, accepting the code's premise that tenderness and manhood are opposites. His cure is the code's cure: he abandons lenity, kills Tybalt, and re-earns his standing among men at the price of banishment and the marriage. For Kahn the speech is the tragedy's pivot because it shows the rite working without any enemy's hand on the lever; the socialization is complete enough that the defecting son shames himself back into line.

### Q: What work does the Friar's charge that Romeo's tears are womanish do in Kahn's reading?

The charge shows, for Kahn, that the patriarchy's gender policing is administered even by the play's kindest authority figure and in its most private space. In the cell in 3.3 the Friar, Romeo's one adult ally, can find no stronger argument against despair than a manhood audit: the form cries out he is a man, the tears are womanish, the grief unmanly. Kahn reads the moment as evidence that the code has no outside in Verona; the alternative household the lovers seek in the Friar's cell still speaks the square's language. The rebuke also sets up her name reading, since Romeo's wish to cut his name out answers a world that has told him his identity and his shame are the same organ.

### Q: How does Kahn read the golden statues promised in the final scene?

Kahn reads the statues as the patriarchal system's final transaction, in which the dead children are converted into monuments and the feud's competition continues by other means. Capulet's handshake is offered as his daughter's jointure, a marriage settlement paid over her corpse, and the fathers then bid against each other in gold, Montague raising Juliet's statue in pure gold, Capulet matching it with Romeo's. For Kahn the exchange proves that the order has absorbed the catastrophe without changing: the houses that consumed their heirs now compete in commemorating them, and the generation that might have inherited Verona is reduced to its decoration. The statues are inheritance with the inheritors removed.

### Q: What does Kahn find in Capulet's phrase "the hopeful lady of my earth"?

Kahn finds the marriage market's whole valuation of a daughter compressed into one phrase of tenderness. When Capulet calls Juliet the hopeful lady of his earth in 1.2, the affection is real, and so is the accounting: she is his land, his estate, and his hope, the vessel through whom the house expects heirs and continuity. The phrase matters to Kahn's argument because it shows the system operating at its most loving rather than its most brutal. The father who speaks it will, within days, threaten to cast the same daughter into the streets for refusing the match, and Kahn's point is that the two speeches are one logic at different temperatures: the daughter is treasured as an asset and punished as one.

### Q: How did the essay's reprinting in The Woman's Part in 1983 fix its place in feminist criticism?

The reprinting placed the essay inside the founding anthology of feminist Shakespeare criticism, The Woman's Part: Feminist Criticism of Shakespeare, edited by Carolyn Ruth Swift Lenz, Gayle Greene, and Carol Thomas Neely for the University of Illinois Press. Anthologization did three things at once: it certified the essay as a representative achievement of the new field, it put the argument into the hands of the graduate students who would teach the next generation, and it fixed Romeo and Juliet as one of the plays on which feminist method had proved itself. Kahn's Verona became a shared reference point, the position colleagues cited in order to extend, qualify, or oppose it, which is the working definition of a landmark text.

### Q: What does Jonathan Goldberg object to in the way Kahn treats the lovers' love?

Goldberg objects that Kahn treats the lovers' love as a natural, healthy force standing outside the patriarchal order and opposed to it, when the love is itself a cultural construction the play helps to produce. In the debate later surveyed by Paul A. Kottman, Goldberg groups Kahn with Janet Adelman as critics who idealize the bond, locating authenticity in the lovers and ideology only in the fathers. The objection has force against the essay's occasional language of natural growth repressed by society. It has less force against the essay's causal analysis, which concerns what the feud does in scene after scene, and which survives the admission that the lovers' idiom, like everything else in Verona, arrived with a history.

### Q: How does Edward Snow's 1985 essay carry Kahn's reading of the lovers' language further?

Edward Snow's "Language and Sexual Difference in Romeo and Juliet," published in 1985 in Shakespeare's "Rough Magic," the collection Kahn co-edited with Peter Erickson, extends her account of the lovers' passage from formal to mutual speech by giving the passage a sexual politics. Where Kahn, following Harry Levin, credits Juliet with initiating the departure from Petrarchan formality, Snow argues that Juliet's language is throughout the more grounded and generous instrument, and that Romeo's development is an education in mutuality administered by a woman's words. The essay thus confirms Kahn's asymmetry thesis, that Juliet grows while Romeo is recalled, from inside the verse itself, and it models how her framework could be developed by critics working scene by scene rather than system by system.

### Q: What does Charles Conaway dispute about Kahn's claim that marriage separates sons from the father's house?

Charles Conaway disputes the direction of the marriage bond: writing on teaching the play in 2011, he argues that courtship and marriage in the period were themselves coming-of-age rituals that guided youths into paternal culture, manhood and household headship, rather than separating them from it. The objection qualifies Kahn's hinge claim, since the institution Romeo enters was designed as patriarchy's own graduation ceremony. The qualification is absorbed by the particulars of this marriage, which was contracted without paternal consent, knowledge, or settlement, and is therefore experienced by the Capulet household as theft rather than succession. Conaway corrects the anthropology; the play's facts, a secret marriage against both fathers, keep Kahn's application of it standing.

### Q: How does Jill Levenson's work on the play's codes of violence build on the ground Kahn cleared?

Jill L. Levenson, editor of the Oxford Shakespeare Romeo and Juliet of 2000, anatomized the play's street honor in her essay "'Alla stoccado carries it away': Codes of Violence in Romeo and Juliet," treating duelling etiquette, fencing fashions, and the choreography of insult as a rule-bound social system a modern audience must be taught to read. That editorial project presupposes Kahn's premise: the violence is not noise around the love story but a codified institution with its own logic, pedagogy, and costs. Where Kahn supplied the system's function, the manufacture of men, Levenson supplied its rulebook, and the combination is now the standard scholarly equipment for the play's first and third acts.

### Q: Does Kahn present Shakespeare as criticizing Verona's patriarchy, or as sharing its values?

Kahn presents Shakespeare as criticizing it, and the claim is built into her thesis sentence, that the feud is an expression of patriarchal society which Shakespeare shows to be tragically self-destructive. Her evidence is structural: the play gives the system's logic its fullest statement in the mouths of its victims, arranges for the lovers' alternative bond to expose the order's cruelty by contrast, and ends with the fathers transacting over the bodies their system consumed. Critics who resist the attribution argue that a commercial playwright of the 1590s cannot be recruited so cleanly to a modern politics. Kahn's answer, implicit in her method, is that the critique is in the construction, whatever the constructor's opinions: the play keeps showing the machine and its bill.

### Q: Why does Kahn call the lovers tragic scapegoats of the feud?

Kahn calls them scapegoats because their deaths perform the feud's final service to the order that killed them: the community discharges its guilt onto the two bodies and purchases reconciliation at their expense. The fathers are reconciled, the Prince distributes pardon and punishment, and the city receives its moral, while the system of households, honor, and arranged marriage that produced the catastrophe survives unindicted. A scapegoat, in the ritual sense Kahn invokes, is not merely an innocent victim but a mechanism of social repair, and that is her bleak point about the ending. Verona is healed by the deaths in a way that lets Verona continue, which is why the monument that swallows the lovers becomes, in her reading, the patriarchy's own emblem.

### Q: Where does Kahn's psychoanalytic method most strain the text?

The strain is greatest in the Friar's cell, where Kahn reads Romeo's wish to locate his name in his anatomy and cut it out as a symbolic attempt at self-castration. The name reading itself is well anchored, since the play broods on names from the orchard scene onward and treats Montague as an enlistment. The castration algebra imports a Freudian equivalence the lines never state, and it has drawn the essay's most consistent objection, registered even by sympathetic reviewers. The damage is local rather than structural. The scene demonstrates her thesis without the flourish, since the Friar's womanish rebuke and Romeo's shame establish the manhood audit in the play's own vocabulary, and the essay's system stands when the paragraph is discounted.

### Q: What role does the Prince play in Kahn's account of Verona's order?

The Prince is, for Kahn, the proof that Verona's law governs eruptions and never causes. Escalus opens the play by threatening the patriarchs' lives if the streets are disturbed again, yet his jurisdiction stops at the household door: the marriage market, the honor code, and the fathers' disposal of their children lie outside his edicts, and his own kin are drawn into the feud's losses before the end. Viewed from the standpoint of the law he embodies, Kahn notes, it is the old men who appear childishly refractory, an inversion that exposes the order's senility. His closing settlement, pardons and punishments distributed over the tomb, completes her pattern, since the law ratifies the fathers' reconciliation and leaves their system standing.

### Q: Why does Kahn treat the play's bawdy as part of the feud's machinery rather than comic relief?

Kahn treats the bawdy as machinery because, in her reading, sexual joking is one of the instruments by which the male group polices its members and defines women as spoils. Mercutio's conjuring of Romeo by Rosaline's body parts is a recall ritual, summoning the straying lover back to the collective by renaming his desire as anatomy; the servants' maidenhead banter prices the enemy's women as territory; even the Nurse's earthiness carries the curriculum of the marriage bed. None of this is relief from the tragedy's concerns, since it states those concerns in the vernacular. The reading permanently changed how the play's comedy is taught: the laughter is the sound of the system at ease, rehearsing in jest the violence it will perform in earnest in the square.
