On the morning of 31 December 1999, on a windswept tarmac at Kandahar's airport, India's foreign minister Jaswant Singh stepped off a special flight from Delhi and walked towards a small group of three men flanked by Taliban fighters. One of those three, a stout cleric with a thick beard and dark glasses, would walk on a few minutes later as the most consequential prisoner India had ever released. His name was Maulana Masood Azhar, and within thirty-one days he would establish Jaish-e-Mohammed in Bahawalpur, the city of his birth. Within seven hundred and seven days he would order the storming of India's Parliament. Within six thousand and two hundred days, his organisation would kill forty Indian paramilitaries on a Pulwama highway. The decision Singh executed in Kandahar that morning has produced more Indian casualties than any single diplomatic concession in the country's history.

This is the central biographical fact of India's twenty-six-year shadow war: every targeted killing across Karachi, Lahore, Sialkot, and Bahawalpur traces back, by a chain of consequence no participant in 1999 could foresee, to the moment a hostage crisis was resolved by handing three militants over to a Taliban regime that would shelter them just long enough for them to walk south into Pakistan. The hijackers wanted four men freed. India released three. One of those three is the subject of this article. The other two, Ahmed Omar Saeed Sheikh and Mushtaq Ahmed Zargar, deserve their own studies. But the man who founded a parallel jihadist enterprise the moment he crossed into Pakistani territory, who married a niece of the chief of Pakistan's most powerful religious party, who recruited fighters at Friday sermons in Karachi, who was photographed in Islamabad addressing crowds of ten thousand armed men eight months after his release, is the figure whose career most exhaustively answers the question that hangs over the entire shadow war: what does a state owe a person it should never have released?

The argument of this profile is simple. The figure born in Bahawalpur on 10 July 1968, drawn into the Harkat-ul-Mujahideen magazine office in the late 1980s, captured in Anantnag in February 1994, and freed in Kandahar five years later, is not best understood as a terrorist who happened to receive Pakistani patronage. He is best understood as the proof of concept for a Pakistani strategic doctrine that treats jihadist commanders as deniable instruments of foreign policy, and as the personal embodiment of a specific Indian failure of imagination in 1999 that has cost the country thousands of lives in the years since. The shadow war is not, in this sense, a separate conflict. It is the slow correction of a single decision made on a single tarmac on a single morning in late December.

## The World That Produced Him

The Bahawalpur into which Muhammad Masood Azhar Alvi was born on 10 July 1968 was a Punjabi market city of dust, canal irrigation, mango orchards, and Deobandi seminaries. The family lived in the Model Town colony, a planned neighbourhood of low brick houses built around a central garden in the postwar years. His father, Allah Bakhsh Shabbir, was the headmaster of a small government school and a part-time prayer leader at a local mosque whose theology drew on the conservative reformism of the Darul Uloom Deoband school of South Asian Islam. The household kept a few dairy buffaloes and a handful of poultry, and Allah Bakhsh supplemented his teacher's salary with the milk and eggs the family sold from a small shed at the back. He was, in 1968, a respected if unremarkable figure in a city of two hundred thousand people, the third of eleven children he and his wife would raise in a country that was barely two decades old and visibly uncertain of its direction.

Bahawalpur in the 1960s sat on a frontier of competing identities. Until 1955 it had been a princely state, ruled by a nawab whose lineage stretched back to the eighteenth century, and the city retained the architecture and habits of court patronage long after political integration into West Pakistan dissolved the formal kingdom. The dominant population spoke Saraiki, a Punjabi dialect with strong Sufi and folk traditions, and the city's older mosques had historically been Barelvi, the school of South Asian Islam that incorporates saint veneration and devotional ritual into orthodox practice. The Deobandi current that shaped the Azhar household was newer, sharper, and politically self-conscious. Its seminaries, built across rural Punjab in the 1950s and 1960s with funding from a mixture of local philanthropy and Saudi religious bursaries, taught a stripped-down theology of textual literalism, gender segregation, and a politicised reading of the early Islamic conquests. The young Masood absorbed this environment from infancy. By the time he was eight, he could recite long passages of the Quran from memory and had begun the supplementary study of Arabic grammar at his father's small mosque school.

The boy's formal schooling lasted only until the eighth class. The Pakistani government school he attended in Bahawalpur was, by his own later telling, a place of disorder and indifference, and his father's preference was clear. In 1981, at the age of thirteen, Masood was enrolled at the Jamia Uloom-ul-Islamia in Karachi's Binori Town, one of the largest and most influential Deobandi seminaries in the country. The Binori Town complex, founded in 1954 by Maulana Muhammad Yusuf Banuri, occupied a sprawling campus near the Sohrab Goth bus terminal, with hostels for two thousand students and a teaching faculty that included clerics who had themselves studied at the original Darul Uloom in India before partition. It was here that the religious imagination of an entire generation of South Asian Sunni militancy was formed. Mullah Omar, the founder of the Afghan Taliban, would later be associated with the same network of Deobandi seminaries that fed Binori Town its students and ideology. Masood Azhar studied at Binori Town for eight years, graduating in 1989 with the formal degree of an alim, a recognised religious scholar within the Deobandi tradition.

The Karachi he encountered as a teenager was not the cosmopolitan port city of the 1950s but a megalopolis being violently restructured by the war next door. The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 had transformed Pakistan, and Karachi most of all, into the rear logistical hub for a CIA-funded, Saudi-bankrolled, ISI-managed campaign of mujahideen warfare against the Soviet army. By the mid-1980s, Karachi's Deobandi seminaries were sending students across the border by the hundreds, and the Binori Town faculty included clerics who openly recruited for the Afghan jihad in their Friday sermons. Masood was, by every account from his classmates published in later years, an indifferent student of the formal curriculum but a precocious reader of jihadist polemic. He failed the physical training that was a prerequisite for combat duty and was redirected to the editorial side of the project. The Harkat-ul-Mujahideen, then a loose grouping of Pakistani fighters who had crossed into Afghanistan to fight under Jalaluddin Haqqani's command, ran a Karachi-based monthly magazine called Sada-e-Mujahid, the Voice of the Holy Warrior, and it was to this office that the new alim was assigned.

Editing Sada-e-Mujahid was the apprenticeship that made him. Between 1989 and 1993 he wrote, commissioned, and edited hundreds of articles on jihadist theology, battlefield reports from Afghanistan, denunciations of Indian rule in Kashmir, and biographical hagiographies of mujahideen who had been killed fighting the Soviets. His prose was vivid, unusually well-cadenced for the genre, and laced with quotations from Urdu poetry and classical Arabic verse. Circulation rose from a few thousand to a reported sixty thousand copies a month. He travelled to Saudi Arabia, the Gulf states, the United Kingdom, and reportedly to Somalia in this period to raise funds and recruit students for the broader Harkat network. By his early twenties he had become a figure of regional reputation in Pakistani jihadist circles, known not as a fighter but as the cleric who could explain to a Bahawalpur farmer's son why he should travel to Khost, why he should fire a rocket at a Soviet helicopter, and why his death would be a wedding rather than a funeral.

The travel patterns of the Sada-e-Mujahid years deserve closer attention because they established the international donor architecture that would later finance Jaish-e-Mohammed's first decade. The fundraising tours took him to a half-dozen Saudi cities including Riyadh, Jeddah, Dammam, and Medina, where he addressed Pakistani-expatriate gatherings at private homes and small mosques. The Saudi religious authorities, in this period of the late 1980s and early 1990s, were officially supportive of the broader Afghan jihad and informally tolerant of the supplementary fundraising for Pakistani militant outfits operating in Kashmir. He was hosted, by his own subsequent accounts published in Al-Qalam, at the homes of several mid-rank Saudi clerics whose names he later cited as patrons of the project. He travelled twice to the United Kingdom in 1991 and 1992, addressing audiences at Deobandi-affiliated mosques in Birmingham, Bradford, and east London. The British Pakistani community of those years included a layer of first-generation immigrants from the Mirpur district of Pakistan-administered Kashmir whose remittance patterns and political grievances made them a natural fundraising base for the Kashmir-focused side of the Harkat agenda. The BBC's later observation that he was "the man who brought jihad to Britain" captures the practical fact that his early-1990s tours laid down a recruitment and donation pipeline that subsequent Jaish-affiliated operations exploited for years.

The Somali trip in 1993, less well documented than the Saudi or British tours but referenced in his own published writings, was undertaken in the immediate aftermath of the United States Operation Restore Hope deployment to Mogadishu and was, by his own account, intended to assess the prospects for jihadist organisational expansion into the Horn of Africa. He met with figures associated with Al-Ittihad al-Islamiyya in Mogadishu and the surrounding regions, returned via Yemen and the Gulf, and produced a long Sada-e-Mujahid essay on the strategic position of the Muslim Horn of Africa that was widely reprinted in the Pakistani religious press. The essay is rarely cited in profiles of his career but is significant because it represents the first documented case of his thinking outside the South Asian theatre, and because it positioned him in his late twenties as a strategic theorist of pan-Islamic jihadist expansion rather than as a regional Kashmir-focused operative. The international scope of his early thinking, by his own framing, was a constitutive feature of his self-conception, even though his subsequent career remained anchored almost entirely to the South Asian conflict.

In 1993 the Harkat-ul-Mujahideen merged with a smaller faction, the Harkat-ul-Jihad-al-Islami, to form the Harkat-ul-Ansar, and the new organisation immediately turned its operational focus from a winding-down Afghan war to the Kashmir insurgency that had erupted in the Indian-administered valley in 1989. Azhar's role in the merged group was a senior one. He was officially the General Secretary, with responsibility for ideological output, fundraising, and the cultivation of relations with the Pakistani religious establishment. The Harkat-ul-Ansar's military commander, Sajjad Afghani, was a Kashmiri who had fought in Afghanistan and now wished to lead operations across the Line of Control into the Indian-administered valley. The two men, the cleric and the commander, made a complementary pair. Afghani provided the operational cadre. Azhar provided the religious authority, the recruitment network, and the international fundraising base. By the end of 1993 the Harkat-ul-Ansar had become one of the three or four most active militant outfits operating in Indian-administered Kashmir, alongside the Hizbul Mujahideen and the older, more established Jamiat-ul-Mujahideen.

The cleric's first journey across the Line of Control came in late January 1994. Travelling on a forged Portuguese passport in the name of Wali Adam Issa, he flew from Dhaka to Delhi via Bangkok and entered Indian-administered Kashmir overland from Srinagar, accompanied by Sajjad Afghani. Their stated purpose was to conduct an internal review of the Harkat-ul-Ansar's Kashmir operations and to mediate disputes between rival commanders. The pair travelled to Anantnag district, took rooms in a private house, and were preparing to return to Srinagar when an Indian Army patrol stopped their auto-rickshaw at a routine roadside check on 11 February 1994. The driver fled. Afghani drew a pistol and was shot in the leg. The cleric, by all accounts including his own later interviews, surrendered without resistance. The forged Portuguese passport was found in his luggage. Within forty-eight hours, after his fingerprints were matched against an Interpol notice and a separate Indian intelligence file, his actual identity was established. The Indian state had captured the senior ideologue of one of the three most active terrorist organisations operating in Kashmir, and it had done so without a single Indian casualty.

What followed was a five-year confinement that would have been a footnote in the longer history of the South Asian jihad if not for the events of December 1999. He was held first at the Kot Bhalwal jail in Jammu, then transferred to the high-security wing at Tihar in Delhi for interrogation, and eventually returned to a Jammu and Kashmir state prison after his case had been formally registered under the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act. He was a model prisoner in the literal sense, polite to his guards, fluent in conversation, and given to the production of long handwritten essays in Urdu on jihadist theology that were periodically smuggled out and published in Pakistani religious journals. He learned passable English from prison-issue newspapers and spent hours each day in physical exercise, which improved a constitution that had always been too soft for combat. He attempted at least one escape, in 1994, when his organisation tunnelled into the Kot Bhalwal compound from a rented house outside the perimeter wall and was discovered by guards before he could be extracted. Sajjad Afghani, the military commander captured with him, was killed in that escape attempt.

His organisation, meanwhile, made his release the central operational objective of the next five years. The Harkat-ul-Ansar's first major attempt was the kidnapping of six Western tourists in the Indian-administered Kashmir valley in July 1995 by a group calling itself Al-Faran, a name that was, on subsequent investigation by Indian and American agencies, an operational cover for a faction of the Harkat-ul-Ansar itself. The kidnappers' core demand was the release of twenty-one Pakistani militants in Indian custody, with Azhar at the top of the list. Negotiations dragged through the summer. One hostage, the Norwegian Hans Christian Ostro, was beheaded in August. The four remaining tourists, two Americans, an Englishman, and a German, were never recovered, and were assumed killed by their captors. The Indian state did not release the cleric in 1995. The same outcome was repeated in 1996, when the Harkat-ul-Ansar attempted to kidnap a New Delhi-based businessman, and again in 1998, when the organisation issued a series of public threats demanding the prisoner's exchange for unspecified concessions. By 1999 the Harkat-ul-Ansar, renamed the Harkat-ul-Mujahideen after the United States designated it a foreign terrorist organisation in October 1997, had concluded that conventional kidnap-for-prisoner tactics were not going to extract their senior cleric. A different operation was required.

That different operation was the hijacking of Indian Airlines flight 814 from Kathmandu to Delhi on 24 December 1999, an event whose tactical conduct, eight-day arc, and decision history is treated in detail in [the IC-814 hijacking complete guide](https://insightcrunch.com/2012/05/10/ic-814-hijacking-complete-guide/) and the broader [origin-point analysis of the 1999 release decision](https://insightcrunch.com/2019/08/12/ic814-release-decision-1999/). For the purposes of this profile, what matters is the position the cleric occupied in the negotiation. Of the thirty-six prisoners initially demanded by the hijackers, the Indian government refused to release thirty-three. Three names remained non-negotiable from the hijackers' side: Maulana Masood Azhar, the financial-administrator with the Karachi seminary network; Ahmed Omar Saeed Sheikh, a London School of Economics dropout convicted of kidnapping British tourists in Delhi in 1994; and Mushtaq Ahmed Zargar, the founder of the Al-Umar Mujahideen who had fought in Kashmir for nearly a decade. On the morning of 31 December, after eight days of negotiation conducted partly through Taliban intermediaries in Kandahar, the Indian cabinet agreed to release the three. Foreign Minister Jaswant Singh personally accompanied them on the flight to Kandahar to ensure the hostage exchange was completed. The aircraft's one hundred and sixty surviving passengers and crew were released. The three militants disappeared, by Taliban escort, into the southern Afghan desert, and within seventy-two hours had crossed into Pakistan via the Chaman border.

That is the world that produced him. A Bahawalpur Deobandi household, a Karachi seminary, an editor's chair at a jihadist magazine, an organisational career inside Harkat, a Kashmir mission ending in capture, a five-year imprisonment broken by the IC-814 deal. The next twenty-four years would be the world he produced.

## The Rise

He was forty-eight hours out of Indian custody when he gave the speech that announced what came next. On 4 January 2000, at the Binori Town mosque in Karachi where he had studied as a teenager, he addressed an audience that Pakistani news agencies estimated at ten thousand people, many of them armed. He had not yet eaten a proper meal since his release. His voice was hoarse, his beard wild, and his Urdu, polished by editing Sada-e-Mujahid for half a decade and seasoned by five years of prison silence, was the polished prose of a man who had thought about exactly this moment for a long time. The speech, transcribed and reprinted in the Pakistani jihadist press, was twenty-three minutes long. It made one specific announcement and several thematic ones. The specific announcement was that he was leaving the Harkat-ul-Mujahideen. The thematic ones were that India would be made to pay for Kashmir, that the United States was an enemy of Islam, that the Indian Parliament was a legitimate target, and that a new organisation would be founded to wage what he called "the army of the Prophet" against the enemies of the faith. The crowd, by every account from journalists present, responded with prolonged automatic-weapons fire into the air.

The new organisation he announced was Jaish-e-Mohammed, formally established in Bahawalpur on 31 January 2000, exactly thirty days after his release on the Kandahar tarmac. The founding meeting was held at the Jamia Masjid Subhan Allah, a large mosque-and-madrassa complex on the southern edge of Bahawalpur city that was simultaneously announced as the new group's headquarters. The founding cadre was almost entirely drawn from the Harkat-ul-Mujahideen, a defection that left the parent organisation effectively gutted. By the cleric's own subsequent claims, between two-thirds and three-quarters of Harkat's Pakistani-Punjab membership followed him into the new outfit within ninety days. The Harkat's Kashmiri commanders, by contrast, mostly stayed with the original organisation, producing a regional split that has persisted in Pakistani jihadist politics ever since. The new group's funding, by accounts later compiled by the United States Treasury and Indian intelligence, came from a mixture of three sources: the cleric's own pre-existing Gulf donor network, fresh contributions from Pakistani-domestic religious philanthropy, and direct subventions from elements of Pakistan's Inter-Services Intelligence directorate that saw in the new outfit a useful instrument for Kashmir operations.

The institutional structure he built in the first eighteen months has persisted, with modifications, for a quarter-century. The supreme command sat with the cleric himself, who took the title Amir-e-Tanzeem, the leader of the organisation. Beneath him sat a Shura Council of approximately a dozen members drawn from the founding cadre, with portfolios for finance, recruitment, training, propaganda, and what the organisation called the operational wing. The propaganda apparatus included two flagship Urdu publications, the weekly Al-Qalam and the monthly Sada-e-Mujahid, the latter title having been carried over from the Harkat days. The financial apparatus included a network of registered charitable trusts that solicited donations through madrassas, mosques, and increasingly through online platforms in the years after 2010. The training infrastructure was concentrated, in the early years, at three sites: the Bahawalpur headquarters, a camp at Balakot in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province that would later become the target of the Indian airstrike of February 2019, and a third site near Kotli in Pakistan-administered Kashmir. The recruitment pipeline ran principally through the Deobandi seminary system, with the Jamia Uloom-ul-Islamia in Karachi continuing to function as the most important supplier of cadres into the new outfit through the first decade of its existence.

What was unusual about the new organisation, by comparison with the Harkat-ul-Mujahideen from which it had been carved, was its almost immediate operational tempo. The Harkat had taken nearly a decade to build the infrastructure for cross-border infiltration into Indian-administered Kashmir. Jaish-e-Mohammed, drawing on the existing Harkat networks and operating with what intelligence assessments later described as a degree of ISI logistical assistance, was conducting infiltration operations within months of its founding. The first publicly claimed Jaish operation was a fidayeen attack on the Badami Bagh army cantonment in Srinagar on 19 April 2000, conducted by a single Pakistani-Punjabi suicide bomber who was killed at the perimeter without inflicting Indian casualties. The first successful operation, by the organisation's own measure, was the suicide truck-bombing of the Jammu and Kashmir state legislative assembly building in Srinagar on 1 October 2001, which killed thirty-eight people, most of them civilians waiting outside the building. The cleric, addressing his cadres at Bahawalpur the following Friday, called the Srinagar attack the inauguration of a new style of warfare against India.

The recruitment pattern of the new outfit's first eighteen months reveals a great deal about how it was differentiated from the Lashkar-e-Taiba and the older Hizbul Mujahideen that occupied the same Kashmir-focused operational space. Where the Lashkar-e-Taiba drew its rank-and-file primarily from rural Punjab through the Markaz Dawatul Irshad seminary system at Muridke, and where the Hizbul Mujahideen drew its cadres from the Indian-administered Kashmir valley itself, Jaish-e-Mohammed targeted recruits from the Bahawalpur, Multan, and Rahim Yar Khan districts of southern Punjab, with a smaller contingent drawn from Karachi's Deobandi seminaries. Three-quarters of the early membership, by accounts later compiled by Pakistani journalists who reported on the organisation's growth, came from these southern Punjab districts. The recruitment age skewed young, with the typical fresh entrant being a seminary student between sixteen and twenty-two years old who had completed at least the basic dars-e-nizami curriculum at a Deobandi madrassa. The training cycle ran for between six and ten weeks, with theological indoctrination and weapons familiarisation conducted in roughly equal measures, and a final phase of cross-border infiltration practice conducted at the Kotli camp in Pakistan-administered Kashmir. The casualty rate among first-deployment cadres was, by the organisation's own internal records that were later obtained by Indian agencies, between thirty and forty per cent on the first cross-border mission, a figure that the recruitment pipeline consistently replenished through the seminary network.

The political alliances the cleric cultivated in his first eighteen months as Amir-e-Tanzeem are essential to understanding the institutional protection his organisation enjoyed in the Pakistani-domestic environment. He had married Saeeda Azhar, a niece of Maulana Fazl-ur-Rehman, leader of the Jamiat Ulema-e-Islam (Fazl) faction and a powerful figure in Pakistan's religious-political establishment with a long-standing seat in the National Assembly, in the late 1990s. The marriage gave him a permanent connection to the most institutionally embedded Deobandi political party in Pakistan and produced political-cover relationships that survived multiple changes of Pakistani government. His relationship with the Pakistani Taliban movement, formalised through several documented meetings between Jaish leadership and senior Taliban figures including Mullah Mohammad Omar in the period before the September 2001 attacks, gave the new outfit access to training facilities in southern Afghanistan that supplemented its Pakistani-domestic infrastructure. His relationship with Osama bin Laden, although less institutionalised than the Taliban relationship, included documented meetings in Afghanistan in early 2000 at which the Al-Qaeda leadership reportedly contributed an initial fundraising infusion to support the new outfit's launch. None of these relationships was unique to him among Pakistani militant leaders of the period, but the combination of religious-political marriage alliance, Taliban-state access, and Al-Qaeda fundraising made his outfit unusually well-positioned within the broader Pakistani jihadist landscape.

Two months later came the operation that defined his organisation in the international imagination, and that brought South Asia closer to nuclear war than any other event in the twenty-five years before the 2025 Operation Sindoor crisis. On 13 December 2001, five Jaish-e-Mohammed fighters in a white Ambassador car bearing forged government number plates and a Home Ministry sticker drove through the gates of the Indian Parliament complex in New Delhi during a session of the upper house. They were stopped by a vigilant police constable, opened fire, and ran in five different directions across the parliament's lawns and corridors. All five were killed by Indian security forces over the course of a forty-minute battle. Eight Indian security personnel and one parliamentary gardener died with them. The Indian government's National Investigation Team, after a six-month investigation, attributed the operation jointly to Jaish-e-Mohammed and Lashkar-e-Taiba, with the cleric named in the formal charge sheet as the senior planner on the Jaish side. The mobilisation that followed, Operation Parakram, kept nearly a million Indian and Pakistani soldiers facing each other along the international border for ten months and brought the two states to the brink of a war that, by the public statements of senior officials on both sides at the time, was understood to carry a real risk of nuclear escalation.

The Pakistani state's response to the Parliament attack was the first of what would become a recurring pattern over the following two decades. Under intense American and British diplomatic pressure, Pakistan banned Jaish-e-Mohammed on 12 January 2002 and placed the cleric himself under house arrest twelve days later. He was held at a guest house in Bahawalpur, not at a regular detention facility, and was permitted to receive visitors, conduct organisational business by telephone, and continue producing the editorials that appeared in Al-Qalam under his name. The Lahore High Court, in a ruling delivered on 14 December 2002, declared the house arrest unlawful on the grounds that the government had not produced evidence sufficient to justify continued detention. He walked out of the Bahawalpur guest house that afternoon. He has not been formally detained by the Pakistani state for any sustained period since. The Operation Parakram mobilisation that followed the Parliament attack imposed costs on both states that are easily underappreciated in retrospective accounts and that materially shaped the strategic environment in which his subsequent career unfolded. The Indian deployment, ordered on 18 December 2001, moved approximately five hundred thousand troops to forward positions along the international border and the Line of Control, supported by armoured formations, artillery batteries, and air force squadrons placed on combat alert. The Pakistani counter-mobilisation moved a comparable force to defensive positions along the same frontier. Both states formally placed their nuclear command authorities on heightened readiness, and the publicly known doctrinal positions of both sides at the time included scenarios in which a conventional Indian advance into Pakistani territory would trigger Pakistani tactical-nuclear use. The standoff lasted until October 2002, when Indian forces were ordered to stand down without having conducted significant combat operations across the Line of Control. The economic cost to India of the ten-month deployment, by subsequent estimates published in the Indian defence press, exceeded ten thousand crore rupees in direct expenses and an unquantified larger sum in opportunity costs and combat readiness degradation. The political consequence, more important than the financial one, was the institutional conviction within the Indian security establishment that conventional military mobilisation in response to terror provocations short of mass-casualty levels was no longer a usable tool. Every subsequent Indian doctrinal shift, from the Cold Start concept to the surgical-strike doctrine to the eventual airstrike posture demonstrated at Balakot, traces back to the lessons learned during the post-Parliament-attack mobilisation.

The pattern, of international pressure producing nominal Pakistani action followed by judicial release within months, has been repeated at least four times in his subsequent career: in 2008 after the Mumbai siege, in 2016 after the Pathankot airbase attack, in 2019 after the Pulwama bombing, and again in 2022 in the run-up to the United Nations sanctions designation that Pakistan was attempting to head off through performative compliance.

The internal turmoil produced by the post-Parliament-attack ban and the subsequent Pakistani decision in mid-2002 to formally join the United States-led war on terror was the most serious organisational crisis the cleric faced in the first decade of Jaish-e-Mohammed. The rank and file of the organisation, drawn substantially from veterans of the Afghan war who had transferred their loyalties from the Soviet front to the Kashmir front in the 1990s, regarded any cooperation with the Pakistani state in the post-9/11 environment as theological apostasy. A faction led by a senior commander named Maulana Abdul Jabbar broke from the cleric's leadership and joined what became the early Pakistani Taliban network, conducting suicide operations against Pakistani officials between March and September 2002 in Islamabad, Karachi, Murree, Taxila, and the cleric's own Bahawalpur. Two assassination attempts against President Pervez Musharraf in December 2003 were attributed by Pakistani investigators to a coalition of dissident Jaish-e-Mohammed cadres and members of the Lashkar-e-Jhangvi sectarian outfit. The cleric, now publicly committed to the Pakistani state's operational red lines while privately rebuilding his Kashmir-focused operational wing, navigated the crisis by producing what intelligence analysts later described as a doctrine of compartmentalised loyalty. Operations against Indian targets in Kashmir were maintained and intensified. Operations against Pakistani officials, even by formerly Jaish-affiliated commanders, were publicly disowned. The boundary held, more or less, for the next decade.

By the second half of the 2000s, with the Pakistani state distracted by its own internal Taliban insurgency and the increasingly severe American pressure of the post-Mumbai-attack period, Jaish-e-Mohammed had reconstituted itself as the principal Pakistan-based vehicle for high-tempo operations against Indian targets. The cleric handed day-to-day operational responsibility for what the organisation called the military wing to his younger brother, Abdul Rauf Asghar, who had been one of the IC-814 hijackers in 1999 and who would, in the years after 2008, run the planning cells responsible for some of the deadliest cross-border operations of the post-2015 period. The cleric himself receded into a more strategic role, focusing on fundraising, ideological output, recruitment, and the cultivation of his Pakistani religious-political alliances. His public appearances became less frequent. His Friday sermons at the Bahawalpur headquarters complex continued, but the audio recordings circulated by the organisation increasingly took the form of pre-recorded statements rather than live addresses. The figure of the Bahawalpur cleric was being deliberately mythologised, and a degree of operational distance was being inserted between the leader and the operations conducted in the leader's name. That distance would matter enormously in the decade that followed.

## Major Actions and Decisions

The most consequential decisions of his post-2008 career fall into four discrete clusters: the cultivation of the deep ISI relationship that allowed Jaish-e-Mohammed to operate from Bahawalpur with a freedom unavailable to most other Pakistani militant groups, the strategic choice to target Indian military installations rather than civilian sites in the post-2015 operational tempo, the handling of the Pulwama operation in February 2019 and its airstrike consequence, and the personal decision to disappear from public life in the period after the Indian airstrike on Balakot. Each cluster reveals something distinct about how the cleric understood his own role and the position of his organisation within the Pakistani strategic architecture.

The ISI relationship is the load-bearing structural fact of Jaish-e-Mohammed's existence after 2003. The publicly available evidence on this relationship has accumulated through three principal channels: the testimony of captured Jaish operatives in Indian National Investigation Agency charge sheets, the leaks of internal Pakistani security documents that periodically surface in the international press, and the analytical work of Pakistani journalists and former officials who have written about the country's strategic-depth doctrine in retirement. The Pakistani journalist Zahid Hussain, in his book Frontline Pakistan, describes the cleric's relationship with the ISI's S-Wing, the directorate responsible for relations with militant proxies, as one in which operational autonomy was traded for institutional protection. The cleric was permitted to run his Bahawalpur headquarters, his madrassa system, and his Kashmir-focused operational wing without serious interference from Pakistani law enforcement. In return, the operations he authorised were expected to fall within parameters set by the ISI's strategic planners, parameters that included the rough timing, the choice of targets, and the public claim of responsibility. The American journalist Carlotta Gall, in The Wrong Enemy, reaches a similar conclusion through different evidence, describing the Jaish leadership as embedded within a Pakistani security architecture that treated the organisation as an instrument of policy rather than a security threat.

The strategic choice to target Indian military installations rather than civilian sites in the post-2015 operational tempo represents a doctrinal evolution that distinguishes Jaish-e-Mohammed from its principal Pakistan-based rival, Lashkar-e-Taiba. Where the Lashkar's operational signature, established in [the 26/11 Mumbai attacks](https://insightcrunch.com/2012/02/04/lashkar-e-taiba-complete-guide/) and reinforced in subsequent operations, has emphasised mass-casualty attacks on civilian sites with international media visibility, the Jaish under the cleric's direction shifted in the second half of the 2010s towards a doctrine of high-symbolic-value strikes on Indian military targets. The September 2016 attack on the Indian Army's Uri brigade headquarters that killed nineteen soldiers and triggered India's first publicly acknowledged surgical strikes across the Line of Control, treated in detail in [the Uri attack analysis](https://insightcrunch.com/2012/05/22/uri-attack-2016-explained/), was attributed to a Jaish faction. The January 2016 assault on [the Pathankot airbase](https://insightcrunch.com/2012/05/16/pathankot-airbase-attack-explained/) was a pure Jaish operation, planned at the Bahawalpur headquarters and executed by a four-man team that infiltrated across the Punjab border and held the airbase perimeter for nearly four days. The February 2019 vehicle-borne improvised explosive attack on a Central Reserve Police Force convoy on the Jammu-Srinagar highway, which killed forty paramilitaries and is reconstructed in detail in [the Pulwama attack analysis](https://insightcrunch.com/2012/05/28/pulwama-attack-2019-explained/), was the highest-casualty single Jaish-e-Mohammed operation against Indian security forces in the organisation's history.

The Pathankot operation merits closer attention because it is the most well-documented case of the cleric's personal involvement in operational planning at a level that the post-2008 mythology had obscured. The four fighters who infiltrated the Pathankot airbase on 1 January 2016 had been trained at the Bahawalpur and Balakot complexes during the autumn of 2015. The handler who directed their operations from inside Pakistan was Shahid Latif, a Sialkot-based commander who had been a Jaish-e-Mohammed operative since 2002 and whose own profile is treated separately in [the Latif elimination article](https://insightcrunch.com/2013/04/19/shahid-latif-pathankot-mastermind/). Indian intercepts of the operational communications conducted between Latif and the Pathankot fighters during the four-day siege, declassified in part by the National Investigation Agency in subsequent charge sheets, indicate that Latif was reporting back to a Bahawalpur-based command cell during the operation. The cleric himself, by the NIA's reconstruction, was present at the Bahawalpur command cell for at least the first thirty-six hours of the operation. Seven Indian security personnel and one civilian died in the assault. All four attackers were killed. The Pakistani government, in a gesture that has since become a textbook case of the diplomacy-without-action pattern, accepted an Indian invitation to send a Joint Investigation Team to inspect the airbase site, and the Joint Investigation Team returned to Islamabad without filing a substantive report.

The Pulwama operation in February 2019 is the case that best illustrates the operational distance the cleric had cultivated between himself and his organisation's actions. The bomber, Adil Ahmad Dar, was a twenty-year-old Kashmiri from Pulwama district who had been recruited into Jaish-e-Mohammed in early 2018 through a combination of personal grievance against Indian security forces, social-media radicalisation, and contact with a Kashmiri Jaish recruiter named Mudasir Khan. Dar's vehicle, a Maruti Eeco minivan packed with approximately three hundred kilograms of a mixed explosive charge that included ammonium nitrate, RDX, and gelatine sticks, was driven into the side of a Central Reserve Police Force bus on the Jammu-Srinagar highway near Lethpora at 3:15 PM on 14 February 2019. Forty paramilitaries died in the explosion. The cleric, then fifty years old and reportedly suffering from chronic kidney disease that required regular dialysis, was at the Bahawalpur headquarters on the day of the attack. By the time the Indian Air Force's Mirage 2000 fighters dropped Israeli-supplied SPICE 2000 precision bombs on the Jaish-e-Mohammed training camp at Balakot in the early hours of 26 February 2019, twelve days after Pulwama, the cleric had reportedly already been moved to a safe house outside Bahawalpur. He has not been seen in a documented public appearance since.

The twelve-day decision arc between the Pulwama bombing on 14 February 2019 and the Indian airstrike on Balakot on 26 February 2019 deserves separate treatment because it represents the most consequential single sequence of crisis management in the Indian-Pakistani relationship since the 2001 to 2002 standoff. The Indian Cabinet Committee on Security met three times in the seventy-two hours after the Pulwama attack, with the participation of the Prime Minister, the Defence Minister, the External Affairs Minister, the Home Minister, the National Security Adviser, the chiefs of the three services, and the heads of the Research and Analysis Wing and the Intelligence Bureau. The military options reportedly tabled at the first meeting included a surface-to-surface missile strike on training facilities in Pakistan-administered Kashmir, a special-forces ground raid analogous to the post-Uri 2016 surgical strikes, and the airstrike option that was eventually executed. The political-strategic argument for the airstrike, advanced by the National Security Adviser and accepted by the Cabinet Committee on Security, was that an airstrike inside Pakistan proper, as distinct from Pakistan-administered Kashmir, would impose a strategic cost on the Pakistani state that the previous post-attack responses had failed to impose, and that the Jaish-e-Mohammed training camp at Balakot in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province represented a target whose destruction could plausibly be characterised as anti-terror rather than anti-Pakistan. The Indian Air Force's 7 Squadron based at Gwalior was tasked with the mission. Twelve Mirage 2000 aircraft, escorted by Sukhoi Su-30 MKI fighters and supported by an airborne early warning aircraft, crossed the Line of Control at high altitude in the early hours of 26 February. Five SPICE 2000 precision-guided bombs were released at the Jaish-e-Mohammed Balakot complex from approximately fifty kilometres standoff range. The Pakistani retaliation the following day produced an aerial engagement in which an Indian MiG-21 and reportedly a Pakistani F-16 were lost, and the Indian pilot Wing Commander Abhinandan Varthaman was captured and subsequently returned. The cleric, by the most credible reconstructions, had been moved from the Bahawalpur residence to a safe house outside the city in the days before the airstrike, a relocation that suggests either Pakistani-state-coordinated protective action or an internal organisational anticipation of the strike that the cleric himself had read correctly. The fourth cluster of major decisions concerns the management of his post-Balakot disappearance. The Indian airstrike on the Balakot training camp, the first time an Indian aircraft had struck a target inside Pakistan proper since the 1971 war, removed the assumption of geographical sanctuary that had governed Pakistani strategic thinking since the introduction of nuclear weapons into the subcontinent in 1998. The cleric's response was to vanish from documented public life. He has issued no audio statement under his own voice that has been authenticated by Indian or Western intelligence since early 2019. His Friday sermons at the Bahawalpur headquarters complex were taken over, after a transition period of approximately six months, by his younger brother Abdul Rauf Asghar. His public political activities, which had included rallies in Lahore and Islamabad as recently as 2018, ceased entirely. Whether this disappearance reflects a deliberate strategic choice on his own part to reduce his targetability, a Pakistani security-state decision to compartmentalise him for diplomatic reasons, or a more serious deterioration of his health that has rendered him incapable of public appearances, is the subject of the active disagreement that runs through the next two sections of this profile.

## The Person Behind the Organization

What is most striking about the figure that emerges from the available evidence on the man's interior life is the gap between the public mythology of the warrior-cleric and the documentary record of a chronic-illness sufferer with literary tastes, family preoccupations, and a near-obsessive interest in administrative detail. The mythology, constructed in the pages of Al-Qalam and Sada-e-Mujahid and propagated through the Bahawalpur Friday sermons of the 2000s and 2010s, presents a figure of unwavering theological certainty whose every breath is dedicated to the war against the enemies of Islam. The documentary record, drawn from the testimony of captured operatives, the leaked correspondence that has surfaced in the international press over the past decade, and the analytical work of scholars who have examined his published writings, reveals a more textured person.

The chronic illness is the most material fact about his daily life, and the one most consistently underemphasised in the public-facing materials his organisation produces. He has suffered from advanced renal failure since at least 2017, requiring regular dialysis at private medical facilities in Karachi, Lahore, and reportedly Riyadh during periods of Saudi medical residence. His weight, photographically documented in the few public images released since 2019, has fluctuated significantly. His mobility, by accounts from former Jaish-e-Mohammed members who have defected to Indian intelligence, is significantly impaired. The dialysis schedule has imposed practical constraints on his movement that the organisation's mythology cannot fully accommodate, and the operational consequences of this medical fact have shaped his post-2019 disappearance in ways that have not been publicly acknowledged by Jaish-e-Mohammed itself.

The literary tastes are a second underappreciated dimension. His personal library, photographically documented in a 2014 profile published by a sympathetic Pakistani Urdu magazine, included extensive holdings of classical Urdu poetry, particularly the work of Mirza Ghalib and Allama Iqbal, alongside the expected jihadist polemic and theological reference works. His own published writings, particularly the long-form essays serialised in Sada-e-Mujahid throughout the 2000s and 2010s, are unusually well-cadenced for the genre, drawing on Urdu poetic structures and classical Arabic rhetorical devices in ways that distinguish his prose from the more functional output of his peers in the Pakistani jihadist press. The American scholar of Pakistani militancy Christine Fair, who has read his writings extensively in the original Urdu, has described his prose as the most consistently literary in the South Asian jihadist canon, a description that the cleric himself, with his self-conscious cultivation of the warrior-poet image, would presumably regard as an unintended compliment.

The family preoccupations are the third dimension. He married Saeeda Azhar, a niece of the Jamiat Ulema-e-Islam (Fazl) leader Maulana Fazl-ur-Rehman, in the late 1990s, and has at least four children by her, the eldest of whom is reportedly involved in the organisation's administrative apparatus. His own siblings, eleven in number including the cleric himself, have been the subject of recurrent operational concern within Jaish-e-Mohammed. The younger brother, Abdul Rauf Asghar, has run the operational wing since 2007 and is himself the subject of a United States Treasury Specially Designated Global Terrorist designation. A second brother, Ibrahim Athar, was one of the IC-814 hijackers in 1999 and has remained active in the organisation's leadership. The elder brother, Muhammad Tahir Anwar, who served as the head of the organisation's military affairs wing for more than two decades, died under unexplained circumstances at his Bahawalpur residence at the age of sixty-two, an event whose suspicious context is examined in [the Tahir Anwar profile](https://insightcrunch.com/2013/05/07/muhammad-tahir-anwar-azhar-brother/). The cleric, by every account from people who have observed him in private settings, is intensely preoccupied with his family's welfare, an emotional fact that the systematic targeting of his inner circle in the period after 2022 has made operationally consequential.

The administrative-detail interest is the fourth dimension, and perhaps the most revealing of how he has actually held his organisation together for a quarter-century in conditions of significant external pressure. He is, by the testimony of multiple former Jaish operatives now in Indian custody, a meticulous record-keeper. Membership rolls, financial accounts, training-camp attendance lists, and operational expense ledgers have been maintained with a discipline unusual in the Pakistani jihadist landscape. The organisation's recruitment, training, and deployment cycles have been managed through what the captured operatives describe as a near-bureaucratic system of internal documentation. This administrative competence, rather than any battlefield charisma or theological brilliance, is the cleric's principal organisational asset. It is also the asset that has made his organisation more resilient to leadership-decapitation strikes than its less institutionalised rivals. When senior commanders are eliminated in the systematic targeting campaign documented in [the Azhar network siege analysis](https://insightcrunch.com/2013/05/13/masood-azhar-network-under-siege/), their replacements step into roles whose responsibilities, reporting lines, and operational expectations are documented with sufficient precision that the organisational machinery continues to function.

The relationship between the public mythology and the private record can be summarised in a single comparative observation. He is presented in the organisation's propaganda as a man of religious certainty conducting a holy war from a position of righteous strength. He has functioned, in the documentary record, as an administrator of an organisation that has survived a quarter-century of internal schisms, external pressures, and the methodical elimination of his closest associates by a campaign whose attribution he is publicly forbidden by Pakistani diplomatic constraint to acknowledge. The first portrait is the one that recruits seventeen-year-old Bahawalpur farmers into the training camps. The second is the one that has actually held the organisation together. Both are real. But the second is the one the shadow war is testing.

The theological position that the cleric has articulated across a quarter-century of Friday sermons and published essays is unusually internally coherent for the South Asian Deobandi-jihadist canon, and is worth specifying because it shapes the recruitment, motivation, and operational discipline of his organisation. He has consistently argued, drawing on the classical Hanafi jurisprudence taught at the Karachi Binori Town seminary where he studied, that the Indian-administered Kashmir valley is dar-ul-harb, an abode of war in the formal Islamic legal sense, and that the religious obligation of jihad against its non-Muslim ruling authority is binding on every able-bodied Muslim until the territory is liberated. He has consistently rejected the more permissive readings of the same canon that would treat Kashmir as a contested but not formally hostile territory, and he has consistently denounced the Pakistani religious-political establishment's periodic attempts to negotiate ceasefire or accommodation arrangements with the Indian state as theological apostasy. He has, in parallel, rejected the takfiri tendency within the broader Pakistani jihadist landscape that would extend the categorisation of dar-ul-harb to elements of the Pakistani state itself, and his theological output has been notably more careful than that of his competitors to draw a defensible line between operations conducted against Indian targets and operations conducted against Pakistani targets. The line, by his own framing, is that the Pakistani state, however imperfect, is a Muslim state whose military and civilian apparatus is not a legitimate target of jihad, while the Indian state is a non-Muslim ruling authority over a Muslim-majority territory whose military and civilian apparatus, by the strict reading of the classical canon he has cultivated, is a legitimate target. The line has periodically come under pressure during periods of Pakistani-state pressure on his organisation, and the post-2002 schism with the faction led by Maulana Abdul Jabbar represented a near-rupture of the line. But the line has held, and the cleric's consistency in defending it has been one of the principal reasons his organisation has retained Pakistani-state institutional protection across the quarter-century of its existence.

The international designation battle that occupied much of the post-2016 period is, in this context, best understood as a proxy fight over which of these two portraits the international community would formally accept. The Indian government's attempt to have the United Nations Security Council formally designate him as an international terrorist began in earnest in 2009, in the aftermath of the 26/11 Mumbai attacks, and was renewed at the Council in 2016 after the Pathankot operation, in 2017, and again in 2019 after Pulwama. The designation was blocked at each attempt by the People's Republic of China, which placed a technical hold on the listing within the Council's 1267 sanctions committee. China's stated rationale, in the public statements of its UN ambassadors over the period, was procedural: that the evidence presented by the listing states did not yet meet the committee's requirements for full designation. The Indian counter-argument, made in joint papers submitted with the United States, the United Kingdom, and France, was that the listing requirements had been met in 2009 and that China's repeated technical holds were a function of strategic-political calculation rather than evidentiary deficiency. The hold was finally lifted on 1 May 2019, eleven weeks after the Balakot strike, in circumstances that Chinese diplomats subsequently described to friendly counterparts as a recognition that the costs of continued obstruction had begun to exceed the benefits. He was added to the Security Council's consolidated sanctions list as a designated international terrorist that day. The travel ban, asset freeze, and arms embargo that the designation imposed had no observable effect on his operational position inside Pakistan.

## Current Status

The disappearance question, the central analytical disagreement that runs through every recent account of his career, can be framed by a single observation. He has not appeared in a documented public setting that has been authenticated by Indian or Western intelligence since early 2019. The Pakistani state has produced no public proof of his presence at any specific location since that date. His organisation has produced no audio or video recording of him that has been forensically authenticated as recent. His family members, with the exception of public statements following the Operation Sindoor strikes on Bahawalpur in May 2025 in which he reportedly acknowledged the deaths of ten relatives in the Indian missile attack on the Jamia Masjid Subhan Allah complex, have made no recent statements that confirm his daily location or his health status. The man who built the organisation has, in operational terms, ceased to be a public person. The four available explanations for this disappearance are not mutually exclusive, and the most accurate account of his current status is probably some combination of all four.

The first explanation is that he has chosen to disappear for tactical reasons of personal security. The Indian shadow war's systematic elimination of his close associates over the period from 2022 onwards, including [the IC-814 hijacker Zahoor Mistry shot in Karachi in March 2022](https://insightcrunch.com/2013/04/13/zahoor-mistry-ic814-hijacker-profile/), the [Pathankot mastermind Shahid Latif killed inside a Sialkot mosque in October 2023](https://insightcrunch.com/2013/04/19/shahid-latif-pathankot-mastermind/), the [Lashkar-e-Jabbar commander Dawood Malik shot in North Waziristan in October 2023](https://insightcrunch.com/2013/04/25/dawood-malik-masood-azhar-aide/), and [the JeM operative Raheem Ullah Tariq killed in Karachi in November 2023](https://insightcrunch.com/2013/05/01/raheem-ullah-tariq-jem-profile/), has demonstrated that being identified as a senior figure in his organisation is a near-decisive operational risk. The cleric has read the pattern correctly, on this account, and has concluded that visibility is incompatible with survival. The disappearance is, in this reading, a rational response to a campaign that has methodically closed in on his network.

The second explanation is that the Pakistani security state has compartmentalised him for diplomatic reasons. The post-Balakot environment imposed costs on Pakistan that included the United Nations Security Council designation in May 2019, the Financial Action Task Force grey-listing that followed, and the consistent international diplomatic pressure that has surrounded any documented public appearance by senior Pakistani-based jihadist figures since. Producing him in public, on this account, would be a diplomatic liability that the Pakistani state has decided not to absorb. The compartmentalisation is, in this reading, a state-managed solution to an embarrassment-management problem, and his absence from public life reflects ISI guidance rather than personal choice.

The third explanation is the chronic-illness account. He has been on dialysis since at least 2017, his weight has fluctuated significantly in the available imagery, and the operational tempo of his late-period public appearances had already been slowing in the period before Balakot. The medical condition that requires regular dialysis is, in any rigorous physical sense, incompatible with the kind of itinerant militant leadership that he had practised in the early 2000s. The disappearance is, in this reading, the natural consequence of advanced renal disease combined with the diabetes and cardiovascular complications that typically accompany it.

The fourth explanation is that he is dead. This is the rarest of the four accounts in mainstream analytical writing, but it is not without supporting circumstantial evidence. The death of his elder brother Tahir Anwar at sixty-two under unexplained circumstances at the Bahawalpur residence, the public attribution of family losses in the May 2025 Operation Sindoor strikes that he made no documented personal appearance to deliver, the absence of any forensically authenticated recent audio or video despite repeated international interest, and the Pakistani state's evident discomfort with any Indian or Western journalist's attempt to verify his presence at a specific location, are all consistent with a leadership figure who has died but whose death has not been publicly disclosed for organisational and diplomatic reasons. The cleric, on this account, is being kept alive in the public mythology of his organisation because his organisational machinery cannot yet absorb the formal admission of his death.

The most epistemically responsible position, given the available evidence, is that the four explanations operate in some combination, and that the truth at any given moment between 2019 and the present has probably involved elements of all of them. He has plausibly chosen to reduce his visibility for security reasons. The Pakistani state has plausibly assisted that choice for diplomatic reasons. His chronic illness has plausibly imposed practical constraints that reinforce the visibility reduction. And the possibility that he has died at some point during this period and that his death has been concealed for organisational reasons cannot be ruled out on the available public record. The shadow war has, in this sense, produced a target who is simultaneously its most consequential and its most epistemically opaque figure: the architect of the organisational system that India is dismantling, whose own status within that system is no longer reliably knowable.

What is reliably knowable is the operational consequence. His name continues to appear on the masthead of Al-Qalam. His Friday sermons are reported in the Bahawalpur Urdu press, but the recordings are pre-recorded and forensically dateable to the period before 2019. His brother Abdul Rauf Asghar has assumed effective day-to-day command of the operational wing, and Indian government claims following the May 2025 Operation Sindoor strikes that Abdul Rauf was killed in the missile attack on Bahawalpur, although disputed by the cleric himself in his public statement on the family losses, would, if true, represent the elimination of the operational successor in the same week as the strike on the headquarters complex. The organisation continues to claim responsibility for operations in Indian-administered Kashmir. The infrastructure he built between 2000 and 2019, in other words, is still functioning. But its founder has receded into a status that is somewhere between exile, illness, and death, and the question of which of those three best describes his current condition is the question the next few years of the shadow war will answer.

## Legacy and Network

The infrastructure he built, viewed at the institutional level, is the most important Pakistani-based militant enterprise built in the post-Cold-War period, and the most thoroughly studied. Jaish-e-Mohammed entered its twenty-fifth year of operations in January 2025 with an estimated active membership of between five and seven thousand cadres, a registered madrassa system that includes more than a dozen formal religious schools across the Pakistani Punjab, a propaganda apparatus that publishes two flagship Urdu titles and a substantial digital footprint, and a financial-administrative architecture that, by the recent assessments of the Indian Financial Intelligence Unit, has migrated significantly to digital-wallet platforms and cryptocurrency infrastructure since the Financial Action Task Force grey-listing of Pakistan in 2018. The full institutional analysis of this apparatus is treated in [the Jaish-e-Mohammed complete guide](https://insightcrunch.com/2012/02/10/jaish-e-mohammed-complete-guide/). For the purposes of this profile, the institutional fact that matters is that the apparatus has survived its founder's effective disappearance from public life. The organisation he built was robust enough to continue functioning without his daily direction.

The network that surrounded him personally, however, has not been robust to the same extent. The systematic targeting campaign that has unfolded in Pakistani cities since 2022 has methodically removed from the operational landscape the figures who served as the connective tissue between the cleric's strategic direction and the organisation's operational output. The pattern of these eliminations, examined in detail in [the comparison of JeM targets](https://insightcrunch.com/2013/05/19/jem-targets-compared/), is best understood as a series of concentric rings closing in on the personal command structure. The outer ring, comprising figures associated with peripheral Jaish-affiliated outfits like the Lashkar-e-Jabbar that Dawood Malik commanded in North Waziristan, was penetrated in the autumn of 2023. The middle ring, comprising operational handlers like Shahid Latif and field commanders like Raheem Ullah Tariq, was penetrated in October and November 2023. The inner ring, comprising the cleric's family circle and his Bahawalpur-resident senior leadership, was penetrated in the period of 2024 to early 2026 with the death of his elder brother Tahir Anwar. The Operation Sindoor missile strikes of May 2025, although operationally distinct from the targeted-killing campaign documented in the Series A profiles, removed the headquarters facility itself, killing ten of his family members in the process. The full institutional response to this systematic dismantling is treated in [the network under siege analysis](https://insightcrunch.com/2013/05/13/masood-azhar-network-under-siege/), and the pattern of replacement promotion that the organisation has used to manage the leadership attrition is mapped there.

The hostage release that produced him in 1999 produced two other figures whose subsequent careers have been part of his operational orbit, and both were the subject of separate Indian operational interest. Ahmed Omar Saeed Sheikh, the London School of Economics dropout released alongside the cleric on the Kandahar tarmac, was responsible for the kidnapping and murder of the Wall Street Journal reporter Daniel Pearl in Karachi in January 2002, and has been imprisoned in Pakistan in a procedurally complicated case ever since. Mushtaq Ahmed Zargar, the third released figure, returned to Kashmir militancy via the Al-Umar Mujahideen and has remained operationally active. The reconstruction of how the three release decisions converged into a single twenty-six-year chain of consequence is the subject of [the IC-814 release decision analysis](https://insightcrunch.com/2019/08/12/ic814-release-decision-1999/), which traces the branching downstream effects of each released figure's subsequent activities. The cleric is, of the three, the figure who built the most institutional infrastructure, but the chain that produced him also produced Pearl's murder and a third commander whose Kashmir operations have continued for twenty-five years.

The international architecture his organisation has built, beyond the immediate Pakistani-Punjab infrastructure, includes a Saudi and Gulf donor network that has periodically been the subject of pressure from the Financial Action Task Force, a Bangladeshi connection that surfaced briefly in the late 2000s before being disrupted by Bangladeshi security operations, and a transnational digital-fundraising apparatus whose contemporary scope is visible in the Indian Financial Intelligence Unit's recent reporting on cryptocurrency wallets associated with Jaish-affiliated front organisations. The full transnational financial picture is treated in the broader Series B coverage of the organisation. The point relevant to the cleric's personal legacy is that the infrastructure he built has acquired a transnational character that survives any individual leadership decapitation and that complicates the question of how far the shadow war's targeted-killing doctrine, focused as it is on physical persons in specific Pakistani cities, can degrade an organisation whose money increasingly moves through channels that the doctrine cannot reach.

The Saudi and Gulf donor architecture that financed the cleric's first decade of organisational expansion was substantially disrupted, although not eliminated, by the Financial Action Task Force grey-listing of Pakistan in June 2018 and the Saudi-led tightening of religious-charity oversight that followed in the subsequent eighteen months. The Saudi General Presidency for the Affairs of the Two Holy Mosques and the parallel Saudi religious-charity oversight bodies imposed new documentation and identity-verification requirements on cross-border religious philanthropy in the period between 2018 and 2020 that closed several of the donor channels his organisation had relied upon since the early 2000s. The compensating shift to digital-wallet platforms and cryptocurrency infrastructure, mapped in detail in recent Indian Financial Intelligence Unit assessments, has been an adaptation rather than a recovery, and the volume of Gulf-origin funding that the organisation now receives is substantially below the levels documented in the period of 2005 to 2015. The financial-architecture shift has had operational consequences as well as administrative ones. Training-camp expenditure, by the inferences that can be drawn from satellite imagery of the camp facilities and from the testimony of former trainees who have surrendered or been captured by Indian agencies, has declined materially since 2020, and the basic infrastructure of the camps at Bahawalpur and the smaller satellite facilities has visibly aged in ways that imply reduced upkeep budgets.

The 2025 Operation Sindoor strikes, treated in the broader Series D analysis but relevant here for their direct effect on the cleric's personal position, struck the Bahawalpur headquarters and killed ten members of his immediate family. He acknowledged the family losses publicly. He did not appear in person to do so. The Indian government's claim that his brother Abdul Rauf Asghar was killed in the strikes has not been confirmed and was not included in his own public statement on the family losses, but the broader operational consequence of the strikes was the formal removal of the Jamia Masjid Subhan Allah complex as a usable headquarters, and the documented destruction of training infrastructure that the organisation has not, by the most recent satellite imagery, fully reconstituted. His personal trajectory in the post-Sindoor period has not been documented in any forensically authenticated public appearance. The chain that runs from his release on the Kandahar tarmac on 31 December 1999 to the missile strikes on his family compound in Bahawalpur on 7 May 2025 is, by the most rigorous available accounting, the longest single causal sequence in the contemporary South Asian strategic landscape. The 1999 decision India made to save the IC-814 hostages produced the man whose family compound India struck twenty-five years and four months later. Whether that strike represents the closing of the chain or merely its most recent link is a question that depends on whether he is alive, and on whether the organisation he built can be sustained without him.

What can be said with reasonable confidence is that the shadow war's central biographical proposition holds. The figure released in Kandahar built the deadliest Pakistani-based vehicle for cross-border operations against India in the contemporary period. He outlived the strategic-depth doctrine that produced him, watched its institutional rationale erode through the post-Balakot period, and presided over the methodical elimination of his closest associates in a campaign whose attribution he was diplomatically forbidden to acknowledge. The Bahawalpur cleric is the man whose career most exhaustively illustrates the consequences of the IC-814 decision, and whose surviving organisational infrastructure is the principal target of the campaign that decision eventually produced. The chain has been long. It is not yet finished. But the man at the centre of it has, in every documented sense, become the organisation rather than its leader, and the question of what survives him is now the central organisational question facing Jaish-e-Mohammed.

The broader analytical context worth emphasising at the close of this profile is that his career has unfolded against a backdrop of three discrete shifts in the Indian-Pakistani strategic equation, each of which has reshaped the operating environment of his organisation. The first shift, between 1999 and 2008, was the maturation of nuclear deterrence as a binding constraint on conventional Indian retaliation, a constraint that the Operation Parakram episode crystallised and that the [Mumbai siege of November 2008](https://insightcrunch.com/2012/02/04/lashkar-e-taiba-complete-guide/) tested without breaking. The second shift, between 2016 and 2019, was the development of the limited-conventional-response doctrine, the surgical-strike concept of September 2016, and the Balakot airstrike of February 2019, a doctrinal evolution that demonstrated that Indian conventional retaliation against Pakistan-based jihadist infrastructure was now possible without crossing the nuclear threshold. The third shift, since 2022, has been the systematic targeted-killing campaign that this series documents, a campaign that has substituted for direct state-on-state retaliation a doctrine of patient, attributable but unacknowledged elimination of named figures whose presence in Pakistani cities has been treated by the Pakistani state as politically protected. The cleric's organisation has been the principal beneficiary of Pakistani strategic-depth doctrine across all three shifts, and the principal target of Indian counter-doctrine across the second and third. He has, in this sense, been the figure whose career best illustrates how the operating environment of Pakistani-based jihadism has changed across a quarter-century, and the figure on whom the costs and consequences of those changes have most concentrated. The Bahawalpur cleric who walked off the Kandahar tarmac on the last day of the twentieth century is, in 2026, the man whose family compound has been struck by Indian missiles, whose closest associates have been shot by motorcycle-borne gunmen in Karachi and Sialkot, whose elder brother has died at sixty-two under unexplained circumstances at his Bahawalpur residence, and whose own physical presence in the world has been impossible to verify for the better part of a decade. Whatever else can be said about that career, it has produced an arc of consequence that the ledger of the South Asian strategic landscape will record under the date 31 December 1999 and against the line item of decisions that should have gone differently, even though, on the day they were made, the alternative was widely understood to be unbearable.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who is Masood Azhar?

He is the founder and effective leader of Jaish-e-Mohammed, a Pakistani Deobandi militant organisation responsible for some of the deadliest attacks against Indian security forces in the past quarter-century, including the 2001 Indian Parliament attack, the 2016 Pathankot airbase assault, and the 2019 Pulwama bombing. Born in Bahawalpur on 10 July 1968, he was a Harkat-ul-Mujahideen ideologue captured by Indian forces in Kashmir in February 1994 and held in Indian custody for almost six years. India released him on 31 December 1999 in exchange for the hostages aboard hijacked Indian Airlines flight 814 in Kandahar. Within thirty-one days of his release he had founded Jaish-e-Mohammed at Bahawalpur, an organisation he has led for the entire quarter-century since.

### Q: How was Azhar released from Indian prison?

He was released as one of three militants traded for the safety of one hundred and sixty hostages on board Indian Airlines flight 814, which had been hijacked from Kathmandu to Delhi by five Pakistani militants on 24 December 1999 and flown via Amritsar, Lahore, and Dubai to Kandahar in Taliban-controlled Afghanistan. After eight days of negotiation, the Indian cabinet under Prime Minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee authorised the release of three militants, including the cleric, in exchange for the hostages. Foreign Minister Jaswant Singh personally accompanied the three released prisoners to Kandahar on 31 December 1999 to complete the exchange. The detailed crisis reconstruction is available in the IC-814 hijacking complete guide.

### Q: Where is Masood Azhar now?

His exact location is not publicly verified. He has not been documented in a forensically authenticated public appearance since early 2019, in the period immediately preceding the Indian airstrike on the Balakot training camp. He is widely reported to reside, when in Bahawalpur, at the Jamia Masjid Subhan Allah complex that served as the Jaish-e-Mohammed headquarters, although the complex itself was struck by Indian missiles during Operation Sindoor on 7 May 2025. He has chronic kidney disease requiring regular dialysis, has been reported to receive medical treatment in Karachi, Lahore, and possibly Riyadh, and his current public presence has been limited to written statements distributed through his organisation's propaganda apparatus rather than personal appearances.

### Q: Why did China block Azhar's UN designation for so long?

The People's Republic of China placed technical holds on the proposal to designate him as an international terrorist on the United Nations Security Council's 1267 sanctions list at four separate attempts between 2009 and 2019. Beijing's publicly stated rationale was procedural, that the evidence presented did not meet the committee's listing requirements. The Indian counter-argument, supported by the United States, the United Kingdom, and France in joint papers, was that the listing standard had been met and that the holds reflected strategic-political calculation rather than evidentiary deficiency. China's strategic relationship with Pakistan, formalised through the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor and the broader strategic-partnership framework, is widely understood to have been the operative consideration. The hold was lifted on 1 May 2019, in circumstances that Chinese diplomats subsequently described as a recognition that continued obstruction was no longer cost-effective.

### Q: How many attacks has Jaish-e-Mohammed carried out under Azhar?

Jaish-e-Mohammed has claimed or been credibly attributed responsibility for several dozen attacks against Indian targets since its founding in January 2000, with the most consequential being the 1 October 2001 Srinagar legislative assembly bombing that killed thirty-eight people, the 13 December 2001 Indian Parliament attack that killed nine Indian security personnel and a parliamentary gardener, the 2 January 2016 Pathankot airbase assault that killed seven Indian security personnel, the 18 September 2016 Uri brigade attack that killed nineteen Indian soldiers, and the 14 February 2019 Pulwama bombing that killed forty paramilitaries. Smaller-scale fidayeen and infiltration operations against Indian security forces in Jammu and Kashmir have been conducted at irregular but persistent intervals throughout the period.

### Q: Is Masood Azhar alive or dead?

The publicly available evidence does not permit a definitive answer. He has not been documented in a forensically authenticated public appearance since early 2019. His statements are now distributed in written form through his organisation's propaganda apparatus rather than as personally delivered audio or video. The four candidate explanations for this absence include tactical security-driven self-concealment, Pakistani state-managed compartmentalisation for diplomatic reasons, advanced chronic illness that imposes physical constraints on public movement, and the possibility that he has died and that his death has been concealed for organisational and diplomatic reasons. The most rigorous reading of the evidence is that the truth probably involves some combination of these factors, and that no single one of them can be conclusively excluded on the basis of public-domain information.

### Q: What happened to Azhar after the Balakot strike?

The Indian Air Force struck the Jaish-e-Mohammed training camp at Balakot in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province in the early hours of 26 February 2019, twelve days after the Pulwama attack. The cleric had reportedly already been moved from his Bahawalpur residence to a safe house outside the city by the time of the strike. He has not been seen in a documented public appearance since this period. His brother Abdul Rauf Asghar took over progressively greater day-to-day operational responsibility through the post-Balakot transition. The cleric's own role since 2019 has been confined to written statements, recorded sermons whose forensic dating remains contested, and the strategic-administrative direction of an organisation that has been functioning largely without his physical presence.

### Q: How many of Azhar's associates have been killed?

The systematic targeted-killing campaign against figures associated with his network in Pakistan, beginning in earnest in March 2022 with the Karachi shooting of the IC-814 hijacker Zahoor Mistry and accelerating through the autumn of 2023, has eliminated at least eight figures whose roles spanned operational handling, training infrastructure, regional command, and personal-circle support. The cluster of October and November 2023, in which Shahid Latif was shot inside a Sialkot mosque, Dawood Malik was killed in North Waziristan, and Raheem Ullah Tariq was shot in Karachi within a span of approximately five weeks, represented the most concentrated phase of the campaign. The May 2025 Operation Sindoor missile strikes on the Bahawalpur headquarters added the documented deaths of ten family members. The full enumeration of the eliminated personnel is treated in the comparative profile of JeM targets.

### Q: What is JeM's relationship with Pakistan's ISI?

The publicly available evidence on the relationship between Jaish-e-Mohammed and Pakistan's Inter-Services Intelligence directorate, drawn from captured-operative testimony in Indian charge sheets, leaked documents, and the analytical work of Pakistani journalists writing in retirement, indicates a long-standing arrangement in which the organisation has been treated as an instrument of strategic-depth doctrine rather than as a security threat. The ISI's S-Wing, responsible for relations with militant proxies, is widely reported to have provided logistical support, training assistance, and protection from law-enforcement action in return for operational compliance with parameters set by Pakistani strategic planners. The relationship has come under stress during periods of intense international pressure, particularly after the 26/11 Mumbai attacks and the post-Balakot environment, but the documentary record indicates that the underlying institutional protection has persisted across changes of Pakistani government and chief of army staff.

### Q: Who is Abdul Rauf Asghar?

He is the cleric's younger brother and, since approximately 2007, the operational commander of Jaish-e-Mohammed's military wing. He was one of the five Pakistani militants who hijacked Indian Airlines flight 814 in December 1999, and he has been implicated by Indian and American agencies in the planning of multiple subsequent operations, including the 2016 Pathankot airbase attack. The United States Treasury designated him as a Specially Designated Global Terrorist in December 2010 for his senior role within the organisation. Indian government statements following the Operation Sindoor missile strikes of May 2025 claimed that he was killed in the attack on the Bahawalpur headquarters complex, although the cleric's own public statement on the family losses did not include him by name among the deceased, and the claim has not been fully confirmed.

### Q: What was the Pulwama attack and what was Azhar's role?

The Pulwama attack was a vehicle-borne suicide bombing carried out by a Kashmiri Jaish-e-Mohammed recruit named Adil Ahmad Dar on 14 February 2019, in which approximately three hundred kilograms of mixed explosives detonated against the side of a Central Reserve Police Force convoy bus on the Jammu-Srinagar national highway near Lethpora, killing forty paramilitaries. The cleric's direct role in operational planning, by the National Investigation Agency's reconstruction in the subsequent charge sheet, was strategic and supervisory rather than tactical, with the operational handling of the recruit and the device having been managed by mid-level Jaish commanders inside Indian-administered Kashmir under instructions transmitted from the Bahawalpur headquarters complex. The full operational reconstruction is available in the dedicated Pulwama analysis.

### Q: How did the Indian Parliament attack happen?

On 13 December 2001, five Jaish-e-Mohammed fighters in a white Ambassador car bearing forged government number plates and a Home Ministry sticker drove through the gates of the Indian Parliament complex in New Delhi during a session of the upper house. They were challenged by a vigilant police constable, opened fire, and ran in five different directions across the parliament's lawns and corridors. All five were killed by Indian security forces over the course of a forty-minute battle. Eight Indian security personnel and one parliamentary gardener died with them. The Indian National Investigation Team attributed the operation jointly to Jaish-e-Mohammed and Lashkar-e-Taiba, with the cleric and Hafiz Saeed of [the LeT leadership](https://insightcrunch.com/2012/02/04/lashkar-e-taiba-complete-guide/) named as senior planners on their respective sides.

### Q: What happened at Pathankot in 2016?

Four Jaish-e-Mohammed fighters infiltrated the perimeter of the Pathankot Air Force Station in Punjab in the early hours of 2 January 2016, beginning a battle inside the airbase that lasted nearly four days and killed seven Indian security personnel. The attackers had been trained at the Bahawalpur and Balakot complexes during the autumn of 2015 and were directed during the operation by a Sialkot-based handler named Shahid Latif. The Pakistani government accepted an Indian invitation to send a Joint Investigation Team to inspect the airbase site in the aftermath, but the team returned to Islamabad without filing a substantive report. Pathankot was the last sustained attempt at Indian-Pakistani diplomatic engagement on terror after a major attack, and its diplomatic failure ended the era of post-attack negotiation.

### Q: Was Masood Azhar's brother killed?

His elder brother Muhammad Tahir Anwar, who served as the head of the organisation's military affairs wing for more than two decades, died at the age of sixty-two at his Bahawalpur residence under circumstances that Pakistani authorities have not publicly explained. The death pattern, the operational role Tahir Anwar had occupied within Jaish-e-Mohammed, and its position within the broader systematic targeting of the cleric's personal circle by the shadow war campaign make the unexplained-circumstances framing operationally suggestive. The dedicated profile of Tahir Anwar examines the circumstantial evidence and the difficulty of attribution in cases where Pakistani authorities have not produced public investigative records.

### Q: How many family members did Azhar lose in Operation Sindoor?

In a public statement issued on 7 May 2025, the cleric acknowledged the deaths of ten members of his family and four close associates in the Indian missile strikes on the Bahawalpur headquarters complex during Operation Sindoor, conducted in retaliation for the [April 2025 Pahalgam attack](https://insightcrunch.com/2012/06/03/pahalgam-attack-2025-explained/) that killed twenty-six tourists in Indian-administered Kashmir. The family members named or identified through Pakistani reporting included his elder sister and her husband, a nephew and his wife, a niece, and five children of his immediate family. He did not include his brother Abdul Rauf Asghar by name in the list of the deceased, although Indian government statements claimed that Abdul Rauf had been killed in the same strike.

### Q: Why does Pakistan continue to shelter Azhar?

The publicly available evidence indicates a long-standing strategic-doctrinal rationale rather than a single recurring decision. Pakistani strategic-depth doctrine, articulated in various forms across successive governments and chiefs of army staff, has treated militant organisations conducting operations against Indian targets as deniable instruments of foreign policy in a context where conventional military action is foreclosed by nuclear deterrence. The cleric's organisation has been the most consequential single instrument within this doctrine for a quarter-century. The Pakistani institutional cost of formally renouncing him, and of arresting and prosecuting him in a manner that would satisfy Indian and Western standards, would be a structural change in the strategic-depth posture rather than a single decision. Pakistan has, on the available evidence, not been willing to absorb that structural change, and the recurrent international pressure has produced periodic performative actions that have not altered the underlying institutional protection.

### Q: What is the IC-814 connection to today's terror?

The release of the cleric, Ahmed Omar Saeed Sheikh, and Mushtaq Ahmed Zargar at Kandahar on 31 December 1999 produced three downstream branches of consequence whose effects are still accumulating. The cleric founded Jaish-e-Mohammed thirty-one days later and has presided over an organisation responsible for the deaths of several hundred Indian security personnel and civilians in the years since. Sheikh was responsible for the kidnapping and murder of the Wall Street Journal reporter Daniel Pearl in January 2002. Zargar returned to Kashmir militancy and remains operationally active. Every significant Jaish operation, from the December 2001 Parliament attack to the 2025 Operation Sindoor escalation, traces back to a release decision that the Indian cabinet authorised under the duress of the IC-814 hostage crisis. The chain of consequence is the longest single causal sequence in the contemporary South Asian strategic landscape.

### Q: Who released the IC-814 hostages and Azhar?

The decision to release the three militants in exchange for the IC-814 hostages was authorised by the Indian Union Cabinet under Prime Minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee, after eight days of negotiation between the hijackers, the Taliban regime in Kandahar, and Indian government representatives. Foreign Minister Jaswant Singh personally accompanied the three released prisoners on the special flight from Delhi to Kandahar to complete the exchange on 31 December 1999. The decision was made under conditions of extreme duress, with one hostage already murdered, the aircraft surrounded by Taliban fighters who were not preventing the hijackers from receiving supplies, and no viable Indian rescue option available at any of the airports the hijacked aircraft had visited. The dedicated origin-point analysis of the release decision examines the cabinet deliberations and the available counterfactuals.

### Q: How is JeM funded today?

The organisation's financial architecture has evolved substantially across the quarter-century of its existence, with three distinguishable phases. The first phase, from 2000 to approximately 2010, relied principally on Saudi and Gulf donor networks, Pakistani-domestic religious philanthropy collected through madrassas and mosques, and direct ISI subventions. The second phase, from 2010 to 2018, saw increasing pressure from international financial-monitoring frameworks, the periodic freezing of registered charitable trusts associated with the organisation, and a partial shift towards informal hawala-based transfers. The third phase, since the 2018 Financial Action Task Force grey-listing of Pakistan, has involved a documented migration to digital-wallet platforms and cryptocurrency infrastructure, with the Indian Financial Intelligence Unit's recent reporting indicating that the organisation has used approximately thirty new wallets per month to avoid detection patterns. The recent reporting also documents a shift towards smaller, more numerous individual transactions that fall below the reporting thresholds of major financial institutions, and a systematic use of trade-based money laundering through small consumer goods import-export businesses owned by sympathetic donors in the Gulf and Southeast Asia. The cumulative effect is a funding base that is more diversified, more digitally mediated, and more difficult to disrupt through any single regulatory intervention than the funding base of any other Pakistani-based militant outfit currently operating.

### Q: Will the shadow war eliminate Masood Azhar?

The publicly available record indicates that the systematic targeted-killing campaign has reached the inner ring of his personal circle, with the deaths of his elder brother Tahir Anwar in early 2026 and the documented losses of ten family members in the Operation Sindoor missile strikes of May 2025 representing the most direct recent reach. Whether the campaign extends to the cleric himself depends on a combination of factors that include his physical location, his health status, the operational risk-reward calculation faced by the campaign's planners, and the diplomatic consequences that any direct strike on him would impose. The campaign's documented trajectory, examined in the network siege analysis, suggests that the proximate inner circle has become operationally accessible and that the question of the cleric himself is not whether he is reachable but whether the strategic calculation favours reaching him. The arguments against direct targeting include the diplomatic precedent that an explicit Indian strike on a UN-designated terrorist resident in a Pakistani city would set, the political-rhetorical advantage that the cleric himself, in his vanished and ailing state, currently provides as a symbol of Pakistan's continuing failure rather than as an active operational threat, and the possibility that his organisational successors might prove more capable than he has been in recent years. The arguments for direct targeting include the deterrent effect, the symbolic closing of the IC-814 chain that his elimination would represent, and the demonstration that no figure within the Pakistani-jihadist landscape, however senior or politically connected, lies beyond the campaign's reach.

### Q: What does Azhar's career reveal about the shadow war?

His career is the most exhaustive single illustration of the proposition that India's shadow war is the slow correction of the IC-814 release decision. He was the most consequential of the three released militants. He built the most institutional infrastructure. His organisation has been responsible for the largest share of the high-symbolic-value attacks on Indian military targets in the post-2015 period. The systematic dismantling of his network, the missile strike on his Bahawalpur headquarters, and the methodical elimination of his close associates in Pakistani cities, all trace back to the 1999 decision and represent the operational machinery India has built to address its consequences. The chain runs from a Kandahar tarmac to a Bahawalpur missile crater. Whether it ends with him, or continues into the post-Azhar phase of the organisation he built, is the central question facing both his organisation and the campaign that has been built to dismantle it. The broader lesson, beyond the specifics of the cleric's own career, is that decisions made under the duress of hostage crises can produce consequences that persist for decades, that the institutional infrastructure built by a single figure can outlast that figure's effective leadership, and that the dismantling of such infrastructure is a slower, more patient, and more attributively complicated project than the original mistake that produced it. The shadow war is, in this reading, a study in the long arithmetic of strategic correction, and the figure at its centre is the human embodiment of why that arithmetic matters.
