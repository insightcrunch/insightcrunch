Lenin is the only major Marxist thinker who also governed, and that single fact bends every attempt to write his life. The biographer of Marx can follow a mind through libraries and manuscripts, because the man's power was posthumous and textual. The biographer of Vladimir Ilyich Ulyanov follows a mind that issued decrees, founded a security police, dispersed an elected assembly and left instructions about his own succession which his successors chose to bury. A serious profile therefore has to keep two accounts open at once. One account holds the ideas: a small number of theoretical contributions, each identifiable, each argued in identifiable texts, each still fought over by scholars who owe the regime nothing. The other account holds the record of the government he led between the autumn of the revolution and his death, a record that includes repression as a deliberate instrument and must be stated in the biography itself, not managed, softened or deferred to some later act of judgement. This profile keeps the two accounts in separate ledgers, as the evidence requires, and it refuses the two standard shortcuts: the chronicle that lists residences and pamphlets without ever saying what the thinking amounted to, and the verdict, favourable or hostile, that decides the case before the life has been allowed to happen.

![Lenin, an intellectual biography, factional war, exile writings and the April reversal - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The organising device of what follows is borrowed from the method this series applied to Marx himself: judge a thinker first by the contributions he claimed, or that his tradition claimed for him, and then assess each contribution on its merits, separately from the fate of the others. Lenin's case yields five such contributions. He built a theory of how a revolutionary party could be organised under conditions of illegality. He produced an analysis of imperialism as a distinct stage of capitalism. He recovered, from Marx's scattered writings on the Paris Commune and the state, a doctrine of smashing the bureaucratic machinery rather than inheriting it. He developed a way of judging when a political situation had become revolutionary, a conjunctural instrument rather than a prophecy. And he worked out a strategy for revolution in a country where the peasantry, not the industrial working class, formed the overwhelming majority. Around these five, the biography arranges one decisive reversal. In the spring of the revolution year, returning from exile to a capital in upheaval, he publicly abandoned the stage doctrine his own party had carried into the crisis, overruled his own leadership, and set the course that ended in October. That reversal, the April reversal, is the hinge of the life, because everything distinctive about the man, the contempt for inherited formulas, the speed of tactical judgement, the willingness to isolate himself inside his own organisation, stands concentrated in those few weeks.

Scope deserves one plain paragraph, because this profile shares its territory with neighbours and the boundaries matter. The party theory as a doctrine in its own right, the imperialism book as a text to be read closely, the state book as a work of political theory, the question whether Leninism broke with Marx, and the assembled case against Leninism each receive a full treatment elsewhere in this series, and each will be signposted where the life touches it. What belongs here is the man: the formation, the working methods, the factional wars, the seizure and exercise of power, the record stated plainly, the decline and the contested succession. Readers who want the arguments at full length should follow the signposts. Readers who want to know who Lenin was, what he thought he was doing, and what followed from the doing, are in the right place.

## A life organised by its arguments

Chronology is the laziest form of biography and the commonest. In Lenin's case it produces a familiar silhouette: the provincial childhood, the executed brother, the Siberian exile, the years of emigration in Munich, London, Geneva, Paris, Kraków and Zurich, the sealed train, the revolution, the stroke, the mausoleum. Every element of that silhouette is true and almost none of it explains anything. The same years, arranged differently, produce a different and more instructive figure: a man whose life was a sequence of arguments, each conducted in print and in committee rooms, each leaving a text behind it, and each narrowing or widening the possibilities of the next. He quarrelled with the legal Marxists over whether capitalism was developing in Russia, and the quarrel produced his first large book. He quarrelled with the Economists over whether workers left alone would rise beyond trade union demands, and the quarrel produced the party doctrine. He quarrelled with his own closest collaborators at a congress in Brussels and London, and the quarrel produced two factions and two names that Russian politics never shook off. He quarrelled with the entire leadership of European socialism over the war, and the quarrel produced the imperialism analysis and the strategy of turning an imperialist war into civil war. He quarrelled, finally and most consequentially, with his own party's settled line in the April of the revolution, and that quarrel produced October.

This arrangement also explains the texture of the surviving evidence. Lenin wrote compulsively and polemically, and the great bulk of his collected works consists of interventions: articles written against a deadline and an opponent, resolutions drafted for meetings he intended to win, letters instructing, rebuking and organising. The systematic books are few and mostly early or occasional, and several of the most consequential texts are unfinished, dictated in illness, or published after his death against the wishes of the men who inherited his party. A biography organised by arguments can use that evidence honestly. It can show the thinking happening, in public and under pressure, rather than reconstructing a philosophy the man never sat down to write. It can also show what the polemical method cost: a habit of treating disagreement as a species of error to be defeated rather than a datum to be absorbed, a habit whose consequences travelled from the pamphlet wars of emigration into the institutions of a state.

Two warnings govern the pages that follow. The first concerns anecdote. Lenin's life attracted legend early, much of it manufactured by the state that succeeded him and some of it manufactured by his enemies, and the most famous anecdote of all, the vow attributed to the schoolboy on hearing of his brother's execution, has no reliable source; it will be flagged as legend where it belongs, not repeated as fact. The second concerns numbers. The scale of the repression conducted under his government is a matter of historical record and of continuing scholarly dispute, and this profile states the record qualitatively and precisely, act by act and institution by institution, while routing the contested arithmetic of the terror to the companion treatment where figures are handled under their own protocol. A biography that invents a toll it cannot source would fail its reader exactly as a biography that hid the terror would.

## Simbirsk: the school inspector's son

Vladimir Ilyich Ulyanov was born in the spring of 1870 in Simbirsk, a provincial town on the Volga, into a household that represented the respectable, striving edge of the imperial service class. His father, Ilya Nikolayevich Ulyanov, had risen from modest origins through education into the inspectorate of public schools, first as a teacher of mathematics and physics, then as inspector and finally director of elementary schools for the province, travelling immense distances to open schools, train teachers and argue for the education of peasant children. The post carried a rank in the imperial table of ranks high enough to confer hereditary nobility, a fact worth pausing on: the future destroyer of the Russian old order began life inside its service nobility, in a large, bookish, disciplined family where advancement through study was the household religion. His mother, Maria Alexandrovna, was the daughter of a physician, educated at home to a standard unusual for the period, musical, multilingual, and by every account the steadier and more enduring presence of the two parents. The children were raised in several languages, examined rigorously, and expected to excel, and Vladimir did excel, finishing school with a gold medal, a distinction that sits oddly beside the later image of the born rebel and should correct it. The rebellion, when it came, was the rebellion of a prizewinning insider.

The father died suddenly in the first month of 1886, when Vladimir was fifteen, and the family's position died with him, pensions and rank notwithstanding. The catastrophe that defined the household arrived the following year. Alexander Ulyanov, the elder brother, a brilliant zoology student at St Petersburg, had joined a small group descended from the People's Will tradition which plotted to assassinate the Tsar on the anniversary of his father's assassination. The plot was betrayed and penetrated by the police before any attempt; Alexander was arrested with explosives in his possession, refused to plead for mercy in terms that would have indicted his comrades, and was hanged in the Shlisselburg fortress in May 1887, aged twenty one. The family was ostracised in Simbirsk society with a completeness that contemporaries recorded: acquaintances crossed the street, calls ceased, and the widow and her children learned what the state they had served did to the families of its enemies. Biographers have never doubted that the execution marked the younger brother permanently. What they doubt, properly, is the form in which popular memory preserved the marking.

### How reliable is the famous story of the brother's vow?

No contemporary document records the words. The story of the younger brother resolving, beside the news of the execution, to take a different path appears in later reminiscence and Soviet-era biography, shaped by people with reasons to give the life a founding scene. Historians treat it as legend with a plausible core, not as evidence.

The distinction matters because the legend does quiet argumentative work wherever it appears. It converts a complicated radicalisation, spread across years of reading, university conflict and police attention, into a single scene of fraternal vengeance, and it invites the reader to explain a life's politics by a family's wound. The documented sequence is less theatrical and more informative. The young Ulyanov was expelled from Kazan University within months of the execution, not for terrorism but for joining a student protest gathering, a modest disturbance for which the authorities, alerted by the family name, imposed the maximum penalty of exclusion and rustication to a village estate under police supervision. It was in that enforced idleness, by his sister's account, that he began the systematic reading, in Chernyshevsky above all, that turned a clever and angry student into a revolutionary of a particular kind: patient, organisational, contemptuous of heroic individual violence of exactly the sort his brother had practised. If a vow was taken at Kokushkino, its text was a bookshelf rather than a sentence, and the path it announced was not assassination but the slower construction of a movement. The point is not pedantry. A biography that begins with a fabricated quotation will fabricate elsewhere, and the reader is entitled to know, at the first famous anecdote, that this one declines to.

## The lawyer who barely practised

Barred from regular university attendance, Ulyanov prepared for the law examinations of St Petersburg University as an external student and passed them with first class honours, a performance that required mastering an entire faculty curriculum alone, from textbooks, while living under intermittent police observation. He then did the thing almost no profile remembers: he practised, briefly and without distinction, as an assistant barrister in Samara, handling a small number of minor cases, mostly for poor clients, and discovering in himself no vocation for the courtroom. The legal training nevertheless left permanent deposits in the thinker. It taught him the discipline of the brief: the marshalling of evidence toward a conclusion, the search for the clause on which everything turns, the treatment of an opponent's text as an exhibit to be dismantled line by line. Readers of his polemics meet the advocate constantly, in the numbered counts of an indictment, in the quotation prosecuted against its author, in the summation that permits the other side no surviving claim. It also taught him the statute book's central lesson, that formal rights and actual power are different things, a lesson the revolutionary later generalised into a theory of the state.

The Samara years matter for a second reason: they were his apprenticeship in the Russian Marxist underground. He organised study circles, quarrelled with the local followers of the People's Will inheritance, translated and read, and began the migration westward in his reading from populism to the texts of Marx and Engels and of Plekhanov, the exiled founder of Russian Marxism. By the time he moved to the capital in the middle of the eighteen nineties, taking a post in a legal office as cover for revolutionary work among factory study circles, the conversion was complete and the vocation settled. He was arrested before the end of 1895 as a founder of the League of Struggle for the Emancipation of the Working Class, spent more than a year in prison working on drafts smuggled out in invisible ink and through visitors, and in 1897 received the standard sentence of the period: three years of exile in eastern Siberia, in the village of Shushenskoye on the Yenisei.

## The first polemic: what the friends of the people are

The apprentice announced himself in print in the Samara period's aftermath with a work whose title is a declaration of war: What the Friends of the People Are and How They Fight the Social Democrats, composed in the year of his move to the capital and circulated in hectographed copies because no legal publisher could touch it. Its target was the populist theorist Mikhailovsky and the liberal Narodnik press, the friends of the people of the ironic title, whose subjective sociology treated society as the product of critically thinking individuals and history as a field for moral judgement rather than material analysis. The young author's performance is recognisably the mature method in embryo: the opponent's texts quoted at length and prosecuted sentence by sentence, the materialist counter position built from Marx's prefaces and Capital's first volume, and a polemical ferocity, directed at men two generations his senior and at the height of their fame, that contemporaries found either exhilarating or insufferable according to their allegiance. The pamphlet also contains, in its later parts, the first sketch of the alliance strategy that organises the biography's central thread: the working class as the people's tribune, taking up the grievances of every oppressed layer, the peasantry above all, rather than abandoning the countryside to the populists who claimed it as their parish. Biographers who skip the early polemics produce a Lenin who springs armed from Iskra's editorial office; the documentary record shows a decade of apprenticeship in which the weapons were forged one quarrel at a time, and the quarrel with the friends of the people, over whether Russia's future would be argued from statistics or from ideals, is the forge's first fire. The companion piece of the same years, a demolition of the populist economists' romance of the peasant commune and the kustar workshop, completed the clearing of the ground on which the statistical masterwork of the exile would be built.

## The formation of a Marxist

What the young lawyer read, and in what order, decided the kind of Marxist he became, and the biography should trace the reading as carefully as the residences. The first formation was Russian and literary. Chernyshevsky's novel What Is To Be Done?, a clumsy, magnificent book about the new people who would remake Russia through reason, discipline and cooperative workshops, reached him in adolescence and returned in the village of his rustication, where his sister recorded that he read and reread it until its ascetic heroes set the pattern of a revolutionary life: total dedication, contempt for comfort, the subordination of private happiness to the cause. The debt is written across his career, from the borrowed title of his most famous pamphlet to the standard he applied to comrades, and it explains a strain in his Marxism that the German texts alone cannot, a Russian moralism about the revolutionary's person that long predates any doctrine of the party. The second formation was statistical and Siberian in anticipation: the zemstvo studies, the factory inspectors' reports, the data through which the populist and Marxist camps fought their war over Russia's economic future. He mastered that literature as other men master scripture, and his first reputation inside the movement was as its most formidable handler of figures, the exile to whom disputants sent their tables for judgement.

The third formation was the encounter with Plekhanov, conducted entirely in print before it was conducted in person. Georgi Plekhanov, the aristocratic revolutionary who had founded the first Russian Marxist organisation in Geneva exile a decade before, supplied the young provincial with his philosophical frame: a materialism of French and German pedigree, a contempt for populist romanticism, and a stage theory of Russia's development in which the working class, not the peasant commune, carried the future. The pupil absorbed the frame so completely that his later rebellion against the teacher's politics never displaced it, and the reader of the wartime Hegel notebooks watches the pupil, thirty years on, annotating the dialectic with the teacher's categories still in his hand. When the two men finally shared an editorial board on Iskra, the collaboration carried the tension of the whole relationship in miniature: Plekhanov the founder, jealous of doctrine and careless of organisation; the younger man the organiser, jealous of the apparatus and prepared to subordinate doctrine, in the short run, to its requirements. Their alliance produced the paper and the programme. Their collision, deferred by the congress of the split, governed Russian Marxism's next twenty years, and it began, as so much in this life began, as a quarrel about an editorial decision taken in a rented room abroad.

## Siberian exile and the first large book

The Siberian exile of the eighteen nineties was a punishment designed for a different century, and in Lenin's case it functioned as a scholarship. Shushenskoye was remote beyond easy imagining, weeks of travel from the capital, and the regime of the exile was surveillance without confinement: he could read, write, hunt, correspond and receive books, and his fiancée Nadezhda Krupskaya, herself sentenced for the same organisational work, was permitted to join him on condition of a church marriage, which duly took place. Krupskaya was no appendage. She became the organisational memory of the faction he later built, its cipher clerk, correspondent and archivist, and their marriage, childless and famously austere, was in substance a working partnership inside a conspiracy. In Shushenskoye he wrote, among thirty or so works, the largest book of his youth, The Development of Capitalism in Russia, published legally under a pseudonym in the year his exile ended. The book is the foundation document of his thought and the least read, a dense statistical argument, built from zemstvo returns and factory data, against the populist claim that Russia lacked the home market for capitalism and would therefore follow a separate path. Its conclusion, that capitalist relations had already penetrated the countryside and stratified the peasantry, fixed the coordinates of his politics for life: revolution in Russia would be made in a capitalist country, by classes capitalism had created, and the peasantry was not a single revolutionary bloc but a divided world of employers and labourers in embryo.

The exile also fixed his polemical generation. The exiles and emigrants of his circle were arguing, in journals and in letters, against the legal Marxists who used Marx to celebrate Russian capitalism's progress and against the Economists who urged workers to confine themselves to workplace demands. Both arguments run through everything he wrote afterward. Against the legal Marxists he insisted that demonstrating capitalism's arrival settled nothing about its political future. Against the Economists he formed the conviction, soon to become doctrine, that spontaneity was a trap: left to itself, the working class movement would produce militancy about wages and conditions, admirable and insufficient, while the theory capable of connecting those struggles to the overthrow of the order had to be constructed, guarded and brought to the movement by an organisation built for the purpose. The reader should notice the pattern early, because it never changes. Lenin's theoretical work is almost always the residue of a factional fight, and the fights are almost always about what an organisation should do next. Abstract questions reach him wearing practical dress, and his answers, however philosophical their vocabulary, are engineered to settle a dispute about conduct.

Released in the opening weeks of 1900, he travelled abroad in the summer of that year to build the instrument the Economists argument demanded: an all-Russian political newspaper, printed abroad and smuggled home, which would give scattered circles a common line and a common centre. The paper was Iskra, the Spark, founded with Plekhanov, Martov and others of the older and younger exile generations, and edited from Munich, then London, then Geneva. Iskra was simultaneously a journal, a network of agents and couriers, and a school of cadres, and its editorial quarrels were the seedbed of the split that arrived three years later. It was for Iskra's readers that he wrote, in the opening months of 1902, the pamphlet that made his name inside the movement and shadowed it ever after, What Is To Be Done?, named in homage to the Chernyshevsky novel of his village exile. The pamphlet receives its own close reading in this series, and its doctrine is summarised, not expounded, in the section on the party question below.

## The congress that produced two names

The Second Congress of the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party met in the summer of 1903, opening in Brussels and fleeing police attention to London, where it ground through weeks of procedural combat in hired halls. Its purpose was unification: one party, one programme, one central leadership for the scattered committees inside Russia and the factions abroad. It achieved the programme, which was orthodox and agreed, and then split the movement on questions that look, from a distance, absurdly small. The famous division came over the wording of the party rules' first paragraph, on what membership meant: Martov's formulation admitted anyone working under the guidance of a party organisation, while Lenin's stricter wording required personal participation in one. The congress voted, narrowly, for Martov. The names that history kept, however, came later and from different votes entirely, on the composition of the central committee and the Iskra editorial board, held near the congress's end after the Jewish Bund and the Economist delegates had walked out over separate defeats. In those thinned votes Lenin's supporters held the majority, and he named them accordingly: Bolsheviks, the majority people, against Mensheviks, the minority people. The labels were a propagandist's gift and a historian's trap, since they froze one evening's arithmetic into permanent identities.

### Why do the names Bolshevik and Menshevik mislead?

The names record a single sequence of votes on the central committee and editorial board at the end of one congress, taken after some delegates had walked out. They describe who held a majority in that room on that day, not the lasting balance between the factions, which shifted repeatedly over the following decade.

The precision matters for everything that follows. For most of the period between the split and the revolution, the Mensheviks were at least as numerous, better connected to the legal workers' movement, and stronger in several regions; the Bolsheviks were twice driven close to organisational collapse, and their leader spent long stretches commanding a faction of exiles and couriers rather than a mass party. The names flattered the smaller faction with the psychology of a majority, and Lenin understood the asset precisely, defending the label ferociously whenever conciliationists proposed to drop it. The congress also displayed, in complete form, the working method that would govern his career. He prepared texts in advance, counted votes, built temporary alliances on single questions, treated procedural outcomes as political facts, and refused, when beaten on the rules paragraph, to concede the larger war, transferring the fight to the editorial board and the central committee where the arithmetic favoured him. Opponents called this manoeuvring factionalism. He called it politics. Both descriptions are accurate, and the biography of his ideas is unintelligible unless the reader holds them together: the doctrine of the disciplined party and the practice of the disciplined faction were born in the same rooms, in the same weeks, from the same man.

After the congress the factions coexisted inside one nominal party for nearly a decade, splitting and reuniting in cycles that exhausted a generation of activists. Lenin built a separate Bolshevik centre, a separate treasury fattened at one stage by armed robberies he authorised and later militants were hanged for, and a separate press, while formally negotiating unity congresses whose resolutions he treated as truces rather than settlements. The formal separation came only on the eve of the war, when he declared the Bolsheviks a party in their own right at a conference in Prague. By then the split of 1903 had organised two rival answers to the question that dominated Russian Marxism: whether the coming revolution needed a conspiratorial general staff or a broad workers' party on the western model. History was about to submit both answers to an examination neither had sat for.

## The party question, stated in outline

The party doctrine is the contribution most associated with his name and the one this profile is forbidden, by its own architecture, to treat at full length: the doctrine has a dedicated treatment in the guide to [the vanguard party explained](/2014/04/01/vanguard-party-explained/), and the pamphlet that founded it has a separate close reading in the guide to [what is to be done readers guide](/2014/03/15/what-is-to-be-done-readers-guide/). What a biography owes the reader is the origin and the shape. The origin was Russian illegality. A workers' party on the German model presupposed legal space: newspapers that could be seized but also reprinted, meetings that could be dispersed but also reconvened, a membership that could be counted. The autocracy offered none of this reliably. From that condition Lenin drew his conclusion in the famous pamphlet of 1902: the movement needed, at its core, a stable organisation of professional revolutionaries, people for whom the work was a vocation and a craft, bound by discipline and trained in conspiracy, around which broader and looser workers' organisations could form. The celebrated sentence of the pamphlet, that an organisation of revolutionaries would overturn Russia, was aimed at the Economists, and its harshest accompanying claims, about consciousness arriving from outside the economic struggle, drew on Kautsky and were aimed at the same target.

### Did Lenin invent the idea of a disciplined vanguard party?

No. The idea that a socialist party needed organisation, programme and discipline was common property in European social democracy, and Marx himself had built political organisations with rules and leaderships. What Lenin added was a specific answer to Russian conditions: how such a party could operate under a police state that made open mass organisation impossible for long.

Two correctives belong in any honest outline, both pressed by serious scholarship. The historian Lars Lih, reading the pamphlet against the party journalism of its moment, argues that its author understood himself as an orthodox follower of the German model's Russian application, worried chiefly that Russian workers lacked the organisational inheritance their German counterparts took for granted, and that the sinister reading of the text as a charter for manipulation projects later practice backwards onto a more conventional argument. The second corrective comes from Lenin himself, who in later prefaces treated the pamphlet as a polemical correction of a specific deviation, its formulations bent, as he put it in substance, by the requirements of the fight. Neither corrective dissolves the text's afterlife. Whatever its author intended, the pamphlet supplied a warrant, quoted for a century, for the subordination of movement to centre, and the institutions built in his name after the revolution claimed descent from it. The biography's task is to register both the intention and the descent without collapsing either into the other, a discipline this profile will need again when it reaches the record of the state.

## The warnings of the comrades: Luxemburg and the young Trotsky

The party doctrine drew, at the moment of its birth, two internal critiques from within Marxism that history made famous, and the biography is obliged to state them at their original strength because they were written before the record existed and cannot be dismissed as hindsight. Rosa Luxemburg, reviewing the split and the organisational plan in the German party's theoretical journal, warned that the ultra centralism proposed for the Russian party inverted the relation between leadership and movement: a central committee that thought for the class would end by substituting its own activity for the class's, and the errors of a genuinely self acting movement, she argued in substance, were historically more fruitful than the infallibility of the best committee. The young Trotsky, then aligned against the Bolsheviks, carried the logic to its terminus in a pamphlet of the same season: the party organisation substitutes itself for the party, the central committee for the organisation, and finally a single dictator for the central committee. Martov's parallel warnings have been noted earlier in this profile. Three features of these prophecies deserve the reader's precision. They were aimed at a tendency, not a blueprint, and their authors spent later years, Luxemburg in her prison manuscript on the Russian revolution, Trotsky in exile, qualifying them in opposite directions, Luxemburg criticising the Bolsheviks' dispersal of the assembly and suppression of dissent while defending the October seizure itself as the century's bravest act. They were also, in their own moment, factional documents, written to win a fight about the Russian party's statutes, and their authors would have been astonished to be read as neutral auditors. And they remain, after every qualification, the standard against which the record section of this biography must be read, because they demonstrate that the danger later realised was visible in advance to Marxists of the first rank who shared the founder's premises and his goals. The founder's answer at the time was contemptuous brevity: the critics were formalists frightened of discipline, and the Russian police state allowed no other kind. The biography registers the answer and its occasion, and leaves the reader with both documents open.

## The rehearsal of revolution

The autocracy's war against Japan in the far east collapsed into defeat, and in the first month of 1905 troops fired on a peaceful procession of workers carrying a petition to the Winter Palace, an event, Bloody Sunday, that detonated a year of strikes, mutinies, peasant seizures and improvisation. From Finnish exile Lenin watched the invention, in the capital, of the soviet: a council of workers' deputies, thrown up by a general strike, which coordinated a city's labour movement outside every party's instruction. His response showed the conjunctural intelligence at its best. The soviet was not in any programme, his included, and the Bolshevik committee in the capital initially distrusted it as a rival; he overruled the distrust from abroad, urging his followers to enter and work in the new organs rather than demand they declare themselves party bodies. He returned to Russia under amnesty in the autumn, wrote for the faction's first legal newspaper, pressed for an armed uprising to coincide with the strike wave, and when the December rising in Moscow was crushed and reaction consolidated, retreated again across the Finnish border, at one point escaping, by the standard accounts, across the ice on foot ahead of the police.

The years that followed were the movement's trough and his education in defeat. The Stolypin reaction combined repression with agrarian reform; party organisations inside Russia were penetrated and periodically annihilated by the political police, whose agents reached, in the affair of Roman Malinovsky, the Bolshevik central committee itself and a seat in the Duma. Lenin defended Malinovsky against accusation longer than the evidence warranted, an error worth recording because it qualifies the legend of his infallible nose for treachery, and the later confirmation of the agent's guilt, from opened archives after the revolution, stands as the episode's bleak punchline. In emigration the factions fragmented further, and he spent the bleakest years fighting recallists who wanted the Duma fraction abandoned, liquidators who wanted the illegal party dissolved, and his own erstwhile allies Bogdanov and Lunacharsky, whose philosophical heresies provoked his one systematic work of philosophy, Materialism and Empirio-criticism, written in a fury of orthodoxy against the Machist epistemology fashionable among Bolshevik intellectuals. The book is the least loved of his major works, philosophically crude by the judgement of many sympathetic readers, and its interest for the biographer lies in what it reveals: for Lenin, epistemology was never an academic specialty but a branch of party discipline, and a deviation about the nature of perception could be treated as seriously as a deviation about the Duma, because both threatened the reliability of the instrument he was building.

## The long divorce: unity congresses and the road to Prague

Between the split's first evening and the formal separation nine years later stretched a decade of attempted remarriages, and the biography should mark their rhythm because it formed the political generation that made the revolution. The unity congress at Stockholm in the first revolution's aftermath seated the factions together under Menshevik majority and adopted, among its agrarian resolutions, positions the Bolshevik leader opposed and then campaigned against from the platform of his own faction's press. The London congress of the following year reversed the majority and reversed nothing else; the factions kept separate centres, treasuries and loyalties under a single party roof, an arrangement every participant understood as an armed truce. The exile years added the smaller sects to the board: the recallists expelled from Bolshevism by their own leader for demanding the Duma fraction's withdrawal, the conciliators in both factions who laboured for reunion, the August bloc assembled by Trotsky in Vienna in a final attempt to unite everyone except the man who had become, by general consent, the obstacle to unity, a compliment the intended obstacle repaid by predicting the bloc's collapse and being right within two years. The Prague conference of the war's approach completed the divorce in form: the Bolsheviks constituted themselves as the party, elected a central committee of their own, and left the other factions to their committees and their claims. The separation's timing deserves the reader's notice, because it preceded the war that made it irreversible by two years, and because the parties that entered the revolution as enemies of a decade's standing were not improvising their hatreds in the revolution's spring. When the April fight came, the founder's isolation inside his own organisation was the last battle of a war he had been fighting since Brussels, and the organisation he overruled in a fortnight was the one institution in Russian politics that a decade of schism had taught to expect, from him, exactly that.

## The legal weapon: the Duma fraction and the daily paper

The trough years taught him the use of legal openings, and the lesson qualifies the caricature of the pure conspirator. After the first revolution the autocracy conceded a parliament, the Duma, emasculated by electoral manipulation and dissolvable at the Tsar's pleasure, and the exile factions quarrelled over whether socialists should touch it. Lenin's position shifted with the conjuncture he read: boycott when the revolutionary wave was rising and participation would legitimise a fraud, participation when the wave had ebbed and the rostrum was the only legal platform from which a deputy could speak to millions. By the war's approach his faction maintained a small Duma group whose speeches, cleared through the exile centre, were printed as leaflets and smuggled into factories, and when the deputies were arrested and tried for treason after the war began, their courtroom defiance became the faction's finest propaganda hour inside Russia. The second legal instrument mattered more. In the spring before the war his party acquired a legal daily newspaper in the capital, Pravda, financed by workers' kopecks and edited under permanent threat of confiscation, which reached a circulation no illegal sheet could approach and gave the faction, for the first time, a daily presence in the factories it claimed to lead. The exile's letters about the paper are a study in his priorities: circulation figures demanded weekly, editors rebuked for dullness, the legal daily treated as the party's true centre of gravity in a way the illegal committees, riddled with police agents, could not be. Historians of the faction's recovery before the war assign the paper a central role, and its suppression in the war's first days, with the deputies' arrest following, measures how completely the autocracy agreed with the assessment.

## How the exile lived

Two decades of emigration made Lenin, and the conditions of those years shaped the writings as surely as any doctrine. The exile's map reads like a gazetteer of cheap Europe: Munich, London, Geneva, Paris, Kraków, Bern, Zurich, with the British Museum's reading room and the great libraries of the continental capitals as the fixed points of a working life organised around print. He worked ferocious hours, bicycled, walked in mountains when funds and summers allowed, played chess with a loser's temper that memoirists recorded with relish, and lived in a sequence of lodgings whose shabbiness visitors remarked on even by the standards of the emigration. Around him Krupskaya ran the correspondence that held the faction together, deciphering letters in chemical ink, maintaining the addresses, the codes and the archive. The household's economy was a patchwork that the legend of the professional revolutionary conceals.

### How did Lenin support himself through two decades of emigration?

On a shifting patchwork that the legend of the full-time revolutionary often conceals: party funds and stipends in some periods, paid journalism and translation in others, help from his mother while she lived, and after her death a modest family inheritance administered by his sister. Exile was cheaper than the capitals, and the household lived frugally by deliberate discipline.

The point of these details is not colour. The emigration was a political environment with its own physics. Cut off from the movement they claimed to lead, the exile leaders competed through journals, conferences and resolutions, and polemic became their substitute for action, sharpened by idleness and by the knowledge that a phrase in a Geneva pamphlet might decide an allegiance in a Samara factory circle. Lenin mastered that environment completely, and some part of his later conduct in power, the treatment of disagreement as factional crime and of factional crime as a matter for organisational sanctions, was learned in rooms where the only sanctions available were words. When the sanctions became arrests, the habit was already formed. His opponents in emigration, Martov above all, saw the danger early and said so in print, in a famous warning that a party organised as Lenin wished would end with the dictator replacing the central committee. That prophecy belongs to the record of the ideas, and the reader should keep it in mind through the sections on the state, where it will be tested against events neither man foresaw in detail.

One address from the last emigration has become a fixture of the biographies, and it deserves its place for what it says about the world the exile inhabited. In Zurich the couple lodged in Spiegelgasse, in a cobbler's house, in the same narrow lane where, a few doors away, the Cabaret Voltaire had opened its doors that same winter and the artists of Dada were inventing the anti art of the century's absurdity within earshot of the professional revolutionary compiling his notebooks on imperialism and Hegel. There is no evidence the two worlds ever met, and the biographers who linger on the coincidence do so because it frames the era entire: the old Europe, in one lane of one neutral city, simultaneously gestating the movement that would abolish its meaning and the movement that would attempt to abolish its property, each convinced the other's revolution was the trivial one. The founder's own Zurich was narrower and poorer than the legend, a round of libraries, landladies, cheap restaurants and factional correspondence, and the narrowness is the point the coincidence decorates. He missed the avant garde entirely. He was not looking at the century's culture; he was reading its bank statistics, and the lane preserved both tenants without either noticing the other, which is as exact an emblem of the life as the record offers.

## The household of the revolution

The biography has mentioned Krupskaya as the faction's organisational memory, and she deserves her paragraph as a figure in her own right, because the literature's habit of treating her as a secretary with a wedding ring falsifies both the marriage and the movement. Nadezhda Konstantinovna Krupskaya was a revolutionary of the same Petersburg circles, arrested and exiled in her own person, who chose the Siberian village of her future husband's sentence and married him there because the regulations required a church wedding before she could remain. She became the exile organisation's centre of correspondence, maintaining the codes, the addresses and the archive, co-authoring in substance the pedagogical writings on which her later career in the education commissariat rested, and serving after the revolution as the deputy head of that commissariat, where she built libraries and literacy campaigns on a national scale. In the succession struggle she was the Testament's courier and its most exposed defender, treated by the troika with a calculated brutality, the telephone threats, the congress humiliations, that the founder's postscript had tried, too late, to make impossible. The second figure of the household's wider circle requires the biographer's restraint. Inessa Armand, the French born revolutionary who organised the movement's work among women and directed its institute for party training, worked in close collaboration with Lenin through the exile and revolutionary years, and their surviving correspondence documents an intellectual intimacy unusual in his guarded emotional life. Memoir and fiction have enlarged the relationship beyond the evidence into romance; the documentary record supports devotion, trust and grief at her early death in the Caucasus, where she had been sent to recover from exhaustion, and the honest biography leaves the enlargement to novelists, noting only that the founder, who attended her funeral in a state witnesses described as shattered, permitted himself so public a grief exactly once.

## War, and the collapse of the international

The outbreak of the European war in the summer of 1914 caught him in Austrian Poland, where he was arrested as a Russian subject and briefly imprisoned before socialist intermediaries secured his release and passage to Switzerland. What he found there destroyed the political world he had inhabited since youth. The Second International, the federation of socialist parties whose congresses had repeatedly pledged resistance to war, dissolved in a fortnight into its national components, the German party voting the Kaiser's war credits, the French entering a sacred union with their government, the majority of the movement's revered leaders blessing the slaughter of one another's members. Lenin's first reaction, recorded by Krupskaya, was disbelief of the newspaper report as a forgery. His settled reaction reorganised his entire politics. If the official movement had capitulated to nationalism, then the movement had to be rebuilt from the minority that had not, and the war itself, an imperialist war for the division of the world, had to be turned, in each belligerent country, into civil war against the government conducting it. Revolutionary defeatism, the doctrine that a socialist should wish the defeat of his own government as the lesser evil, placed him at the far edge even of the anti-war left, and at the Zimmerwald and Kienthal conferences of the anti-war minority he fought the pacifist centre as bitterly as he fought the open patriots.

### Why did Lenin insist that the war changed everything?

Because the war collapsed the international he had served. In August of the war's first year the great socialist parties of Europe voted war credits for their own governments, and Lenin read the collapse as proof that opportunism had captured the movement's leaderships. From that verdict followed his wartime strategy: defeatism, a new international, and the turn toward civil war.

Out of the war years came two bodies of work that outlasted the strategy. In the libraries of Bern and Zurich he filled notebooks with excerpts on Hegel's logic, an encounter that left marks on his later thinking about contradiction and the leap in development, and then, in the first half of 1916, wrote the short book that became his most translated economic work, Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism. Its argument, that capitalism had passed into a monopoly stage dominated by finance capital and the export of capital, and that the world had been completely divided among the great powers so that any redivision meant war, is expounded and assessed in this series' guide to [lenin imperialism readers guide](/2014/04/15/lenin-imperialism-readers-guide/), and the biography need only mark its function in the life. The book armed the wartime strategy with an economics: if the war was structural rather than accidental, then peace on the old basis was a fantasy and revolution in the belligerent states was a practical possibility rather than a pious hope. It also introduced the analysis that governed his judgement of situations ever after, the search for the weakest link in the imperialist chain, the point where the system's contradictions concentrated and a break, once made, might spread.

The other wartime production was organisational and prophetic at once. Watching the February revolution in Russia break out in bread queues and mutinies while he lectured to socialist youth in Zurich, he told his audience, weeks before the fall of the monarchy, that his generation might not live to see the decisive battles of the coming revolution. The remark is the essential corrective to every account of Lenin as the revolution's confident engineer. He did not predict its date, because nobody did. What he possessed, and what the remark's falsification displayed, was a readiness, built over twenty years, to act on a conjuncture the moment it declared itself, and a party, or the skeleton of one, positioned to receive his instructions. When the news from Petrograd arrived he began, within days, to write the letters that announced the reversal: instructions to Bolsheviks returning to Russia that contradicted, point by point, the line his own party's leadership in the capital was already pursuing.

## The blue notebook and the unwritten books

In the last Zurich winter, while the war ground on and the revolution he had stopped predicting approached unannounced, the exile compiled the document from which his most famous book would be quarried: a large notebook, bound in blue, in which he excerpted everything Marx and Engels had written about the state, arranged under his own headings, with marginal verdicts on the commentators. The notebook, known to scholarship as the blue notebook and published long after his death, is the workshop of the state doctrine in its raw state, and it corrects two legends at once. Against the legend of improvisation, it shows the state book of the revolutionary summer as the product of systematic preparation undertaken before anyone knew there would be a summer to use it in. Against the legend of completeness, it shows the doctrine's foundation to be an anthology, a dossier of quotations assembled for a polemic, from which the finished argument would have to be selected under pressure, as in the event it was, in a Finnish hut, between flights from the police. The second part of the state book, the part that was to treat the experience of the Russian revolutions themselves, was never written, and the postscript's joke about preferring to live the experience covers a genuine loss to the tradition: the one assessment of October's state building by the state book's author does not exist, and every later claim about what he would have concluded from the apparatus his government built is speculation wearing his authority.

The unwritten shelf is longer than the one famous gap. A popular textbook of Marxism, projected in the pre-war years as the movement's catechism and abandoned to the pamphlets that did its work piecemeal. A systematic treatment of the dialectic, toward which the Hegel notebooks were the quarry and which the war, the revolution and the state consumed. A history of the party he had founded, which he urged on others and never attempted, perhaps because its author knew how much of it would have to be an indictment of its author's decisions. The biography ends its account of the writings here deliberately, with the absences, because the absences explain the afterlife better than the shelf of finished volumes does. A thinker who leaves his system complete invites commentary; a thinker who leaves his system's keystone, the theory of the state in practice, unwritten, invites completion by others, and the century of Leninism, orthodox and heretical alike, is the record of those completions, each claiming the founder's warrant for the part he never wrote. The reader of the contributions table should hold the blue notebook in mind as the table's silent sixth row: the contribution that exists as preparation and promise, whose consequence was everything the successors built in its empty place.

## February: the revolution nobody led

The event that made the April reversal possible was made by nobody, and the biography must mark the leaderlessness plainly because it explains both the reversal's opportunity and its necessity. In the last week of the old February, in a capital short of bread and frozen in one of the war's hardest winters, women textile workers struck on their international day and took the streets for bread, and the strike became general before any party had decided anything; regiments ordered to fire on the crowds mutinied regiment by regiment until the garrison of the capital had passed to the revolution; and the Tsar, stranded by railway disruption at his headquarters and advised by his own generals, abdicated for himself and his son into the hands of a provisional committee of the parliament he had spent a decade emasculating. Power in the streets belonged from the first days to another body, the Petrograd soviet of workers' and soldiers' deputies, improvised on the model of the first revolution and dominated by the Menshevik and Socialist Revolutionary intellectuals who believed, on the stage doctrine the April theses would attack, that the bourgeois revolution must be governed by the bourgeoisie. The soviet's first order, addressed to the army, democratised the armed forces at a stroke, subordinating officers to elected committees in matters outside operations and dissolving, in practice, the discipline on which the war effort rested. Between the committee that governed without force and the soviet that held force without governing stood the dual power, the unstable condominium the exile in Zurich identified, from newspaper reports, as the revolution's defining structure within days of its formation. Every party of the left was surprised by February, his own included, and the founder's distinction in the weeks that followed was not prophecy but speed: he drew the conclusions from the surprise faster, and carried them further, than the men who had lived it. The biography that credits him with February understands nothing; the one that watches him read February, from a reading room in Zurich, through cables and newspapers, understands the method that April displayed.

## Letters from afar: the month of waiting

Between the monarchy's fall and his arrival in Petrograd there stretched the most revealing month of the life, the weeks in which the revolution proceeded without him and he conducted it, as far as he could, by post. The belligerent governments blocked the obvious routes home; the Entente barred known defeatists, and the road through Germany required a negotiation with the enemy state that no other major figure of the revolution was willing to touch. In Zurich he paced, cabled, drafted farewell letters to Swiss comrades, and wrote for the party paper at home a series of Letters from Afar, analysing the revolution's first days from newspaper reports with a penetration that still reads as uncanny: the dual power of government and soviet identified as the revolution's central unstable fact, the militia question posed, the provisional government characterised as the dictatorship of the bourgeoisie continuing the war. The letters also show the reversal in gestation. The first letter still works partly within the old stage vocabulary, completing the bourgeois democratic revolution under soviet pressure; by the fifth, the analysis has hardened into the position the theses would announce, that the soviet form had superseded the sequence. Party editors in Petrograd printed the first letter in edited form and held the rest, an act of censorship by his own side which he never forgave and which the biography should weigh as evidence for the April fight's reality: the line he reversed was not a straw position but the settled editorial policy of his own newspaper, defended by comrades who had made the revolution's first month while he read about it abroad.

The negotiation for the German transit, conducted through Swiss socialists and the German legation, produced the famous conditions: the carriage to be treated as extraterritorial, no inspection of passports or baggage, the travellers to include emigrants of several factions, and payment arranged in the ordinary way of railway tickets, with the whole agreement published to strip it of conspiracy's appearance. The German general staff's calculation, that the defeatists would damage the Russian war effort, was recorded in its own documents, and Lenin's answer to the charge, repeated for the rest of his life, was that the calculation cut both ways and that a revolutionary used the enemy's stupidity as he used a railway timetable. The train crossed Germany, took ship and rail through Sweden and Finland, and delivered its passengers to the Finland Station, where the biography's hinge begins to turn.

## The April reversal

The return journey became legend in its own right: the sealed train, a carriage granted extraterritorial status by agreement with the German government, which transported the exiles across a Germany at war with their country in the calculation that they would damage the Russian war effort. The calculation was correct and the scandal permanent; opponents never let the party forget the German transit, and the biography should record both the fact and its exploitation without flinching from either. The train reached the Finland Station in Petrograd at night in the middle of April, and Lenin, climbing onto an armoured car outside the station by the standard accounts, told the crowd that the revolution they had begun had to go forward to socialism. His party's leadership, in print, had spent the weeks since the monarchy's fall saying almost the opposite. The Bolshevik newspaper Pravda, under editors returned from Siberian exile including Kamenev and, in those weeks, Stalin, had settled on a policy of conditional support for the provisional government and pressure upon it to make peace, on the orthodox reasoning that Russia faced a bourgeois democratic stage which socialists should help complete and criticise. Lenin had attacked that line from Switzerland in letters his own editors published in cut form. Now, in person, he detonated it.

The April Theses, read to two party meetings on the day after his arrival and published in Pravda over the leadership's resistance, demanded the following: no support of any kind for the provisional government; exposure of its promises as annexationist continuity; transfer of power to the soviets of workers' and soldiers' deputies as the only possible form of revolutionary government; a republic of soviets rather than a parliamentary republic; abolition of the standing army, police and bureaucracy in favour of an armed people and elected, recallable officials paid working wages; nationalisation of the land and of the banks; and, as the immediate perspective, not the introduction of socialism by decree but soviet control over production and distribution, with the party's name and programme to be remade accordingly. Senior Bolsheviks received the document as a bombshell from another planet. Kamenev replied in Pravda that the scheme was unacceptable, since the bourgeois democratic revolution was unfinished; delegates at the city conference voted the theses down before the argument had run a fortnight; and Lenin, isolated at the summit of the organisation he had built, did the thing that defines him in this biography. He took the argument downward, to the district committees, the factory meetings and the party's rank and file, and upward through every conference the calendar offered, and within weeks the April party conference adopted his line as the party's own.

### What exactly did the April Theses reverse?

They reversed the sequence his own party carried into the revolution. The party leadership had treated the fall of the monarchy as the start of a bourgeois democratic stage to be completed before socialism could be discussed, and had offered the provisional government conditional support. Lenin demanded no support, power to the soviets, and preparation for a second stage.

### Why did Lenin's own party resist the April Theses?

Senior Bolsheviks had run the party through the first weeks of revolution and reached their own settlement with events: the provisional government watched critically, the soviets as pressure, the stage completed over time. Lenin arrived calling that settlement a betrayal of the party's purpose, and the first votes went against him before argument turned the conference.

Why does this episode earn the title this profile gives it, the April reversal, and the status of the life's hinge? Because orthodoxy was the party's proudest possession and Lenin was its chief custodian, and the theses broke the orthodoxy at its load-bearing point. The stage doctrine, the belief that history moved through assigned phases and that Russia's assigned phase was a bourgeois democracy to be matured before socialism could be posed, was not a Menshevik eccentricity; it was the common sense of Russian Marxism, shared by Plekhanov, by the Menshevik leadership, by most Bolsheviks and, in his own earlier formulations, by Lenin himself, whose two-tactics pamphlet of the first revolution had constructed an elaborate machinery for completing the democratic stage under working class hegemony. The theses abandoned the machinery on the evidence of six weeks. The soviets, an organ nobody's programme had foreseen, had made the old sequence obsolete in fact, and Lenin's theoretical act was to say so in theory: to declare that the form the revolution had invented outranked the sequence the doctrine had prescribed, and that a party which could not revise its stages when reality revised them had become a museum guard. His opponents inside Marxism, from Plekhanov, who called the theses delirium, to the Menshevik internationalists who shared his anti-war stand, understood the break exactly and named it heresy. He accepted the name as a compliment and built a strategy on the heresy: all power to the soviets, peace, land, bread, and the patient explanation, through the summer's crises, that turned a faction into the government of a continent.

The reversal also displays, in the purest form on record, the man's relation to his own party, which the rest of the biography confirms at every scale. He founded factions, split organisations and expelled deviationists in the party's name for two decades, and then, at the summit of his authority, treated the party's settled line as one more obstacle to be overridden by argument, persistence and the threat of appeal to the ranks. Both halves of that sentence are the same man. The party was sacred to him as an instrument, and expendable in any given form the moment the instrument blunted. His successors, who inherited the instrument and not the judgement, applied his principle of overriding orthodoxy most lethally against his own final positions, a sequel the succession section of this profile takes up. For the biographer the lesson of April is simpler and worth stating flatly: Leninism, at its origin, is not the worship of a doctrine but a method for discarding doctrines, and every later attempt to freeze it into catechism has had to begin by forgetting the month in which it was born.

## The peasant question, the thread through the life

One thread runs from the first book to the last article, and a biography organised by arguments should draw it tight, because it connects the statistician of the eighteen nineties with the dying auditor of the nineteen twenties. The young exile's study of capitalism in Russia had concluded that the village was differentiating into rural bourgeoisie and rural proletariat, and for twenty years his agrarian programme wrestled with the consequences: against the Socialist Revolutionaries' land socialisation, which treated the peasantry as a single toiling class, he argued for programmes that recognised the differentiation and aimed, at the limit, at the nationalisation that would clear the ground for large scale production. The first revolution taught the programme's inadequacy in its pure form, since the village rose as a village, for the land and against the landlords, indifferent to the distinctions the theory required, and the pamphlets of those years show the theorist learning from the pupil class he had sorted so confidently: confiscation first, theory's refinements after, and the alliance with the whole peasantry against the autocracy as the revolution's immediate form. October completed the education in irony. The land decree enacted the rival party's programme, mandates and all, because the village had already written its answer in seizures the government could only ratify, and the theorist of differentiation presided over the most undifferentiated land settlement in the country's history, the black repartition, as the price of the alliance.

The war then presented the bill in the form this profile has followed, requisitioning and revolt, and the last writings returned to the village as the regime's unsolved foundation. The cooperative article of the final winter proposed, in substance, a generational truce: socialism in the countryside was to arrive, if it arrived, through the peasants' own cooperative associations, under a state that had learned accounting and patience, across a whole historical epoch of cultural work among a population the regime had alienated. Historians dispute whether the piece was a tactical coda or a genuine re-founding of the project's agrarian half, and the dispute matters because the successor's collectivisation answered the same question with the opposite instrument, violence at scale, within the decade. For the biography the thread's lesson is simpler. The peasantry was the one social force that defeated every formulation its theorist built for it and extracted, at each defeat, a more honest one, and his intellectual life can be read as the record of those extractions, from the statistical tables of the Siberian book to the dictated concessions of the last winter.

## The national question and the federal settlement

The alliance strategy had a second front, the empire's nationalities, and the biography follows it briefly because it produced the founder's most durable institutional legacy and his final political fight. Before the war he had taken up the national question against two opponents at once: the Austrian socialists' scheme of cultural autonomy detached from territory, and Rosa Luxemburg's refusal, from Polish experience, of the slogan of national self determination as a bourgeois snare. His answer, worked out in the resolutions and pamphlets of the war's approach and in the wartime theses, made the right of nations to secession a democratic demand the working class movement was bound to defend precisely in order to unite workers across the border the demand might create, a dialectical construction his critics found too clever and his defenders found prophetic. The revolution applied it under duress. Finland's independence was recognised by his government within weeks of October; Poland's followed the war's fortunes rather than any decree; and the union eventually assembled from the soviet republics took a federal form, against the autonomisation plan favoured by the general secretary, under which the republics would have entered the Russian federation as subordinate units. The dying leader's intervention in that drafting quarrel, and his fury at the conduct of his own appointees in Georgia, produced the last dictated memorandum of the life, in which the founder of the state warned that its Great Russian core was reproducing the chauvinism of the old empire and assigned the blame, by name, upward. The federal settlement that resulted, a union of formally sovereign republics with a constitutional right of secession printed in its fundamental law, outlived its author by the whole span of the state itself, and when that state dissolved, it dissolved along the federal seams he had insisted on drawing. Few of his texts can claim so literal an afterlife, and the biographer records it as the national contribution that the fivefold scheme, organised around the tradition's own claims, has no row for, because the tradition preferred to count it as policy rather than theory.

## The five contributions, weighed one by one

With the hinge in place, the contributions can be assessed in the order the series method requires: first as he and his tradition stated them, then as the scholarship has weighed them. The reader should hold the assessment's ground rule in view. A contribution is a move inside an argument, and it can be original without being correct, correct without being original, and consequential in practice for reasons its author would have disowned. Each of the five gets its paragraph of statement and its paragraph of weighing here, and the table that follows this section gathers the results into the artifact this profile is required to leave findable.

### What did Lenin actually contribute to Marxist theory?

a theory of party organisation under illegality, an analysis of imperialism as a stage of capitalism, a recovery of Marx's writings on the state, a method for judging when a situation is revolutionary, and a strategy for revolution in a mainly agrarian society.

The party contribution came first in time and in fame. Stated soberly, it holds that under conditions where the state forbids open organisation, a revolutionary movement requires a core of full-time, disciplined, centrally coordinated cadres, selected for reliability and trained in illegal work, with broader workers' organisations radiating from that core and a press serving as its collective organiser. The weighing has swung across a century. Detractors have read the doctrine as the seed of the one-party state, a warrant prepared in advance for the suppression of dissent, and have pointed to the ease with which the organisational maxim travelled from pamphlet to police practice. Defenders, and behind them a strand of archival scholarship, have replied that the text of 1902 breathes the air of orthodox social democracy, that its author spent the following decade fighting for internal party democracy against his own apparatus men when the conjuncture allowed, and that the emergency core was designed for a police state, not as a constitution for a workers' government. The sober residue is this: the contribution solved a real problem, the survival of a movement under the Okhrana, and it bequeathed a real danger, a theory of organisation whose safeguards were conjunctural rather than principled, so that when the conjuncture changed the safeguards lapsed and the core remained.

The imperialism contribution was the most systematic. Stated, it holds that by the opening of the twentieth century competitive capitalism had been succeeded by a monopoly stage: industry concentrated, banks fused with industry into finance capital, capital exported to colonies and dependencies in search of returns, the globe partitioned among a handful of powers, and war for repartition therefore structural rather than accidental. The weighing divides the economics from the strategy. As economics, the book borrowed heavily and visibly from the liberal Hobson and the Marxist Hilferding, simplified where its sources were subtle, and has been faulted for freezing a transitional configuration into a final stage; its defenders answer that the categories of monopoly, finance and capital export still organise real inquiry and that the book's predictions about the fusion of state and monopoly capital aged better than its rivals. As strategy, its contribution was the operational concept of the weakest link and the conclusion that revolution might begin not where capitalism was most developed but where its chain was most strained, a conclusion that reversed the tradition's expectations and licensed the Russian gamble. Few books of its size have moved so much history with so derivative a first half.

The state contribution was the most textual. In hiding in Finland in the summer of the revolution, with the seizure of power weeks away, Lenin returned to Marx's writings on the Paris Commune and produced State and Revolution, a work of recovery as much as invention: against the parliamentary cretinism he attributed to the socialist parties, it restated, with chapter-and-verse insistence, Marx's doctrine that the working class cannot lay hold of the ready-made state machinery and wield it for its own purposes, but must smash the bureaucratic and military apparatus and replace it with Commune forms, elected and recallable officials, rotation of administrative tasks, and the arming of the population. The book has a full close reading elsewhere in this series, in the guide to [state and revolution readers guide](/2012/12/15/state-and-revolution-readers-guide/), and the biography marks its strangeness instead: it is at once the most libertarian text he ever wrote, envisaging the withering of administration into accounting and control performable by any literate worker, and the preface to a government that built the most intrusive apparatus in Russian history. He never finished it. The postscript, written after October, remarks that it was pleasanter and more useful to live the experience of revolution than to write about it, a joke whose accuracy the record section of this profile must weigh without smiling.

The conjuncture contribution was the most personal, being less a doctrine than a discipline he practised and intermittently formulated. Its classic statement distinguishes a revolutionary situation, marked by the ruling order's inability to continue unchanged, a sharp worsening of the suffering and want of the oppressed classes, and a significant rise in the masses' independent activity, from revolution itself, which requires in addition the subjective factor: a revolutionary class capable of action strong enough to break the government. Around the distinction he built habits of analysis that fill the wartime and revolutionary writings, the insistence that capitalism develops unevenly so that timing differs by country, the attention to national and peasant movements as reserves of the revolution, and the contempt for any politics that recites stages while the moment passes.

### What did Lenin mean by a revolutionary situation?

He meant a conjunction, not a mood. In his wartime formulation the symptoms were an impossibility for the ruling classes to rule unchanged, a sharpening of want and suffering among the oppressed, and a marked rise in the independent activity of the masses. Even then, he insisted, a subjective factor decided whether the situation became a revolution.

The alliance contribution closed the list and carried the largest consequences beyond Russia. From the first revolution onward he argued that in a country of peasants the working class could win only in alliance with the rural majority, first for the democratic revolution against the autocracy and landlordism, and then, after the April turn, by carrying that alliance into the struggle for power itself, winning the poor peasantry and neutralising the rest with the slogans of peace and land. Against the tradition's suspicion of the peasantry as a reactionary sea, he treated the village as a differentiated political terrain to be worked with the same conjunctural attention as the factory. The weighing records both the triumph and the bill. The alliance made October possible and the civil war winnable, because an army of peasant conscripts would not fight for the landlords' restoration. It also built the regime's central contradiction into its foundations, since the state that owed its survival to the peasantry proceeded to requisition the peasantry's grain, and the biography's record section follows that contradiction to Kronstadt and to the great retreat of the New Economic Policy.

## The contribution and consequence table

The five assessments converge on a pattern that a table states better than prose can, because the pattern lives in the relation between columns. Read down the first two columns and the life is a thinker's: five claims, five texts or bodies of text, each intelligible inside the Marxist tradition he inherited. Read across into the third substantive column and the thinker becomes a founder of institutions, some of which honoured the claims and some of which inverted them. Read the final column and a third life appears, the afterlife of the ideas in hands that never knew the man, where the same sentence could serve as a constitution, a slogan or an indictment. The contribution and consequence table is this profile's artifact, and its lesson is the reason the biography keeps its accounts separate: a claim, its institutional consequence and its independent fate are three different objects, and most bad writing about Lenin consists of substituting one for another.

Two consequences in the table deserve emphasis because they structure everything after them in this profile. The first is the inversion in the state row. No thinker of the tradition wrote more fiercely against bureaucracy as a caste standing over society, and no government of the tradition built one faster; the biography that follows must therefore treat the apparatus not as an accident that befell his ideas but as an outcome his ideas failed to prevent and his practices, in war conditions, actively selected. The second is the portability in the conjuncture row. The five claims were designed as a set, yet history disassembled the set, adopting the timing instrument across the political spectrum while discarding or indicting the rest. That disassembly is the standing refutation of any account, friendly or hostile, that treats Leninism as a single block to be accepted or rejected entire. The man himself, in April, had treated his own tradition's block exactly so, and taken from it what the moment required.

## The warrant, the refused trial, and the German gold

The July repression raised a decision that split the founder from several of his oldest comrades, and the biography records it because it reveals the hierarchy of his loyalties under maximum pressure. The provisional government issued a warrant for his arrest on charges of treason and armed revolt, and a current inside the party, supported by respected figures of the older generation, argued that he should surrender and convert the trial into the revolution's tribunal, as the Duma deputies had done under the Tsar. He refused, from hiding, with contempt for the parallel: the government that had shot demonstrators in July would not stage a fair trial in August, and the accused, he judged, would be murdered in transit or in custody before any tribunal convened. The party's central committee, after argument, endorsed the refusal, and the leader crossed into Finland. Historians have generally treated the fear as reasonable in the atmosphere of the July days, when officers and crowds had lynched suspected Bolsheviks in the capital's streets; what the episode fixed was the precedent that the founder's person stood outside the calculations he demanded of others, a precedent his defenders call prudence and his critics call the first exemption.

The treason charge itself rested on the allegation of German pay, and the allegation's long afterlife obliges the biography to state the evidence with archival precision. That the German government subsidised defeatist, separatist and revolutionary agitation inside the Russian empire, as a weapon of war, is documented in Germany's own archives beyond serious dispute: money moved through intermediaries, most famously the socialist turned businessman Alexander Parvus and his Copenhagen institute, toward causes ranging from Ukrainian separatism to strike funds. That the Bolshevik organisation received German funds, and specifically that its leader knew and approved, is a separate claim, and on it the evidence thins into channels and probabilities that historians continue to contest: transfers associated with the intermediary Ganetsky and the Stockholm bureau are documented in part, their destination and the leadership's knowledge are not documented to the same standard, and the famous Sisson documents produced to prove the case in the following year were forgeries. The responsible verdicts in the scholarship divide between those who hold some German money probably reached the party's enterprises through intermediaries, a common wartime practice among all the belligerents' clients, and those who require the proof the archives have not yielded. What is not contested is the political fact: the charge, true in its general frame and unproven in its personal application, was the most effective weapon the revolution's enemies ever found against its leader, and the sealed train, a documented fact requiring no forgery, did the weapon's work for it in the public mind for a century.

## The summer of the soviets: the education of a party

Between the April conference and the October seizure stretched six months in which the reversal was tested against events almost weekly, and the biography slows down here because the testing, not the seizure, is where the method shows its workings. In the revolution's first crisis, over the foreign minister's note pledging the government to the old war aims, armed crowds filled the capital's streets and the soviet's executive, dominated by the parties of compromise, pulled them back from the brink of a power nobody had planned to take; Lenin, whose theses had just been adopted, counselled against adventure and absorbed the lesson that slogans ripen at speeds the party does not set. Through the following weeks the Bolsheviks grew inside the factories and the garrison on the patient explanation he had prescribed, while the government lurched through coalition reshuffles and a failed summer offensive at the front destroyed the army's remaining discipline and the government's remaining credit in a single month. The July Days followed, an armed movement from below, half demonstration and half insurrection, which the party first tried to restrain and then to lead into orderly retreat before the government recovered its nerve; the repression that followed, the wrecking of the party's press, the arrests, the branding of its leader as a paid agent of Germany and his flight to Finland, reduced the organisation in a fortnight to something like its pre-war condition, and the founder, shaving his beard in a workman's lodging and writing the state book in a Finnish hut, drew the conclusion the conjunctural method existed to draw: the moment had been misread by the street, not by the analysis, and the analysis predicted that the pendulum would return.

It returned through the Kornilov affair, when the commander in chief's march on the capital against the government converted the persecuted party, in a week, into the indispensable defender of the revolution, armed its Red Guards with government rifles, and filled the soviets with its majorities as the parties of compromise bled authority they never recovered. The founder's reading of the affair, conducted from hiding in a stream of letters, remains the method's masterclass: no support for the government whose weakness had invited the coup, total practical cooperation in defeating the general, and the political harvest gathered without mercy from the discrepancy between the two. By the autumn the question inside his own central committee had inverted. The obstacle to insurrection was no longer the party's weakness but its leader's impatience against its caution, and the founder, threatening resignation to the ranks if the committee delayed past the congress of soviets, forced the vote that the October section of this profile records. The summer's education had a price the biography should not omit. The agent charge, fabricated and believed in good faith by honest opponents, fixed in the public mind an association between the party and foreign gold that its enemies exploited for generations, and the founder's own wartime doctrine, that a revolution's enemies would use any weapon, had been demonstrated on his own person before he was permitted to demonstrate his replies.

## The contributions under the archive's light

The opening of the Soviet archives in the final decade of the old century altered the evidence under every column of the table without overturning the table's design, and a short audit of the alterations belongs in a biography that claims documentary discipline. On the party contribution, the archive strengthened the revisionists: the pre-war party's internal life proved more argumentative, its leader's authority more frequently defied, than either the cult or its enemies had supposed, and the pamphlet of the Iskra years settled further into its context of orthodox party building. On the state contribution, the archive darkened the record's side of the row: directives in the founder's hand, confirming, urging and in specific instances ordering repressive measures and their extension, removed the deniability that an earlier generation of sympathetic scholarship had preserved by attributing excess to local initiative and civil war chaos, and the debate among historians moved accordingly, from whether the centre directed the terror to how its direction interacted with the periphery's zeal. On the conjuncture contribution, the archive was kinder, restoring the extent to which the April and October decisions were arguments conducted against real resistance inside the leadership, in votes and letters that the official history had ironed into unanimity. On the alliance, the opened records of the requisitioning years, the reports of rural revolt reaching the Kremlin in mounting volume, confirmed the biography's central thread in the founder's own incoming post. The lesson the audit teaches is the one this profile has practised throughout: documents move the picture at its joints, and the joints of this life, intention and institution, centre and locality, doctrine and decree, are exactly where the archive was always going to bite. The chapters that follow apply that lesson without further ceremony, to the founder's government exactly as to his party.

## October, and the government of a besieged capital

The summer between the April conference and the seizure of power displayed the conjunctural method under maximum load. The party grew explosively in the factories and garrisons; the July Days, a premature armed demonstration, drove its leader into hiding in Finland with a price on his head and a press campaign branding him a German agent; the Kornilov affair, an attempted military coup against the provisional government, returned his party to legality as the government's necessary defenders and armed its workers' militia with state rifles. Through each oscillation he recalibrated, sometimes daily: restraint when the moment was unripe, at the cost of denunciations of cowardice, and then, from hiding, a barrage of letters demanding insurrection as an art to be treated with the seriousness of a science, timed to the congress of soviets, against the hesitations of his own central committee, two of whose most senior members published their dissent and were denounced by him as strikebreakers. The insurrection, when it came in late October by the old calendar, was almost bloodless in the capital, a transfer of railway stations, bridges, telegraph offices and finally the Winter Palace to the military revolutionary committee of the soviet. The analysis of the seizure as an event belongs to this series' companion treatment of [october revolution marxist analysis](/2014/05/01/october-revolution-marxist-analysis/); the biography registers the personal fact. At fifty paces of remove, in hiding, in disguise, he had willed a reluctant committee into the act on which everything else in his life, and in this profile, depends.

What followed in the first weeks combined the April programme with improvisation under collapse. The new Council of People's Commissars issued decrees on peace and on land, the second adopting wholesale the peasant programme of a rival party, the Socialist Revolutionaries, on the stated reasoning that a revolutionary government enacts the will the countryside had already written. The banks were seized, the courts abolished and remade, the church disestablished, the calendar reformed, the old ministries occupied by commissars who found the machinery deserted by its officials and sabotaged by strike. It is important to the record's honesty to state that the government's first intentions, as documented in its decrees and in its leader's writings of those months, ran toward the Commune model of the state book: elected administration, workers' control, the militia principle. It is equally important to state that within weeks the government had founded the institution that came to symbolise the opposite trajectory. Both statements are true, and the space between them is the biography's remaining problem.

The Vikzhel episode, in the seizure's immediate aftermath, deserves its sentence in the biography because it tested the coalition question while coalition was still possible. The railwaymen's union executive, commanding the transport on which the capital's food depended, demanded a homogeneous socialist government drawn from all the soviet parties, with the founder's name specifically canvassed for exclusion by some negotiators, and talks opened in which several of his own senior colleagues were prepared to concede a broad coalition. The founder killed the negotiation from the central committee, denouncing the concession as treason to the revolution's mandate, and the colleagues who had entertained it briefly resigned from the government in protest before returning. The episode is the hinge between October and the one-party state in miniature: the alternative was not the old regime but a plural socialist government of the soviet parties, it was available for a matter of days, and it was refused, on principle and by name, by the man this profile follows. His reasoning, that a coalition with parties committed to compromising with the propertied classes would strangle the decrees on peace and land, was stated publicly and is part of the record. So is the refusal.

## The first winter of power: peace, land and the move to Moscow

The new government's survival through its first winter was not foreordained, and the sequence of decisions that secured it belongs to the biography before the record is indicted or defended. The peace question came first and nearly broke the leadership. The German terms at Brest-Litovsk were predatory beyond the party's imagining, stripping the former empire of its western borderlands and their industry, and the central committee divided three ways: sign nothing and fight a revolutionary war the army could not wage; stall in Trotsky's formula of neither war nor peace; or sign and buy the breathing space. Lenin carried signature by the margin of a threatened resignation, judging with characteristic coldness that a government which fell for a principle would be replaced by one that signed worse. The treaty held for the life of the German empire and collapsed with it, and the vindication of the judgement has been claimed ever since by the method's defenders as the April discipline applied to diplomacy: reality first, doctrine's dignity afterward.

In the same months the government moved the capital from Petrograd to Moscow, under German guns and against every sentiment of the revolution's birthplace, and built the Red Army from the wreckage of the old one, with Trotsky at the war commissariat conscripting former imperial officers under political commissars, a decision Lenin defended against his own side's horror at the spectacle of tsarist generals in soviet service. The Left Socialist Revolutionaries, coalition partners since the winter, revolted in the summer after Brest and the grain decrees, assassinating the German ambassador and briefly seizing the telegraph in Moscow; the rising was suppressed within a day and the coalition era ended, leaving the one-party state a fact before it was ever proclaimed a principle, a sequence the record section's plainness requires the reader to hold exactly. Land, meanwhile, had been settled in the countryside's own way: the decree adopting the peasant mandates had licensed the seizure of the estates, and through the first year the village divided the land in a black repartition that the government ratified because it could not have prevented it. The alliance strategy had passed its first examination. Its second, the feeding of the cities from that same village, began immediately, and its failure is the road from this section to Kronstadt.

## The record of the regime, in its own account

This profile now pays the debt contracted in its opening paragraphs. The record of the government Lenin led is part of his biography, not an appendix to it, and it is stated here as a sequence of documented acts and institutions, attributed where historians dispute their interpretation, and without the arithmetic this profile has forsworn. The assessment of that record, including its contested figures, is assembled in the companion treatment, the [case against leninism](/2014/06/15/case-against-leninism/); what follows is the statement without which no assessment could begin.

In the December of the revolution's first winter, six weeks after the seizure of power, the government founded the All-Russian Extraordinary Commission, the Cheka, under Felix Dzerzhinsky, charged with combating counter-revolution and sabotage. It began as an investigative body in a single Petrograd building and grew, within the lifetime of its founder's government, into a security apparatus with its own troops, its own tribunals in practice, and powers of arrest, hostage-taking and execution exercised outside the ordinary courts. Its founder's instructions from the head of the government survive in the documentary record and include directives to apply terror as a method and to treat categories of class enemies as liable to preventive repression. In the January following, the Constituent Assembly, elected by universal suffrage in the freest vote Russia had held, met in the Tauride Palace, refused to endorse the soviet government's authority, and was dispersed after a single sitting; the guard commander informed the delegates, in the anecdote every history repeats, that the guard was tired, and the deputies who returned the next day found the doors barred by bayonets. The act extinguished, at its first test, the principle that governmental legitimacy in Russia would be settled by counting votes, and it was defended by the government's leader in print as the subordination of formal democracy to the higher democracy of the soviets. The biography records the defence alongside the act, and the reader may weigh them together.

The peace of Brest-Litovsk, signed in the March of that year after the government's leader threatened resignation to carry it against his own central committee, purchased the regime's survival at the price of immense territories, and divided his party nearly to splitting. Through the spring and summer the civil war became general: Czech legionaries in revolt along the Trans-Siberian railway, White governments forming at the periphery, foreign contingents landing at Archangel, Murmansk and Vladivostok, and the economy of the soviet heartland collapsing into requisition and barter. In the last days of August a socialist revolutionary assassin, Fanny Kaplan, shot and seriously wounded the head of the government in Moscow, hours after the assassination of the Petrograd Cheka chief. The government's answer was formal. A decree of the following week proclaimed the Red Terror as policy: mass arrests, the shooting of hostages drawn from the former possessing classes in announced reprisal for assassinations and plots, and the concentration camp system extended as an instrument of class isolation. The biography states these as the government's own published measures, in its own decrees, and notes, as the context the record itself requires, that White forces in the territories they held practised terror of their own, including pogroms and mass executions of prisoners, and that foreign intervention was a fact of the war rather than a pretext invented for it. Context explains the conditions under which choices were made. It does not make the choices, and this profile does not permit it to.

### How should the civil war be weighed in judging the record?

As context that explains conditions without settling verdicts. The war was savage on every side, White forces practised their own terror, and foreign armies intervened on Russian soil. Those facts bear on why emergency measures found support. They do not by themselves determine which measures were necessary, which were chosen, and which outlasted any emergency.

The war's institutional residue is the part of the record that outlasted the emergency, and it can be enumerated with the same plainness. Grain requisitioning, the prodrazvyorstka, by which detachments seized the peasantry's marketed surplus at fixed nominal prices, turned the alliance on which the regime rested into an armed antagonism and provoked rural revolts across the grain provinces in the war's last years. Rival socialist parties, Mensheviks and Socialist Revolutionaries who had shared platforms with the Bolsheviks for two decades, were progressively excluded from the soviets, arrested in waves, and by the war's end eliminated as a legal presence, although individual members were still, in the early period, occasionally amnestied into government service. In the March of the war's final year the naval garrison of Kronstadt, celebrated by the government's own side as the pride and glory of the revolution for its role in October, rose against the regime demanding soviet elections, free speech for socialist parties and an end to requisitioning; the rising was suppressed by assault across the ice at heavy cost, and its surviving participants were shot, imprisoned or exiled through the security organs. At the party congress meeting in the same days, the government carried the resolution On Party Unity, which banned organised factions inside the ruling party itself and authorised the expulsion of members who formed them; its leader defended the ban as an emergency measure for a besieged fortress of a party. It remained in force, as the fortress's constitution, for the rest of the state's existence. The pattern across these acts is the fact the biography must leave standing: measures announced as wartime expedients hardened into the permanent structure of the regime, and the hardening occurred under the authority, and in several cases the direct drafting, of the man whose state book had promised the opposite trajectory. Defenders of the record answer, and the answer is given its full strength in the objection section below, that the state book's trajectory was a destination and the war a detour imposed by enemies. Critics answer that the detour was chosen at each junction by the driver. The biography's duty ends at the clarity of the record; it has been discharged when the reader can see both the destination promised and the road taken, in the same field of vision.

## The closing of the presses

One act of the first days belongs in the record's front rank and is sometimes misplaced by narrative histories into the civil war's emergencies, although it preceded them. Within days of the seizure of power the new government issued its decree on the press, closing the newspapers of the propertied parties on the stated ground that the bourgeois press was a weapon in the hands of a class at war, and the founder defended the closures in council and in print against socialists who protested that the revolution was strangling its own freedoms in its first week. His defence deserves the biography's plainness, because it states the governing principle without disguise: freedom of the press, in his formulation, meant freedom from the domination of capital over the means of publication, the printing works and paper stocks were to pass to the state and the working organisations, and the newspapers of the dispossessed classes were to be suppressed for exactly as long as the dispossessed resisted their dispossession. The temporary measure, like the others catalogued in this record, did not lapse. The censorship apparatus formalised in the following years, with its central administration for literature and publishing, administered the principle for the life of the state. The reader who wishes to understand how the Commune book's author closed newspapers in his first week has the materials in this profile's two accounts, and the biography declines to reconcile them, since the founder himself never attempted the reconciliation in print: the same winter produced the decree on the press and the freest months of publishing Russia's radical underground had ever known, for the parties not yet suppressed, and both facts are the record.

## The first constitution and the categories of the excluded

The record's institutional plainness extends to the state's founding document, adopted in the first summer of the civil war, and the biography includes it because constitutions reveal what decrees conceal. The constitution of the Russian republic proclaimed the dictatorship of the proletariat and the working peasantry in soviet form, vested authority in the congress of soviets and its central executive, and weighted representation toward the urban working class over the countryside whose alliance had made the state possible, on the stated arithmetic that the proletariat's political maturity entitled it to leadership within the alliance's own constitution. It also disenfranchised, by category, the classes the revolution had dispossessed: employers of hired labour, rentiers, private traders, clergy, former police officials and several further classes of person lost the vote and eligibility by status rather than by any individual act, creating the population the Soviet vocabulary itself named the deprived, whose disabilities extended in practice to rations, housing, education and employment and were heritable in effect by their children. The founder defended the exclusions as the frankness of a class state that admitted what bourgeois constitutions hid, and the defence belongs in the record alongside the categories. The document's freedoms, of speech, press, assembly and conscience, were guaranteed to the working people and conditioned on the revolution's interests in terms the security organs administered, and the gap between the text's liberties and the practice's permissions became, within the founder's lifetime, a standard exhibit of both his critics, who read the text as camouflage, and his defenders, who read the practice as war. The biography's method requires the plainer statement: the constitution was the Commune book's state translated into a besieged country's law, and the translation's losses, elected officials subordinated to party appointment, recall unexercised, the excluded classes defined by birth and former occupation, are measurable in the document itself without reference to any later deformation.

One further element completes the record's plainness, because it concerns the theory of the state the founder carried into the decrees. In his own definitions of the period, dictatorship was named without euphemism as rule resting directly on force and unrestricted by laws, and terror was discussed in his writings not as an excess to be deplored but as an instrument whose necessity the class enemy's resistance determined. The biographer is obliged to register this, because it forecloses the comfortable theory that the practice contradicted the doctrine at every point. On the state book's Commune forms, the practice diverged from the text; on the definition of dictatorship as unrestricted force, the practice and the text agreed, and the agreement is part of the record. His defenders reply that the definitions were formulated under invasion and civil war and aimed at the coercion of a dispossessed minority by the majority's state. His critics reply that a definition unrestricted by laws contains no internal stopping point, and that the institutions built under it behaved accordingly long after the invasion ended. Both replies are given here in the forms their holders use, and the profile's table has already placed the two halves of the founder's state doctrine, the Commune promise and the dictatorship definition, in adjacent columns of the same row, which is where the reader's judgement should find them.

## The chairman at work

Between the crises, a working method governed the government, and the biographer should describe it because so much of the regime's character was set at one desk. Lenin chaired the Council of People's Commissars with a chairman's intolerance for drift: agendas circulated in advance, speakers cut short, decisions minuted on the spot, and a blizzard of notes, telephone messages and dictated instructions radiating outward to commissariats, provincial committees and individuals, demanding reports, denouncing delay, and returning obsessively to verification, to the checking of execution as the point where revolutions, in his repeated formulation, actually failed. He read drafts pen in hand, annotated decrees, telephoned subordinates at midnight, and conducted the government's business in a small Kremlin office whose austerity, the plain desk, the bookshelves, the cat by some accounts, became part of the legend his successors manufactured. He also mastered, earlier than most of his colleagues, the politics of the appointment: the slow recognition that in a state fused with a party, the disposition of cadres was the disposition of power, a recognition his final writings turned against the man who had mastered it better, the general secretary whose office controlled the files.

The same years show the private man receding behind the office in ways the memoir literature, friendly and hostile, agrees on. The marriage to Krupskaya continued as a partnership of work; the household took in his sister Maria; there were no children, a fact the hagiography passed over in silence and the hostile literature in insinuation, and which the honest biography simply records. He hunted, walked, kept cats, played chess badly and angrily, and after the assassination attempt worked through pain and the lodged bullet that surgery of the period judged safer to leave in place, until it was removed years later in an operation that preceded, by weeks, his first great stroke. The physical decline and the political decline of his design are entangled in the record from this point, and the biography follows them together.

The famine on the Volga in the first year of the peace presented the chairman with the purest test of his hierarchy of goods, and his handling of it belongs in the working portrait. The drought had struck a countryside already stripped by requisitioning, and the government's first instinct, to manage the catastrophe as a matter of internal order, collapsed before the scale of the starvation in the provinces his own alliance strategy had courted. He authorised, over the suspicions of colleagues who smelled intervention in every sack of foreign flour, the appeal to international relief and the agreement with the American Relief Administration, whose feeding stations, run by the recent enemy's personnel on soviet soil, sustained the starving regions through the worst year on conditions that included the administration's operational independence. The decision cost the regime prestige it could measure and saved lives it could not have saved unaided, and the founder treated the transaction in his correspondence with the unsentimental clarity of the Brest negotiation: a government that let its peasants die for its dignity would deserve the judgement history was preparing for it in either case. The episode is recorded here without the mortality figures this profile has forsworn, and with the other half of the record kept in view, since the same famine season carried the church confiscation campaign described in its own section above. The juxtaposition is not an editorial trick. It is the biography's method in its final form: the same chairman, in the same months, signing the relief agreement that admitted the catastrophe and the instructions that exploited a different catastrophe for the destruction of an enemy institution, and the reader left, as the reader of this whole profile has been left, with both documents open on the same desk.

## The international wager

The government had been founded on a wager its leader never concealed: that the Russian revolution was the opening of a European one, and that without revolution in the advanced countries, above all Germany, the isolated soviet state would be strangled, deformed or forced into accommodations that consumed its purpose. The founding of the Communist International in the spring of the war's aftermath institutionalised the wager, gathering the left wings of the old parties into a new international with its seat in Moscow and its conditions of admission, the famous twenty one points, drafted substantially by Lenin to enforce the organisational model of his faction on a world scale: centralism, discipline, the combination of legal and illegal work, and the expulsion of reformists as a condition of membership. The wager half succeeded and wholly failed in instructive proportions. Communist parties of mass significance arose across Europe and Asia, permanently altering the politics of the century; the German revolution, on which the strategy's timetable depended, was defeated in successive waves, and by the time of the last writings the Russian leadership faced the fact its founder had always treated as the catastrophe to be avoided: socialism to be built, or at least defended, in one country, and a backward one. The phrase itself belongs to the succession struggle after his death, and the biography flags it here only to mark the boundary of his own thought. Lenin's framework presupposed the international horizon to the end, and his final economic writings, on cooperation and on the alliance with the peasantry, read as attempts to buy time for a revolution elsewhere that his health would not let him witness. Readers weighing his legacy, in the succession section below, should notice how much of what later called itself Leninism consisted of answers to a predicament he had defined as intolerable and temporary.

## The eastern turn: the colonial theses and Baku

The international wager had a second theatre, and the founder's direction of it in the revolution's third summer marks the last major extension of his thought. At the Communist International's second congress he drafted the theses on the national and colonial questions, and then did the thing the biography has learned to watch for: he amended his own draft in committee under the criticism of the Indian delegate M. N. Roy, softening the theses' confidence in alliances with bourgeois nationalist movements and sharpening the distinction between the movements communists might support and the classes they should organise independently. The episode is small and the tradition made much of it, because it showed the founder, at the height of his authority, accepting a colonial delegate's correction on the question the delegate's world understood better. The theses as adopted committed the International to the support of liberation movements in the colonies and the backward countries as reserves of the world revolution, the alliance strategy of the Russian village projected onto the map of empire, and the congress of the peoples of the east at Baku, convened weeks later with its thousands of delegates from Persia, Turkey and Central Asia, dramatised the projection for the world press. The weighing belongs to the century that followed, in which the colonial revolution did indeed become the system's central drama, though rarely under the management the theses prescribed, and in which the national movements used the alliance's support with exactly the independence Roy's amendments had predicted. For the life, the eastern turn matters as the final widening of the conjunctural map: the weakest link, first located in Russia, was redrawn along the whole periphery of the imperial system, and the founder spent his last active summers arguing that the revolution's survival would be decided as much in the patience of Asia's awakening as in the factories of a Europe that had declined, so far, to rise.

## Genoa and Rapallo: the revolution learns diplomacy

The wager on European revolution failing, the government he led learned the diplomacy of survival, and the founder supervised the lesson without ever quite endorsing its spirit. At the Genoa conference of the famine's aftermath, the soviet delegation appeared before the assembled capitalist states to negotiate trade and recognition, and in the conference's shadow, at Rapallo, signed with Germany the treaty by which the two outcast powers recognised each other, renounced claims and opened the road to a decade of clandestine and commercial collaboration. Lenin, too ill to travel within the year, directed the delegation's line from Moscow and approved the Rapallo stroke as the international system's first crack exploited by soviet diplomacy, while his writings of the same season returned, almost wistfully, to the arithmetic of the breathing space: every year of peace was a year in which the correlation of forces might change, the peasantry might be reconciled, the economy taught to account. The diplomacy of the breathing space became the state's permanent second language, spoken by commissars he trained, and it sits in the biography as the final form of the conjunctural method, the weakest link analysis applied to chancelleries. Whether the method's master would have recognised the state that fluency eventually served is a question the succession section has already taught the reader to leave open.

## Specialists, officers and the electrification formula

A second pragmatism ran beneath the diplomacy, the regime's systematic conscription of the old world's skills, and it produced the founder's most quoted definition of the new order. The revolution had inherited an economy whose engineers, agronomists, statisticians and officers overwhelmingly despised it, and the founder's policy, fought through against his own side's instincts, was to employ them at high salaries under political supervision rather than to improvise a proletarian expertise that did not exist: former tsarist officers staffed the Red Army's commands under commissars' countersignatures, bourgeois engineers ran the factories their class had owned, and the founder defended the arrangement as the purchase of competence with the only currency available, remarking in substance that socialism would be built, for a generation, by hands trained under capitalism. The policy's tensions never resolved, since every specialist failure could be read as sabotage and every sabotage trial of later years drew on suspicions the arrangement itself generated, but its emblematic success arrived in the plan for electrification, the GOELRO scheme drafted by a commission of engineers under an old Bolshevik who was himself an electrical engineer, which the founder adopted as the regime's second programme and glossed in the formula that became the era's slogan: communism, he told the congress of soviets that adopted the plan, is soviet power plus the electrification of the whole country. The formula deserves its place in the biography because it states the founder's final synthesis in his own arithmetic: political power of the soviet form, plus the technical modernisation the old order had failed to deliver, equals the new society, with the plus sign doing the work that the stage doctrine had once done and the peasantry, the alliance and the terror alike left unmentioned in the equation. The engineers' plan outlasted the regime's founder and much of the regime, and the dams it projected still stand on the rivers it surveyed, which is more than can be said for most of the paper the revolution produced.

## War communism's theory, and the trade union debate

Before the retreat can be understood, the advance it retreated from needs its own account, because war communism was not only an improvisation under siege but, for a season, a theory in its own right. The nationalisation of industry down to small workshops, the abolition in practice of money's role in state exchange, the rationing system, the labour conscription and the militarisation of striking workers' discipline in the transport collapse: these measures began as expedients and were theorised, by Bukharin above all in his economics of the transition period, as the direct birth pangs of a planned order, coercion itself reinterpreted as the midwife of the new society. Lenin's relation to the theorisation was characteristically double. He sponsored the practice, signed the decrees, defended the requisitioning as the alternative to starvation of the front and the cities, and praised Bukharin's book; he also retained, and expressed when the emergency loosened, the older view that the measures were wartime necessities imposed by ruin rather than the discovered highway to socialism, a reservation the retreat would make official. The trade union debate of the last winter before Kronstadt displayed the regime's internal argument at full stretch. Trotsky proposed to shake up the unions into organs of production discipline, in effect their absorption into the state; the Workers' Opposition demanded the transfer of economic management to the producers themselves through their unions, the nearest thing to a syndicalist challenge the regime produced from inside; and Lenin steered the congress to a middle formula, the unions as schools of communism and transmission belts between party and class, defending their residual independence on the frank ground that the workers needed protection against their own state, which he described, in a phrase the official cult later found embarrassing, as a workers' state with a bureaucratic twist. The debate matters to the biography because it was the last occasion on which the party argued its constitution in public with its founder participating as one disputant among others, and because his formula conceded, on the record, the very gap between the state and the class in whose name it ruled that the record section of this profile documents in institutional form.

## Retreat: the tax in kind and the New Economic Policy

The Kronstadt rising and the peasant revolts of the war's end forced the last great reversal of his career, and it deserves the name reversal beside April's, because it overruled a different kind of orthodoxy: the regime's own. At the congress that banned factions he carried, in the same days, the replacement of grain requisitioning by a fixed tax in kind, leaving the peasant free to trade the surplus above it, and from that concession unfolded the New Economic Policy: a partial restoration of markets, small private manufacture and trade, foreign concessions courted, the commanding heights of banking, heavy industry and foreign trade retained by the state. He defended the policy in the vocabulary of his whole life, as a retreat undertaken in order to advance later on a broader front, comparing it, in a characteristic military figure, to a fortress's army falling back to prepared positions, and he insisted, against the anguish of militants who saw the revolution sold, that a state which could not yet feed itself through socialised production had to learn to trade, to account and to measure. The weighing of the retreat has never closed. One tradition reads it as proof of the conjunctural method's honesty, the April discipline applied to economics, doctrine discarded when the harvest refused it. Another reads it as the confession that the October wager on the peasantry had failed in its first form, and that the regime survived by renegotiating with the class it had requisitioned. Both readings draw on his own words, and the biography notes only the fact both must accommodate: the retreat was designed by the same man, in the same month, as the faction ban, so that the regime's economic liberalisation and its political closure proceeded as a single act of statecraft, each half incomprehensible without the other.

## Church, culture and the famine year

Two policies of the final active year complete the portrait's shadows and lights, and both are documented to an unusual standard. The first is the campaign against the church during the famine on the Volga. The government decreed the confiscation of church valuables, formally to purchase food abroad, and the patriarch's resistance to the seizure of consecrated objects supplied the occasion for a confrontation the founder's private correspondence, published from the archives after the Soviet collapse, shows him directing with cold deliberation: the famine was the moment, the instruction ran in substance, when the church could be smashed as an institution with the least political cost, and resistance by clergy was to be met with shootings in numbers sufficient to fix the lesson in memory for decades. The letter is among the most damaging documents in the archival record, and it belongs in the biography unsoftened, precisely because it cannot be filed under civil war improvisation; it is strategy, dictated in an office, about an enemy already disarmed. The second policy runs the other way. In education and culture the founder's tastes were conservative and his patronage real: the commissariat of enlightenment under Lunacharsky protected monuments, museums and theatres through the civil war's worst winters, the avant garde's Proletkult movement was curbed for its claim to a separate proletarian culture manufactured on command, and the leader's own reported pleasures, in Gorky's memoir account, were Pushkin, Beethoven and the classics, with modernist experiment tolerated as a citizen's right and declined as a personal taste. He is reported by the same memoirist to have refused a favourite sonata on the ground that music made him want to pat the heads of people who, in the world's real arrangements, deserved harder treatment, an anecdote every school of biography quotes and none can verify beyond the memoir that carries it. The pairing is the man in miniature: an institutional ruthlessness about enemies, joined to a genuine, unfashionable reverence for the culture those enemies had made, and a suspicion of his own tenderness as a political liability.

## Illness, the last writings, and the silenced warning

The first great stroke struck in the May following the faction ban congress's second spring, paralysing his right side and taking his speech for a season; he recovered sufficiently to resume work by the autumn, quarrelling, characteristically, with the physicians' regime and with his colleagues' solicitude in roughly equal measure. A second stroke in December ended his active government. What he did with the interval defines the end of the biography's main narrative, because in a dictated sequence of notes and articles, produced in short daily sessions against medical prohibition, he conducted a final audit of the regime he had founded and found it wanting in the particulars he could still reach. The notes to the congress, dictated across the Christmas days of that year and known ever since as the Testament, assessed the leading figures of the central committee with a portraitist's economy, warned that the relations between the two most prominent successors, Stalin and Trotsky, held the danger of a split that could destroy the party, and in a postscript added days later, after a personal clash over the treatment of Krupskaya, urged the removal of Stalin from the post of general secretary on the grounds quoted in the answer below. The last articles widened the audit: on cooperation, reimagining the road to socialism through the peasantry's own associations over a whole historical epoch; on the reorganisation of the state inspectorate, denouncing the apparatus as borrowed from the old regime and barely repainted; on the nationalities question, after the Georgian affair had shown him his own cadres abusing a small republic, a dictated memorandum of real fury against Great Russian chauvinism, assigning blame upward to the general secretary and his allies. A third stroke in the following March destroyed speech and ended the audit. He lived ten more months at Gorki, outside Moscow, largely unable to communicate, while the colleagues he had assessed manoeuvred around his silence, and he died there in the January of 1924, aged fifty three.

### What did Lenin's final dictated notes actually ask for?

Known as the Testament, the notes assessed the leading figures of the party and warned that a split between the two most prominent successors threatened the whole project. A postscript urged the removal of the general secretary on grounds of rudeness, disloyalty and concentrated authority. The congress that received the notes declined to act on them fully.

How complete was the alarm? The misreading to correct runs in both directions, and the brief for this profile named it precisely: the late writings show neither full continuity nor full repentance. He did not renounce the one-party state, the terror's institutions or the faction ban; the inspectorate he proposed to reform the apparatus was itself an organ of the apparatus, and the cooperative road presupposed the regime whose survival was never in question in his pages. What he registered, with unique authority and increasing desperation, was the bureaucratisation of the revolution: the swelling of an administrative caste recruited from the old order's clerks, the conversion of party office into career, and the concentration of appointment power in a secretariat whose holder he judged unfit. The historian Moshe Lewin built the classic account of this final phase, Lenin's Last Struggle, arguing that the dying leader was opening a fight against the bureaucratic degeneration of his own creation when speech failed him. Other historians discount the coherence of the fight, noting the fragments' brevity and the impossibility of knowing what a recovered Lenin could have carried against the machine. The biography leaves the two readings where the evidence leaves them, and notes the sequel as fact: the Testament was read to delegations at the congress after his death, not published, not acted on, and suppressed in the country of its author for three decades, while the general secretary it named for removal consolidated the power the document had warned against. The suppression is itself biographical evidence, of what the successors understood the founder's authority to be worth once it pointed at them.

## The succession struggle inside the sickroom

The last year of the life was also the first year of the succession, and the two processes occupied the same rooms. As the leader's speech failed in stages, the colleagues organised the future in his name and increasingly against his interventions. A troika of Zinoviev, Kamenev and Stalin governed in coalition against Trotsky, the war commissar whose prestige from the civil war made him the succession's obvious alternative and whose absence through illness at the decisive season his enemies used without mercy. The dying man's attempted counter-moves survive in the documentary record like dispatches from a besieged headquarters: the proposal to enlarge the central committee with working class members as ballast against the apparatus, the alliance he sought with Trotsky on the nationalities question and on the monopoly of foreign trade, the instruction to Krupskaya that was answered by the general secretary's telephone rudeness, the incident that precipitated the Testament's postscript. After the final stroke the struggle proceeded around a silence the whole leadership had an interest in preserving, since a recovered Lenin was the one eventuality every faction feared more than a dead one. The widow became the Testament's guardian and its first casualty, pressed by the troika to surrender the document, permitted its restricted reading to the congress delegations, and progressively marginalised as the decade turned, until she was reduced to protesting, in letters the leadership suppressed, against the cult being manufactured over the coffin. The recruitments of the Lenin Levy, the mass admission of workers to the party in the founder's memory in the months after his death, transformed the organisation's composition in the name of honouring him, diluting the old guard whose memories might have resisted the apparatus; historians who study the party's sociology treat the levy, as much as any congress vote, as the succession's decisive act. None of this belongs to the founder's intentions. All of it belongs to his biography, because the instrument he built conducted its first succession exactly as it had conducted its faction fights, by control of the agenda, the files and the door, and the founder's documented alarm is unintelligible unless the reader sees the machine already moving while he watched it, unable to speak.

## The strongest objection, stated at full strength

Every profile of this man owes its reader one passage in which the case against its own method is made as strongly as its enemies can make it, and the objection to this profile's method, the separation of the ideas from the record, is formidable. It runs as follows. The separation is not a scholarly discipline but the oldest apologetic device in the tradition's armoury, the mechanism by which the theory survives every one of its implementations. When the regime flourishes in any particular, the theory takes the credit; when it disperses assemblies, founds security police and proclaims terror, the circumstances take the blame, and the theory is wheeled out, unsullied, for the next attempt. A doctrine whose founder held absolute power for six years and used it to build the institutions described in the record section is not a philosophy that happened to be misapplied by strangers. It is a theory of power, tested by its author, in power, with the results inscribed in decrees he signed. Judged by the standard he applied to every rival, by the practice that verifies the idea, the ideas fail with the record, and the biographer who keeps them in separate accounts is not being fair but being had.

The answer this profile offers begins with a concession, recorded in the brief itself: the objection has particular force here, precisely because the theory in question is a theory of how to take and hold power, and cannot plead the innocence of a doctrine of ethics or aesthetics ambushed by events. The man invited the test. What can be said in reply is, first, that the standard the objection applies would, applied consistently, disqualify nearly every political tradition that has ever governed, including several the objectors themselves inhabit, and that a standard which condemns everything explains the differences between things poorly. Second, the separation is not an acquittal machine unless it is rigged, and in these pages it has not been rigged: the record stands in the biography's own voice, the contributions are weighed with their institutional consequences in the same table, and the reader who concludes that the consequences damn the contributions has been given every fact required for that conclusion by the text itself. Third, the ideas demonstrably had fates independent of the regime, in movements and arguments the regime opposed, suppressed or never reached, and a history of the ideas that stops at the Kremlin wall cannot explain their century. The objection, answered this far and no further, retains permanent value as a discipline, the same value this profile found in it: it forbids the biographer the comfort of the two sealed boxes, and it requires that every contribution, in the table and out of it, carry its consequence in the adjacent column, where the reader can see it.

## Lenin and Stalin: one project, or a broken succession?

No question in the biography is asked more often, and none is more frequently answered by assumption rather than argument. Was the regime of the successor the founder's project continued, or his project broken? The evidence available to the reader of this profile has been arranged, deliberately, so that both constructions can be built from it, because the honest state of the scholarship is a substantive division, and the division deserves its evidence stated in parallel.

### Was Stalin the inevitable heir?

Historians divide, and the division is substantive rather than rhetorical. One reading stresses continuities of institution and practice that made a personal dictatorship likely whoever prevailed. Another stresses Lenin's late alarm, the Testament's specific warning, and the roads not taken, including the alternative policies argued for inside the party through the nineteen twenties.

The continuity construction rests on institutions and precedents. The one-party monopoly, the security police with extrajudicial powers, the faction ban that criminalised organised disagreement inside the party, the doctrine that the party's rule embodied the working class whatever actual workers voted, the habit of governing by campaign and quota and terror against categories: all of these existed, functioned and were defended in print before the founder's death, and the successor inherited them as going concerns rather than inventing them. On this reading, associated in its sharpest form with historians such as Richard Pipes, the succession changed the scale and the personnel of the system without changing its logic, and the founder's late alarm reads as a quarrel about manners within a house he had built. The discontinuity construction rests on the same record read at a different joint. The founder's terror was aimed, in its own stated categories, at class enemies and armed opponents in a civil war; the successor's greatest terror consumed the party itself, the officer corps and the founder's surviving comrades, a qualitative turn the founder's Testament had, in effect, predicted as the system's ruin. The founder's last economic writings pointed toward a long accommodation with the peasantry; the successor abolished that accommodation in forced collectivisation, at a cost in famine which the record of the founder's government, for all its requisitioning, does not parallel in kind. Historians such as Stephen Cohen argued, with Bukharin's defeated programme as the evidence, that an alternative succession existed inside the party's own politics and lost a contest, not a law of nature. The biography takes no side in the construction war, beyond insisting on its reality: the question is not settled by the founder's intentions, which are documented in both directions, nor by the successor's invocations of him, which were instruments of rule. It is settled, if at all, by the reader's weighing of institutions against turns, and the profile has tried to give that weighing honest materials.

One posthumous fact belongs in this section because it governed all the others. Within days of the death the successor party renamed Petrograd for the founder, embalmed the body against the widow's wishes and installed it in a mausoleum that became the state's central shrine, and began the manufacture of an official Lenin, systematised into a doctrine with the founder's name joined to Marx's, against which deviation could be measured and punished. The manufacture required the suppression of the Testament, the editing of the record and the demotion of the widow, and it was conducted by men who had every reason to fear the founder's documented opinions about themselves. Trotsky, in exile, answered with a counter-manufacture, the founder as the betrayed prophet of permanent revolution. Between the shrine and the counter-shrine the historical man, faction fighter, pamphleteer, chairman and dying auditor of his own regime, has had to be recovered by scholarship ever since, and the next section surveys the recovery.

## The manufacture of Leninism

The doctrine that governed much of the following century under his name was assembled after his death, at speed, by the men who buried his Testament, and the biography should mark the assembly line. The word Leninism itself had begun as an opponents' label, a term of abuse in Menshevik polemic for the factional style of the Bolshevik leader, and was converted after his death into the official name of the orthodoxy, most efficiently in the lectures on the foundations of Leninism delivered by Stalin in the spring after the funeral, which codified the founder as the Marxism of the imperialist epoch in a catechism of numbered theses. Codification required a canon, and the canon required editing: the collected works were issued under party supervision, the inconvenient documents were delayed or buried, the widow's protests were filed away, and a research institute bearing his name became the shrine's scriptorium. Trotsky's counter-codification, mounted from exile, preserved the founder as the prophet of permanent revolution and internationalism against the national doctrine of the successors, and between the two manufactures the historical figure, whose signature act had been the public discarding of a catechism in April, was frozen into the one thing his method had existed to prevent. The irony is not decorative. It explains why the recovery work of the later scholarship, surveyed in the next section, had to begin by prising the man loose from his own doctrine, and why the self-assessment method of this profile, contributions weighed one by one against consequences, is not a neutral choice but a restitution: it returns to the subject the right his successors denied him, the right to be judged in parts.

## The funeral and the mausoleum: the state's first ritual

The death, when it came in the January of the new year, was managed by the successors as the state's first great ritual, and the management is the biography's proper ending because it converted the man into the instrument the manufacture section describes. The body was brought from Gorki to lie in state in the Hall of Columns, where the queues of mourners in the bitter cold were numbered by the organisers in the hundreds of thousands, the frost, the braziers and the delegations of peasants and factory workers composing a genuine popular grief that the ritual's designers did not need to invent and were careful to harness. The widow's wishes, for a private burial beside his mother and brother in Petrograd, were set aside by the commission on the funeral, in which the general secretary sat; the embalmers were summoned, the temporary wooden mausoleum raised on Red Square within the week, and the funeral oratory, delivered by the troika and the opposition alike in the congress of soviets then sitting, performed the succession struggle in liturgical form, each speaker swearing fidelity to a founder whose instructions about the succession lay suppressed in the party's safe. Trotsky, absent in the Caucasus for his health, missed the funeral entirely, an absence he later charged was engineered by false information about the date and which damaged him beyond easy repair with the party's grieving ranks. The enrolment campaign in the founder's name followed within weeks, and the city of the revolution's birth took his name before the month was out. A biography that began with a schoolboy in Simbirsk ends, properly, with the observation that the state's first use of the founder was as its own foundation stone, laid over his widow's objections and his Testament's warnings, in a tomb designed for pilgrimage. What the pilgrims were brought to venerate was not the man of this profile, the faction fighter who broke catechisms, but the catechism he would have broken.

## How the biographers divide

The recovery has proceeded in schools, and a reader meeting the literature deserves the map before the territory. The hostile school, whose most formidable English-language representative is Richard Pipes, reads the life backward from the regime: the ideas are indicted through the institutions, the organisational doctrine is treated as the terror in embryo, and the founder's intelligence is acknowledged precisely to establish his responsibility. The sympathetic tradition, running from Isaac Deutscher's essays through the party histories of the Trotskyist movement, reads the life forward from the oppression it fought: the terror is contextualised into the civil war's necessities, the founder's flexibility is celebrated as the method's essence, and the successor figures as the gravedigger of the revolution rather than its heir. Between and around these poles the archival scholarship of the decades since the Soviet collapse has complicated every inherited certainty. Robert Service's biography, the first full English life built on the opened archives, produced a portrait more personal and less systematic than either school wanted: a leader of genuine theoretical power and genuine ruthlessness, whose private kindnesses and public cruelties the narrative refuses to reconcile into a single key. Lars Lih's work recovered the orthodox, Erfurt-model Lenin whom both schools had buried, the admirer of German social democracy whose most famous pamphlet worried about workers' backwardness in terms his contemporaries found conventional. Orlando Figes placed the life inside the people's tragedy of the whole society, distributing causation beyond any single will. Dmitri Volkogonov, the Soviet general turned archivist, published documents on the terror's administration that settled some factual questions and inflamed all the interpretive ones. The reader of this profile has been given, at each stage, the attributed version rather than the editorial one wherever the schools diverge, because a biography of this subject that conceals the division of its sources is asking for a trust the sources have not earned.

## The prose weapon: Lenin as a writer

Posterity has filed him as an organiser and a ruler, and undervalues the instrument through which both careers were conducted: he was one of the most effective political writers of his century, in volume, in range and in the specific art of the devastating summary. The collected works run to dozens of volumes, and their bulk is journalism, resolutions, letters and speeches rather than treatises, yet the journalism has a signature as recognisable as a fingerprint. He wrote in short declarative sentences and shorter paragraphs, built arguments as numbered indictments, defined his terms at the outset and held opponents to their own definitions without mercy, and salted analysis with proverbs, classical tags and a peasant earthiness that made doctrine sound like common sense overheard. His doctrine of style, stated in the party press writings, was a politics in itself: write so the least prepared reader can follow, repeat the essential point until it lodges, and treat obscurity as a class failing, the intellectual's self-indulgence. The method had a shadow the biographer should record. The polemical arsenal included the label as a weapon, the suffixes of deviation, the vocabulary of renegacy, opportunism and social chauvinism applied with a taxonomist's confidence, and labels in his hands had careers: applied to persons, they prepared organisational consequences, and in the state years the distance from the label to the file in the security organ's cabinet could be short. To read him well is to hold the two facts together, that the clarity was a democratic gift to readers his rivals wrote over, and that the taxonomy of enemies was a technology of exclusion whose later operators needed to invent nothing.

## The party school: Longjumeau and the manufacture of cadres

One institution of the exile years embodied the founder's theory of the party more intimately than any pamphlet, and it survives in the record mostly as anecdote, which the biography should correct. In the summer before the war's approach he established, in a village outside Paris, a party school for workers summoned from Russia, some two dozen students selected by the underground committees, housed communally, fed simply, and lectured for a season by the exile leadership on political economy, the agrarian question, the history of the movement and the craft of organisation, with the founder taking the central subjects himself. The school's purpose was the manufacture of the professional revolutionary as a reproducible type: workers pulled from the factory floor, given the theory whole rather than in leaflets, and returned to the underground as organisers who could argue with intellectuals and outlast policemen. Its graduates' later careers ran through the revolution's cadres in numbers disproportionate to the school's size, and the experiment's model, the residential party school under central direction, was reproduced by the regime he founded at scale, in the communist universities and party schools of the following decade, where the students were numbered in thousands and the curriculum's centre had shifted from argument to catechism. The contrast between the village school, where the founder's lectures invited the heckling his students remembered with pride, and the later institutions that bore the model's name is the April pattern in educational form: a method built for a living movement hardening, in other hands and other conditions, into the ritual it had been designed to prevent. The school's other product was less tangible and perhaps more consequential. It demonstrated, to the faction's own satisfaction, that workers could be made into theorists on demand, a demonstration the founder cited for the rest of his life whenever the party's intellectuals doubted the class's capacity, and it underwrote the confidence with which, in the revolution's spring, he appealed over his own educated leadership to the party's working class ranks and won.

## The friendships and their wreckage: Martov, Bogdanov, Gorky

A biography of arguments should close its account of the exile world with the three relationships whose wreckage maps the man's emotional economy, because Lenin conducted his friendships inside his politics and ended them there too. Julius Martov was the closest collaborator of the Iskra years, the co-editor whose pen the founder rated above every other in the movement, and the split of the congress divided, in the standard image of the memoirists, two men who completed each other's sentences. Their subsequent twenty years, Menshevik against Bolshevik, were fought with a bitterness proportionate to the intimacy, and yet the record preserves the counterpoint: the founder's private acknowledgements of Martov's honesty amid the denunciations of his politics, the regime's permission, unique among the opposition's leaders, for the dying Martov to leave Russia for medical treatment abroad, and the founder's reported judgement that his old friend was the most honest of opponents and the most hopeless of politicians. The friendship's wreck became the movement's founding parable of what the cause demanded, and both men, in their final illnesses abroad and at home, were attended by the knowledge of what it had cost.

Alexander Bogdanov was the alliance of the first revolution's years, the Bolsheviks' second figure, philosopher, physician, organiser of the party schools on Capri and in Paris, and the founder's most dangerous internal rival, dangerous precisely because he commanded a following among the worker cadres the founder prized. Their break, over philosophy as much as organisation, produced the materialism book and the expulsion of the Bogdanov group, and the biography reads the episode as the party doctrine's shadow in personal form: a disagreement about epistemology and school curricula processed, by the machinery of faction, into an excommunication. Bogdanov's afterlife, directing the institute for blood transfusion in the new state and dying by an experiment on himself, closed a career that the founder's victory had diverted from politics into science, and the historians of the party's culture treat the diversion as among the revolution's quieter losses, the road of a proletarian culture built by its most original thinker, foreclosed by the founder's orthodoxy before the state existed to foreclose it.

Maxim Gorky was the outsider of the three, the famous writer whose money, fame and conscience made him the revolution's most valuable fellow traveller and its most troublesome intimate. The founder valued him, visited him on Capri, quarrelled with his god building heresy, used his name as the movement's letterhead to the world, and endured, in the revolution's first year, the spectacle of Gorky's newspaper publishing the Untimely Thoughts, a running indictment of Bolshevik violence and cultural vandalism by the revolution's own greatest living writer. The regime closed the newspaper in the first summer of the civil war, in the press campaign this profile has recorded, and the founder's letters of the same season mix genuine solicitude for Gorky's health with the instruction that he leave Petrograd for his own safety, an exile arranged as medical advice. Gorky went, and his return a decade later, to become the literary ornament of the successor's state, belongs to another biography. For this one the friendship's lesson completes the triptych. Martov was the equal he out-argued, Bogdanov the lieutenant he excommunicated, Gorky the conscience he managed; in each case the relationship survived exactly as long as it could be contained within the politics, and the man who emerges, formidable, devoted and, in the precise sense, friendless by design, is the man the April conference and the Kremlin inherited. The biographer who finds this cold should set beside it the record's other constant: no one who worked beside him, in any of the three camps the friendships became, accused him of serving himself. The wreckage was never purchased for comfort, money or safety. It was purchased for the cause as he defined it, and the price list is part of the definition's cost.

## The philosopher in the notebooks

Twice in the life the polemicist stopped and read philosophy as if the revolution depended on it, and the two episodes frame his intellectual character better than any treatise he finished. The first produced the official philosophy book of the trough years, the defence of materialism against the Machist fashion among his own intellectuals, written in haste and anger and marked, as sympathetic critics conceded from the start, by a crudity about its opponents' positions that the advocate's method explains and does not excuse. The second episode reversed the first's temper. Alone in the Bern library in the war's first autumn, with the international in ruins around him, he worked through Hegel's Science of Logic in a set of notebooks that remained unpublished for years, and the reader of the conspectus watches the catechism loosen in his hands: aphorisms conceding that intelligent idealism stands nearer to intelligent materialism than the stupid variety, marginalia scolding Marx's own followers for skipping the Logic, and the famous self-directed remark, in substance, that none of the Marxists had understood Marx's masterwork because none had studied the dialectic it presupposed. Scholars dispute how far the notebooks transformed the politics that followed, whether the April flexibility and the state book's audacity draw on the Hegel reading or merely coincide with it, and the dispute is recorded here as the literature's standing question rather than settled either way. What the notebooks settle is the portrait's shading. The supposed monolith read his tradition's hardest book, in exile, in wartime, with a pencil, and argued with it in the margins as an equal. A biography that omits the pencil has missed the man as surely as one that omits the police files.

## Lenin's Marx, and the quarrel with Kautsky

The founder's relation to his own founder needs a section, because the most consequential argument of the revolutionary year was conducted as a dispute over Marx's correct reading. Lenin presented the April turn not as innovation but as restoration: Marx, on his account, had never taught the stage catechism the orthodox recited; the Paris Commune writings showed Marx expecting the old state to be broken, not inherited through electoral ripening, and the late Marx of the Russian correspondence had contemplated paths for Russia that bypassed the full capitalist sequence. The scholarly question whether this restoration was faithful, whether Leninism broke with Marx or recovered him, has its own full treatment in this series at [was leninism a break with marx](/2014/05/15/was-leninism-a-break-with-marx/), and the biography registers the biographical fact beneath the scholarship: the man experienced himself, sincerely and combatively, as Marx's executor against Marx's epigones, and the experience fuelled the confidence with which he overruled his own party in April. No figure embodied the epigones for him like Karl Kautsky, the pope of Marxism in the generation between Engels and the war, whose authority the young Lenin had revered and whose wartime centrism he came to treat as the movement's central corruption. Their dispute, fought in pamphlets of extraordinary violence, including his denunciation of Kautsky as a renegade for abandoning the dictatorship of the proletariat, is treated at length in the [lenin vs kautsky debate](/2014/07/01/lenin-vs-kautsky-debate/), and it closes the circle of the life with a symmetry the novelists would reject as contrived. The April reversal was Kautsky's orthodoxy discarded by its most gifted student, in the name of the master the orthodoxy claimed to guard.

## The verdict of the Marxist schools themselves

A biography of a Marxist by Marxists' own standards should record how the tradition's later schools, which owed him their existence, divided over the inheritance, since the division maps the contributions more honestly than any external poll. The first codifier from within the movement's intellectual wing was Georg Lukács, whose short study of the founder, written in the year of the death, fixed the image of Lenin as the thinker of the conjuncture, the man whose genius was the actuality of the revolution read in the concrete moment, an image this profile's hinge section has effectively adopted. Gramsci, writing from a fascist prison, built his theory of hegemony in sustained silent dialogue with the founder's practice, treating the alliance strategy and the party as a collective intellectual as the tradition's living core and the war of position in the west as the April method translated for societies where the state wore civil society as armour. The Frankfurt tradition, by contrast, kept the founder at a wary distance, its theorists drawn to the early Marx of the manuscripts and inclined to file the Russian's organisational legacy among the causes of the movement's authoritarian deformation. Trotsky's school preserved the founder as the betrayed alternative to his successor, a reading whose documentary passion produced indispensable editions and a systematic blindness about the founder's own record in power. The Western Marxism that Perry Anderson surveyed defined itself, in part, by its migration away from the founder's terrain of party and state toward philosophy and aesthetics, a migration that is itself a verdict of a kind. The schools agree on nothing except the premise this biography began with: that the man cannot be treated as a footnote to his predecessor, and that any Marxism describing itself must first say which of the five contributions it is keeping, and which it is prepared to bury.

The schools' division has one further use for the reader of this profile, since it predicts the shape of every new biography before it is opened. A life written from the continuity school's premises will organise itself around the organisational doctrine and arrive at the terror as at a destination; a life from the sympathetic tradition will organise itself around the conjunctures and arrive at the same terror as at an ambush laid by circumstance; a life from the archival generation will organise itself around the documents and arrive, characteristically, at a man more improvised and more implicated than either predecessor allowed. The present profile has attempted the fourth organisation, the subject's own: contributions first, consequences in the adjacent column, the record stated in the biography's own voice and the verdicts left where verdicts belong, with the reader. Whether the attempt has succeeded is not the author's call. The standard it set itself can, however, be checked, and the checking is the reader's final exercise: take any contribution from the table, and ask whether the text has anywhere flinched, in the claim, in the consequence, or in the fate, from the strongest form of what the documents show.

## For students and exam candidates

Build the answer on the spine this profile has used, because it is the spine examiners can reward: five contributions, one reversal, two accounts. The contributions most often examined are the party doctrine, the imperialism analysis and the state book, and the safe treatment of each names the text, the claim and one serious objection, rather than reproducing the tradition's slogans. The reversal is the highest-value material in the life, because it lets an answer discuss the stage doctrine, the soviets and originality inside Marxism in a single episode with dates and opponents attached. On the record, examiners reward the candidate who states the acts precisely and separates the statement from the verdict, and they penalise both the answer that omits the terror and the answer that substitutes denunciation for chronology. Two traps recur in scripts. The brother's vow is legend and should be flagged as such if used at all. And Bolshevik did not mean a standing majority, as the naming section explains; an answer that knows why earns the mark that a hundred answers repeating the translation miss.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The life teaches best as a sequence of documents against their moments: the pamphlet of the Iskra years beside the police state it answered, the April Theses beside the Pravda line they overthrew, the state book beside the decrees of the following winter, the Testament beside the congress that buried it. Set the pairs and let students find the distances themselves; the distances are the lesson. The 1903 naming repays ten minutes of any seminar, because it trains the habit of asking what a label originally counted. The hardest classroom problem is the record section's balance, and the practical solution is documentary discipline: decrees and dates on the table, attribution for every interpretation, and the civil war admitted as context while being refused as solvent. Students who arrive with a verdict, in either direction, should be sent to the contribution and consequence table and required to argue from a column they did not choose. The profile has been written so that such an exercise is possible without the teacher importing a single external text.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For the researcher, the open questions cluster where the archives meet the intentions. The Malinovsky affair still disciplines any account of the pre-war party's penetration by the police, and the documentary trail of the terror's administration, directives, confirmations and local initiatives, remains the terrain on which the argument about centre and periphery is actually fought. The Testament's transmission, its reading to delegations, its suppression and its foreign publication, is a case study in how a founder's text becomes a weapon whose custody decides its effect. For the historian of ideas, the two facts most often garbled in general accounts are the ones this profile has laboured: the factional names record a thinned vote at the end of one congress rather than a balance of forces, and the April Theses reversed the line of the author's own party, not a position he had never held. Journalists borrowing the life for analogy should borrow those two facts first, since both correct the cartoon versions, of the born majority and the lifelong blueprint, that analogy usually trades in. A reader assembling a working shelf on the life can keep the rival biographies straight, and file new claims against them, with the help of a structured notebook companion such as the [Marxism study notes at VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), where each contribution in the table can be tracked against the record sections as further reading accumulates.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Lenin?

Vladimir Ilyich Lenin, born Ulyanov in Simbirsk in 1870, was the Russian revolutionary who led the Bolshevik party, founded the soviet state and reshaped Marxism into a strategy for seizing and holding power. A lawyer by training and a professional revolutionary by vocation, he spent two decades in exile writing and faction fighting before returning in the revolution year to overrule his own party's strategy, lead the October seizure of power, and head the government through civil war until illness ended his work. He died in 1924, leaving a body of theory, a state, and a contested succession.

### Q: What did Lenin actually contribute to Marxist theory?

Five things, on the assessment this profile defends. He theorised the party capable of surviving illegality, a core of professional revolutionaries around which wider movements could organise. He analysed imperialism as a monopoly stage of capitalism that made war structural and revolution possible at the chain's weakest link. He recovered Marx's writings on the state and the Commune into a doctrine of smashing the bureaucratic machinery. He built a method for judging when a situation is revolutionary. And he worked out the strategy of the worker and peasant alliance for revolution in an agrarian country, sealed by the April reversal of his own tradition's stage doctrine.

### Q: Why was Lenin's brother executed?

Alexander Ulyanov, a gifted zoology student, joined a small terrorist group descended from the People's Will which plotted to assassinate the Tsar with explosives on the anniversary of his father's murder. The plot was penetrated by the police before any attempt was made, and Alexander refused at trial to plead in any terms that would shift blame onto comrades. He was hanged at the Shlisselburg fortress in 1887, aged twenty one. The execution ostracised the family and marked the younger brother permanently, although the famous vow attributed to the schoolboy on hearing the news has no reliable source and is treated by historians as legend.

### Q: What were the April Theses?

They were the ten theses Lenin read to party meetings the day after he reached Petrograd from exile in the spring of the revolution, and then published against his own leadership's resistance. They demanded no support for the provisional government, the transfer of power to the soviets, a republic of soviets rather than a parliamentary republic, abolition of the standing army, police and bureaucracy, and nationalisation of land and banks. Their revolutionary core was the reversal they performed: the party had treated the revolution as a bourgeois democratic stage to be completed, and the theses declared the stage doctrine obsolete in the presence of the soviets. Within weeks the party conference adopted them.

### Q: Why are they called Bolsheviks?

The name records arithmetic, not essence. At the party congress held in Brussels and London in 1903, after the Bund and the Economist delegates had walked out, Lenin's supporters won the late votes on the central committee and the editorial board of the party newspaper, and he named them Bolsheviks, majority people, against the minority people, the Mensheviks. The substantive split at the congress, over the definition of party membership, had actually been won by Martov's side. For most of the following decade the supposed minority was at least as strong as the supposed majority, which is why historians treat the labels as a propagandist's asset rather than a description.

### Q: Was Lenin trained as a lawyer?

Yes, and the training left marks on everything he wrote. Expelled from Kazan University for joining a student protest, he was barred from attendance and prepared alone for the law examinations of St Petersburg University, passing with first class honours as an external student. He practised briefly in Samara as an assistant barrister, handling a handful of minor cases, before revolutionary work absorbed him entirely. The advocate's habits survived the career change: the brief marshalled toward a single conclusion, the opponent's text prosecuted clause by clause, the numbered indictment. Colleagues and enemies alike recognised the courtroom inside the pamphlets.

### Q: What is Lenin's Testament?

It is the set of notes he dictated in the last days of his active life, after his second stroke, assessing the leading figures of the central committee. The notes warned that the party faced a danger of splitting along the line between its two most prominent successors, and a postscript, prompted by a clash over the treatment of his wife, urged the removal of Stalin as general secretary, citing his rudeness, his disloyalty and the unlimited authority concentrated in the post. The notes were read to delegations at the congress following his death, never published in his own country for three decades, and never acted on, which is why historians treat their custody as part of the succession story itself.

### Q: Did Lenin show any alarm about the direction of the regime?

Yes, in specific and documented directions, although the alarm never became a repudiation of the regime itself. In his final dictated writings he denounced the bureaucratisation of the state apparatus, proposed reorganising the inspectorate meant to control it, attacked Great Russian chauvinism in the party's handling of Georgia, and warned against the concentration of appointment power in the general secretary's hands. Historians such as Moshe Lewin read these fragments as the opening of a last struggle against the bureaucratic degeneration of his own creation. He did not renounce the one-party state or the terror's institutions, and readings that turn the alarm into repentance go beyond the documents.

### Q: How did Lenin die?

He died at Gorki, outside Moscow, in January 1924, aged fifty three, after a sequence of strokes that destroyed first his mobility and then his speech. The first major stroke struck in the spring of 1922; he recovered enough to work for some months, was struck again that December, dictated the Testament and his last articles in the interval, and suffered a third stroke the following March which ended his political life. He lived ten further months largely unable to communicate while his colleagues contested the succession around him. A bullet lodged in his body from the assassination attempt of 1918 had been removed by surgery shortly before the first stroke, a proximity the medical literature discusses without settling.

### Q: Was Lenin popular in Russia?

The honest answer separates the moments. In the autumn of the revolution his party had won majorities in the soviets of the two capitals and real support among soldiers, sailors and factory workers, on the programme of peace, land and bread, and the seizure of power ratified that support in the institutions that then counted. Beyond those constituencies the picture thins: the peasant majority's attitude was conditional and turned hostile under grain requisitioning, as the revolts of the civil war's end demonstrated, and the suppression of rival socialist parties removed the instruments by which popularity could have been measured or renewed. After his death the state manufactured a cult of him, mausoleum, renamed city and all, which tells the historian about the successors' needs rather than the country's feelings.

### Q: How does the April reversal change the standard picture of Lenin?

The standard picture holds a doctrinaire: a man with a finished blueprint who imposed it on events. April shows the opposite operation. The blueprint existed, the stage doctrine, held by his party and in part by his own earlier writings, and he discarded it in public, against his own leadership, because the soviets had made it obsolete in fact. The reversal does not soften the portrait, since the same man could be merciless toward other people's revisions, but it relocates his distinctiveness. What he contributed was not fidelity to a system but a method for abandoning systems at the moment the conjuncture turned, and any honest account of the tradition he founded has to explain why his successors made a catechism of the man who broke one.

### Q: What should a biographer separate between Lenin the tactician and Lenin the theorist?

The biographer should separate the shelf life of the judgements. The tactical writings are brilliant and perishable: instructions for a congress, a boycott, a moment in a war, whose value expired with the vote they were written to win, and which include misjudgements, of agents, of timings, of allies, that the legend of infallibility conceals. The theoretical contributions are fewer, slower and arguable: the party form, the imperialism analysis, the state doctrine, the conjunctural method, the alliance strategy. Confusing the two bodies of work produces both errors of the literature, the hagiography that treats every instruction as philosophy and the denunciation that treats the philosophy as a mere alibi for the instructions. The man's own practice joins them, since his theory was engineered to settle tactical disputes, but the assessment of each must proceed on its own clock.

### Q: Why did Lenin's years in exile shape the writings?

Because exile was his workplace, his battlefield and his distortion at once. It gave him the libraries of London, Munich and Zurich, the archives and the leisure for the large books, and it gave his prose its polemical edge, since in emigration argument was the only form of action available and a pamphlet was an artillery barrage. It also removed him from the movement he claimed to lead, so that his picture of Russia arrived through letters, agents and newspapers, filtered and delayed, and his judgements of distance were sometimes superb and sometimes catastrophically wrong, as in the years he defended a police agent on his own central committee. The writings carry both marks permanently: the scholar's apparatus and the faction fighter's aim, the patience of the reading room and the impatience of the man writing for a country he could not see.

### Q: How did Lenin handle being overruled by his own party?

Badly in manner and brilliantly in method, and the combination is a key to the career. He did not accept adverse votes as verdicts; he treated them as positions to be reversed by further argument, and where the body that voted was too senior to move he appealed past it, to conferences, to district committees, to the rank and file, with resignation threats held in reserve for the votes that mattered most, as over peace with Germany. In April he was voted down and simply continued the argument until the organisation adopted his line entire. The method built a party that expected its leader to be right eventually, which is one reason the party found his absence, in illness and after death, so impossible to fill: the institution had been designed around a man who reversed its decisions by force of preparation, and it possessed no procedure for replacing that force.

### Q: What evidence would revise the picture of Lenin as a systematic thinker?

Several kinds, and the honest biographer should name them in advance. A discovered manuscript setting out a connected doctrine, of the order of the state book completed and extended, would strengthen the systematic reading against the conjunctural one defended here. Fuller stenographic records of the government's inner deliberations, where they survive incompletely, could shift the balance between the improviser and the planner in either direction. The documentary base of the terror's administration continues to be worked, and findings about the flow of directives between the centre and the local organs bear directly on where responsibility for particular measures sits. What would not revise the picture is another memoir, friendly or hostile, of the kind the literature already holds in surplus: the life has reached the stage where only documents move it.
