The Land Acquisition Act 1894 ended the way it had lived, by an act of state power exercised over people who had no vote in the matter. A statute written for a colonial government that answered to London was repealed by the Parliament of a democratic republic only after one hundred and nineteen years of continuous operation, during sixty-six of which the republic itself had been the acquiring authority and had used the statute more heavily than the empire ever did. The repeal therefore poses two questions at once. The first is mechanical: how did this law work, step by step, from the first notification to the final vesting of land in the government. The second is political, and it is the harder one: why did a free country keep a law like this for two-thirds of a century, and what finally made its replacement unavoidable.

![The Land Acquisition Act 1894 and the acquisition sequence from notification to vesting, showing where the urgency clause removed the landowner hearing - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The mechanical answer can be given in a sentence, and this article will spend most of its length unpacking it. The Act allowed the appropriate government to acquire any land needed for a public purpose or for a company through a sequence of notification, objection, declaration, enquiry, award and possession, paying compensation measured by the market value of the land on the day the process began. Inside that sequence sat one provision that mattered more than all the others. Section 17, the urgency clause, allowed the government to direct that the single hearing the statute gave a landowner should not take place at all, and to take possession of the land fifteen days after notice, before any award had fixed what the land was worth. A law whose only pre-award protection could be switched off by the party it protected against is a law whose real character is set by that switch, and the history of land acquisition in India is largely the history of how readily governments used it.

The wider field, including the statutes that stood beside the 1894 Act and the law that replaced it, is mapped in [the field guide to land acquisition law in India](/2013/10/01/land-acquisition-law-india-guide/), and this article stays inside its own territory: the 1894 statute itself, its machinery, its compensation code, its treatment by the courts, its amendment in 1984, its repeal, and the savings provisions that keep it generating litigation long after its formal death. A reader who understands these things understands why Indian land politics sounds the way it does, because almost every phrase in that politics, public purpose, urgency, solatium, lapse, was coined or hardened inside this Act.

## What the 1894 Act replaced, and the state it was built for

The Act of 1894 was a consolidation, not an invention. Compulsory acquisition arrived in India with the East India Company's need for roads, cantonments and public buildings, and the first general measure on the subject was Regulation I of 1824 in Bengal, which allowed land to be taken for public purposes on payment of compensation assessed through a process that leaned heavily on the judgment of the acquiring authority itself. As the railway age made acquisition a routine instrument of government rather than an occasional expedient, Parliament at Westminster's Indian legislature produced the first all-India statute, Act VI of 1857, and then the more elaborate Act X of 1870, which introduced a reference to the civil court for disputed compensation and began the separation between the executive act of taking and the judicial act of pricing that the 1894 Act would perfect.

The Land Acquisition Act 1894, Act 1 of 1894, gathered this line of legislation into a single code of eight Parts and fifty-five sections, and the purposes it served are written into its architecture. A government that intends to build railways, canals, roads and cantonments across a subcontinent needs three things from an acquisition law. It needs certainty, so that a project is never defeated by a holdout on the route. It needs speed, so that the interval between deciding to build and taking the ground is short. And it needs the price to be administratively determined, because a state that had to bargain parcel by parcel with every owner on a canal line would build nothing. The 1894 Act delivers all three, and it delivers them by concentrating every discretionary judgment in the executive: the government decides that land is needed, the Collector who conducts the process is an officer of that government, the declaration of need is made conclusive against challenge, and the one participatory step in the sequence, the objection hearing, is expressly made dispensable.

### When did the Land Acquisition Act 1894 come into force?

The Land Acquisition Act 1894 is Act 1 of 1894. It received assent on 2 February 1894 and came into force on 1 March 1894, consolidating the earlier acquisition statutes of 1857 and 1870 into one code. It remained in force, substantially unaltered in structure, until its repeal took effect on 1 January 2014.

The constitutional setting in which the statute would operate changed around it without changing it. Under the Government of India Act 1935, acquisition was a divided subject, with the federal and provincial legislatures each competent for their own purposes under the scheme of the legislative lists; the Constitution of 1950 placed "acquisition and requisitioning of property" in Entry 42 of the Concurrent List, so that Parliament and the state legislatures could both legislate on it, with the central Act of 1894 continuing as the general law under the Constitution's continuance of existing laws. That placement explains a feature of the Act's career that puzzles readers who expect a colonial statute to have been a central imposition: for most purposes the acquiring government was a state government, the Collector was a state officer, and the politics of any given acquisition were state politics conducted through a Union-supplied form. It also explains why the Act could be amended in its application by states and why its replacement in 2013, though enacted by Parliament, was immediately followed by states testing how far their own acquisition laws and rules could go.

None of this is a criticism the draftsman would have resisted. The colonial state did not pretend that acquisition was a transaction between equals. Eminent domain, the sovereign power to take private property for public use, was understood on all sides as an attribute of sovereignty that the subject experienced rather than negotiated, and the statute's compensation provisions were the conscience of the arrangement: the taking was unilateral, but the price was to be assessed by stated rules, and a court could be asked to check the arithmetic. What the twentieth century would discover is that this settlement, defensible or not in a colony, travelled unchanged into a republic whose Constitution made property a fundamental right and then, later, a constitutional right, and whose development ambitions required land on a scale the canal builders never imagined. The statute did not change. The state wielding it did, twice over.

## The working vocabulary: definitions that decided outcomes

Statutes of this age hide their politics in their definitions, and Section 3 of the 1894 Act is no exception. "Land" was defined to include benefits to arise out of land and things attached to the earth or permanently fastened to anything attached to the earth, which is why the compensation inquiry reached wells, trees, structures and standing crops as components of the one asset rather than as separate claims. "Person interested" included all persons claiming an interest in the compensation, with a person interested in an easement over the land deemed interested in the land itself; the definition was the gate through which every claimant had to pass, and its operation is taken up in the questions at the end of this article. "Collector" meant the Collector of a district and included a deputy commissioner and any officer specially appointed to perform the Collector's functions, a flexibility that let governments staff acquisition with revenue officers and land acquisition officers dedicated to the work, so that the district's senior revenue authority and the acquiring officer were usually the same administrative family. "Court" meant a principal civil court of original jurisdiction, which fixed the forum for references and explains why compensation litigation ran through the district judiciary. "Company" was defined by reference to the companies legislation and extended to societies and cooperative forms, feeding the Part VII track. The "appropriate Government" was the Central Government for the purposes of the Union and the State Government in other cases, a division that made land acquisition an instrument states wielded daily under a central code, land being a subject on which both could legislate with the central Act supplying the shared machinery.

The definition that did the most work was "public purpose" in Section 3(f), and its form is instructive. It was an inclusive definition that declared the expression to include a catalogue: the provision of village sites in districts where the government declared it expedient to acquire land for that purpose; the provision of land for town or rural planning; the provision of land for planned development from public funds in pursuance of a final development plan and for the development of areas for residential purposes; housing and education and health schemes sponsored by government; land for a corporation owned or controlled by the state; and, in the clause that states added and courts applied most elastically, land needed in connection with any other scheme of development sponsored by government. An inclusive definition does not confine the term to the catalogue, and the courts consistently read public purpose by its ordinary width, any purpose in which the general interest of the community, as opposed to the particular interest of individuals, is involved. The practical consequence was that the definitional gate barely filtered anything a government chose to notify, which is why the real contests migrated to the conclusive declaration and to colourable exercise, and why reformers in 2013 concluded that the filter had to be moved from definition to procedure, into consent thresholds and social impact assessment, rather than tightened in words.

## The Land Acquisition Act 1894 sequence: Part II, step by step

The operative machinery of the 1894 Act sits in Part II, running from Section 4 to Section 17, and its logic is a funnel. Each step narrows the landowner's room to resist, widens the finality of what the state has done, and moves the dispute, if any survives, away from the question whether the land should be taken at all and towards the only question the statute was willing to litigate at length, which is what the land was worth. Reading the sequence in order is the fastest way to see the statute's priorities, because the order is itself an argument.

It begins with Section 4. Whenever it appeared to the appropriate government that land in any locality was needed or was likely to be needed for a public purpose or for a company, a notification to that effect had to be published in the Official Gazette and in two daily newspapers circulating in the locality, at least one of them in the regional language, and the Collector had to cause public notice of the substance of the notification to be given at convenient places in the locality. Three features of this opening step deserve attention. The threshold is appearance, not proof: the government need only be of the view that land is likely to be needed. The notification is of a locality and a likelihood, not yet of a final taking, yet it has immediate legal consequences, because the compensation for everything that follows is valued at the date of this notification, and because publication triggers the power in Section 4(2) for authorised officers to enter upon the land, survey it, take levels, dig, set out boundaries and do the other acts necessary to ascertain whether the land is adapted to the purpose. A landowner's first encounter with acquisition was therefore often a survey party on the field, armed with a gazette notification the owner had never seen.

Section 5 then required payment for damage done in the survey, and Section 5A created the hearing. Any person interested in land notified under Section 4 could, within thirty days of the publication of the notification, object in writing to the Collector to the acquisition of the land or of any land in the locality. The Collector was bound to give the objector an opportunity of being heard, in person or by pleader, to make such further enquiry as he thought necessary, and then to submit the case for the decision of the appropriate government with a report containing his recommendations on the objections. The decision of the government on the objections was final. This was the only point in the entire pre-award sequence at which the statute contemplated the landowner speaking to the question whether the acquisition should happen, and its limits define it as much as its existence does. The objection could not question the compensation, which belonged to a later stage. It could address the taking itself and the choice of land, but the tribunal was the acquiring government deciding on a report by its own officer, the hearing was a matter of administrative grace hardened only slightly into duty, and the outcome, once the government decided, was final within the statute. Everything the landowner's side of Indian land politics has ever said about wanting to be heard before the taking, rather than paid after it, is a complaint about the narrowness of this one section.

If the government was satisfied, after considering the Section 5A report where one existed, that particular land was needed for a public purpose or for a company, Section 6 required a declaration to that effect, signed by a Secretary to the government or an authorised officer and published in the Gazette and the newspapers, stating the district, the purpose, the approximate area and where the plan could be inspected. A proviso inserted by the amendment of 1984 barred any declaration made after the expiry of three years from the publication of the Section 4 notification, a limit whose significance is taken up with the 1984 amendment below. The decisive sentence is Section 6(3): the declaration was to be conclusive evidence that the land was needed for a public purpose. Those words closed the courthouse door on the merits of the taking. A court could not go behind the declaration to ask whether the purpose was genuinely public or the need real; the declaration proved itself. The one narrow ground on which the Supreme Court allowed an attack was colourable exercise of power, the proposition, settled in Somawanti v. State of Punjab in 1963, that a declaration made for a purpose foreign to the statute, or in bad faith, is not an exercise of the power at all and so does not earn the conclusive label. That exception matters and is examined with the judicial record below; what matters here is that it is an exception about the honesty of the process, not about the wisdom of the project.

After the declaration the Act moved into its administrative middle. Under Section 7 the government directed the Collector to take order for the acquisition, under Section 8 the land was marked out, measured and planned, and under Section 9 the Collector gave public notice and individual notices to occupiers and persons interested, inviting claims to compensation for all interests in the land. Section 10 empowered the Collector to require statements of interests, names of co-proprietors, mortgages and rents. Then came the enquiry and the award under Section 11: the Collector enquired into the objections to measurement, into the value of the land at the date of the Section 4 notification, and into the respective interests of the claimants, and made an award under his hand stating the true area of the land, the compensation he determined should be allowed, and the apportionment of that compensation among the persons interested. After 1984 no award could be made without the previous approval of the appropriate government or an authorised officer, and the same amendment inserted Section 11A, which required the award to be made within two years of the publication of the declaration, failing which the entire proceedings for the acquisition lapsed, with periods during which action was stayed by a court excluded from the computation. Under Section 12 the award, once filed in the Collector's office, was final and conclusive evidence, as between the Collector and the persons interested, of the true area, the value and the apportionment, subject only to the reference to court that Part III provided. Payment followed under Section 31, with deposit in court where a claimant refused to consent to receive the money or a dispute of title made direct payment unsafe.

The enquiry deserves a closer look, because it was the landowner's one evidentiary forum before the reference and its character set the quality of the award. The Collector conducting it wielded, under Section 14, the powers of a civil court to summon and enforce the attendance of witnesses and to compel the production of documents, and the parties could appear and lead evidence on measurement, on value and on their interests. In practice the enquiry's centre of gravity was the valuation material: the sale statistics of the locality, the exemplars the claimants produced, the classification of the land in the revenue records. The award that emerged was required to state the true area, the compensation and the apportionment, and it operated, once filed, as the conclusive administrative statement of all three as between the Collector and the persons interested. Two consequences followed that recur in the case law. The first is that defects in the enquiry, a failure to consider material exemplars, an award that disclosed no reasoning on a contested head, were the standard grounds on which reference courts discounted the Collector's figure, even though the reference was decided on fresh evidence. The second is that the award's apportionment, made on the revenue record and the claims filed, frequently set the terms of family and title disputes that the civil courts then spent decades resolving under Section 30, because the money could not move until the shares were settled and every claimant knew that the fund, unlike the land, would wait.

Possession came last, and its lateness in the sequence is the point that the urgency clause would undo. Under Section 16, when the Collector had made an award, he could take possession of the land, which thereupon vested in the government free from all encumbrances. Section 48 preserved one executive escape: the government remained free to withdraw from the acquisition of any land of which possession had not been taken, paying compensation for the damage the proceedings had caused, but once possession was taken the acquisition was complete and could not be undone by either side. For a landowner, then, the ordinary course of the 1894 Act offered a thirty-day window to object at the start, a valuation fixed at the start, an executive decision on the objection, an award within two years of the declaration, and a court reference on price after the award. Everything else belonged to the state.

### What does it mean that acquired land vests in the government free from all encumbrances?

Section 16 provides that when the Collector takes possession after making an award, the land vests in the government free from all encumbrances. Every mortgage, lease, lien and private interest that burdened the land is extinguished at that moment, the government takes a clean title, and former claimants are left to their shares of the compensation instead.

### The 1894 sequence and where a landowner could act

| Step | What the government did | What the landowner could do | Time limit | What urgency removed |

| Preliminary notification, Section 4 | Declared land in a locality likely to be needed; officers gained power to enter and survey | Learn of the proposal, organise, prepare an objection | Objection window opened from publication | Nothing at this step |
| Objection and hearing, Section 5A | Collector heard objections and reported; government decided, its decision final | Object in writing and be heard by the Collector | Thirty days from publication of the notification | The entire step: Section 17(4) could direct that Section 5A not apply |
| Declaration, Section 6 | Declared the land required; declaration was conclusive evidence of public purpose | Challenge only for colourable exercise of power | Declaration barred after three years from notification | The interval for organising between notification and declaration |
| Notice and claims, Section 9 | Collector called for claims to compensation from all persons interested | File a claim to compensation and state interests | Date fixed in the notice | Nothing directly; the clock to possession shortened |
| Enquiry and award, Sections 11 to 12 | Collector fixed area, compensation and apportionment; award became final on filing | Put evidence of value before the Collector | Award within two years of declaration, or proceedings lapsed | Sequence inverted: possession could precede the award |
| Possession and vesting, Section 16 | Took possession; land vested in government free from all encumbrances | Receive or refuse the compensation offered | After the award in the ordinary course | Fifteen days after the Section 9 notice, with no award made |
| Reference to court, Section 18 | Collector referred the dispute at the instance of a person interested who had not accepted the award | Seek a court determination of measurement, amount, entitlement or apportionment | Six weeks from the award or notice, or six months from the award, whichever expired first | Nothing; the reference survived, but over land already lost |

Read the last column from top to bottom and the article's central claim states itself. The urgency provision did not merely accelerate the sequence. It removed the hearing outright, compressed the landowner's time to prepare into a fifteen-day notice, and reversed the order of the two events the landowner cared about most, so that possession came first and the argument about price came afterwards, over land that had already changed hands in law.

## The sequence at work: one village, one highway, two fates

Abstractions about funnels and switches become concrete if the sequence is walked through once at ground level, and the following passage is an illustration built from the Act's own steps, with figures chosen only to show the arithmetic the statute performs. Imagine a national highway widening notified across the fields of a village within reach of a growing city. The Section 4 notification appears in the Gazette and in two newspapers, one in the regional language, naming the locality and the likelihood that land is needed for a public purpose. From the date of that publication, the price of every parcel in the notification is frozen for compensation purposes, although the announcement of the highway will itself begin moving the real price within the year. Survey parties enter, take levels and mark the alignment. A farmer whose holding the alignment bisects files his objection within the thirty days Section 5A allows, saying that the line could shift to the village's inferior grazing land and that the cut will leave him with two fragments neither of which his well can serve. The Collector hears him, as the section requires, and reports to the government; the government, which has already sanctioned the alignment, is not satisfied that the objection should prevail, and its decision is final. The declaration follows under Section 6, conclusive evidence that the land is needed for a public purpose, and no court will entertain the argument that the grazing land was the better route. Notices under Section 9 invite claims. The Collector's enquiry values the field at the rates recorded for comparable agricultural sales near the notification date, adds the severance damage for the fragments, allows something for the well and the trees, adds the twelve per cent additional amount for the interval and the thirty per cent solatium, and files the award; within weeks the government takes possession, and the field vests in it free from all encumbrances. The farmer who thinks the recorded sales understate the truth, because everyone in the locality knows the transactions shown in the deed registry are written below the real price as a matter of custom, accepts the money under protest and files his Section 18 application within six weeks. His real case begins there, in a courtroom, about money, years after the highway has opened over his field.

Give the same village a third fate, the one the calendar imposed on thousands of real proceedings. Suppose the award in the ordinary case had been made, but the highway was then shelved in a funding freeze: the compensation, disputed by half the village, was neither collected nor deposited in court, and possession was never taken because there was nothing to build with. The file sleeps. On 1 January 2014 the 2013 Act commences, and Section 24(2) asks of this file two questions: was the award made five years or more before commencement, and had possession been taken or compensation paid. On the village's facts the answers make the proceedings deemed to have lapsed, unless the five years are interrupted by the exclusion the courts later read into the provision for periods of judicial stay. The land, which the record has treated as acquired for a decade, is the farmers' again in law, subject to the state beginning the whole acquisition afresh under the new Act, with its social impact assessment, its consent requirements where they apply, and its multiplied compensation. Multiply that file by the unfinished acquisition drives of every state and the scale of the transition litigation explains itself: the repeal of the 1894 Act was not an epilogue to its story but one of its largest chapters, and it was fought, characteristically, over the two questions the old Act had always made decisive, possession and payment.

Run the same village through the urgency variant and the difference is the whole argument of this article. The notification in this variant recites that the provisions of Section 17(1) apply, and a direction under Section 17(4) states that Section 5A shall not apply. There is no objection to file, because the section that would have received it has been switched off; the farmer's knowledge that the grazing land would serve is never recorded anywhere in the proceeding. The declaration can issue at once. The Section 9 notice appears, fifteen days run, and possession is taken before any award exists, the Collector offering the statutory compensation for the standing crop that the sudden dispossession destroys. Title vests in the government on a file that contains no valuation at all. The award follows when the administration reaches it, and the farmer's remedy is identical in form to his neighbour's in the ordinary case, a reference on price, but it is prosecuted by a man already off the land, against a project already built, with the burden of the contest resting on the party least able to carry it. Nothing in the second story breaks the Act. Every step in it is a step the Act authorises in terms, which is precisely the indictment the critics of the statute pressed for decades: the harshest acquisition India routinely performed was not an abuse of the 1894 Act but one of its two designed modes, and the choice between the modes lay wholly with the taker.

## Section 17: the clause that was the whole statute

Section 17 is headed "Special powers in cases of urgency", and its two operative limbs did different kinds of damage to the sequence just described. The first limb, Section 17(1), provided that in cases of urgency, whenever the appropriate government so directed, the Collector could, although no award had been made, take possession of any land needed for a public purpose on the expiration of fifteen days from the publication of the notice under Section 9(1), and the land thereupon vested in the government free from all encumbrances. The second limb, Section 17(2), dealt with a catalogue of genuine physical emergencies, a sudden change in the channel of a navigable river, an unforeseen emergency requiring a railway administration to take immediate possession to maintain traffic or build a river-side station, and later additions covering structures and systems for irrigation, water supply, drainage, road communication and electricity, and allowed immediate entry and possession after the Section 9 notice, with a safeguard of forty-eight hours' notice before taking possession of a building. In every such case Section 17(3) obliged the Collector, at the time of taking possession, to offer compensation for standing crops and trees and for damage caused by the sudden dispossession, the offer being folded into the eventual award if refused.

The third limb was the one that mattered most, and it is the least visible in summaries of the Act. Section 17(4) provided that where, in the opinion of the appropriate government, the urgency provisions applied to any land, the government could direct that the provisions of Section 5A should not apply, and on such a direction a declaration under Section 6 could be made at any time after the publication of the Section 4 notification. That is, the same opinion that authorised early possession also authorised the complete deletion of the objection hearing, and it authorised the declaration to follow the notification without the interval in which objections would otherwise have been heard and reported on. The dispensation and the acceleration were usually combined, and the standard form of an urgent acquisition was a notification under Section 4 read with Section 17(4), a declaration under Section 6 read with Section 17(1), and possession on fifteen days' notice, with the award and the argument about money trailing behind a transfer of title that had already happened.

Here is the claim this article exists to make, stated plainly so that it can be cited and disputed: urgency was the whole statute. The 1894 Act contained exactly one meaningful protection for a landowner before the award, the right to object and be heard under Section 5A, and it also contained a power to switch that protection off by declaring urgency. Every other safeguard in the Act operated after the taking: the reference to court improved the price, the interest provisions compensated for delay in payment, the lapse rule in Section 11A punished administrative sloth, but none of them restored a voice before the event. The practical severity of Indian land acquisition for over a century therefore depended entirely on how readily governments invoked a clause designed for emergencies, and the record is that they invoked it readily, in ordinary development acquisitions, planned industrial estates, housing schemes and road widening programmes where the emergency was nothing more than the government's own timetable. A clause drafted for rivers changing course became the standard operating procedure for acquiring land from people whose only statutory entitlement was to be heard before they were dispossessed, and to be heard by the dispossessor.

The practice of urgency compounded what the text allowed. Because Section 17(4) required only the government's opinion that the urgency provisions applied, acquiring departments developed the habit of reciting the opinion in the standard notification itself, so that the dispensation of the hearing was not a decision taken case by case after the notification but a drafting choice taken before it. Landowners' challenges therefore had to attack not a reasoned order but a formula, and the courts' insistence that urgency rest on material was answered, in file after file, by affidavits asserting the project schedule. There was also a fiscal logic to the routine use of urgency that is rarely named: possession taken early, before the award, started the vesting at once and confined the contest to money, whereas an acquisition that proceeded in the ordinary order invited injunction litigation at the declaration stage that could freeze a project for years. Governments did not invoke urgency only because they were in a hurry; they invoked it because it was the procedurally dominant strategy the statute offered them, available in every case, priced at nothing, and defeasible only on grounds the landowner bore the burden of establishing. A power so priced will be used at the rate it was used, and any account of the clause that stops at governmental impatience mistakes an incentive for a motive.

The honest counter-argument deserves its full weight, because it was the argument of every acquiring government and it is not frivolous. Section 5A hearings in a large acquisition could draw hundreds of objections, many of them repetitive, many of them directed not at the choice of land but at the price, which the hearing could not address, and a project of linear infrastructure, a highway or a canal, could be stalled for years by litigation over the hearing's adequacy even where the need for the land was not seriously in dispute. Governments argued, and courts often accepted, that the hearing was the least valuable part of the process for the landowner as well, since the government's decision on objections was in practice a foregone conclusion in a notified project, and that the real protection lay in the compensation code and the reference to court. There is force in the observation that a right to be heard by a body that has already decided is a thin right. But the conclusion governments drew from it, that the hearing could be deleted without loss, does not follow, and the courts that scrutinised urgency most carefully said so: the hearing was thin partly because it could be deleted, and the knowledge that an objection would be heard, recorded and reported on by a named officer imposed a discipline on the choice of land that vanished when Section 17(4) was signed as a matter of routine.

## What the courts did with urgency, and with public purpose

The judicial record on the 1894 Act divides into two halves that pull in opposite directions, and an accurate account has to hold both. On the threshold questions, whether land could be taken and whether the hearing could be dispensed with, the courts developed real doctrine but applied it indulgently. On the valuation questions, what the land was worth and how long the state could take to pay, the courts were the landowner's only effective allies, and the long life of the Act's compensation litigation is the proof.

The governing propositions on urgency were settled early and restated often. The existence of urgency was a matter for the satisfaction of the appropriate government, and a court would not substitute its own view of whether a project was urgent. But the satisfaction had to be formed on material that bore on urgency, the dispensation of the Section 5A enquiry was an exception to the only participatory rule in the Act and was not to be treated as the norm, and a long and unexplained delay between the declaration of urgency and the taking of possession contradicted the claim that the matter could not wait for a hearing. In Narayan Govind Gavate v. State of Maharashtra, decided in 1977, the Supreme Court insisted that the government make out a case of urgency on relevant considerations rather than by incantation, precisely because dispensing with the enquiry extinguished a valuable right. In Om Prakash v. State of Uttar Pradesh, decided in 1998, the same Court upheld a dispensation where the land was required for the planned development of an industrial area, holding that a scheme of that character could legitimately be treated as brooking no delay. An earlier decision to similar effect, State of Uttar Pradesh v. Pista Devi, delivered in 1986, had upheld the dispensing with the enquiry for a housing scheme at Meerut, reasoning that the pressing need for housing plots could constitute urgency; the case is routinely cited by acquiring authorities for the breadth of the urgency concept and by landowners for its confinement to the facts of an acknowledged housing shortage, and it illustrates the doctrinal pattern exactly, a principle announced with safeguards and a result delivered for the state. Between those two poles, most invocations survived. High Courts across the country quashed acquisitions where urgency was recited for land that then lay idle for years, or where the notification itself betrayed that the purpose was routine, but the ordinary development acquisition, defended by an affidavit asserting urgency, usually passed.

The tail of the story showed what the doctrine looked like when it finally bit. In the Greater Noida acquisitions, governments had invoked urgency to acquire village land that was then allotted to private builders for residential and commercial development, with possession taken and the hearing dispensed with on the strength of an urgency the timeline contradicted. In Dev Sharan v. State of Uttar Pradesh, decided in 2011, the Supreme Court quashed such an acquisition, holding that the urgency clause could not be used to defeat the landowners' right to object where no genuine urgency existed and the end use was private profit; companion decisions in the same season reached the same result on similar facts. And in Kedar Nath Yadav v. State of West Bengal, decided in 2016, the Court confronted the acquisition at Singur, where land had been acquired for a motor car factory for Tata Motors under the company provisions of the Act, and held the acquisition bad, ordering the land returned to its owners, on grounds that included the failure to follow the procedure the Act prescribes for acquisition for a company and the character of the public purpose asserted. These were exceptional outcomes in a body of case law that mostly upheld the state, but they established the durable point: the urgency power and the conclusive declaration were formidable, not unlimited, and their limits were dishonesty of purpose and falsity of urgency, the two ways an acquisition could be attacked at the root rather than at the price.

A third strand of the case law policed the procedure itself, on the settled principle that a statute authorising the expropriation of private property is to be construed strictly and its mandatory conditions complied with exactly. Acquisitions fell, across the decades, for defects that look small beside the project's scale and were fatal because the Act made them conditions: notifications not published in the modes the section prescribes, declarations signed without authority or issued after the governing time limit, objection hearings reduced to a formality in which the report disclosed no engagement with what the objectors had said, and possession recorded in ways the courts would not accept as the statutory act. The strand mattered out of proportion to its success rate, because it was the one discipline the acquiring administration could not draft around: it made the file itself the battlefield, and it is why the working documents of the 1894 Act, the gazette notifications, the declaration, the award, the possession memorandum or panchnama by which possession was recorded, carry such evidentiary weight in the litigation that still runs. A landowner who could not contest the purpose or the price at the threshold could still win the whole case on the paperwork, and every experienced acquisition lawyer read the file before reading the law.

On public purpose the structure of the doctrine followed from Section 6(3). "Public purpose" was defined inclusively and expansively in Section 3(f), sweeping in village sites, town and country planning, planned development from public funds, housing schemes, education and health schemes, land for local authorities, and land needed in connection with any scheme of development; the definition was a net, not a filter. Once the declaration issued, it was conclusive evidence of need, and the surviving challenge was colourable exercise: a declaration procured for a purpose the Act did not authorise, a taking of A's land dressed as public purpose in order to hand it to B, could be struck down because it was not an exercise of the statutory power at all. Somawanti v. State of Punjab settled that framework in 1963, and it endured because it matched the statute's design, which had never intended the merits of a project to be re-argued in court. The cost of that design fell on landowners whose land was taken for purposes that were formally public and substantively private, a category that grew as development policy blurred the line, and it is no accident that the sharpest judicial corrections, at Singur and Greater Noida alike, arrived through the colourable-exercise and urgency doors rather than through any general review of public purpose, because those were the only doors the Act left unlocked.

## The long middle: a statute without clocks, from 1894 to 1984

One feature of the original Act is easy to miss because the amendment of 1984 removed it from view: for its first ninety years the statute contained no enforceable timetable at all. There was no deadline for the declaration after the notification, and no deadline for the award after the declaration. A Section 4 notification could hang over a locality for years while a project waited for funds or for political weather, and during those years the land lived under what practitioners called notification blight: the owner remained in possession, but the compensation, whenever it came, would be computed at the value on the notification date, buyers treated notified land as radioactive, and the owner could neither sell at the real price nor improve with any confidence that the improvement would be paid for, since Section 24 refused value to improvements made after notification without sanction. The state's option cost it nothing to keep alive; the owner's asset paid the carrying charge. When governments are criticised for acquiring in haste, the other half of the record deserves mention: they also acquired in dawdling, and the dawdling was as lawful as the haste, because the Act disciplined neither. The clocks of 1984, the three-year bar on declarations and the two-year lapse rule for awards, were Parliament's late acknowledgment that an acquisition power without a timetable is two powers, the power to take and the power to freeze, and that the second had been exercised free of charge for ninety years.

The decades between independence and 1984 also filled in the Act's margins. Parliament returned to the company provisions in 1962, in the tightening that produced Section 38A, treating an industrial concern as a company for defined purposes, and Section 44A, the restriction on a company transferring or diverting land acquired for it without sanction. In 1967 a further amending and validating Act repaired classes of proceedings that judicial decisions on the notification and declaration sequence had unsettled, a characteristic episode in the Act's life in which the courts read the procedure strictly and the legislature answered by validation rather than by letting projects fail. Beneath the central text, the states worked their own variations, since acquisition was administered by state machinery: states substituted their own revenue nomenclature for the Collector, adjusted the machinery for their improvement trusts and housing boards, and in some cases enacted parallel acquisition regimes for particular authorities, so that the lived law of acquisition in, say, a Maharashtra industrial area or a Karnataka housing layout was the 1894 Act read through a layer of state amendment and local practice. This layering matters for any reader tracing an old matter: the filename of the grievance is the central Act, but the operative text on a given date in a given state may carry state insertions that a bare central reprint will not show.

It was also in this long middle that the Act acquired its characteristic litigation culture. Because the taking was effectively unreviewable and the price was everything, a specialised bar and a specialised valuation jurisprudence grew up around Sections 23 and 24, with its own doctrines and its own folklore: the comparable sale instance as the gold standard of proof, the suspicion of transactions in the same notification, the treatment of a small plotted parcel's price as evidence for a large tract subject to a deduction for the cost of development, the belting method by which a deep tract abutting a road was valued in strips of diminishing worth, and the recognition of potentiality, the value a parcel carried because of what it could become, tempered always by the rule that the acquiring project's own enhancement was to be left out of account. The Supreme Court's guidance in Chimanlal Hargovinddas v. Special Land Acquisition Officer, delivered in 1988, organised these doctrines into a checklist of plus and minus factors that trial courts were directed to weigh, and it remained the working manual of reference courts for the rest of the Act's life. None of this sophistication touched the decision to acquire. It was a jurisprudence of the invoice, elaborated over a century, in a legal system that had agreed not to argue about the order.

## The compensation code: what the land was worth, and when

Compensation under the 1894 Act was governed by Sections 23 and 24, the first listing the matters to be considered and the second the matters to be neglected, and the pair together amount to a theory of what compulsory purchase owes the person purchased from. The theory is austere. The state owes the value of what it takes, measured at the moment it announces the taking, plus the collateral damage the taking inflicts on what the owner keeps, plus defined additions for the delay built into the process and for the compulsion itself. It does not owe the value the land will have in the project the state is building on it, it does not owe anything for the owner's hopes of a better use, and it owes nothing at all to anyone whose loss is real but whose interest in the land the record does not show.

Section 23(1) directed the court, and through Section 15 the Collector, to take into consideration six matters. First, the market value of the land at the date of the publication of the notification under Section 4(1): the anchor of the whole award, and the provision through which the Act systematically underpaid in a rising market, because the years between notification and possession, years in which the announcement of the project itself could transform local land prices, were years the valuation refused to look at. Secondly, the damage sustained by reason of the taking of standing crops or trees on the land at the time possession was taken. Thirdly, severance damage, the loss where the acquired parcel was cut away from the owner's remaining land and the remainder was diminished. Fourthly, injurious affection, damage by reason of the acquisition injuriously affecting the owner's other property, movable or immovable, or his earnings. Fifthly, where the acquisition compelled the owner to change his residence or place of business, the reasonable expenses incidental to the change. Sixthly, damage bona fide resulting from the diminution of the profits of the land between the publication of the Section 6 declaration and the taking of possession. Section 24 then excluded the mirror image considerations: no enhancement for the urgency of the acquisition, no value drawn from a special adaptability the land had for the acquiring purpose, no use in contravention of law, no improvements made after notification without sanction, and no prospects that existed only because of the scheme underlying the acquisition.

Two additions sat on top of the Section 23 computation, and both came to matter enormously in practice. Section 23(1A), inserted by the amendment of 1984, required an additional amount calculated at twelve per cent per annum on the market value, for the period from the publication of the Section 4 notification to the date of the award or the date of taking possession, whichever was earlier; it was Parliament's acknowledgment that the valuation anchor at the notification date froze the price while the process consumed years, and it functioned as a crude indexation for that freeze. Section 23(2) required, in addition to the market value, a solatium, a sum expressed as a percentage of the market value "in consideration of the compulsory nature of the acquisition". The original Act fixed the solatium at fifteen per cent of market value. The amendment of 1984 raised it to thirty per cent, and a page that quotes the thirty per cent figure for an acquisition of the 1960s or 1970s is wrong in a way that matters, because for most of the Act's life the consolation for compulsion was priced at half the rate later readers assume. Interest completed the code. Where the court on a reference awarded more than the Collector, Section 28 allowed interest on the excess at nine per cent per annum from the date possession was taken to payment into court, rising to fifteen per cent for any part of the excess still unpaid one year after possession; and where the compensation awarded was simply not paid or deposited on or before possession, Section 34 imposed interest at the same nine and fifteen per cent rates until payment or deposit. Before the amendment of 1984 these rates stood at six per cent, and the raising of them in 1984 was as deliberate a part of that amendment's rebalancing as the solatium increase.

How the code worked in the hands of its users explains its reputation better than its text alone. The Collector's valuation habitually began from the sale statistics maintained by the registration department for the locality, and the landowner's standing complaint was that registered sale prices in India systematically understate real consideration, because parties understate price in the deed to save stamp duty, so that the official evidence base for market value was a record of polite fictions. Against that base the Collector applied the other codes of his office, the classification of the soil in the revenue record, the circle or guidance values where the state maintained them, and the precedent of neighbouring awards, each of which pulled the figure towards administrative consistency and away from the price a particular parcel might have fetched in the particular week of notification. The reference court, unconstrained by the Collector's statistics, took oral and documentary evidence of specific transactions and heard the parties' valuers, and the gap between the two processes is one reason references so often enhanced the award and why the state's own appellate lawyers treated a Collector's award as an opening position. The interest provisions then compounded the significance of the gap: an enhancement won after eight years of litigation carried interest on the excess at nine and then fifteen per cent across those years, so the sum finally paid could bear little relation to the sum first offered, a fact acquiring departments understood as litigation risk and landowners understood as the only indexation the system honestly provided for the years the valuation date had frozen.

One further feature of the code must be understood, because it explains the shape of the litigation. The compensation provisions of the 1894 Act operated inside a constitutional settlement about property that independent India fought over for three decades. When the Constitution commenced, Article 31(2) permitted acquisition only for a public purpose and under a law providing for compensation, and in State of West Bengal v. Bela Banerjee, decided in 1954, the Supreme Court read that word to require a just equivalent of what the owner had been deprived of. Parliament answered with the Fourth Amendment of 1955, which made the adequacy of compensation non-justiciable. The contest resumed in [the Bank Nationalisation case](/2013/01/15/bank-nationalisation-case-judgment/), R. C. Cooper v. Union of India, decided in 1970, where the Court struck down the nationalisation of the major banks on grounds that included the hollowness of the compensation principles Parliament had prescribed, and Parliament answered again with the Twenty-fifth Amendment of 1971, substituting the word "amount" for "compensation" in Article 31(2). The Forty-fourth Amendment of 1978 finally removed property from the fundamental rights altogether, deleting Article 31 and Article 19(1)(f) and leaving the subject to Article 300A, under which no person may be deprived of property save by authority of law. The consequence for the 1894 Act was paradoxical. The statute's own internal code, market value at notification plus solatium plus interest, survived every round of that constitutional war untouched, and it was within that code, in references and appeals over valuation, that landowner and state fought their real battles for the rest of the Act's life.

## The partial taking: severance and the economics of what remains

A distinctive cruelty of acquisition practice, and one the compensation code at least attempted to price, was the partial taking. Highways, canals and railway lines rarely consume a holding whole; they slice it, and the owner's loss is not confined to the strip taken. Section 23(1) answered with two heads that practitioners learned to build cases on. Severance damage compensated the diminution in the value of the retained land caused by its separation from the part taken: the field cut off from its well, the plot severed from its road frontage, the farm divided into fragments too small for the machinery that worked it. Injurious affection reached further, to the damage the acquisition and the use of the acquired land inflicted on the owner's other property or earnings: the house left beside a cutting, the mill whose water channel the canal diverted, the business whose access the new road removed. Both heads required proof of actual diminution, usually through the same comparable-sales evidence as the main valuation, and both were vulnerable to the acquiring side's answering argument that the project had enhanced the retained land, an enhancement the courts could set against the damage where the scheme's benefit to the remainder was real. The litigation over these heads is where the human geometry of acquisition was argued most concretely, because it was here that the owner's evidence described not an asset price but a working landscape disassembled. It is also here that the code's limit showed plainest: the heads compensated the titleholder's property losses, and the sharecropper on the severed fragment, whose loss was total, had no head under which to present it.

## Payment, deposit and the enforcement of awards

The money provisions of Part IV, which follow the reference provisions, governed what happened between the award and the money reaching a hand, and they were litigated almost as hard as the valuation. Section 31 required the Collector, on making the award, to tender payment of the compensation to the persons entitled and to pay it to them unless prevented by one of the contingencies the section names: refusal to consent to receive it, absence of a person competent to alienate the land, or a dispute as to title or apportionment, in any of which cases the money was to be deposited in the court to which a reference would be submitted. Deposit in court was not a technicality. It stopped the state's interest liability on the amount deposited in the manner the interest sections contemplate, it transferred the contest over entitlement to the forum equipped to decide it, and it marked the boundary of the Collector's responsibility; the long-running argument under the 2013 Act's transition provision about whether deposit in the government treasury was payment at all, resolved by the Constitution Bench in the landowner's disfavour only in part and on reasoning about tender, grew directly out of this section's insistence on the court as the place of deposit. Section 32 required compensation deposited for persons under disability, minors and persons of unsound mind among them, to be invested in government or approved securities so that the income stood in place of the land's yield until the owner could take the capital. Section 33 did the same for money deposited in other cases, on the application of a party interested. Section 51A, a later insertion, made certified copies of registered documents admissible in evidence of the transactions they recorded, a small provision that greased the entire comparable-sales jurisprudence by letting parties prove the price evidence without producing original deeds. And Section 53 applied the Code of Civil Procedure to proceedings before the court, which is why an award under the Act could be executed like a decree and why unpaid enhanced compensation was recovered from governments through the execution courts, sometimes by attachment of government property, in proceedings that could outlast the careers of the officers who had signed the original notification. ## The reference to court, and the litigation that outlived the projects

Part III of the Act, Sections 18 to 28A, converted the Collector's award from the last word into the first offer. Section 18 provided that any person interested who had not accepted the award could, by written application to the Collector, require the matter to be referred for the determination of the court, whether the objection was to the measurement of the land, the amount of the compensation, the persons to whom it was payable, or the apportionment among those interested. The limitation was tight: six weeks from the date of the award for a person present or represented when it was made, and in other cases six weeks from the receipt of the Collector's notice under Section 12(2) or six months from the date of the award, whichever period expired first. Acceptance mattered; a claimant who accepted the compensation without protest was held to have accepted the award and lost the reference, which is why the files of acquisition litigation are full of receipts endorsed "under protest". On the reference, the court applied the same Sections 23 and 24, took evidence of comparable sales and potentiality, and by Section 25, in the form the 1984 amendment gave it, could not award less than the Collector had awarded. Section 26 prescribed the form of the court's award and treated it as a decree; Section 54 gave an appeal to the High Court; and Section 28A, inserted in 1984, allowed a person who had never sought a reference to apply to the Collector within three months of a court's award on the same notification, and have his own compensation redetermined on the basis of that award, a provision that converted one landowner's successful reference into a rising tide for the whole notified locality.

### How were disputes about the sharing of compensation decided under the 1894 Act?

The Collector's award apportioned the compensation among the persons interested, and where a dispute about apportionment arose, Section 30 required the Collector to refer the dispute to the decision of the court. The court then determined the shares according to each claimant's interest in the land, and the money followed the title as the court declared it.

The nature of the reference deserves a sentence of its own, because it shaped strategy on both sides. The courts treated the Collector's award not as a judgment under appeal but as in substance an offer by the government, which the reference court disregarded and replaced with its own determination on the evidence before it; the claimant's task was to prove the higher value, the award's figure enjoyed no presumption of correctness, and the material the Collector had used was not evidence in the reference unless independently proved. That doctrine made the reference a genuine second chance rather than a review, and it explains why the enhancement percentages in reported cases can be so large without implying error in any appellate sense. It also allocated the costs of the system's design with familiar asymmetry: the second chance was available only to those who could fund years of litigation and wait for interest to make the wait worthwhile, so that within a single notified village the final price of identical fields diverged according to the staying power of their owners, a divergence Section 28A was belatedly designed to compress.

The result of this architecture was a litigation tail of extraordinary length, and it is a structural feature of the Act rather than an accident of Indian court delay. The award fixed value at a notification date that might be a decade before the appeal was heard, and every element of the computation was contestable: the choice of comparable sales, the deduction for development charges where large tracts were valued by the belting method, the treatment of potentiality, the trees and wells and structures, the apportionment among co-sharers whose family arrangements had never been written down. A reference decided in the district court could travel to the High Court under Section 54 and from there by special leave to the Supreme Court, and because interest at nine and then fifteen per cent accrued on the excess throughout, the state's incentive to settle and the claimant's incentive to persist were both alive for the entire journey. Acquisitions notified in the 1970s and 1980s for dams, steel plants and urban development authorities were still being argued over quantum in the twenty-first century, not because anyone disputed the taking, which was long complete and irreversible, but because the price was the one question the Act had left open and it had left it open without a clock. Any reader with an old-Act matter should hold the general map this section gives and take advice on the individual case, because the limitation rules and the protest question are unforgiving and turn on the file, not on general principle.

### Did the 1894 Act allow the government to occupy land temporarily?

Yes. Part VI of the Act, through Section 35, allowed the temporary occupation and use of waste or arable land for a public purpose for a term not exceeding three years, on terms settled by agreement with the owner or, failing agreement, by reference to the court, with the land restored and compensation paid for damage when the term ended.

## Acquisition for companies: Part VII and its tightening

From its first day the 1894 Act served two masters. Its long title speaks of acquisition "for public purposes and for Companies", and Part VII, Sections 38 to 44B, built a parallel track by which the sovereign power of taking could be exercised for the benefit of a company, a destination the ordinary public-purpose track reached only through the generous width of the Section 3(f) definition. The Part VII procedure differed from the ordinary sequence in ways that acknowledged the anomaly of using compulsion for a private balance sheet. Section 39 made two things preconditions: the previous consent of the appropriate government, and the execution of an agreement between the company and the government. Section 40 required a previous enquiry, conducted by an officer appointed by the government, into whether the land was needed for the construction of dwelling houses for workmen employed by the company or for amenities directly connected with such housing, or for the construction of some building or work for a company engaged in an industry or work serving a public purpose, or whether the company had offered the best terms for settling with the owners and had made proper enquiries. Section 41 prescribed the contents of the agreement, including the payment by the company of the cost of the acquisition, the terms on which the land would be held, and the terms on which the public would be entitled to use works constructed on it; Section 42 required the agreement to be published in the Gazette and gave it the force of the provisions of the Act itself, so that its terms bound like statute rather than like contract. Section 50 then threw the cost of the whole proceeding onto the company or local authority at whose instance the land was acquired.

Two further provisions of Part VII shaped how the company track felt in litigation. Section 43 excepted cases where the government was already bound by an agreement to provide land for a company, sparing the enquiry machinery there. Section 50, which charged the cost of acquisition to the company or local authority at whose instance it was made, also gave that body a defined and deliberately limited place in the reference proceedings: the authority or company could appear before the court and adduce evidence for the purpose of determining the amount of compensation, but it could not itself demand a reference under Section 18, however directly the enhancement would fall on its own funds. The allocation is characteristic of the Act's procedural justice: the party paying could watch and testify on the price, the party losing the land could litigate the price, and the party deciding the taking in the first instance was the government that had consented to it.

The track was tightened twice in independent India, and both tightenings tell the same story of a power used more broadly than its justification. The amendment of 1984 inserted Section 44B, which barred acquisition under Part VII for a private company that was not a government company except for the workmen's housing purpose named in clause (a) of Section 40(1); the industrial-estate and factory acquisitions that had flowed through Part VII for private enterprises were, on paper, closed off, though governments continued to reach the same results through the public-purpose track by acquiring for development authorities that allotted to industry. Section 44A, an earlier insertion, forbade a company for which land had been acquired from transferring it, or using it for a purpose other than the acquisition purpose, without the previous sanction of the appropriate government, a provision honoured mainly in the breach until courts began to wield it. It was this company track, and the persistent suspicion that the state's coercion was being rented out, that produced the Act's most famous defeat: the Singur acquisition, conducted for a named private manufacturer through the public-purpose route, collapsed in the Supreme Court in 2016 in part because the Court examined what the acquisition actually was rather than what its notifications called it. The lesson the Part VII story teaches is that the 1894 Act's elasticity was not only in its urgency clause. A statute that will acquire for companies under enquiry, for public purposes under a conclusive declaration, and for development authorities in between, has made the identity of the beneficiary a matter of drafting choice, and landowners learned to litigate the choice.

### Could only part of a house or building be acquired under the 1894 Act?

Section 49 protected the owner. Where part of a house, manufactory or other building was proposed for acquisition, the owner could express a desire that the whole property be acquired, and the government could not take possession of the part unless the owner withdrew that desire or the court decided the question against the owner.

## The development authority: the Act's dominant late form

By the last third of the Act's life, its most characteristic user was neither a railway administration nor a named private company but the development authority, the statutory body created by state after state to assemble land, lay out plots and allot them for industry, housing and commerce. The Act accommodated the form without strain. A development authority or town planning authority fell within the widened definition of "local authority" in Section 3(aa), acquisition for its purposes was a public purpose under the conclusive declaration, and the cost of the acquisition was charged to the authority under Section 50, so the state government's role reduced to consenter and conductor while the authority planned, paid and allotted. The plotted development model that built the industrial estates and the new urban extensions of the republic ran on this combination, and it produced a distinctive grievance that the older dam and railway acquisitions had not: the farmer's field was notified at agricultural value, frozen at the notification date, and emerged from the authority's layout as serviced plots allotted at a multiple of the award, the increment created by the change of use and the public investment around it. The Act had an answer of form: Section 24 excluded from compensation any value attributable to the scheme underlying the acquisition, so the increment belonged in law to the scheme and not to the owner. Whether that answer was justice or expropriation of the betterment was argued for fifty years in exactly those terms, and it is the economic core of the demand, eventually met in the 2013 Act's multipliers and its sharing provisions in defined cases, that the dispossessed should participate in the value the taking itself creates. The development authority chapter of the Act's life is therefore not a footnote to the company chapter but its successor: the anomaly Part VII had managed for private companies, the public-purpose track managed for public authorities allotting to private hands, with the same landowner on the same side of the counter.

## The amendment of 1984: more money, some clocks, the same machine

The Land Acquisition (Amendment) Act 1984, Act 68 of 1984, which commenced on 24 September 1984, was the one substantial central overhaul the statute received in its one hundred and nineteen years, and it is best understood as a repricing rather than a reform. Its occasion was the accumulation of grievance about compensation that the 1970s had made politically impossible to ignore: awards frozen at notification-date values through years of inflation, interest at six per cent that rewarded the state's delay, and proceedings that drifted without any deadline the landowner could enforce. The Law Commission and parliamentary debate had circled the statute for years, and the amending Bill, introduced in 1982, took the shape the transitional provisions still record, carrying several of its benefits back to awards made on or after 30 April 1982, the date of the Bill's introduction.

The changes moved every money term in the landowner's favour. The solatium under Section 23(2) rose from fifteen per cent of market value to thirty per cent. The new Section 23(1A) added the additional amount of twelve per cent per annum on market value for the interval between the Section 4 notification and the award or the taking of possession, whichever was earlier, a direct statutory answer to the frozen valuation date. The interest rates under Sections 28 and 34 rose from six per cent to nine per cent for the first year after possession and fifteen per cent thereafter, converting the state's delay from a cheap loan into an expensive one. The same amendment inserted Section 28A, the redetermination right that let non-litigating owners claim the benefit of a neighbour's court-enhanced award, and it rewrote Section 25 so that the court on a reference could not award less than the Collector's figure. On procedure, the amendment imposed the discipline of clocks without disturbing the allocation of power. Section 11A required the award within two years of the declaration, on pain of the entire proceedings lapsing, with court-stayed periods excluded. The proviso to Section 6 barred declarations made more than three years after the Section 4 notification. Section 11 was amended to require the government's previous approval of the award and to permit consent awards where all persons interested agreed in writing. Section 13A allowed correction of clerical errors, and the publicity of the Section 4 notification itself was widened to require publication in two daily newspapers, one in the regional language, so that the opening of the process could less plausibly pass unknown to the people it concerned. Section 44B, described above, narrowed the company track.

Two quieter features of the amendment repay attention, because both generated their own afterlife. The first is the transitional provision, Section 30 of the amending Act, which carried the enhanced solatium and the new interest rates into defined classes of pending proceedings, reaching back to awards made on or after 30 April 1982, the date the amending Bill had been introduced in the Lok Sabha. The choice of that date was a legislative judgment about fairness between landowners whose proceedings happened to straddle the reform, but it drew a line through the middle of a continuum, and the courts spent years deciding which side of it particular awards, references and appeals fell on, including the recurring question whether a reference court deciding after the commencement could apply the enhanced rates to an award made before the window opened. The episode is a miniature of the larger truth this article keeps meeting: in acquisition law, the boundary provisions of a reform are worth as much money as the reform. The second feature is what the amendment did to the Collector's office. The requirement of the government's previous approval for every award, subject to classes of cases the government could exempt, was sold as a check on low awards and operated in part as one; but it also inserted the acquiring government as a revising authority over its own officer's valuation before the landowner ever saw it, and the consent award, the new Section 11(2), which let the Collector award on agreed terms where all persons interested consented in writing, created a negotiated track whose existence quietly acknowledged that the imposed track's outputs were widely disbelieved. Both devices left the landowner's formal position untouched and his practical position marginally better, which is the amendment in one sentence.

What the amendment did not do is the analytically important part. It did not touch Section 5A's character as a hearing before the acquiring government, and it did not touch Section 17(4)'s power to delete that hearing; the urgency clause emerged from the great reform of the Act entirely unscathed. It did not create any obligation of rehabilitation or resettlement, and it did not enlarge the class of persons the Act compensated beyond those with an interest in the land of record. It did not disturb the conclusive declaration, the notification-date valuation anchor, or the Collector's double position as the government's officer and the landowner's first judge. The structure of 1894, in which the state proposes, hears, decides, values and takes, with the courts confined to price, survived the only serious attempt to amend it with every load-bearing member intact. That survival is the strongest single piece of evidence for this series' recurring thesis about such statutes: the 1984 Parliament was not captured by the colonial design, it examined the design, raised its price, and kept it, because a government that builds needs a machine that takes land, and no majority volunteered to blunt its own machine.

## Independent India: the relic that was a workhorse

The standard story told about the 1894 Act is a story about inheritance: a colonial statute, drafted for an extractive state, survived into the republic by inertia and was finally swept away when democracy caught up with it. The record does not support the inertia. Independent India did not merely retain the Act; it used it with an intensity the colonial state never approached, because the development programme of the republic, river valley projects, steel towns, mines, industrial estates, cantonments, universities, and the planned expansion of cities, was a land programme before it was anything else, and the 1894 Act was the instrument to hand. The large dams of the plan era displaced populations counted in the hundreds of thousands across the decades; Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru, who called the dams the temples of modern India, was also writing to chief ministers within a few years of independence to complain that the displaced were being neglected and that the human side of the projects was failing. The instrument made both the building and the neglect administratively routine, because the Act measured the state's obligation in rupees per acre and stopped there.

The scale can be fixed by naming the canonical projects, because each generation of the republic has its acquisition landscape. The river valley projects came first: Hirakud across the Mahanadi in Odisha, under construction from the late 1940s, submerged villages in their hundreds and displaced their populations with compensation practice that became a byword for inadequacy; Bhakra on the Sutlej and Rihand in Uttar Pradesh wrote the same story in other basins. The steel towns followed, Bhilai, Rourkela and Durgapur, each assembled by notification over farmland and each acquiring not only fields but the commons and grazing lands on which the village economy silently rested, and each leaving a residue of oustee agitation that outlasted the construction camps by decades. Then came the industrial estates and new towns of the state industrial development corporations, the mining belts of the coalfields and of iron ore country, the cantonments and ordnance factories, the national highway corridors, and the urban development authorities whose plotted layouts converted the agricultural fringe of every large city. What unites the list is not ideology; governments of every complexion appear in it. What unites it is the instrument. In each case the same eight-Part statute, written when the telegraph was the fastest administrative technology in the country, supplied the legal form, and in each case its design decisions, the conclusive declaration, the frozen valuation date, the invisible non-titleholder, were reproduced at the scale of the project.

That stopping point is the Act's most consequential silence. Nothing in the 1894 Act obliged the acquiring state to rehabilitate or resettle anyone. The compensation code ran to the "person interested", defined in Section 3(b) as a person claiming an interest in the compensation, which in practice meant the holder of a recorded title or a recognised interest in the land. The tenant without a recorded tenancy, the sharecropper, the landless labourer whose family had worked the field for generations, the forest dweller whose community's use of the land predated the record of rights, the artisan and the grazing household of a village whose common lands were acquired: these were displaced by the acquisition as surely as the owner, and to them the Act owed nothing, because their loss was not an interest in the land the law could see. Resettlement where it happened was policy, announced project by project and honoured unevenly, never entitlement. The consequence is written across the social history of Indian development, in the oustees of the Hirakud and Rihand dams still pressing claims generations later, in the long agitation against the Sardar Sarovar dam on the Narmada led by the Narmada Bachao Andolan under Medha Patkar, and in the eventual political recognition that a compensation cheque to the titleholder is not an answer to the destruction of a livelihood system. When replacement finally came, its longest title word was not compensation but the pair the 1894 Act had never contained: rehabilitation and resettlement.

The political end of the Act began when the geography of acquisition changed. The classic acquisitions had taken rural land for the state itself, for dams and public sector plants, and resistance, however bitter, confronted a government that could present itself as the beneficiary's trustee. From the 1990s the frontier moved to land for private industry, special economic zones and real estate, acquired by the state and transferred onward, and the political chemistry changed with it. At Nandigram in West Bengal in 2007, a proposed chemical hub to be built on acquired farmland met resistance that turned violent, left villagers dead in police firing, and forced the abandonment of the project; at Singur in the same state, the acquisition for the Tata small car plant survived the courts of first instance and died in the Supreme Court a decade later; in Odisha, the proposed POSCO steel plant, for years the largest single foreign investment project announced in India, was resisted on the ground for over a decade and never built. These were not disputes about the rate of solatium. They were refusals of the 1894 settlement itself, the claim that the state could decide, value and take with the owner heard only on price, and they made the statute a national political liability that no amendment of the 1984 pattern could repair. A Law Commission or a committee could have repriced the Act again; what the moment demanded was a different answer to the question who must consent, who must be consulted, and who must be rebuilt after displacement, and that required a new statute.

## The road to replacement: two decades of failed and successful drafting

Replacement was attempted long before it succeeded, and the failures are part of the explanation of the final Act's shape. Through the 1990s and 2000s the policy conversation moved in stages: first the recognition, driven by the dam movements, that rehabilitation needed a national policy; then national rehabilitation policies were announced in 2003 and again in revised form in 2007, as executive policy rather than law, binding projects weakly and landowners not at all. The legislative attempt came in 2007, when the government introduced a Land Acquisition (Amendment) Bill together with a companion Rehabilitation and Resettlement Bill, proposing to narrow the definition of public purpose, to require rehabilitation by statute, and to leave the acquisition machinery substantially in place. Both Bills were passed by the Lok Sabha in February 2009 and lapsed on the dissolution of that House without passage in the Rajya Sabha. The pattern is worth pausing on: even a Parliament confronted with Nandigram could agree in one House to reform the statute and could not carry the reform through, which is the political form of the thesis this article has pressed throughout, that acquiring governments do not readily disarm.

The successful attempt began again with the Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Bill 2011, introduced in the Lok Sabha on 7 September 2011 and referred to the Standing Committee on Rural Development, whose report was presented on 17 May 2012. The Bill that emerged from that process had absorbed the lesson of the agitations: it fused acquisition and rehabilitation into a single statute with a single long title, put social impact assessment and the consent of a defined proportion of affected families for private and public-private projects at the front of the process, multiplied rural compensation, and confined the urgency power to defence, national security and natural calamity emergencies. Its passage in 2013, with support across the aisle in the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha, was possible because the electoral arithmetic of land had changed: the movements had made the old Act a liability to every party that expected to face a village electorate, and the statute that replaced it was drafted to be campaigned on. That origin explains both its generosity and its complexity, and it explains why the counter-attack, when it came through the ordinance of December 2014, aimed precisely at the consent and assessment provisions that the agitations had won.

## The replacement: what the 2013 Act kept, discarded and added

The Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act 2013, Act 30 of 2013, was passed by the Lok Sabha on 29 August 2013 and by the Rajya Sabha on 4 September 2013, received the President's assent on 26 September 2013, and came into force on 1 January 2014. Its full design belongs to [the article on the LARR Act 2013](/2013/11/01/larr-act-2013-explained/), and the head-to-head reckoning belongs to [the comparison of the 1894 Act with the 2013 Act](/2014/02/15/land-acquisition-1894-vs-2013/); what belongs here is the shape of the succession, stated as the three moves every repeal performs.

What it kept was the skeleton of eminent domain itself. The state may still acquire land compulsorily for public purposes, the Collector still conducts the acquisition and makes the award, market value still anchors compensation, and the reference to court still exists for the owner who rejects the award. The 2013 Act is a reform of the 1894 settlement, not its abolition, and the continuity is deliberate: no Indian government, of any party, has proposed that infrastructure wait upon universal consent.

What it discarded was the 1894 theory of the landowner as a price-taker. Gone is the conclusive declaration doing duty for justification, gone is valuation frozen at the first notification without multiplier, gone above all is the urgency clause as a routine instrument; the new statute confines its urgency power to defence, national security and emergencies arising from natural calamity, and forbids the use of urgency to defeat the social impact process in ordinary development acquisition. The hearing that Section 5A rationed and Section 17 deleted is replaced by a social impact assessment conducted in consultation with local self-government institutions, with public hearings in the affected area, before the acquisition is approved at all.

The succession did not end the argument, and the coda belongs in any honest account of the repeal. Because acquisition sits in the Concurrent List, the 2013 Act's settlement was exposed to erosion from below as well as from the Centre: several states, complaining that the consent and assessment requirements stalled their projects, moved to enact their own acquisition legislation or to secure amendments within their sphere, and the map of Indian acquisition law began to differentiate again by state, as it had under the layered operation of the 1894 Act. The central ordinance route having failed, the practical contest over how much of the 2013 discipline survives has been fought in state legislatures and in the courts' reading of repugnancy. A reader comparing the two statutes should therefore resist the tidy picture of a bad law replaced by a good one on a single date; what happened was a change in the default terms of taking, followed by the same centrifugal politics that had shaped the old Act's career.

What it added was the population the 1894 Act could not see. Rehabilitation and resettlement entitlements run to affected families defined to include livelihood losers without title, consent requirements attach to acquisition for private companies and public-private partnership projects, the solatium rises to one hundred per cent of the compensation amount, and rural market value is multiplied before the additions begin. Each addition answers a named failure of the old Act, and the article on compensation and consent rules takes up the detail of the new machinery. Whether the additions made acquisition so slow and expensive that development itself pays the price is the live argument about the 2013 Act; it is engaged below in the verdict, because it is the strongest form of the counter-reading this brief requires.

## Repeal, savings and the afterlife of the 1894 Act

Repeal in Indian statute law is rarely a clean execution, and the death of the 1894 Act was drafted to be a managed succession. Section 114 of the 2013 Act repealed the Land Acquisition Act 1894 outright, but the general savings of Section 6 of the General Clauses Act 1897 preserved rights acquired and liabilities incurred under the repealed statute, and Section 24 of the 2013 Act, the transition provision, laid down the specific rules for proceedings in flight on the day the new law commenced, 1 January 2014. Section 24(1) splits the pending world in two. Where no award under Section 11 of the 1894 Act had been made, the proceedings did not lapse, but the compensation was to be determined under the 2013 Act, so the landowner took the benefit of the new price code within the old proceeding. Where an award had been made, the proceedings continued under the 1894 Act as if it had not been repealed, which is the precise mechanism by which the savings and lapse position keeps the repealed statute at work. Section 24(2) then created the deemed lapse: where an award had been made five years or more before the commencement of the 2013 Act, but physical possession of the land had not been taken or the compensation had not been paid, the proceedings were deemed to have lapsed, and the appropriate government was free, if it still wanted the land, to begin acquisition afresh under the new Act; and a proviso directed that where the compensation for a majority of the land holdings had not been deposited in the account of the beneficiaries, all the beneficiaries named in the Section 4 notification would be entitled to compensation under the 2013 Act. The detailed working of these rules, and of the rules on compensation and consent that govern a fresh start, is set out in [the article on LARR compensation and consent rules](/2014/02/01/larr-compensation-consent-rules/), at the point where the savings position is applied.

The litigation Section 24 generated was immediate and vast, because it put a price on administrative delay across three decades of half-finished acquisitions. Two questions consumed the courts. The first was the meaning of the lapse clause itself: whether "possession has not been taken or compensation has not been paid" lapsed a proceeding when either condition held, as the landowners contended, or only when both did. The second was the meaning of payment: whether compensation tendered and refused, or deposited in the government treasury rather than in court, counted as paid. An early three-judge decision in Pune Municipal Corporation v. Harakchand Misirimal Solanki, decided in 2014, read the clause in the landowners' favour on both points, and a wave of acquisitions was declared lapsed on its authority. The question was then referred to a Constitution Bench, and in Indore Development Authority v. Manoharlal, decided in 2020, five judges restated the position: the "or" in Section 24(2) is to be read conjunctively, lapse follows only where, through the inaction of the authorities over the five-year period, neither possession was taken nor compensation paid; compensation tendered under Section 31 and refused is not unpaid; the period during which interim orders of a court prevented possession or payment is excluded; and Section 24 does not reopen concluded proceedings or revive claims that were already stale when the new Act commenced. The path to that restatement was not smooth, and the intermediate confusion is itself part of the record a reader of old files must know. High Courts divided on whether deposit of compensation in the government treasury, the routine administrative practice under the old regime, satisfied the word "paid", and on whether the lapse clause operated when only one of possession and payment was missing. A three-judge bench's landowner-friendly construction in the Pune Municipal Corporation case governed for several years and produced declarations of lapse in large numbers; a later bench, in litigation arising from Indore, doubted that construction and the conflict was placed before five judges, whose 2020 conclusions displaced the earlier reading on the points described above. For proceedings that straddle the two regimes of interpretation, the date of the order and the bench that made it can matter as much as the facts, which is a final, fitting indignity the 1894 Act contrived to impose: even its repeal could not be applied without a constitutional adjudication about what its successor meant.

The doctrinal detail belongs to the article that owns the lapse jurisprudence; the point for the 1894 Act's biography is that its afterlife was litigated to the Constitution Bench, which is the measure of how much unfinished business a statute of one hundred and nineteen years leaves behind.

The interaction between the specific transition code in Section 24 and the general savings in the General Clauses Act is the technical heart of the afterlife, and it rewards a plain statement. Section 6 of the General Clauses Act 1897 provides that a repeal does not, unless a different intention appears, affect rights accrued or liabilities incurred under the repealed enactment or legal proceedings in respect of them, which may be continued as if the repealing Act had not passed. Section 24 of the 2013 Act is exactly such a different intention, expressed in part: it displaces the general rule for the categories it covers, sending no-award proceedings into the new compensation code and awarded proceedings forward under the old one, and its deemed-lapse clause goes further than savings altogether by extinguishing a defined class of proceedings the general rule would have preserved. Everything Section 24 does not touch, the general savings carry: the completed acquisition, the accrued right to interest, the pending reference, the liability under an award. The layering explains why arguments in post-2014 acquisition cases so often proceed in two registers at once, the specific transition rule for the proceeding's stage and the general savings for the rights the stage had already produced, and why the Constitution Bench's construction of Section 24 was careful to mark where the specific code ended, holding that the section was not a charter for reopening what the old Act had concluded.

Beyond Section 24, the old Act persists by simpler means. Every acquisition completed under it remains governed by it for the enforcement of its awards, references filed under Section 18 within time continue to be decided by courts applying Sections 23 and 24 of a repealed statute, appeals under Section 54 continue to the High Courts, and execution proceedings for unpaid enhanced compensation continue against state treasuries. A repealed Act that still fills cause lists is not a paradox; it is the normal condition of a statute that governed the transfer of millions of acres and priced them all contestably. The 1894 Act also persists as precedent: its propositions on market value, on comparable sales, on the deduction for development, on potentiality and on belting, were absorbed into the case law that courts carry into the interpretation of the 2013 Act's compensation provisions, so that the repealed statute continues to instruct the living one.

## Verdict: colonial in design, Indian in its long career

The counter-reading this article set out to engage can be put at its strongest. The 1894 Act was colonial in origin and in design, and its most criticised features, the undefined public purpose armoured by a conclusive declaration, the single dispensable hearing, the valuation frozen at notification, the refusal to see anyone but the titleholder, served a state accountable to nobody it displaced. All of that is true, and none of it explains the statute's career, because the empire that wrote it used it sparingly beside the republic that inherited it. Independent India retained the Act for sixty-six years, fought its constitutional wars around it without replacing it, amended it in 1984 by repricing rather than redesigning, and drove it at an intensity of dams, plants and cities that dwarfed the canal and cantonment era. To attribute the harshness of Indian land acquisition to colonialism alone is to let six decades of Indian governments off a hook they earned, one notification at a time. A statute retained unchanged by a successor state for sixty-six years is not a relic that survived by inertia; it survived because it was useful, and the honesty of saying so is the beginning of understanding why its replacement took the form it did.

The second counter-point, pressed by state governments in the debate over the 2013 Act, is equally entitled to engagement rather than dismissal. The 1894 Act was procedurally simple and quick. A determined government could move from notification to possession in months, the compensation code was calculable, and the courts' role was confined to a money question everyone understood. The 2013 Act is neither simple nor quick; social impact assessment, consent, rehabilitation planning and multiplied compensation have made acquisition a process measured in years and priced at levels that acquiring bodies call prohibitive, and the attempt through the ordinance of December 2014 and the amendment Bill of 2015 to strip back the consent and assessment requirements for defined categories of projects failed in Parliament and lapsed, leaving the demanding statute in place. A landowner whose land is taken under a slow and generous statute may still prefer the money to the process, and a state that cannot assemble land cannot build; both propositions are true, and they mark the real trade the republic has chosen. What can be said for the choice is that the 1894 Act's speed was not a neutral efficiency. It was speed purchased by deleting the hearing, freezing the price and ignoring the displaced, and the bill for that speed arrived as the resistance that stopped Singur, Nandigram and POSCO outright. The old Act did not, in the end, deliver projects faster; it delivered conflicts slower to start and impossible to finish, and left their price arrears to be computed by courts for fifty years. On the evidence of its own record, that is the verdict the 1894 Act earns: a formidable instrument of taking, an inadequate instrument of justice, and a statute whose replacement was delayed not by its foreign birth but by its domestic usefulness.

## What the 1894 Act still teaches the reader of Indian statutes

A repealed statute repays study when its design decisions keep recurring, and the 1894 Act's decisions recur constantly in Indian legislative drafting. It teaches, first, that the location of a safeguard matters more than its existence: a hearing placed before the point of no return, deletable by the party it checks, is a different institution from the same hearing placed where it cannot be switched off, and readers of any new statute should ask of every protection the question this article has asked of Section 5A, who can dispense with it, on what showing, and at whose cost. It teaches, secondly, that valuation dates and interest rates are policy in disguise: the choice to price land at the notification date transferred the project's appreciation from the owner to the state as surely as any tax, and the interest amendments of 1984 show a legislature adjusting that transfer without ever naming it. It teaches, thirdly, that a statute's definition of the person it protects is its real distributive clause: the 1894 Act's confinement of compensation to interests of record decided, in advance and invisibly, that the costs of Indian development would fall on the rural poor in rough proportion to the informality of their rights, and the 2013 Act's contrary definition of the affected family is the single most important sentence in the successor statute. And it teaches, finally, that repeal is a beginning rather than an end wherever a statute has generated rights in motion: the savings provisions, the transition rules and the Constitution Bench litigation over Section 24 are the 1894 Act's last lesson in its own subject, which is that the state's power to take includes, in practice, the power to decide for decades afterwards what the taking will turn out to have meant. A reader who has followed the Act from the survey party on the field to the execution petition in the district court holds the template by which to read every Indian statute that moves property from the citizen to the state, and that template, rather than nostalgia or indignation, is what the one hundred and nineteen years are for.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did land acquisition work under the 1894 Act?

Acquisition ran through a fixed sequence in Part II of the Act. The government published a preliminary notification under Section 4 that land in a locality was likely to be needed for a public purpose, which also authorised officers to enter and survey. Persons interested could object in writing within thirty days and be heard by the Collector under Section 5A. The government then issued a declaration under Section 6, which was conclusive evidence of public purpose. After notice and claims under Section 9, the Collector enquired and made an award under Section 11 fixing area, compensation and apportionment, and on the award he could take possession under Section 16, whereupon the land vested in the government free from all encumbrances.

### Q: What was the urgency clause and why was it criticised?

The urgency clause was Section 17 of the Act. Its first limb allowed the Collector, on the government's direction, to take possession fifteen days after the Section 9 notice even though no award had been made, and its fourth limb allowed the government to direct that Section 5A should not apply, deleting the landowner's only hearing and letting the Section 6 declaration follow the notification at once. It was criticised because a power drafted for genuine emergencies, such as a river changing course, was invoked as routine in ordinary development acquisitions, so the single protection the Act gave a landowner before dispossession was switched off by the very government the protection was meant to check.

### Q: What compensation did the 1894 Act provide?

Compensation was computed under Section 23. The core was the market value of the land at the date of the Section 4 notification, to which the Collector or the court added damage for standing crops and trees taken, severance damage to land retained, injurious affection to other property or earnings, reasonable expenses of changing residence or place of business, and loss of profits between declaration and possession. An additional amount of twelve per cent per annum on market value ran from notification to award or possession under Section 23(1A), and a solatium was paid in consideration of the compulsory nature of the acquisition, fifteen per cent of market value originally and thirty per cent after the amendment of 1984. Interest compensated for delayed payment.

### Q: Could a landowner object to acquisition under the 1894 Act?

Yes, but within narrow limits. Section 5A allowed any person interested to object in writing to the Collector within thirty days of the publication of the Section 4 notification, and the Collector had to give the objector an opportunity of being heard before reporting to the appropriate government with recommendations. The government's decision on the objections was final. The objection was the landowner's only chance to resist the taking itself before the award, and it had three structural weaknesses: it was heard by an officer of the acquiring government, it could not raise the amount of compensation, and under Section 17(4) the government could direct that Section 5A should not apply at all, removing the hearing entirely in cases declared urgent.

### Q: Why did the 1894 Act survive so long after independence?

It survived because it was useful to the governments that inherited it. The development programme of independent India, dams, steel plants, mines, industrial estates and planned cities, required land on a vast scale, and the 1894 Act offered a tested machine for taking it: a conclusive declaration, an executive-run process, a compensation code confined to recorded titleholders, and a hearing that could be dispensed with. Parliament amended the Act in 1984 to raise compensation and impose time limits but left the structure intact, and the political cost of the statute only became unsustainable when acquisition shifted towards land for private industry and real estate and movements at Singur, Nandigram and elsewhere turned resistance into a national question that repricing could no longer answer.

### Q: When was the 1894 Act repealed?

The Land Acquisition Act 1894 was repealed by Section 114 of the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act 2013, Act 30 of 2013. The 2013 Act received the President's assent on 26 September 2013 and came into force on 1 January 2014, and the repeal took effect with that commencement. The repeal was not a complete erasure. Section 24 of the 2013 Act carried proceedings in which an award had already been made forward under the 1894 Act as if it had not been repealed, and the general savings of the General Clauses Act 1897 preserved accrued rights and liabilities, which is why the 1894 Act continues to be applied by courts to old proceedings.

### Q: What happens to acquisitions begun under the 1894 Act?

Section 24 of the 2013 Act governs. Where no award under Section 11 of the 1894 Act had been made by 1 January 2014, the proceedings continued but compensation was to be determined under the 2013 Act. Where an award had been made, the proceedings continued under the 1894 Act as if it had not been repealed. Where an award had been made five years or more before the 2013 Act commenced and neither possession had been taken nor compensation paid through the inaction of the authorities, the proceedings were deemed to have lapsed under Section 24(2), as the Constitution Bench explained in Indore Development Authority v. Manoharlal, and the government had to start afresh under the new Act if it still wanted the land.

### Q: Did the 1894 Act require rehabilitation of displaced people?

No. The 1894 Act contained no obligation to rehabilitate or resettle anyone. Its compensation code ran only to a person interested, meaning a person claiming an interest in the compensation for the land, which in practice meant the recorded titleholder and others with a recognised interest. A tenant without a recorded tenancy, a sharecropper, a landless labourer, or a forest dweller whose livelihood depended on the land without title received nothing from the statute, however complete the displacement. Rehabilitation, where it occurred at all, was a matter of project policy announced case by case, not of legal entitlement. The absence was one of the central reasons the Act was replaced, and the 2013 Act's rehabilitation and resettlement provisions were drafted to answer it.

### Q: What was the role of the Collector under the 1894 Act?

The Collector was the executive engine of the entire process. Defined to include the district Collector and any officer appointed to perform the Collector's functions, he caused the public notice of the Section 4 notification to be given, heard objections under Section 5A and reported on them to the government, had the land marked out and measured, issued the Section 9 notices inviting claims, conducted the enquiry under Section 11, made the award fixing area, compensation and apportionment, tendered payment under Section 31, and took possession under Section 16. He also made references to court under Section 18. The structural criticism of the Act begins here: the Collector was an officer of the acquiring government performing functions that determined the rights of the people the government was dispossessing.

### Q: What is a reference to court under Section 18 of the 1894 Act?

A reference was the route by which the Collector's award could be tested in a civil court. Any person interested who had not accepted the award could apply in writing to the Collector requiring the matter to be referred to the court, objecting to the measurement of the land, the amount of compensation, the persons entitled, or the apportionment. The application had to be made within six weeks of the award for a person present or represented when it was made, and otherwise within six weeks of the Collector's notice or six months of the award, whichever expired first. On the reference the court applied Sections 23 and 24 afresh, heard evidence of value, and could not award less than the Collector's amount.

### Q: What did the 1984 amendment change in the 1894 Act?

The Land Acquisition (Amendment) Act 1984, Act 68 of 1984, in force from 24 September 1984, repriced the Act without redesigning it. The solatium on market value rose from fifteen to thirty per cent, a new Section 23(1A) added twelve per cent per annum on market value for the period between notification and award or possession, and interest under Sections 28 and 34 rose from six per cent to nine per cent for the first year after possession and fifteen per cent thereafter. Procedurally, Section 11A required the award within two years of the declaration on pain of lapse, a proviso to Section 6 barred declarations more than three years after notification, Section 28A created redetermination, and Section 44B narrowed acquisition for private companies. The urgency clause and the conclusive declaration were untouched.

### Q: What agreement did a company have to sign before land was acquired for it under the 1894 Act?

Part VII of the Act required two preconditions before land could be acquired for a company: the previous consent of the appropriate government, and an agreement between the company and the government executed under Section 41. Before consent, Section 40 required an official enquiry into whether the land was needed for purposes the Act recognised, including dwelling houses for the company's workmen or a building or work for a company engaged in an industry serving a public purpose. The agreement had to state the terms on which the company would pay the cost of the acquisition, the terms on which the land would be held, and the terms on which the public would be entitled to use any works built on it, and on publication in the Gazette under Section 42 it had the force of the Act itself.

### Q: What interest was payable when compensation was delayed under the 1894 Act?

Two provisions carried interest, at the same rates. Section 34 applied where the compensation awarded was not paid or deposited on or before the taking of possession: the Collector had to pay the awarded amount with interest from the date possession was taken until payment or deposit. Section 28 applied where a court on reference awarded more than the Collector: interest ran on the excess from the date of possession to payment into court. After the amendment of 1984 the rate was nine per cent per annum for the first year from possession and fifteen per cent per annum for any period after that year expired; before 1984 the rate stood at six per cent. The interest rules made long delay by the state expensive, and they are a large part of why old awards grew so much in execution.

### Q: What happened if the Collector missed the two-year deadline for an award under the 1894 Act?

Section 11A, inserted by the amendment of 1984, made the deadline fatal. The Collector was required to make the award under Section 11 within two years from the date of publication of the Section 6 declaration, and if no award was made within that period the entire proceedings for the acquisition of the land lapsed, so the government would have to begin again with a fresh notification if it still wanted the land. In computing the two years, any period during which action under the declaration was stayed by an order of a court was excluded. One qualification mattered in practice: where possession had already been taken under the urgency power in Section 17 and the land had vested in the government, the Supreme Court held in Satendra Prasad Jain v. State of Uttar Pradesh that the lapse of proceedings could not divest the government of land already vested.

### Q: Could the government withdraw from an acquisition under the 1894 Act?

Yes, up to a defined point. Section 48 provided that, except in a case covered by the special provision for land it had become urgently necessary to possess, the government was at liberty to withdraw from the acquisition of any land of which possession had not been taken. Withdrawal was not costless: the government had to pay compensation for the damage suffered by the owner in consequence of the acquisition notices and proceedings, together with the costs the owner had reasonably incurred in prosecuting the proceedings, and matters such as the amounts to be recovered back and the treatment of any money already paid were governed by the same section. Once possession had been taken under Section 16 or Section 17, withdrawal was no longer possible, because the land had vested in the government and the acquisition was complete.

### Q: Who counted as a person interested under the 1894 Act?

Section 3(b) defined a person interested expansively but in a way that still ran through title. The expression included all persons claiming an interest in the compensation to be made on account of the acquisition, and a person was deemed to be interested in land if he was interested in an easement affecting it. In practice the class covered owners, co-sharers, mortgagees, lessees and others who could show a legally recognised interest in the land or in its compensation, and such persons could file claims, object under Section 5A, and seek a reference under Section 18. The definition's limit was as important as its width: a person whose livelihood depended on the land but who held no interest the law recognised, such as an unrecorded tenant or a landless labourer, stood outside it and outside the compensation code altogether.

### Q: Was the amount of compensation under the 1894 Act open to challenge as inadequate?

Within the Act, inadequacy was the very ground of the Section 18 reference, and courts routinely enhanced the Collector's market value on evidence. The constitutional question was different: whether Parliament could prescribe a measure of compensation a court could not test for adequacy at all. In State of West Bengal v. Bela Banerjee in 1954 the Supreme Court read the compensation promised by Article 31(2) of the Constitution as a just equivalent, and Parliament responded with the Fourth Amendment of 1955 to make adequacy non-justiciable. The contest continued through the Bank Nationalisation case in 1970 and the Twenty-fifth Amendment, until the Forty-fourth Amendment of 1978 removed Article 31 and left property to Article 300A. From then on the fight over amounts ran inside the Act's own code of market value, solatium and interest, not over the Constitution's floor.

### Q: How long could an acquisition take from notification to possession under the 1894 Act?

The Act set outer clocks but left wide spaces between them. Objections under Section 5A had to be filed within thirty days of the Section 4 notification. After the amendment of 1984, the Section 6 declaration could not be made more than three years after the notification, and the award had to follow within two years of the declaration under Section 11A or the proceedings lapsed. In an ordinary course measured by those limits, the journey from notification to award and possession could lawfully occupy the better part of five years, and in practice contested acquisitions took far longer, with court stays excluded from the two-year computation stretching the timeline further. An urgent acquisition inverted the experience: with Section 5A dispensed with and possession available fifteen days after the Section 9 notice, possession could precede the award entirely.

### Q: Why do 1894 Act cases remain in litigation for decades?

Because the Act closed the question of the taking early and left the question of the price open without a terminal clock. The declaration was conclusive and possession was irreversible, so litigation concentrated on market value, and every element of value was contestable on evidence: comparable sales, potentiality, deductions for development, trees and structures, and apportionment among co-sharers. A reference in the district court could be appealed to the High Court under Section 54 and carried further, while interest at nine and fifteen per cent on any excess kept the stakes growing throughout. The repeal added a second generation of disputes under Section 24 of the 2013 Act about which proceedings had lapsed, which reached a Constitution Bench in 2020. Acquisitions notified in the 1970s and 1980s can therefore still be in court on quantum long after the project they served has been built, altered or abandoned.

### Q: What is Section 28A redetermination under the 1894 Act?

Section 28A, inserted by the amendment of 1984, spread the benefit of a successful court reference across a notified locality. Where a court, on one landowner's reference, allowed compensation in excess of the Collector's award for land covered by a Section 4 notification, other persons interested in land under the same notification who had not themselves sought a reference could apply in writing to the Collector within three months of the court's award and require their own compensation to be redetermined on the basis of the amount the court had awarded. The Collector then held an enquiry, gave the persons interested a reasonable opportunity of being heard, and made a fresh award; a person who did not accept that award could in turn require a reference to the court under the ordinary provisions.
