The principal federal consumer protection agency in the United States possesses authority broad enough to reach nearly every deceptive or unfair practice in commerce, and yet FTC rulemaking produced only a small body of binding consumer protection rules across the decades after Congress confirmed that authority in 1975. The operative question of the article is therefore a question about institutional design rather than about substantive ambition. Why did an agency armed with Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, a prohibition written as an open standard against unfair or deceptive acts or practices, govern markets principally through complaints, consent orders, and policy statements instead of through rules announced in advance for an entire industry? The answer carries a namable claim that recurs through every stage of the history. The Federal Trade Commission became, in practical terms, a rulemaking agency that cannot make rules, not because Congress withheld the power, and not because commissioners lacked subjects worthy of regulation, but because the procedure attached to the power priced each rule beyond the capacity of the institution to pay except in rare and protracted proceedings.

![Federal Trade Commission consumer protection rulemaking and enforcement - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-64.webp)

The route through that history begins with the founding choice of 1914, when Congress enacted a standard rather than a catalogue of prohibited practices and assigned specification of the standard to case by case adjudication, a choice the Wheeler-Lea Act of 1938 extended by adding unfair or deceptive acts or practices beside unfair methods of competition. Definition arrived later and from an unexpected direction. The deception standard of 1983 and the unfairness standard first stated in 1980 gave Section 5 administrable tests, yet both standards lived in policy statements rather than in legislative rules, and Congress codified the unfairness test alone, at 15 U.S.C. 45(n), in 1994. Specification of substance therefore proceeded faster than specification through rules, because a policy statement required no hearing, no cross examination, and no record built for substantial evidence review.

Procedure entered the account in force with the Magnuson-Moss legislation that took effect in January 1975, which added Section 18 and surrounded trade regulation rules with an advance notice, a prevalence finding, an informal hearing, cross examination on disputed issues of material fact, and review on a closed record. Political reaction then hardened procedure into institutional caution. The children's advertising proceeding of the late nineteen seventies, the closure of the agency on May 1, 1980, and the Federal Trade Commission Improvements Act of 1980, Public Law 96-252, taught commissioners that an ambitious rule could endanger the agency itself. Enforcement case by case filled the space rules left vacant, and for decades Section 13(b) supplied the monetary force that orders alone lacked, until the Supreme Court ended that redress practice on April 22, 2021, in AMG Capital Management, LLC v. FTC. The sections that follow trace each stage in sequence, from open standard to enforcement substitute to the narrower money routes that survived the 2021 decision, and each stage returns to the same institutional question of which instrument, at which procedural price, would carry the protection that Section 5 promised in general terms to consumers in every market.

## An Open Standard Instead of a Rulebook

Federal consumer protection law in the United States rests on a sentence that refuses to say what it forbids in any concrete instance. Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act of 1914 declared a category of conduct unlawful, empowered a new commission to prevent that conduct, and then left the content of the category to be worked out in proceedings against particular firms on particular facts. No schedule of banned advertisements followed the declaration. No list of prohibited sales tactics or oppressive terms accompanied the grant of authority. Congress instead enacted a standard, a form of words whose meaning depended on application, and assigned the task of application to an expert agency subject to review in the federal courts. A prohibition written at the level of principle could reach practices that no legislator in 1914 or in 1938 had imagined, because the words asked whether a method was unfair or whether an act was deceptive rather than whether a practice appeared on a closed register of offenses. The same breadth also created a permanent problem of legitimacy and predictability, because firms learned the boundary of lawful conduct largely from orders entered against other firms, from complaints that settled without adjudication, and from guidance that carried no force of law in the manner of a duly promulgated rule.

The central paradox of Section 5 therefore appeared at the moment of enactment and deepened with each amendment. Congress handed the Federal Trade Commission the broadest consumer protection mandate in federal law, measured by the range of commercial conduct the words could cover, and Congress later surrounded the writing of binding rules under that mandate with procedures so elaborate that rulemaking became the exceptional path rather than the ordinary one. Enforcement actions became the substance of federal consumer protection. Complaints, administrative hearings, cease and desist orders, consent agreements, and policy statements did the work that a code of trade regulation rules might have been expected to do in another statutory scheme. Section 5 thus joined two features that pulled in opposite directions. Open texture made the prohibition durable across a century of changing markets, and procedural burden made the most direct instrument for clarifying that texture, the trade regulation rule, difficult to use.

### What did the original prohibition cover?

Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act of 1914 declared unfair methods of competition in commerce unlawful and empowered the Federal Trade Commission to prevent such methods through administrative proceedings ending in cease and desist orders directed at individual respondents after case specific factual findings were made.

That single sentence carried the whole of the original design. The Act of September 26, 1914 created the Federal Trade Commission as a body of five commissioners and transferred to the new agency functions that had belonged in part to the Bureau of Corporations in the Department of Commerce. The statute gave the Commission powers of investigation, powers to require reports, and authority to gather information about business conduct across the economy. Substantive authority, however, ran through the declaration that unfair methods of competition were unlawful and through the procedure that followed from the declaration. Where the Commission had reason to believe that a person, partnership, or corporation had used an unfair method of competition, and where proceedings in the matter appeared to serve the public interest, the Commission could issue a complaint, hold a hearing, make findings of fact, and order the respondent to cease and desist. Findings of fact, if supported by testimony, bound reviewing courts. The remedy was prospective and specific. An order told a named respondent to stop a described practice. The statute created no criminal offense for a violation of the standard itself, and the original scheme gave the Commission no general power to extract money from a respondent for past conduct merely because the conduct violated Section 5.

The wording mattered in a second respect. The 1914 text spoke of methods of competition, and the noun competition anchored the prohibition to rivalry among sellers. A practice fell within the sentence because of a relationship to competition, not because of a freestanding relationship to consumer welfare. False advertising illustrated the limit with unusual clarity. A misleading claim could injure buyers in an obvious way, and the Commission in its early decades treated deceptive promotion as a core concern, yet the statutory sentence required a competitive frame. Deception mattered, under the original text, because a dishonest seller diverted trade from honest rivals and because markets degraded when firms competed through falsehood rather than through price and quality. Consumer injury entered the analysis as a consequence that accompanied competitive injury, as evidence of unfairness in rivalry, and as part of the public interest that justified proceedings. Consumer injury alone, detached from injury to competitors or to the competitive process, occupied uncertain ground under the words Congress had chosen in 1914.

The Supreme Court confirmed the narrow reading in Federal Trade Commission v. Raladam Company, decided in 1931. The Commission had proceeded against a seller of a preparation marketed as an obesity treatment, on findings that the preparation could not be used generally without danger to health except under medical direction and that representations about the preparation misled purchasers. The Court held the matter beyond the jurisdiction of the Commission because the record lacked the necessary showing about competition. A method, in the language of the decision, had not only to be unfair and in the public interest to restrain, but also to be a method of competition in commerce, with substantial competition, present or potential, shown to have been injured or clearly threatened with injury. Deception of the public, without that competitive showing, did not suffice. The result exposed the gap between the social problem of deceptive selling and the statutory instrument Congress had supplied. The most sympathetic imaginable consumer case, a dangerous product sold through misleading claims, failed under the 1914 sentence for want of proof about rivals.


### How did the prohibition acquire a consumer protection reach?

Congress closed the Raladam gap through the Wheeler-Lea Act, signed on March 21, 1938, which amended Section 5 to declare unlawful unfair or deceptive acts or practices in commerce alongside unfair methods of competition in commerce. The added phrase changed the jurisdictional question in a consumer case. After the amendment, the Commission no longer had to offer evidence establishing injury to an actual or potential competitor in order to proceed against deception, a point Commissioner R. E. Freer explained in an address delivered on May 17, 1938, less than two months after enactment. The amendment also provided civil penalties for violations of final cease and desist orders, which strengthened the practical force of orders that the Commission issued under the expanded standard. Later amendments restated the commerce language in the form codified by 2019, which prohibited unfair methods of competition in or affecting commerce and unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce. The operative distinction survived every restatement. One clause addressed methods of competition, and a second clause addressed acts or practices, and the second clause made the consumer a direct object of protection rather than an incidental beneficiary of honest rivalry.

The separation between the two clauses carried doctrinal consequences that administrators and courts elaborated over the following decades. An act could be deceptive without any inquiry into market structure, market shares, or the fate of competitors, because deception turned on the representation, omission, or practice and on the likelihood that the representation misled a consumer acting reasonably under the circumstances. An act could also be unfair without being deceptive at all, a possibility contained in the disjunctive structure of the phrase unfair or deceptive. A practice might involve no false statement and no misleading omission, and yet might impose injury that the statutory adjective unfair reached. The Commission explored that possibility most famously in the statement of basis and purpose that accompanied the cigarette advertising rule effort of 1964, which described three considerations for unfairness. The considerations asked whether the practice offended public policy as established by statutes, the common law, or otherwise, whether the practice was immoral, unethical, oppressive, or unscrupulous, and whether the practice caused substantial injury to consumers or to competitors or to other business persons. Consumer injury stood in that formulation as an independent ground of concern, placed beside public policy and beside the character of the conduct itself.

The Supreme Court recognized the breadth of that reasoning in Federal Trade Commission v. Sperry and Hutchinson Company, decided in 1972. The case concerned trading stamps and efforts by the issuing company to suppress a free and open secondary trade in stamps among consumers and independent exchanges. The Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit had reversed a Commission order on the ground that Section 5 empowered the Commission to restrain only practices that violated the antitrust laws, or that were deceptive, or that were repugnant to public morals. The Supreme Court rejected that confinement. Legislative and judicial authorities, the Court wrote, convinced the justices that the Commission did not arrogate excessive power to itself if, in measuring a practice against the standard of fairness that Congress had mandated, the Commission, like a court of equity, considered public values beyond those enshrined in the letter or encompassed in the spirit of the antitrust laws. The opinion quoted the 1964 criteria, including substantial injury to consumers, in a footnote that later administrators and courts treated as an endorsement of the criteria as a framework for analysis. Equity supplied the governing metaphor. A court of equity had traditionally responded to the facts of a controversy rather than to a closed list of wrongs, and the Court placed the Commission in that tradition for purposes of the fairness inquiry.

Sperry and Hutchinson settled one question and left another open for the remainder of the century. The settled question concerned source material. Unfairness under Section 5 drew on public policy found in statutes and in the common law, on judgments about oppressive and unscrupulous conduct, and on the reality and scale of consumer injury, and no antitrust violation was necessary to sustain Commission action. The open question concerned weight and priority among the criteria. A practice might offend a policy expressed in another statute without causing measurable monetary injury to any identifiable buyer, or might cause widespread small losses without conduct that any observer would describe as unscrupulous. The 1964 formulation listed considerations without ranking them, and the 1972 opinion quoted the formulation without converting the list into a test with necessary elements. A standard governed, administrators applied the standard to facts, and the meaning of the standard accumulated through decisions rather than through a definitional rule that specified thresholds in advance.


### Why did Congress legislate a standard rather than a catalogue?

The open texture of Section 5 reflected a deliberate legislative judgment about the limits of enumeration. During the debates of 1914, members of Congress considered proposals to define unfair competition through lists of named devices and practices, and those proposals failed. The difficulty was practical as well as philosophical. Trade practices changed with technology, with distribution methods, and with the organization of firms, and a catalogue drafted against the abuses of one decade risked obsolescence in the next decade. A catalogue also invited evasion by design. A seller constrained by a list of prohibited representations retained every representation omitted from the list, and counsel could argue that omission signaled permission. The legislative record of the Federal Trade Commission Act contains the recognition that an attempt to draw the line in advance, practice by practice, posed an endless task, because ingenuity in the marketplace generated methods faster than a legislature could name methods. Congress therefore chose the opposite strategy. A general prohibition, administered by a specialized body that developed expertise through investigation and adjudication, promised coverage that a definition could not supply.

The expectation embedded in the statute deserves emphasis, because later criticism of the Commission often treated vagueness as an accident or as a failure of draftsmanship. Vagueness was the mechanism. Congress anticipated that the Commission would give the standard content case by case, building a body of administrative decisions that identified unfair methods in concrete settings, and that courts reviewing those decisions would participate in the same process of specification. The Supreme Court described the method in Raladam itself, in the course of limiting the Commission, by observing that the phrase unfair methods of competition was not susceptible of precise definition and would be developed through a gradual process of inclusion and exclusion in judicial and administrative decision. Together, the early decisions framed a division of labor. The Commission investigated, prosecuted, and reasoned from facts, and the courts policed the outer boundary of the standard while allowing the interior of the standard to fill with accumulated examples.

Comparison with neighboring statutes sharpens the distinctiveness of the choice. The Clayton Act, enacted in the same year of 1914, proceeded in substantial part by enumeration. Provisions of the Clayton Act named price discrimination between purchasers, exclusive dealing arrangements and tying conditions, acquisitions of stock in competing corporations, and interlocking directorates, and attached conditions and thresholds to each named practice. Enumeration offered notice. A firm could consult the text and learn, with tolerable confidence, whether a contemplated transaction fell within a described category. Enumeration also offered a democratic warrant of a particular kind, because the legislature had identified the wrong and the agency merely detected instances of the wrong. Section 5 offered neither advantage in the same measure. Notice under Section 5 came from precedent, from consent orders, and from advisory materials. Democratic warrant came at one remove, because Congress had chosen the standard and had chosen the institution, without choosing the applications that gave the standard operative meaning in any given market.

None of that design implied unlimited discretion, and the history of Section 5 contains repeated episodes in which courts or Congress narrowed a Commission reading that had pressed the standard toward a general charter to police commercial morality. The equity metaphor in Sperry and Hutchinson authorized attention to public values beyond the antitrust laws, and the same period produced political resistance to a Commission described by opponents as a national nanny, a phrase that captured anxiety about an agency empowered to condemn conduct on grounds of immorality or oppression without a tighter connection to injury. Later policy statements responded to that anxiety by tightening the unfairness inquiry around substantial consumer injury, around the capacity of consumers to avoid injury reasonably, and around countervailing benefits to consumers or to competition. Congress wrote a standard broad enough to require narrowing, because the alternative of enumeration had been considered and rejected as inadequate to the subject matter.

The breadth of the standard also explains a feature of Section 5 that distinguishes the statute from most of the federal consumer protection law enacted afterward. Congress in later decades frequently legislated about specific markets and specific abuses, in credit reporting, in debt collection, in telemarketing, and in other defined settings, and those statutes supplied detailed duties and detailed remedies within bounded fields. Section 5 remained underneath and around those statutes as a residual prohibition of general application, capable of addressing a deceptive practice in a market that no specific statute covered. Generality also produced resilience. When commerce migrated across technologies, from print advertising to radio and television broadcasting and then to electronic networks, the words unfair and deceptive required no amendment to follow the migration, because the words described qualities of conduct rather than instruments of communication.

Against that background, the rulemaking history of the statute presents a genuine puzzle rather than a footnote. An agency armed with a standard of exceptional breadth, and authorized after 1975 through Section 18 of the Act, added by the Magnuson-Moss Warranty and Federal Trade Commission Improvement Act, to issue trade regulation rules that defined unfair or deceptive acts or practices with specificity, might have been expected to govern primarily through rules. A rule announced obligations before enforcement began, applied across an industry rather than to named respondents, and supported civil penalties against violators with actual knowledge that the conduct was prohibited by rule. The statute after 1975 nevertheless conditioned that instrument on procedures beyond the notice and comment process that governed ordinary federal rulemaking under the Administrative Procedure Act. Advance notice of proposed rulemaking, a showing that the practices addressed were prevalent, an informal hearing with opportunities for oral presentation and, on disputed issues of material fact, cross examination and rebuttal, a statement of basis and purpose addressing the record, and judicial review under a substantial evidence standard formed part of the required path. Each element added months or years and added points at which a proceeding could stall or fail. The elaboration had a rationale in legislative distrust of the breadth that Section 5 conferred, since a rule converted an open standard into binding industry wide law, and Congress demanded a record and a process proportionate to that conversion. The consequence, intended or not, was that the conversion seldom occurred.

Federal consumer protection therefore took a shape that the bare text of Section 5 does not predict. The substance of the law accumulated in administrative complaints and in the orders that resolved complaints, in consent agreements negotiated under the shadow of litigation, in closing letters and staff guidance, and in policy statements that announced enforcement intentions without the legal status of rules. Doctrine about deception, about substantiation of advertising claims, and about endorsements grew through that accumulation, and practitioners advised clients by reading enforcement history as a common law of the marketplace. The arrangement preserved flexibility and preserved the case by case method that Congress had contemplated in 1914. The arrangement also transferred costs to regulated firms, which faced a standard whose applications were knowable in retrospect more readily than in prospect, and to consumers, whose protection depended on the enforcement priorities and resources of the Commission in any given period rather than on rules of general applicability that private parties and other enforcers could invoke. A rulemaking agency that cannot make rules, in the practical sense that the procedural price of a rule exceeds the institutional capacity to pay the price except in rare instances, governs by other means. Section 5 made those other means the ordinary law of American consumer protection, and the sections that follow trace the procedures, the contests, and the redress that resulted from that founding choice of a standard over a rulebook.

## The Deception and Unfairness Standards

Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act declares unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce unlawful, and that spare sentence leaves the hardest work to definition. A rule written by the Commission under its trade regulation authority can name a practice, describe the conduct covered, and attach consequences to a violation, but the standards that decide whether an unnamed practice falls within Section 5 at all come from a different kind of document. The deception standard and the unfairness standard both live in policy statements, letters the Commission sent to members of Congress and then appended to adjudicated orders, where the agency explained in general terms how it intended to apply two words that Congress had left undefined. Those two statements supply the governing tests for most consumer protection enforcement under Section 5, and a rulemaking that invokes the Act draws its substantive boundaries from them.

### What counts as deceptive under the FTC standard?

A practice counts as deceptive when a representation, omission, or practice is likely to mislead a consumer acting reasonably under the circumstances and the misleading aspect is material, meaning likely to affect a purchase or other consumer decision about a product or service.

The Commission set out that test in its Policy Statement on Deception, dated October 14, 1983. The statement took the form of a letter to Representative John D. Dingell, chairman of the House Committee on Energy and Commerce, and the Commission appended the statement to its decision in Cliffdale Associates, reported at 103 F.T.C. 110, with the statement beginning at page 174. The letter answered congressional questions about the legal standard the agency applied in deception cases, and it distilled decades of adjudicated cases into three elements. First, a representation, omission, or practice must be likely to mislead the consumer. Second, the likelihood of deception is judged from the perspective of a consumer acting reasonably under the circumstances. Third, the representation, omission, or practice must be material. A seller's good faith, honest intent, or lack of knowledge does not answer the question, because the test asks what the practice does to consumers rather than what the seller meant to do.

The first element reaches three distinct forms of conduct, and the breadth matters for rulemaking as much as for adjudication. A representation is an express or implied statement, and it can travel through words, pictures, product names, demonstrations, testimonials, or the arrangement of a sales presentation. An omission is a failure to disclose information, and an omission becomes actionable under the statement when the seller has conveyed an impression that the omitted information would correct or qualify. Silence standing entirely alone, with no representation to give it meaning, presents a harder case under the deception test than silence that leaves a half truth intact, which is one reason conduct involving pure nondisclosure often gets analyzed under the unfairness standard instead. A practice is conduct that misleads through its operation rather than through a statement, such as a sales procedure designed so that consumers sign contracts whose terms differ from the terms described orally. Likelihood is the operative measure in each form, and the Commission does not have to prove that any particular consumer was in fact deceived.

The reasonable consumer benchmark supplies the perspective from which likelihood is judged, and the statement treated that benchmark as an objective standard anchored in the audience the practice reaches. A consumer acting reasonably does not read every advertisement as a lawyer reads a contract, does not assume that puffery states a measurable fact, and does not ignore clear disclosures that accompany a claim, but that same consumer also takes an advertisement as a whole and relies on the ordinary meaning of words and images without conducting independent research to test them. When a practice targets a particular group, such as children, older adults, or people suffering from a serious disease, the Commission evaluates the practice from the perspective of a reasonable member of that group, because an appeal aimed at a defined audience predictably affects members of that audience according to their characteristics.

The net impression doctrine follows from that audience centered analysis and prevents a seller from defeating a claim through careful fragmentation of a message. The Commission examines the entire communication, including headlines, pictures, sounds, demonstrations, and qualifying text, and asks what overall impression the communication creates for the reasonable consumer. A statement that is true word for word can deceive when its placement, emphasis, or context creates a false overall impression, and a fine print disclosure that contradicts the dominant message does not cure the deception, because the reasonable consumer absorbs the dominant message rather than the contradiction buried beneath it. Disclosures can qualify a claim effectively when the disclosures are clear and conspicuous enough to change the net impression, and the analysis asks whether the disclosure actually reaches the consumer as part of the message rather than whether the disclosure exists somewhere in the document.

The distinction between express claims and implied claims organizes the proof. An express claim states its message directly, in so many words, so that identification of the claim requires no inference. An implied claim conveys its message through suggestion, imagery, context, or omission, so that reasonable consumers understand the message even though the seller never stated it outright. A picture of a car crossing a desert without water implies a claim about durability, and a label that calls a drink a fruit juice while picturing whole fruit implies a claim about contents, whether or not any sentence asserts either proposition. For express claims and for implied claims that the seller intended consumers to draw, the Commission ordinarily relies on its own reading of the communication, using the language, images, and context as evidence of the message conveyed. For other implied claims, the statement contemplated extrinsic evidence, such as consumer testimony, surveys, expert analysis, or copy testing, to establish what message reasonable consumers actually take away.

Materiality, the third element, connects the misleading message to a consumer decision, and it functions as a screen that keeps trivial inaccuracies outside Section 5. A material representation, omission, or practice is one that is likely to affect a consumer's choice of, or conduct regarding, a product or service, such as the decision to buy, to pay a particular price, to choose one brand over another, or to use a product in a particular way. A false statement about a characteristic that no reasonable consumer would weigh in a decision fails the test even though the statement is false and even though someone might believe it. The statement then establishes a series of presumptions that allocate the burden of proof in recurring situations. Express claims are presumed material, because a seller who takes the trouble to state a claim expressly presumably does so in the belief that consumers will consider the claim in deciding whether to buy. Claims involving health, safety, or other areas that the statement described as ones consumers generally consider important receive the same presumption, as do claims about a product's central characteristics, including purpose, efficacy, quality, durability, and cost. A seller may rebut a presumption with evidence that the claim did not in fact matter to consumers, but the presumption spares the Commission the cost of proving decision relevance claim by claim in the categories where relevance is predictable. Omissions receive parallel treatment when the seller knew or should have known that consumers would need the omitted information to evaluate a representation that the seller had made.


The unfairness standard covers the ground that the deception standard leaves open, namely conduct that harms consumers without misleading anyone. The Commission set out that standard in its Policy Statement on Unfairness, dated December 17, 1980. That statement also took the form of a congressional letter, addressed to Senator Wendell H. Ford and Senator John C. Danforth of the Consumer Subcommittee of the Senate Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation, who had written to the Commission on June 13, 1980 to request its views before oversight hearings on the unfairness concept. The Commission appended the statement to its decision in International Harvester Company, reported at 104 F.T.C. 949, with the statement beginning at page 1070. The 1980 statement narrowed a jurisdiction that earlier cases had described in sweeping moral terms. In Federal Trade Commission v. Sperry and Hutchinson Company, decided in 1972, the Supreme Court had sustained Commission authority to weigh considerations such as whether a practice offended public policy, whether the practice was immoral, unethical, oppressive, or unscrupulous, and whether the practice caused substantial injury. The 1980 statement responded to criticism that those criteria gave the agency an unbounded mandate to condemn conduct that commissioners disliked, and it replaced the open ended criteria with a test grounded principally in consumer injury. The statement declared that the Commission would act against a practice on unfairness grounds only when the practice causes or is likely to cause substantial injury to consumers, when the injury is not reasonably avoidable by consumers themselves, and when the injury is not outweighed by countervailing benefits to consumers or to competition.

Substantial injury, the first element, is most often monetary, and it includes money extracted through unauthorized billing, costs imposed through practices a consumer never agreed to, and losses that flow from a product or service that fails to perform as a contract promised even though no statement misdescribed it. The statement set two limiting qualifications that keep the element disciplined. The injury must be substantial in aggregate as well as real in kind, so a small harm inflicted on a very large number of consumers can qualify while a trivial inconvenience inflicted on a handful cannot, and purely speculative or conjectural harm does not qualify at any scale. Emotional distress and other subjective harms, standing alone, ordinarily do not make a practice unfair under the statement, although subjective value can count when a practice defeats a preference that consumers paid to satisfy, and the statement directed the agency toward harms that evidence can measure rather than toward offense that observers merely feel.

Reasonable avoidability, the second element, locates the boundary between market discipline and legal intervention. When consumers can protect themselves through choices available in the marketplace, by reading terms that sellers disclose plainly, comparing competing offers, or walking away from a transaction whose costs are visible, the resulting injury is reasonably avoidable and the unfairness standard does not apply. The market, rather than the Commission, supplies the remedy in that setting, because intervention would protect consumers from decisions that consumers were positioned to make well. Injury becomes unavoidable in the relevant sense when a practice interferes with the ability to choose, as when a seller structures a transaction so that consumers learn material costs only after commitment, when a practice exploits a lock in created by an earlier purchase, when information needed for avoidance is unavailable from any source other than the seller, or when avoidance would demand expenditures or expertise beyond what a reasonable consumer would bring to the transaction. The element asks about practical avoidability rather than theoretical possibility. The fact that a determined consumer, armed with specialist knowledge and unlimited time, might have escaped an injury does not make that injury reasonably avoidable for the consumers whom the practice actually reached.

The balancing element, the third component, weighs the injury against countervailing benefits to consumers or to competition that the same practice produces. Many practices impose costs on some consumers while delivering lower prices, wider availability, greater convenience, or product features that other consumers value, and the statement required the Commission to ask whether the practice, judged in net terms, harms consumers at all. A practice that saves consumers more than it costs them, even though identifiable individuals lose, ordinarily fails the test, and the inquiry extends to competitive benefits, because a practice that injures consumers in one transaction while intensifying competition that lowers prices across a market may produce a net gain. The 1980 statement treated established public policy as a supporting consideration rather than as an independent ground, explaining that policy drawn from statutes, common law, or the Constitution could help confirm an injury analysis, especially where a legislative judgment identified a harm that markets had failed to correct, but that public policy alone, disconnected from substantial and unavoidable consumer injury, would rarely if ever justify an unfairness finding. That subordination answered the central objection to the Sperry and Hutchinson criteria, under which a practice that merely offended an announced policy might have drawn liability without proof that anyone had been hurt.

Congress converted the policy statement test into statutory law fourteen years later. Section 9 of the Federal Trade Commission Act Amendments of 1994, enacted as Public Law 103-312, added subsection (n) to Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. 45(n) under the heading covering the standard for unfairness determinations and public policy consideration. The subsection is structured as a limitation on authority rather than as a grant, and its architecture preserves the 1980 elements in a particular order. The first sentence provides that the Commission has no authority under Section 5, or under the rulemaking provision at 15 U.S.C. 57a, to declare an act or practice unlawful on unfairness grounds unless the act or practice causes or is likely to cause substantial injury to consumers, the injury is not reasonably avoidable by consumers themselves, and the injury is not outweighed by countervailing benefits to consumers or to competition. The second sentence permits the Commission, in determining whether an act or practice is unfair, to consider established public policies as evidence to be considered along with all other evidence. The final sentence then bars public policy considerations from serving as a primary basis for an unfairness determination. The statutory structure therefore does three things at once. The first sentence makes each element a condition that the Commission must satisfy, so failure on avoidability or on balancing defeats an unfairness theory regardless of how serious the injury appears. The second sentence preserves public policy as an evidentiary input, so a statute or judicial doctrine that condemns a practice can support the injury analysis. The final sentence demotes public policy from a ground of liability to a source of corroboration, which forecloses the theory that a practice is unfair merely because the practice offends a policy that the Commission favors. Because subsection (n) speaks directly to rulemaking authority under Section 57a as well as to adjudication under Section 5, a trade regulation rule premised on unfairness must rest on the same three elements, and the codification binds the Commission in a way that the 1980 letter never did, since Congress, rather than the agency, fixed the test and only Congress can relax it.

The location of both standards in policy statements, rather than in legislative rules, reflects the circumstances that produced them and shapes the legal weight that each document carries. Neither statement went through notice and comment rulemaking, neither was promulgated under Section 18 of the Act, and neither binds a court, a respondent, or the Commission itself in the formal sense that a rule binds. A policy statement announces how an agency expects to exercise discretion, and the Commission remained free in principle to depart from an announced standard in a later case, although departure without explanation would expose an order to reversal as arbitrary and would undermine the predictability that the statements were written to create. The statements bind no one formally, yet both documents govern practice in fact. Commission staff screen complaints and draft proposed complaints and proposed rules against the elements, respondents and their counsel structure defenses around the elements, and state legislatures and courts applying state consumer protection statutes modelled on Section 5 have drawn on the federal tests as interpretive guidance. Courts reviewing Commission orders have treated the statements as considered explanations of the statute by the agency charged with administering the statute, entitled to respect proportionate to their reasoning, while reserving final authority over statutory meaning to the judiciary. Congress eventually gave that account a divided verdict. The unfairness test received codification in 1994, which converted a self imposed limit into a statutory command, while the deception test received no comparable codification, a silence that left the 1983 statement in its original posture as authoritative agency guidance that courts consult without treating as legislation.

The two standards divide the field of consumer harm along the line of information. The deception standard reaches conduct that distorts the information on which consumers decide, whether the distortion arrives as a false statement, a misleading implication, a misleading omission, or a sales practice engineered to produce a false impression. The unfairness standard reaches conduct that injures consumers through its operation rather than through its message, including practices that impose charges consumers never authorized, terms that lock consumers into obligations they had no practical means to escape, and failures to disclose safety risks that consumers had no other way to learn about. International Harvester illustrates the second category in its original setting. The case concerned tractors whose fuel systems could eject a spray of fuel, known as fuel geysering, when an operator removed the fuel cap under particular conditions, creating a risk of serious burns. The Commission concluded that the failure to disclose the risk did not satisfy the deception standard on the facts presented, yet the same failure could be tested for unfairness by asking whether the resulting injury was substantial given the severity of burns, whether operators could reasonably avoid a risk that the manufacturer alone understood, and whether the modest cost of a warning was outweighed by any benefit from silence, an analysis under which the practice failed. A false health claim illustrates the first category with equal clarity. An advertisement that states without substantiation that a supplement cures a disease deceives under the 1983 test because the express claim is likely to mislead reasonable consumers and health claims carry a presumption of materiality, and no inquiry into avoidability or balancing is needed, because the deception standard contains no defense that a false claim produced benefits outweighing the deception.

## Hybrid Rulemaking Under Section 18

The procedural law governing a Federal Trade Commission trade regulation rule has no close counterpart elsewhere in the federal regulatory system, and the reason lies in the sequence that produced it. Congress first gave the Commission a broad prohibition to enforce and a slender, ambiguous sentence about rules, then watched a court read that sentence generously, and then confirmed the power in explicit terms while surrounding its exercise with conditions designed to slow the Commission down, expose its factual reasoning to adversarial testing, and make a reviewing court examine the whole evidentiary record rather than the plausibility of the agency explanation alone. The result, codified at Section 18 of the Federal Trade Commission Act and at Section 57a of Title 15 of the United States Code, is commonly described as hybrid rulemaking. The proceeding begins like ordinary informal rulemaking under the Administrative Procedure Act, with published notices and written comments, but it acquires features borrowed from adjudication, including a presiding officer, a hearing, cross examination on factual disputes, a recommended decision, and a closed record, before it ends with judicial review of a kind ordinarily reserved for formal proceedings.

### A Power Contested Before Codification

Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, codified at Section 45 of Title 15, declared unfair methods of competition and unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce to be unlawful, and for decades the Commission enforced that declaration principally through adjudication, proceeding case by case and building the meaning of the prohibition out of cease and desist orders. Whether the Commission could move faster by announcing binding substantive rules in advance divided lawyers inside and outside the agency. The statutory hook was Section 6(g) of the Act, codified at Section 46(g) of Title 15, which empowered the Commission to make rules and regulations for the purpose of carrying out the provisions of the Act. Read broadly, that language authorized legislative rules defining the practices Section 5 forbade, with consequences for every business within a rule coverage rather than for a single respondent after a hearing. The distinction mattered because a rule announces the standard in advance, applies across an industry from its effective date, and allows the Commission to seek penalties for violations without relitigating the underlying judgment that the practice is unfair or deceptive in every enforcement action.

The test arrived through a rule requiring the posting of octane ratings on gasoline pumps, adopted on the theory that failure to post the rating was an unfair or deceptive practice. Trade associations and petroleum refiners challenged the rule on the ground that the Commission lacked substantive rulemaking authority altogether, and the contest reached the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit. In National Petroleum Refiners Association v. Federal Trade Commission, reported at volume 482 of the Federal Reporter, Second Series, at page 672 and decided in 1973, the court sustained the Commission, treating Section 6(g) as a genuine grant capable of supporting substantive rules under Section 5. Yet affirmation by a court of appeals on a contested reading of a 1914 sentence left the authority exposed in ways that a statute would not, since a later court could narrow the reasoning, the Supreme Court had not spoken, and every new rule invited a fresh threshold challenge to the power itself. The legislation of 1975 chose ratification with conditions, and the conditions became the enduring part of the settlement.

The statute was [the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act of 1975](/2018/11/15/magnuson-moss-warranty-act-1975/), formally the Magnuson-Moss Warranty and Federal Trade Commission Improvement Act, enacted as Public Law 93-637 and signed by President Gerald Ford on January 4, 1975. The Act added Section 18 to the Federal Trade Commission Act, giving the Commission express authority in Section 18(a)(1)(B) to prescribe rules that define with specificity acts or practices that are unfair or deceptive within the meaning of Section 5(a)(1), including requirements prescribed for the purpose of preventing such acts or practices. Section 18(a)(2) confined the field by providing that the Commission has no authority elsewhere in the subchapter, apart from Section 18 itself, to prescribe a rule with respect to unfair or deceptive acts or practices, while expressly preserving authority over rules concerning unfair methods of competition. For consumer protection, ambiguity about the existence of the power ended. What replaced ambiguity was procedure, set out in subsections (b) through (e) of Section 57a.

### What Must Happen Before a Rule Is Proposed?

An FTC trade regulation rule takes a long time because Section 18 requires an advance notice, a prevalence finding, a detailed proposal, written comments, an informal hearing, cross examination on disputed material facts, a decision on a closed record, and judicial review for substantial evidence, with each stage open to challenge.

The first stage has no equivalent in the baseline scheme of the Administrative Procedure Act, which permits an agency to begin with a notice of proposed rulemaking. Section 18(b)(2)(A) requires the Commission, before publishing any notice of proposed rulemaking, to publish an advance notice of proposed rulemaking in the Federal Register. The advance notice must contain a brief description of the area of inquiry under consideration, a statement of the objectives the Commission seeks to achieve, and a description of possible regulatory alternatives under consideration, and the notice must invite the response of interested parties, including suggestions and alternative methods for achieving the stated objectives. The invitation for suggestions creates a first body of material that opponents can invoke at every later stage. Section 18(b)(2)(B) adds a congressional dimension by requiring the Commission to submit the advance notice to the Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation of the Senate and to the Committee on Energy and Commerce of the House of Representatives.

That threshold finding is prevalence. Section 18(b)(3) permits the Commission to issue a notice of proposed rulemaking only where the Commission has reason to believe that the unfair or deceptive acts or practices that are the subject of the proposed rulemaking are prevalent, and the statute specifies the only two routes by which a prevalence determination may be made. The Commission must either have issued cease and desist orders regarding such acts or practices, or possess other information indicating a widespread pattern of unfair or deceptive acts or practices. The requirement distinguishes trade regulation rules from rules issued under many other statutes, where an agency may regulate a practice that is novel, localized, or anticipated rather than demonstrated. A trade regulation rule under Section 18 is by design a response to conduct already shown to be widespread, proven either through the Commission enforcement record or through information of comparable breadth, such as state enforcement or documented consumer injury across a market. Prevalence returns at the close of the proceeding as a mandatory component of the statement of basis and purpose.

Only after the advance notice, the committee submissions, and the prevalence determination may the proposal itself appear. Section 18(b)(2)(C) requires the Commission, thirty days before publication of a notice of proposed rulemaking, to submit that notice to the same two congressional committees. Section 18(b)(1) then sets the content of the proceeding. The Commission proceeds in accordance with Section 553 of Title 5, the Administrative Procedure Act provision on informal rulemaking, without regard to references in that section to the formal hearing provisions of Sections 556 and 557, and must also satisfy the added conditions of Section 18. Under Section 18(b)(1)(A), the notice of proposed rulemaking must state with particularity the text of the rule the Commission proposes to promulgate, including any alternatives, together with the reason for the proposed rule. Particularity exceeds the Administrative Procedure Act baseline, and a text stated with particularity narrows the range within which the final rule may move without fresh notice. Under Section 18(b)(1)(B), interested persons must be allowed to submit written data, views, and arguments, and all such submissions must be made publicly available, because the submissions become part of the rulemaking record on which the final rule must rest.

### The Informal Hearing and the Contest Over Facts

Section 18(b)(1)(C) requires the Commission to provide an opportunity for an informal hearing in accordance with subsection (c), and subsection (c) converts that hearing into the most adjudicative feature of the process. The label informal distinguishes the proceeding from formal rulemaking under Sections 556 and 557 of Title 5, but the Section 18 hearing carries its own apparatus. Under Section 18(c)(1)(A), hearings are conducted by hearing officers, and under Section 18(c)(1)(B) the officer who presides is responsible to a chief presiding officer who is not responsible to any other officer or employee of the Commission, an insulation designed to give the presiding officer a measure of independence from the staff advocating the rule. Except as required for the disposition of ex parte matters as authorized by law, Section 18(c)(1)(C) forbids a presiding officer from consulting any person or party with respect to any fact in issue unless the officer gives notice and an opportunity for all parties to participate. The presiding officer makes a recommended decision based on findings and conclusions as to all relevant and material evidence. Section 18(c)(5) requires a verbatim transcript of any oral presentation and cross examination, available to the public.

The substantive entitlement at the hearing appears in Section 18(c)(2). An interested person is entitled to present a position orally or by documentary submission, or both, and, if the Commission determines that there are disputed issues of material fact that are necessary to resolve, the interested person is entitled to present rebuttal submissions and to conduct, or to have conducted, such cross examination of persons as the Commission determines to be appropriate and to be required for a full and true disclosure with respect to such issues. Cross examination inside a rulemaking proceeding is the feature that most sharply separates Section 18 from ordinary notice and comment, where an agency may receive thousands of comments without any witness answering a question from an opposing participant. The entitlement is conditional rather than absolute, and each condition generates its own dispute, because a participant must frame a disagreement as a disputed issue of material fact, concrete enough that testimony could resolve it and important enough that the rule outcome could turn on the resolution. Section 18(c)(3) authorizes the Commission to prescribe rules and rulings to avoid unnecessary costs or delay, including reasonable time limits on oral presentations and requirements that cross examination be conducted by the Commission on behalf of the person entitled to it. Section 18(c)(4) addresses group representation, permitting limits where persons with the same or similar interests cannot agree on a single representative. Each such ruling can be carried into court, because Section 18(e) makes the denial or limitation of cross examination an independent ground of judicial relief where the ruling precluded disclosure of disputed material facts necessary for a fair determination of the proceeding as a whole.

### The Statement That Closes the Proceeding

Section 18(b)(1)(D) permits the Commission to promulgate a final rule only on the basis of the matter in the rulemaking record, together with a statement of basis and purpose. The record is defined in Section 18(e)(1)(B) and consists of the rule, its statement of basis and purpose, the transcript required by subsection (c), any written submissions, and any other information that the Commission considers relevant to the rule. The definition gives the record a closed and identifiable content, which distinguishes Section 18 review from the more fluid inquiry in ordinary informal rulemaking, where courts reconstruct the basis of decision from the administrative record the agency assembles for litigation.

Section 18(d)(1) prescribes the minimum content of that statement. The statement must address the prevalence of the acts or practices treated by the rule, carrying the threshold determination of subsection (b)(3) through to the final decision. The statement must address the manner and context in which such acts or practices are unfair or deceptive, obliging the Commission to explain the mechanism of deception or unfairness as the practice actually occurs in the marketplace. The statement must also address the economic effect of the rule, taking into account the effect on small business and consumers.

Judicial review under Section 18(e) completes the design. Within sixty days after a rule is promulgated, any interested person, expressly including a consumer or a consumer organization, may file a petition for review in the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit or in the circuit where the petitioner resides or has a principal place of business,. The court must hold unlawful and set aside a rule on the grounds specified in subparagraphs (A) through (D) of Section 706(2) of Title 5, taking due account of the rule of prejudicial error, or on either of two additional grounds stated in Section 18(e)(3). The first is evidentiary, namely that the Commission action is not supported by substantial evidence in the rulemaking record taken as a whole, with evidence defined as any matter in the rulemaking record. The second covers a Commission determination that a petitioner was not entitled to cross examination or rebuttal submissions, or a ruling limiting such examination or submissions, where the determination or ruling precluded disclosure of disputed material facts that was necessary for a fair determination of the proceeding taken as a whole. Section 706(2)(E) of Title 5 does not apply to such a rule, because Section 18 supplies a substantial evidence test of its own tied to the defined record.

The choice between the substantial evidence standard and the arbitrary and capricious standard of Section 706(2)(A) is one of emphasis with real consequences. Arbitrary and capricious review asks principally whether the agency examined the relevant data and articulated a satisfactory explanation, including a rational connection between the facts found and the choice made. Substantial evidence review directs the court to the record itself and asks whether the record, considered as a whole and including whatever fairly detracts from the agency conclusion, contains such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support the conclusion. Under Section 18 the reviewing court therefore works from a closed record that includes hearing testimony tested by cross examination, and factual findings on prevalence, consumer understanding, compliance cost, and market behavior must find support in that record rather than in staff expertise asserted in a preamble. The standard changes the litigation posture of every participant from the first day, because participants build the record as trial counsel build a trial record, with the court of appeals audience in view.

### The Arithmetic of Delay

The practical consequence of the design follows from simple addition that turns into multiplication. Each procedural stage consumes calendar time on its own, and each stage also creates the material and the grievances for the stages after it. An advance notice invites a round of comments that staff must read and answer in the architecture of the proposal. A prevalence finding requires enforcement history or marketwide evidence assembled in defensible form. A proposal stated with particularity requires drafting of regulatory text and alternatives at a level of finish that ordinary informal rulemaking often reaches only at the final stage. A hearing requires a presiding officer, a schedule, witnesses, and a transcript, and cross examination extends the hearing in proportion to the number of factual disputes the Commission has recognized as material. A statement of basis and purpose addressing prevalence, manner and context, and economic effect must then digest the entire accumulation into findings that can survive substantial evidence review, and the review itself adds a final interval during which the rule validity remains contested.

The historical yield under these procedures was correspondingly small, and the rules that completed the course illustrate both the reach of the instrument and the effort completion required. The Funeral Industry Practices Rule, known as the Funeral Rule, took effect on April 30, 1984, requiring funeral providers to furnish itemized price information and forbidding specified misrepresentations and required purchases, and the Commission revised the rule in 1994. The Used Motor Vehicle Trade Regulation Rule, known as the Used Car Rule, was promulgated on November 19, 1984, at volume 49 of the Federal Register at page 45692, requiring dealers to display a buyers guide on used vehicles disclosing warranty terms, and that rule was updated in 1995. The clustering of the two best known completions in a single year, nearly a decade after Public Law 93-637, conveys the tempo the procedures imposed even on priorities the Commission pursued with determination. Section 18 rations rulemaking by making rulemaking expensive, and expensive in a currency, staff time measured against a closed record, that no change in agency enthusiasm can devalue.

### A Baseline and a Successor for Comparison

The contrast with the Administrative Procedure Act baseline clarifies how much of Section 18 is addition rather than elaboration. Section 553 of Title 5 requires notice of proposed rulemaking published in the Federal Register, an opportunity for interested persons to participate through submission of written data, views, or arguments, and, after consideration of the relevant matter presented, a final rule accompanied by a concise general statement of basis and purpose, ordinarily published at least thirty days before the rule effective date. No advance notice is required. No hearing of any kind is required, and no participant holds a statutory entitlement to test another participant evidence by cross examination. No presiding officer issues a recommended decision, no prevalence finding conditions the proposal, and no statutorily defined record confines the basis of decision. Judicial review proceeds principally under the arbitrary and capricious standard of Section 706(2)(A), with the substantial evidence test of Section 706(2)(E) reserved for cases governed by the formal proceeding provisions or by another statute that imposes it, which is precisely the role Section 18 performs for trade regulation rules.

Congress revisited the allocation when it created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau in Title X of the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act, enacted as Public Law 111-203 on July 21, 2010, and conferred authority over federal consumer financial law, including authority to address unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts or practices in connection with consumer financial products and services. The Bureau received rulemaking machinery built on the Administrative Procedure Act model rather than on the Section 18 model. Bureau rules proceed through notice and comment, accompanied by the analytical duties the Dodd-Frank Act itself imposes, including consideration of benefits and costs to consumers and covered persons. No advance notice of proposed rulemaking is a statutory precondition to a proposal, no prevalence finding gates the proceeding, no informal hearing with cross examination on disputed issues of material fact interrupts the comment process, no presiding officer renders a recommended decision, and judicial review follows the ordinary Administrative Procedure Act standards rather than a substantial evidence test attached to a statutorily defined rulemaking record. The comparison is a preview of an argument the article returns to in treatment of the Bureau, and the comparison discloses the judgment Congress made in 2010 about the experiment of 1975, since Congress declined to reproduce the hybrid apparatus for the new regulator.

## The Backlash and the Restrictions That Followed

The backlash against the Federal Trade Commission in the late nineteen seventies was not a reaction against rulemaking in the abstract. Congress had deliberately equipped the Commission with trade regulation rulemaking power, and officials of both parties had demanded a more energetic agency a decade earlier. The reaction formed against a style of rulemaking in which a broad prohibition on unfair acts or practices was used to propose industry wide answers to questions of health, family life, and commercial speech that legislators regarded as theirs to decide. Defenders described an agency using authority Congress had granted. Detractors described an unelected body settling contested social policy under a standard with no clear stopping point. That collision produced ridicule, a funding crisis that closed the agency for a day, and a statute that burdened every later proceeding and taught Commission officials that an ambitious rule could place the institution itself at risk.

### A Commission Rebuilt to Act

The activist Commission was itself the product of an earlier backlash, directed at passivity rather than excess. A student task force organised by Ralph Nader examined the agency during the summer of 1968 and published its report as a book in January 1969, portraying a Commission preoccupied with minor matters, slow to act, secretive, and too close to the businesses it was supposed to police. President Richard Nixon then asked the American Bar Association to study the agency. The resulting 1969 report of the ABA Commission to Study the Federal Trade Commission, chaired by Miles W. Kirkpatrick with Robert Pitofsky as counsel, considered abolition and concluded that the agency had to change fundamentally or face abolition. Two hostile diagnoses, one from consumer advocates and one from the organised bar, pointed in the same direction. The Commission was condemned less for choosing the wrong targets than for lacking a coherent programme of consumer protection.

The appointments and legislation that followed gave that criticism institutional force. Kirkpatrick himself became chairman under Nixon, and the Commission recruited officials committed to a systematic attack on fraud and on practices that harmed consumers in large numbers. In 1975 the Magnuson-Moss legislation confirmed and structured the power to issue trade regulation rules under Section 18 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, supplementing case by case adjudication with a procedure for declaring categories of practice unlawful across an industry. More than a dozen rulemaking proceedings were initiated in the months and years after 1975. Funeral industry practices, voluntary standards and certification, insurance, and advertising all generated proceedings or proposed restrictions that organised industries had reason to monitor as a single expanding programme rather than as isolated enforcement choices.

Leadership sharpened the impression of expansion. President Jimmy Carter made Michael Pertschuk chairman in 1977, and Pertschuk presented consumer protection as a contest between organised corporate influence and families with little representation in Washington. Supporters heard a chairman willing to use the powers granted in 1975. Business groups heard a regulator prepared to treat dissatisfaction with a lawful market outcome as evidence that the outcome was unfair. Timothy J. Muris, a former Commission official, later characterised the Commission of that decade as an agency that tried to become the second most powerful legislature in Washington. Pertschuk and his allies rejected that premise. One side treated breadth as fidelity to a broad statute. The other treated breadth as proof that the statute was being converted into a general licence to govern commerce.

### What did the Federal Trade Commission propose for children's television advertising?

The Commission sought comment in 1978 on banning television advertising aimed at children under eight, banning advertisements for highly sugared products for older children, and requiring health disclosures for other sugared products, all on the theory that such advertising was unfair to children unable to grasp selling intent.

The proceeding, known by the compressed name KidVid, began with petitions rather than with a staff invention. Action for Children's Television petitioned in April 1977 for mandatory rules against televised candy advertising directed at children, and the Center for Science in the Public Interest filed a companion petition concerning sugared snack foods and heavily sugared breakfast cereals. Petitions also came from Consumers Union and the Committee on Children's Television. A Commission staff report followed in February 1978, and the notice of proposed rulemaking appeared on April 27, 1978, proposing no final regulatory text. It invited comment on three staff approaches. Advertising directed at audiences with a substantial proportion of children under eight could be banned altogether. Advertising for highly sugared products associated with serious risks of tooth decay could be banned for older children, described as children between eight and twelve. Advertising for other sugared products could be required to carry balancing health and nutrition disclosures, financed by advertisers.

The legal theory mattered as much as the remedies. The advertisements were not alleged to be false in the ordinary sense, nor was the principal claim that a commercial lied about ingredients or price. The claim was that very young children could not understand that an advertisement was intended to sell a product, and that exploiting that limitation was inherently unfair. For older children, the staff connected promotion of sugared foods to tooth decay and to pressure placed on parents at purchase. Pertschuk later framed the proceeding as an extension of the common law tradition against commercial exploitation of minors, stressing that the Commission had been petitioned by parents, teachers, pediatricians, dentists, and mainstream family organisations. Defenders presented the case as a limited response to a recognised vulnerability, consumer protection in its most defensible form.

Opponents answered that the theory had no natural boundary. Broadcasters, networks, advertising agencies, and makers of candy, cereal, and toys argued that parents, not the Commission, decided what children ate. Industry representatives argued that a ban on truthful advertising raised serious First Amendment problems, that definitions of sugared products and child audiences would be difficult to administer, and that unfairness was being used to displace parental judgement rather than to correct deception. Hearings during six weeks in early 1979 drew more than two hundred witnesses, and the record ran to tens of thousands of pages. Affected industries also sued, challenging in one action whether Pertschuk was disqualified by bias and in another the special procedures adopted for the proceeding. A district court agreed with the disqualification claim, an appeals court reversed, and Pertschuk withdrew voluntarily. Litigation, lobbying, and testimony proceeded together, each reinforcing the claim in Congress that the agency had taken on a fight it could not confine.

Ridicule gave that claim a durable public name. On March 1, 1978, before the notice of proposed rulemaking had appeared, The Washington Post published an editorial titled The FTC as National Nanny, calling the contemplated intervention preposterous and saying that it would turn the agency into a great national nanny. In Pertschuk's later account, delivered in a 1981 lecture on Congress and the Commission, the damage came from the source and the tone as much as from the disagreement. The Post was generally sympathetic to an activist Commission, so its mockery signalled to broadcasters, food manufacturers, and advertisers that the proceeding was open political territory, and it signalled to Congress that intervention carried little political risk. Pertschuk also argued that the label surrendered the symbol the Commission had relied upon, the defence of the family, by recasting federal officials as substitutes for parents.

### A Funding Fight That Closed an Agency

Congress answered first through money and authorisation. Beginning in 1978, the Commission lost the security of regular funding and operated for stretches on temporary appropriations while authorisation bills became vehicles for restrictions on particular proceedings and on the agency as a whole. Authorisation measures moved through both chambers in late 1979 and early 1980, each carrying restrictions aimed at the agency. Proposals in the fights of 1979 and 1980 sought, in different combinations, to end or limit the funeral industry proceeding, to restrict investigations involving agricultural cooperatives, insurance, and trademarks, to confine advertising rules to deception, and to give Congress a legislative veto over Commission rules. The exact contents shifted between chambers and bill versions, and the safest summary is that a substantial bloc in each chamber wanted the authorisation statute to function as a leash, not merely as an extension of appropriations authority.

The leash tightened into a closure because of a legal opinion that changed the meaning of a funding lapse. Attorney General Benjamin Civiletti advised President Carter on April 25, 1980, that the Antideficiency Act required agencies to stop operations during a lapse in appropriations, apart from action needed for an orderly termination. Funding for the Commission expired days afterward, amid disagreement over the terms on which the agency should continue. On May 1, 1980, the Commission closed. About one thousand six hundred employees were furloughed, meetings and court dates were cancelled, confidential files were packed for secure storage, and United States Marshals were sent to at least two field offices to make certain that operations had stopped. Placed in [the history of United States consumer protection law](/2019/02/15/us-consumer-protection-law-history/), the episode stands apart for its mechanism as well as its cause. It was the first shutdown of a federal agency enforced because of a lapse in appropriations.

The closure lasted one day, and its brevity was part of its force. The House approved an extension of funding on May 1 by two hundred eighty four to ninety six, the Senate approved it by seventy one to ten, and the Commission reopened after the extension was secured. The shutdown cost an estimated seven hundred thousand dollars, most of it in salaries for a day on which employees were paid not to work and files were then unpacked and restored. Members who had delayed funding to extract concessions had not necessarily expected that consequence, and contemporary accounts described surprise that a shutdown was enforced at all. Enough legislators were nevertheless prepared to let the money lapse, during a dispute over limiting its powers, that only the spectacle of marshals, boxes, and closed doors ended the standoff within hours.

### Restrictions Written Into Law

The settlement arrived on May 28, 1980, when President Carter signed the Federal Trade Commission Improvements Act of 1980, Public Law 96-252. The act extended the authorisation of appropriations while attaching conditions that reflected the fights of the preceding three years. It left the Commission in existence, left trade regulation rulemaking in existence, and left Section 5 authority over unfair and deceptive acts or practices in existence. What it removed was room for the kind of proceeding that had provoked the crisis. Congress acted surgically against KidVid, generally against unfairness as a basis for advertising rules, and procedurally against every future rule that might test congressional patience in the same way.

The children's advertising provision was drafted to prevent evasion by relabelling. The act provided that the Commission had no authority to promulgate any rule in the children's advertising proceeding pending on the date of enactment, or in any substantially similar proceeding, on the basis of a determination that such advertising constituted an unfair act or practice. A deception based rule was not barred, and enforcement against a particular deceptive advertisement remained possible, but the theory on which KidVid had been built was removed from that subject. Related provisions barred the use of authorised funds during the fiscal years ending in 1980, 1981, and 1982 to initiate a new rulemaking intended to produce a rule regulating commercial advertising on an unfairness basis, a restriction later extended into the following fiscal year. A later Commission account stated that the prohibition on new unfairness based advertising rulemaking was continued through appropriations legislation until 1994. That longer continuation rests on that account and is stated here at that level of confidence, rather than as a provision of Public Law 96-252 standing alone.

Procedure supplied the second set of restraints. The act subjected future trade regulation rules to disapproval within ninety days after promulgation by a concurrent resolution adopted by both houses of Congress, the device known as a two house legislative veto. It required an advance notice of proposed rulemaking before a proposed rule, and regulatory analyses for proposed and final rules, setting out the need for action, the alternatives considered, and the expected effects. Advance notification to Congress and committee review were built into that structure, so that a controversial rule would reach Capitol Hill as a text that could be attacked and, under the veto provision, killed. Other provisions narrowed fields that had become separate sources of friction. The act restrained rulemaking on voluntary standards and certification, allowed the funeral industry rule to proceed only in a restricted form centred on price disclosure and deceptive or coercive practices, limited antitrust investigation of agricultural cooperatives, restricted investigation of insurance, and barred action to cancel a registered trademark on the ground that the name had become generic. Each item answered a constituency that had learned to seek relief from Congress.

The legislative veto did not survive constitutional review in its original form. In Immigration and Naturalization Service v. Chadha, decided in 1983 and reported at 462 U.S. 919, the Supreme Court held a one house legislative veto in the immigration laws unconstitutional. Chief Justice Warren Burger wrote for the Court that action legislative in purpose and effect had to satisfy the requirements of passage by both houses and presentation to the President, and that convenience could not excuse a shortcut around that procedure. Chadha did not itself decide the fate of the Commission's two house veto, which required action by both chambers. Its reasoning nevertheless undermined the device, because a concurrent resolution of disapproval was not presented to the President. Justice Byron White, dissenting, treated the trade regulation veto as an example of what was at stake and quoted a 1978 statement by Representative James T. Broyhill warning that the Commission's broad authority allowed it to regulate almost every aspect of American commercial life. After 1983, Congress retained weapons that needed no veto clause, including appropriations conditions, authorisation delays, and statutory amendments.

### The Lesson the Institution Kept

The children's advertising proceeding ended without a rule. A final staff report dated March 31, 1981, revised the age at which children were treated as too young to understand selling intent from under eight to six and under, and the Commission terminated the rulemaking in 1981, with notice published that autumn. Howard Beales, who worked on the proceeding as a young Commission economist and later directed the Bureau of Consumer Protection, argued in a retrospective account that the congressional reaction could not be explained by industry lobbying alone, formidable as that lobbying had been. In his assessment, the reaction answered a proposal widely perceived as gross overreach. Protecting parents from the purchase requests of their children was not, without more, a sufficient basis for federal action, and a practice that burdened families was not for that reason alone unfair or deceptive.

The Commission applied a related discipline to unfairness itself. In its 1980 Unfairness Policy Statement, the Commission stated that an unfair practice was one that caused or was likely to cause substantial injury to consumers, that the injury was not reasonably avoidable by consumers, and that it was not outweighed by countervailing benefits to consumers or to competition. The statement replaced open ended appeals to public policy with a test organised around injury, avoidability, and benefit. Congress later wrote the core of that test into statute in the Federal Trade Commission Act Amendments of 1994, which defined unfairness in Section 5 in terms of substantial injury, reasonable avoidability, and countervailing benefits. Unfairness survived, because Congress had never repealed the word and the Commission had never surrendered it. The expansive confidence that unfairness could supply a general charter for rules about the structure of family and market life did not survive on the same terms.

That settlement explains the caution that followed more convincingly than any single change of party or chairman. Case by case enforcement risks reversal in a particular matter and the ordinary costs of litigation. An industry wide rule on a contested theory invites every affected firm to organise at once, gives trade associations a single target, supplies Congress with a constituency complaint that crosses district lines, and exposes unrelated proceedings to retaliation through authorisation and appropriations bills. KidVid showed that retaliation could reach the existence of the agency in practical terms, through a lapse that closed its doors, and the 1980 act showed that even a failed rule could leave permanent procedural burdens behind. Beales also observed that more than a decade passed after termination of the children's proceeding before Congress proved willing to reauthorise the agency again, a measure of how long institutional credit took to rebuild. Officials deciding whether to begin a later rulemaking did not need to imagine those consequences in the abstract.

Neutral assessment requires the counterweight to be stated as plainly as the criticism. The activist Commission had been created by deliberate congressional choices, first in the demand for reform after 1969 and then in the confirmation of rulemaking power in 1975. Its defenders could fairly observe that the vulnerability of young children to selling techniques was a genuine consumer problem, that deception doctrine fit that problem awkwardly, and that an agency forbidden to address a harm unless the harm could be called a lie would leave part of its statute unused. Critics could fairly answer that a genuine problem did not settle who should solve it, that national rules on advertising to children involved speech and parental authority as well as health, and that a standard capable of reaching those questions could reach almost any disfavoured practice. The backlash settled neither the moral question about advertising to children nor the economic question about sugar. It settled a question of institutional survival. After 1980, a broad consumer rule had to be defended not only as lawful and supported by a record, but also as worth the retaliation it might bring upon the Commission and its whole programme. That burden, learned in a single proceeding and preserved in procedure, is the hinge on which the agency's subsequent approach to rulemaking turned.

## Enforcement Case by Case Instead of Rules

When hybrid rulemaking under the Magnuson Moss procedures became slow enough to deter rulemaking itself, the Federal Trade Commission changed instruments rather than surrendering the field. Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act remained a broad prohibition on unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce, and the agency enforced that prohibition one respondent at a time. The working law of consumer protection therefore came to be built from complaints, settlements, and occasional litigated decisions rather than from trade regulation rules adopted in advance for an entire market. A rule announces an obligation before enforcement begins and applies to every firm within its terms. An enforcement action works in reverse order. Conduct occurs, staff investigates, a complaint frames the conduct as a violation, and the resulting order speaks most directly to the firm named in the caption. Between those instruments lies the central question of the model, namely how much general law a sequence of individual cases can produce.

The administrative track ran under Part 3 of the Commission Rules of Practice. A Part 3 proceeding began with a complaint issued by the Commission and prosecuted by complaint counsel, the staff assigned to litigate the matter. The case was tried before an administrative law judge, who received evidence and issued an initial decision with findings and a proposed order. Either side could appeal to the full Commission, which then issued a decision and order of its own. A respondent ordered to cease and desist could seek review in a federal court of appeals, with further review in the Supreme Court possible through the ordinary appellate route. Where the Commission ruled against complaint counsel, the matter ended inside the agency. The design placed the Commission in an unusual combination of roles, since the same institution authorized the complaint, supplied the prosecutors, heard the appeal, and issued the final administrative order, subject to judicial review at the instance of a respondent. Defenders treated that combination as expert decision making on a developed record, and detractors treated the same combination as a reason to discount the neutrality of the process. The structure also explains why so few Part 3 cases produced a fully litigated decision, because every stage cost time and money and a negotiated order offered an exit.

The second track ran through federal court. Under Section 13(b), the Commission could proceed directly in federal district court, without prior administrative proceedings, and seek a permanent injunction against a person, partnership, or corporation violating or about to violate a provision of law the Commission enforced. Court actions suited cases in which staff wanted a judge to freeze assets or halt a scheme. The choice between tracks belonged to the Commission rather than to a respondent or an injured consumer. Section 5 gave no private right of action, a point federal courts settled early, with the District of Columbia Circuit decision in Holloway v. Bristol Myers in 1973 among the authorities most often cited for the refusal to imply a private claim. A practice could therefore injure many consumers and generate no case, because staff judged other matters more urgent or because resources ran out.

### Does an FTC consent order create law for other companies?

No. A consent order binds only the company or person who signs it. A competitor faces no obligation under that order, and a court treats the order as a settlement rather than a decided holding. Other firms may read the order as a warning about agency priorities, but warning and law differ in enforceability and in legal effect.

The overwhelming majority of matters on both tracks ended without a litigated decision, and the instrument that ended them was the consent order. In a typical settlement, the respondent signed an agreement containing a proposed order, the Commission accepted the agreement and placed the proposed order on the public record for a period of public comment, commonly thirty days, and the order became final after that process ran its course. The respondent gave up the conduct named in the complaint, accepted compliance and reporting obligations, and agreed to supervision that, for administrative orders under the Commission policy announced in 1995, often ran for twenty years. The bargain made sense for both sides. Settlement at that scale changed the character of the output. A litigated decision contains findings of fact, an analysis of the statute, and a holding that later decision makers must distinguish, follow, or reject. A consent order contains terms. The accompanying complaint states allegations, and allegations accepted for settlement purposes remain allegations rather than adjudicated findings, because no judge tested the evidence and no opinion explained why the statute reached the conduct on a contested record.

What a consent order could do for the signing firm was substantial. An order could prohibit the practice charged, require a firm to change the way a product was advertised, sold, billed, or serviced, impose record keeping and monitoring duties, and require compliance reports and access to books and records for the life of the order. An order could also reach beyond the charged practice through fencing in relief, a remedy that prohibits conduct different from the violation alleged but related to that violation, on the theory that a firm charged with one deceptive practice should not retain freedom to approach the line from a slightly different direction. Fencing in provisions could bar a category of claims, cover other products, or forbid a business method. Violation of a final order carried serious consequences, because the Commission could return to federal court and seek civil penalties and further injunctive relief, and a court could punish defiance of a court entered order through contempt. For the signing party, the document functioned as a private statute tailored to that firm.

What a consent order could not do was equally definite, and the limits were structural rather than accidental. A consent order created no generally applicable law. The order bound the signing parties and any successors within the language of the order itself, and no one else. A competitor engaged in the identical practice the day after an order became final violated no term of that order, because the competitor had promised the Commission nothing. Staff remained free to investigate the competitor, and the competitor remained free to argue that the practice was lawful on a contested record. Nor did a consent order operate as precedent in the way a rule or a litigated decision operates. A rule binds through its text until amended or invalidated, and a litigated appellate decision binds lower courts within a circuit and persuades courts elsewhere through a reasoned opinion. A consent order persuades, where it persuades at all, through repetition and through the judgment of counsel that the Commission was likely to press the same theory again. Lawyers read consent orders closely for exactly that reason, mining the orders for signals about substantiation, disclosure placement, billing, and data security. Reading for signals differs from reading law, because an order gives no account of the evidence staff found persuasive and no discussion of defenses that might have succeeded at trial.

The guidance deficit fell hardest on firms that were never parties to any case. A firm inside an order possessed a document that specified, in operational language, what that firm had to do. A firm outside every order possessed the statute, litigated decisions of uneven age, settlements negotiated by other firms under other pressures, and whatever soft instruments the Commission chose to publish. Those soft instruments multiplied as rulemaking receded, and each occupied a deliberate position below the level of a rule. Policy statements announced how the Commission understood deception, unfairness, or advertising substantiation, with the deception policy statement of 1983 and the unfairness policy statement of 1980 serving as foundational examples. Guides and business education materials translated enforcement experience into practical instruction, and workshops gathered testimony on emerging practices. None of those instruments, standing alone, imposed a legal obligation enforceable through penalties, and the Commission generally said as much in the instruments themselves. A firm could depart from business guidance and defend the departure on the ground that the statute, rather than the guidance document, supplied the governing standard. Soft instruments informed discretion without surrendering discretion, which explained both the attraction to the agency and the dissatisfaction of firms that wanted a standard firm enough to plan around.

A related pattern appeared across federal financial regulation, where agencies built programs on broad readings of general statutory language, firms organized conduct around those readings, and courts then narrowed the instrument after the fact in litigation over a particular action. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau supplied the clearest parallel after the Dodd Frank Act of 2010 created that bureau and armed the director with authority over unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts or practices in consumer financial markets. In PHH Corporation v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, a panel of the District of Columbia Circuit held in 2016 that the single director structure of the bureau, with removal only for cause, was unconstitutional, and also rejected the bureau reinterpretation of the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act. The full court of appeals sustained the structure in 2018, and the Supreme Court in Seila Law v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau held in 2020 that the removal restriction violated the separation of powers while allowing the bureau to continue with a director removable at will. The broader sequence of [court challenges to financial regulation](/2014/01/15/financial-regulation-court-cases/) forms a subject of its own, and a paragraph here serves only as a preview. Case by case development does not escape judicial narrowing, because every litigated case invites a court to trim the agency theory, and the rare case that reaches a court can trim remedies or authority for an entire program rather than for one respondent alone.

Part of the gap left by federal enforcement was filled from below, by state law. Every state adopted a statute against unfair or deceptive acts or practices, and the District of Columbia and the territories adopted comparable laws as well, in a family of enactments often called little FTC Acts because many borrowed the operative language of Section 5 or directed state courts to give weight to federal interpretations of that language. The state statutes collected under the heading of [state consumer protection statutes](/2019/02/01/federal-vs-state-consumer-protection/) differed from the federal model in the feature that mattered most to an injured consumer. Where Section 5 withheld a private claim, state law supplied one. The National Consumer Law Center survey of the state enactments recorded that all of the state unfair and deceptive acts or practices statutes except the statute of Puerto Rico authorized private damage actions, and an earlier count in the scholarly literature placed the number of statutes with an express private right at forty two, with courts in additional states construing a statute to imply a private claim. Many statutes sweetened the private remedy to make small claims rational to bring. A prevailing consumer could recover attorney fees under many state provisions. Many added multiple, minimum, or punitive damages for knowing conduct, with Massachusetts Chapter 93A and the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act frequently cited for treble damage provisions of unusual strength. A practice that no federal official would examine could therefore support a private suit, and a state attorney general could proceed under state authority without waiting for a federal docket decision.

State law filled only part of the gap. State statutes varied enormously in coverage and remedial strength. Some statutes exempted regulated industries, insurance, public utilities, or conduct permitted under other regulatory schemes, and some imposed pre suit notice requirements, reliance or ascertainable loss elements, or restrictions on class actions that narrowed the private remedy in practice. State courts also diverged in deference to federal interpretations, so a national firm could confront fifty related but distinct standards rather than one federal standard. Private plaintiffs and class counsel selected cases for private return, which favored claims with clear documentation, solvent defendants, and damage theories suited to aggregation, and disfavored claims involving diffuse deception or injuries that were serious but difficult to quantify. The state system supplied the withheld private remedy unevenly, through a patchwork tracking state choices and litigation economics.

### Why do defenders prefer enforcement to rulemaking in fast changing markets?

The strongest defense began with institutional fit. J. Howard Beales, who directed the Bureau of Consumer Protection from 2001 to 2004, argued in a Technology Policy Institute discussion with Timothy Muris, a former chairman of the Commission, that the case by case approach was much more flexible than rulemaking because a case allowed the agency to take into account the particular circumstances that produced a practice before deciding whether the practice helped or harmed consumers. Rulemaking, on the Beales account, was an exercise in generalization. A rule required a fairly bright line between acceptable and unacceptable conduct, and drawing that line well required detailed knowledge of an industry structure, of the reasons a practice occurred, and of the effects of the practice in differing circumstances. The Commission, Beales observed, regulated no single industry. Agency expertise lay in applying general principles to particular facts. That uncertainty was the price of accuracy, because a flexible standard could refuse to condemn a practice that served consumers in one setting merely because a similar practice had injured consumers in another.

The defense gained force from the procedural record of the alternative. Jeffrey Lubbers of American University compared in a 2015 George Washington Law Review article the time required for rulemakings conducted under the Magnuson Moss procedures with the time required for rules issued under ordinary Administrative Procedure Act notice and comment procedures, and the Magnuson Moss rulemakings took significantly longer. Lubbers argued for allowing the Commission to use ordinary Administrative Procedure Act procedures like most agencies. Defenders of enforcement drew a practical lesson from that comparison, because hybrid rulemaking, with hearings and cross examination on disputed issues of material fact, invited the ossification associated with procedures piled on top of notice and comment. A proceeding that consumed years could produce a rule for a technology the market had abandoned, while enforcement could end with an order written for the practice actually in use. Alden F. Abbott, who served as general counsel of the Commission from 2018 to 2021, pressed a related argument in a June 2021 Mercatus Center policy brief on competition regulation, concluding that the Commission should stick with enforcement as the primary tool and weigh the legal risks, error costs, and rule of law harms of rulemaking before redirecting scarce resources toward drafting and defending rules. Defenders added a claim about record quality, since an investigation assembled documents, employee testimony, and complaints tied to identifiable transactions, and a sequence of such cases allowed standards to track technology and marketing practice in close sequence, in a method resembling common law development.

The response denied that flexibility and record quality answered the question firms actually asked. A firm deciding whether to launch a practice needed to know the legal consequence before capital was committed, and the enforcement model answered with a probability rather than a standard. Maureen K. Ohlhausen, a commissioner who later served as acting chairman, made the guidance objection concrete in a dissent from the statement of enforcement principles on unfair methods of competition issued on August 13, 2015. The Ohlhausen dissent faulted the statement for offering highly general principles with no specific examples of lawful or unlawful conduct, for failing to address case law in which courts had struck down earlier assertions of Section 5 authority, and for leaving businesses with a document that constrained agency discretion in no meaningful way and was likely to produce greater uncertainty and burden. Guidance without examples told a firm almost nothing about practices at the margin, where the cost of guessing wrong included an investigation, a long consent order, or a federal court action. An agency that reserved judgment until a case arrived could always explain a decision afterward, and explanation afterward was the one form of guidance that arrived too late for the firm that had to decide beforehand.

A second objection concerned distribution. Enforcement yielded industry specific and firm specific results rather than general rules, and the specificity created competitive distortions a rule would have avoided. When one firm signed a consent order with fencing in provisions, that firm operated under restrictions no rival shared, even where rivals engaged in related conduct staff had not examined. The ordered firm could find advertising claims closed to the firm and open to competitors, data practices supervised for the firm and unsupervised elsewhere, and compliance costs imposed on one balance sheet in a market where price competition punished unilateral cost. Settlement practice also produced no public reasoning to distinguish a hard legal boundary from a bargain struck to avoid a Part 3 trial, protect a pending transaction, or end publicity. Compliant firms were left in the worst position, receiving signals of varying authority and no assurance that a practice cleared by analogy to one settlement would survive a later Commission with a different view.

Both readings had to answer the same settled structural point, and neither reading dissolved that point. Obligations under Section 5 in practice were the sum of decided cases and consent orders, not a code. The statute supplied the prohibition, the policy statements supplied interpretive frames for deception and unfairness, and the litigated decisions supplied holdings on the records that produced those decisions. Everything else that governed firm behavior, from substantiation for a health claim to the security program treated as reasonable, came from the accumulated output of enforcement and from guidance the Commission could revise without rulemaking procedures. A Commission that departed sharply from earlier positions changed the practical law without amending a rule or facing judicial review of a final rule, and a Commission that honored earlier positions too rigidly froze standards the markets had outgrown. The enforcement model lived between those two failures, defended as flexibility by Beales and Abbott, criticized as guidance failure by Ohlhausen and Wilson, and constrained by the fact that a consent order bound a signer and instructed everyone else only by example. After the 2021 decision in AMG Capital Management v. Federal Trade Commission, the model also carried weaker monetary remedies in federal court than the model had carried during the decades when Section 13(b) supported restitution and disgorgement, and the full account of that redress contraction belongs to the section that follows.

## The Loss of Monetary Relief and What Remains

For much of the period covered by this article, the central fact of Federal Trade Commission enforcement was not a rule and not an administrative order. The central fact was money, and the money moved through a provision that said nothing about money at all. Section 13(b) of the Federal Trade Commission Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. 53(b), entered the statute in 1973 as a grant of access to federal court. The Commission received authority to seek a temporary restraining order or a preliminary injunction against conduct violating a law the Commission enforced, and, in proper cases, to seek a permanent injunction after proper proof. Administrative proceedings under Section 5 moved through complaint, hearing, and Commission decision before an order became final, and judicial review could prolong the sequence. Section 13(b) answered that problem with speed and with a court. A judge could halt conduct at the start of a case and bar the conduct permanently if the Commission proved a violation.

The text stopped there. No sentence in Section 13(b) authorized a refund, authorized a court to order a defendant to surrender profits, or set a standard for measuring a monetary award. The provision spoke of injunctions, and an injunction in ordinary legal usage directs a party to do an act or to refrain from doing an act. That narrow grant acquired a larger meaning because federal courts possessed equitable powers, and because earlier Supreme Court precedent had treated a grant of equitable jurisdiction as carrying the traditional remedies of equity unless Congress had restricted them. An order barring a seller from repeating a false claim left the proceeds of past false claims with the seller, and left consumers who had paid in reliance on those claims without repayment from the proceeding that had established the deception.

### How did an injunction provision become a redress programme?

From the early 1980s onward, the courts of appeals supplied an answer that favored the Commission. The courts read the permanent injunction language together with the equitable authority of a federal court in a statutory enforcement case, and treated restitution and disgorgement as relief ancillary to an injunction rather than as a separate remedy requiring a separate grant. Restitution returned money to persons who had parted with money under the unlawful practice. Disgorgement stripped a defendant of gains obtained through the violation. A representative decision was Federal Trade Commission v. U.S. Oil and Gas Corp., 748 F.2d 1431, decided by the Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit in 1984, which accepted monetary relief as part of the complete relief a court could grant in a Section 13(b) action. The Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit reached a comparable conclusion in Federal Trade Commission v. World Travel Vacation Brokers, Inc., 861 F.2d 1020, decided in 1988. Other circuits followed, and a working consensus formed.

The consensus rested on implication. Each decision derived monetary power from equitable tradition and from the purpose of consumer protection, not from words in Section 13(b) naming a monetary remedy. In testimony delivered on April 27, 2021, the Commission stated that seven of the twelve courts of appeals had interpreted Section 13(b) to authorize district courts to award the full range of equitable remedies necessary to provide complete relief for consumers, including disgorgement and restitution. Seven circuits represented a substantial foundation. A foundation built on implication remained exposed to a court prepared to ask what the enacted words authorized and to treat the surrounding statute as the measure of the answer.

For roughly four decades, the Commission built a consumer redress programme on that foundation. Section 13(b) offered advantages that no other provision matched. The Commission could file directly in federal court without completing an administrative proceeding, could ask the court that heard the merits to freeze assets, enjoin the conduct, and order payment in a single action, and could structure settlements around a figure payable for distribution to injured consumers. A respondent negotiating with Commission staff after 1980 negotiated in the shadow of an action that combined an injunction with a demand for the money obtained through the challenged practice. That combination gave the Commission leverage that a bare power to obtain a cease and desist order could not supply, and gave consumers a prospect of repayment without individual suits over losses often too small to justify private litigation costs.

The scale matched the institutional importance, on figures the Commission reported. In testimony delivered on April 27, 2021, the Commission stated that Section 13(b) enforcement had produced 11.2 billion dollars in refunds to consumers during the five years preceding that testimony, a period running from 2016 to 2020, across telemarketing fraud, anticompetitive pharmaceutical practices, data security and privacy, scams directed at older consumers and at veterans, and deceptive business practices. The figures are the Commission's own, presented as evidence in a legislative argument about the worth of the authority. A remedy that returned money counted in the billions over a five year span was not an incidental feature of enforcement, because an injunction against a practice already abandoned repaid nothing and compensated nobody.

### What did the Supreme Court hold in the AMG case?

The case that ended the programme arose from payday lending. The Commission sued Scott Tucker and companies under his control, including AMG Capital Management, LLC, alleging deceptive loan terms in violation of Section 5(a). The district court granted a permanent injunction and directed payment of 1.27 billion dollars in restitution and disgorgement, and the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit affirmed the monetary award on the authority of circuit precedent. The Supreme Court decided AMG Capital Management, LLC v. Federal Trade Commission, 593 U.S. 67 (2021), on April 22, 2021. The decision was unanimous, and Justice Stephen Breyer wrote the opinion of the Court. The holding was direct. Section 13(b) did not authorize the Commission to seek, and did not authorize a court to award, equitable monetary relief such as restitution or disgorgement.

The reasoning proceeded from the words of the provision and from the structure around the provision. A permanent injunction, the Court reasoned, is not the same thing as an award of equitable monetary relief, and the phrase granting the former does not naturally grant the latter. The surrounding language of Section 13(b) pointed toward prospective relief, expressed through formulations keyed to a person who is violating or is about to violate a law the Commission enforces. A provision framed around stopping conduct fitted an injunction governing future behavior, and fitted less comfortably with a retrospective transfer of money earned from completed transactions. Section 5 had, since 1914, channeled enforcement through Commission proceedings culminating in cease and desist orders. A reading of Section 13(b) that allowed the Commission to bypass that channel and obtain money directly in court would have allowed a limited procedural provision to displace the principal enforcement design of the statute.

The decisive structural argument compared Section 13(b) with provisions in which Congress had authorized money expressly and had conditioned the authorization. Section 5(l), codified at 15 U.S.C. 45(l), authorized courts to grant mandatory injunctions and other equitable relief in the enforcement of final Commission orders, and authorized civil penalties for violation of a final order. Section 19, codified at 15 U.S.C. 57b, authorized courts to grant relief necessary to redress injury to consumers, including the refund of money or the return of property, in defined circumstances. Each grant came with a sequence. Section 5(l) presupposed a final order. Section 19 presupposed either a rule violation or a final cease and desist order, and in the order based route a further standard concerning the character of the conduct. The Court considered it highly unlikely that Congress would have enacted provisions expressly authorizing conditioned and limited monetary relief if Section 13(b) had already implicitly allowed the same relief, and more, without satisfying those conditions. The textualist defence of the holding, as the Court stated the defence, was therefore fidelity to enacted language and legislative structure rather than a judgment about the value of redress. Congress had written monetary authority into the statute in specific places and specific terms, and a court had no warrant to write a broader version into a provision that spoke only of injunctions. Nothing in the opinion barred the Commission from using Section 5 or Section 19 to obtain restitution, and the Court observed that the Commission remained free to ask Congress for further remedial authority.

Cracks in the consensus had appeared before April 2021. In 2019, the Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, in Federal Trade Commission v. Credit Bureau Center, LLC, overruled circuit precedent and held that Section 13(b) authorized injunctions alone. In 2020, the Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit rejected disgorgement under Section 13(b). The Supreme Court granted review against that divided background and chose the restrictive line. Cases pending on April 22, 2021, that depended on Section 13(b) for a monetary component lost that component on that date, whatever the merits of the underlying allegations.

The institutional consequence followed within days, described in the Commission's own terms. In a statement issued on April 22, 2021, Acting Chairwoman Rebecca Kelly Slaughter stated that the ruling had deprived the Commission of the strongest tool available to the agency for helping consumers, and urged Congress to restore and strengthen the powers of the agency. On April 27, 2021, the Commission testified before the Subcommittee on Consumer Protection and Commerce of the House Committee on Energy and Commerce, presenting an urgent request for a legislative correction to Section 13(b). The testimony called Section 13(b) the primary and most effective means by which the Commission returned money to consumers, and warned that the Commission would be far less effective in protecting consumers if Congress failed to act promptly. Letters and testimony during the remainder of 2021 carried the same request to committees of both chambers. No replacement authority had been enacted through December 31, 2021.

The request also had a longer institutional context. Congress had created a separate consumer finance agency in 2010, through [the creation and powers of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](/2013/12/01/cfpb-creation-and-powers/), partly in response to gaps and limits in the existing allocation of authority among established regulators, including the Commission, whose jurisdiction over much of the financial sector was partial and whose remedial toolkit depended on provisions of uneven strength.

### Can the FTC get money for consumers after AMG?

After April 22, 2021, the Commission could seek money through Section 19 for a rule violation or after a final administrative order that satisfied a demanding standard, and through civil penalties for rule and order violations, although each route required procedural steps that a direct Section 13(b) action had allowed the Commission to avoid entirely.

Section 19 supplied the closest substitute, and the substitute exposed the distance between the two provisions. Under Section 19(a)(1), codified at 15 U.S.C. 57b(a)(1), the Commission may commence a civil action in federal court against a person that violates a rule respecting unfair or deceptive acts or practices, and a court may grant relief the court finds necessary to redress injury to consumers, including rescission or reformation of contracts, the refund of money or the return of property, and the payment of damages. The route is genuine redress, measured by consumer injury rather than by a penalty schedule, and a rule violation opens the route without a prior administrative case against the particular defendant. The burden lies in the predicate. A redress action of that kind requires a rule, and a rule that supports Section 19 relief requires the rulemaking process described in the preceding sections of this article, with a record, a statement of basis and purpose, and exposure to judicial review. For conduct outside an operative rule, Section 19(a)(1) offered nothing, however clear the deception and however large the loss.

Section 19(a)(2) covered the ground left open by the absence of a rule, at a price measured in time and in proof. Under that provision, the Commission may bring a redress action against a person that has engaged in an unfair or deceptive act or practice with respect to which the Commission has issued a final cease and desist order applicable to that person. The Commission must first litigate an administrative case to conclusion and allow the order to become final, a sequence that restores the delay Section 13(b) had been used to escape. The statute then imposes a standard with no analogue in the former Section 13(b) practice. The Commission must satisfy the court that the act or practice to which the cease and desist order relates is one which a reasonable person would have known under the circumstances was dishonest or fraudulent. Deception that misleads consumers in fact may fail that standard where the conduct rested on an ambiguous representation or a novel practice. The standard filters redress toward flagrant fraud and away from the large middle range of conduct that Section 5 reaches. Between the length of the sequence and the narrowness of the standard, Section 19(a)(2) functioned in 2021 as a remedy for a subset of cases rather than as a replacement programme.

Civil penalties supplied a different kind of money. A penalty is paid to the government as punishment and deterrence, is not calculated as the sum of consumer losses, and does not by itself deliver repayment to consumers who bore the loss. Under Section 5(l), a person that violates a final Commission order after the order has become final is liable to a civil penalty, recoverable in federal court. Under Section 5(m)(1)(A), codified at 15 U.S.C. 45(m)(1)(A), a person that violates a trade regulation rule respecting unfair or deceptive acts or practices, with actual knowledge or knowledge fairly implied on the basis of objective circumstances that the act or practice is unfair or deceptive and is prohibited by the rule, is liable to a civil penalty. Both routes require an antecedent act, either a final order directed at the respondent or a rule of general application, before money becomes available, and each requires a federal court action to collect, because the Commission possesses no general authority to impose a monetary sanction by administrative order in a consumer protection case. Penalty amounts are set by statute and adjusted for inflation by regulation, so the amount in a given year depends on the adjustment in force for that year. A per violation penalty can exert substantial leverage in a mass marketing case, where each sale may constitute a separate violation. Leverage, however, is not redress, and a penalty punished a violation without necessarily restoring the consumers who paid.

The Penalty Offense Authority offered a third route, narrower than a rule in one respect and broader than an order in another. Section 5(m)(1)(B), codified at 15 U.S.C. 45(m)(1)(B), authorizes a civil penalty against a person that engages in an act or practice the Commission has determined to be unfair or deceptive in a litigated Commission proceeding, where the person had actual knowledge that the act or practice was unfair or deceptive and prohibited by the Federal Trade Commission Act. The determination must rest on a prior Commission decision other than a consent order, which is the feature that gives the authority reach. A firm that was never a respondent in the original case can face penalty exposure for conduct matching the conduct condemned in that case, provided the knowledge element is established. The authority had lain largely dormant for decades before 2021, because Section 13(b) supplied a more direct path to a larger remedy, and because building a penalty offense case required the Commission to assemble a chain of old litigated determinations, to give fair notice to firms engaged in comparable conduct, and to prove knowledge in federal court.

The revival came in October 2021. In early October 2021, the Commission sent a Notice of Penalty Offenses to more than 70 for profit colleges, identifying practices concerning graduate employment and earnings prospects found unfair or deceptive in earlier litigated proceedings. On October 13, 2021, the Commission sent a Notice of Penalty Offenses to more than 700 companies, including advertisers, retailers, and consumer product companies, concerning endorsements and testimonials, including false claims of endorsement, misrepresentation of an endorser as an actual or recent user, and failure to disclose an unexpected material connection. On October 26, 2021, the Commission sent a further notice to more than 1,100 businesses that pitched money making ventures, including multilevel marketing companies, coaching operations, and gig economy platforms, concerning false or misleading claims about potential earnings. That notice warned of civil penalties of up to 43,792 dollars per violation, the maximum stated by the Commission for that period. The Commission stated that inclusion on a recipient list did not suggest that a recipient had engaged in deceptive conduct. The notices served a legal function, supplying evidence from which actual knowledge could be established in a later penalty action.

Each element of that mechanism carried a burden. The Commission had to locate litigated determinations, many decades old, that condemned conduct in terms specific enough to match a later practice. Consent orders, which resolve the large majority of Commission administrative matters, could not serve as the determination, because the statute excludes consent arrangements from the predicate. The Commission had to prove that a defendant engaged in the same kind of conduct, rather than conduct merely deceptive in a general sense, and had to prove actual knowledge, an element that the October 2021 mailings were designed to support for listed recipients and practices. A penalty punished the conduct without recreating the distribution machinery that Section 13(b) settlements and judgments had supported for return of money to identified consumers.

The structural point is a point about sequence. Before April 22, 2021, a procedural provision adopted in 1973 to obtain injunctions had, through equitable interpretation, produced an enforcement model in which the Commission selected and settled cases for the money that a court could order returned or a respondent agreed to pay. The model depended on a reading that the Supreme Court rejected on that date, on textual and structural grounds presented as compelled by the design Congress had enacted. After that date, the money tools that remained ran through instruments the Commission largely could not make quickly or at scale. Redress under Section 19 required a rule produced through the extended rulemaking process described in the preceding sections of this article, or a final order produced through extended adjudication, followed in the order based route by proof that a reasonable person would have known the conduct was dishonest or fraudulent. Penalties under Section 5(l) and Section 5(m)(1)(A) required a final order or a rule. Penalties under the Penalty Offense Authority required a litigated determination from an earlier era and proof of actual knowledge. Every path to money began with a step the Commission had historically completed in a small number of matters each year, relative to the volume of deception in the markets the Commission policed. Procedure had produced the enforcement model between the early 1980s and 2021, because a procedure for injunctions had been read to carry a remedy. Procedure confined the model after April 22, 2021, because the remaining procedures for rules and orders set the ceiling on the money the agency could reach.

The instruments described across the preceding sections differ less in the conduct they reach than in the sequence each instrument demands before money, penalties, or binding obligations become available. | Consumer redress | Section 19, 15 U.S.C. 57b | Refunds, rescission, damages and other relief a court finds necessary | A rule violation, or a final order plus proof the conduct was dishonest or fraudulent | The principal surviving route to money for consumers |
| Civil penalties for rule and order violations | Sections 5(l) and 5(m)(1)(A), 15 U.S.C. 45(l) and 45(m)(1)(A) | Per violation penalties set by statute and adjusted for inflation | Federal court penalty action | Unchanged |
| Penalty offense authority | Section 5(m)(1)(B), 15 U.S.C. 45(m)(1)(B) | Civil penalties against a firm acting with actual knowledge that conduct was found unlawful in a prior litigated proceeding | A prior litigated Commission determination plus formal notice to the firm | Dormant for decades and revived by notices sent in October 2021 |
| Policy statements and guidance | Section 5 interpreted through agency statements | No independent legal obligation | Internal adoption without rulemaking procedure | Unchanged, and no substitute for a rule |

## The Enforcement Substitute for FTC Rulemaking

The history assembled in the preceding sections describes a single pattern repeated under changing conditions. Congress granted authority in the broadest available form, first as an open standard against unfair methods of competition in 1914, then as a prohibition of unfair or deceptive acts or practices after the Wheeler-Lea Act of 1938, and then as express rulemaking power under Section 18 in 1975. Each grant promised regulation of general application. Each grant delivered regulation of a narrower kind, because the instrument attached to the grant determined the output more reliably than the grant itself. Adjudication produced orders against named respondents. Policy statements produced tests that governed practice without binding any court. Rulemaking produced a small number of durable rules at a cost measured in years per rule. The gap between statutory authority and delivered regulation, the central thread of the series, arose in the Commission record from procedure rather than from substance, since disagreement about whether deception or unfairness existed rarely decided whether a rule emerged. The prevalence finding, the hearing, the cross examination entitlement, and substantial evidence review decided that question before the merits were reached.

The enforcement substitute filled the gap with instruments that traded generality for speed. A consent order could be negotiated in months, could impose detailed operational duties on the signing firm, and could carry fencing-in relief beyond the charged practice, yet the order bound no competitor and explained no reasoning that a later firm could test. A litigated order supplied reasoning and precedent, at the price of a Part 3 proceeding and appellate review. Section 13(b) supplied the missing element of money, and the scale of that supply explains why the loss of monetary relief in 2021 altered the entire model rather than one remedy within the model. A programme that returned 11.2 billion dollars in refunds between 2016 and 2020 rested on an implied remedy inside an injunction provision. When AMG Capital Management, LLC v. FTC, 593 U.S. 67, rejected that implication unanimously on April 22, 2021, the remaining routes to money all began with a rule or a final order, the two artifacts the Commission produced most slowly. The October 2021 penalty offense notices showed an agency rebuilding leverage from litigated determinations assembled decades earlier.

Procedure therefore explains both halves of the record. Hybrid rulemaking made rules rare, and rare rules made Section 19 redress rare in rule based cases, forced redress in other cases through a final order and proof of dishonest or fraudulent conduct, and pushed penalties toward predicates that enforcement had seldom generated in advance. The 1980 restrictions compounded the effect by removing unfairness as a basis for advertising rules in the subjects that had provoked the backlash and by teaching later commissioners that institutional survival belonged in the calculation beside legal merit. None of that history shows an agency indifferent to consumer injury. The record shows an agency whose choices were sorted, at every stage, by the procedural price of the instrument selected, so that the law consumers received was the law that complaints, settlements, guidance, and occasional rules could produce within appropriations and within judicial review.

For students and practitioners, the practical discipline follows from that sorting. A Section 5 problem begins with classification of the instrument in hand, since a rule, an order, a policy statement, and a penalty offense notice carry different obligations, different defenses, and different routes to money, and confusion among those instruments produces errors that no command of the deception or unfairness tests can repair. Careful readers [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) because the sequence from standard to statement to order to decision controls the meaning of each document in the chain. Systematic review also matters, and candidates who [practice and revise US government and civics material on ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/us-government-civics-study.html) build the institutional context, congressional procedure, appropriations conflict, and judicial review, within which a consumer protection rule either survives or fails before any court reaches the underlying practice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does the FTC define unfair or deceptive practices?

The Federal Trade Commission defines deception through the Policy Statement on Deception of October 14, 1983, under which a representation, omission, or practice is deceptive when the practice is likely to mislead a consumer acting reasonably under the circumstances and the misleading aspect is material to a consumer decision. The Commission defines unfairness through the Policy Statement on Unfairness of December 17, 1980, under which a practice is unfair when the practice causes or is likely to cause substantial injury that consumers cannot reasonably avoid and that countervailing benefits to consumers or to competition do not outweigh. Congress codified the unfairness test at 15 U.S.C. 45(n) in 1994.

### Q: Why is FTC rulemaking so slow?

Rulemaking under Section 18 of the Federal Trade Commission Act is slow because Congress required steps that ordinary notice and comment rulemaking omits. The Commission must publish an advance notice of proposed rulemaking, find that the practices addressed are prevalent, state the proposed text with particularity, receive written comments, hold an informal hearing, allow cross examination and rebuttal on disputed issues of material fact, and issue a statement of basis and purpose addressing prevalence, manner and context, and economic effect. A reviewing court then tests the rule for substantial evidence in the closed rulemaking record. Each stage adds time and creates material that later stages must digest and defend.

### Q: What happened to the FTC in 1980?

In 1980 the Federal Trade Commission faced a funding lapse during a congressional fight over limits on agency authority, and the Commission closed on May 1, 1980, after Attorney General Benjamin Civiletti advised that the Antideficiency Act required agencies to stop operations during a lapse. About one thousand six hundred employees were furloughed for one day before Congress extended funding. On May 28, 1980, President Carter signed the Federal Trade Commission Improvements Act of 1980, Public Law 96-252, which barred a rule in the children's advertising proceeding on an unfairness basis, restricted unfairness based advertising rulemaking, and added procedural burdens including a legislative veto and regulatory analyses.

### Q: Can the FTC get money back for consumers?

After April 22, 2021, the Commission can obtain money for consumers principally through Section 19 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. 57b. A court may grant refunds, rescission, and damages when a firm violates a trade regulation rule. A court may also grant redress after a final cease and desist order, provided the Commission proves that a reasonable person would have known the conduct was dishonest or fraudulent. Civil penalties under Sections 5(l) and 5(m) punish violations and are paid to the government rather than distributed as refunds. Section 13(b) no longer supplies monetary relief after the AMG Capital decision.

### Q: What did AMG Capital decide about the FTC?

In AMG Capital Management, LLC v. FTC, 593 U.S. 67, decided unanimously on April 22, 2021, the Supreme Court held that Section 13(b) of the Federal Trade Commission Act did not authorize the Commission to seek, and did not authorize a court to award, equitable monetary relief such as restitution or disgorgement. Justice Stephen Breyer wrote that a permanent injunction is not the same as an award of money, and that Congress had authorized monetary relief expressly and conditionally in Sections 5(l) and 19. The decision ended a practice under which Section 13(b) actions had returned 11.2 billion dollars in refunds between 2016 and 2020, on the Commission figure.

### Q: Is there a private right to sue under the FTC Act?

No private right of action exists under Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act. Federal courts refused to imply a private claim, with Holloway v. Bristol-Myers, decided by the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit in 1973, among the authorities most often cited for that refusal. Enforcement of the federal standard belongs to the Commission, which decides whether a matter merits a complaint. Consumers seeking a private remedy generally proceed under state consumer protection statutes, often called little FTC Acts, many of which authorize private damage actions, attorney fees, and multiple damages that the federal statute withholds.

### Q: What is the FTC unfairness standard in statute?

The statutory unfairness standard appears at 15 U.S.C. 45(n), added by Section 9 of the Federal Trade Commission Act Amendments of 1994, Public Law 103-312. The subsection provides that the Commission has no authority under Section 5, or under the rulemaking provision at 15 U.S.C. 57a, to declare an act or practice unlawful on unfairness grounds unless the act or practice causes or is likely to cause substantial injury to consumers, the injury is not reasonably avoidable by consumers themselves, and the injury is not outweighed by countervailing benefits to consumers or to competition. Established public policy may serve as evidence, but public policy may not serve as a primary basis for a determination.

### Q: What is the FTC penalty offense authority?

The penalty offense authority appears at Section 5(m)(1)(B) of the Federal Trade Commission Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. 45(m)(1)(B). The provision authorizes a civil penalty against a firm that engages in an act or practice the Commission determined to be unfair or deceptive in a prior litigated proceeding, other than a consent order, when the firm acted with actual knowledge that the conduct was unlawful. The Commission revived the dormant authority in October 2021, sending notices on endorsements and testimonials to more than 700 companies on October 13 and on money making claims to more than 1,100 businesses on October 26.

### Q: What is a trade regulation rule?

A trade regulation rule is a rule the Federal Trade Commission prescribes under Section 18 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. 57a, defining with specificity acts or practices that are unfair or deceptive under Section 5. A rule applies across an industry from the effective date, announces obligations before enforcement begins, and supports civil penalties against a violator who acted with actual knowledge that the conduct was prohibited. The Funeral Industry Practices Rule, effective April 30, 1984, and the Used Motor Vehicle Trade Regulation Rule, promulgated on November 19, 1984, are the best known completed examples from the early period of Section 18.

### Q: What steps must the FTC complete before a trade regulation rule takes effect?

Before a trade regulation rule takes effect, the Commission must publish an advance notice of proposed rulemaking in the Federal Register, submit that notice to the designated Senate and House committees, determine that the practices addressed are prevalent, publish a notice of proposed rulemaking stating the text with particularity thirty days after submission to the committees, receive written submissions, hold an informal hearing with cross examination on disputed issues of material fact where required, and promulgate the final rule on the basis of the rulemaking record with a statement of basis and purpose. Judicial review may follow within sixty days of promulgation.

### Q: How does an FTC consent order differ from a rule?

A consent order differs from a rule in scope, in legal status, and in the process that produces each document. A consent order binds only the company or person who signs the order, settles allegations without adjudicated findings, and functions as a tailored set of obligations for that respondent, often including fencing-in relief and compliance reporting. A trade regulation rule binds every firm within the terms of the rule, follows the Section 18 hybrid rulemaking procedure, and supports civil penalties against any violator with the required knowledge. Other firms may read a consent order as a warning about enforcement priorities, but a warning creates no obligation enforceable against a nonsignatory.

### Q: What is Section 13(b) of the FTC Act?

Section 13(b) of the Federal Trade Commission Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. 53(b), authorizes the Commission to proceed directly in federal district court and to seek a temporary restraining order, a preliminary injunction, and, in proper cases, a permanent injunction against a person violating or about to violate a law the Commission enforces. Courts of appeals from the early 1980s onward read the provision, together with equitable powers, to allow restitution and disgorgement. The Supreme Court rejected that reading in AMG Capital Management, LLC v. FTC on April 22, 2021, leaving Section 13(b) as an injunction provision without monetary relief.

### Q: How does Section 19 let the FTC obtain consumer redress?

Section 19 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. 57b, authorizes the Commission to bring a civil action in federal court for relief a court finds necessary to redress consumer injury, including rescission or reformation of contracts, refund of money or return of property, and payment of damages. Section 19(a)(1) applies when a firm violates a trade regulation rule. Section 19(a)(2) applies after a final cease and desist order against that firm, and the Commission must also satisfy the court that a reasonable person would have known under the circumstances that the conduct was dishonest or fraudulent.

### Q: Are FTC policy statements legally binding?

Policy statements of the Federal Trade Commission are not legally binding in the manner of a trade regulation rule. The deception statement of 1983 and the unfairness statement of 1980 announced how the Commission intended to exercise discretion under Section 5, without notice and comment rulemaking and without promulgation under Section 18. Neither statement binds a court, a respondent, or the Commission itself in a formal sense. Both statements govern practice in fact, because staff screen cases against the stated elements and respondents structure defenses around those elements. Congress later codified the unfairness test at 15 U.S.C. 45(n) in 1994, which gave that test statutory force.

### Q: What penalties can the FTC seek for violating a trade regulation rule?

For a violation of a trade regulation rule, the Commission may seek civil penalties in federal court under Section 5(m)(1)(A), codified at 15 U.S.C. 45(m)(1)(A), against a person who violated the rule with actual knowledge or knowledge fairly implied on the basis of objective circumstances that the act or practice was unfair or deceptive and prohibited by the rule. Penalty amounts are set by statute and adjusted for inflation by regulation, and each violation may count separately, which creates substantial exposure in a mass marketing case. Penalties are paid to the government as punishment and deterrence and do not by themselves refund consumers.

### Q: Why did Congress write a broad standard instead of listing prohibited practices?

Congress wrote a broad standard in 1914 because enumeration presented practical limits that the legislative debates identified directly. Trade practices changed with technology, distribution methods, and firm organization, so a catalogue drafted against the abuses of one decade risked obsolescence in the next. A list also invited evasion, since a seller constrained by named practices retained every practice omitted from the list and counsel could argue that omission signaled permission. Congress therefore chose a general prohibition administered by a specialized commission that would develop meaning through inclusion and exclusion in administrative and judicial decisions, accepting reduced advance notice as the price of coverage across changing markets.

### Q: What was the children's advertising proceeding?

The children's advertising proceeding, known as KidVid, began with petitions filed in 1977 and a notice of proposed rulemaking published on April 27, 1978. Commission staff proposed comment on banning television advertising directed at children under eight, banning advertisements for highly sugared products for children between eight and twelve because of tooth decay risks, and requiring balancing health disclosures for other sugared products. The theory rested on unfairness, on the ground that young children could not understand selling intent. The Washington Post called the agency a national nanny on March 1, 1978. Congress barred an unfairness based rule in the proceeding in Public Law 96-252 in 1980, and the Commission terminated the rulemaking in 1981.

### Q: How does the FTC build a case through administrative adjudication?

Administrative adjudication at the Federal Trade Commission proceeds under Part 3 of the Commission Rules of Practice. The Commission issues a complaint, complaint counsel prosecutes the matter, and an administrative law judge receives evidence and issues an initial decision with findings and a proposed order. Either side may appeal to the full Commission, which issues a decision and order. A respondent subject to a cease and desist order may seek review in a federal court of appeals. The Commission combines functions in that design, authorizing the complaint, supplying the prosecutors, and deciding the appeal, subject to judicial review at the instance of a respondent.

### Q: What must the FTC prove before a court awards redress after an administrative order?

Before a court awards redress after an administrative order, the Commission must proceed under Section 19(a)(2) of the Federal Trade Commission Act and satisfy several predicates in sequence. The Commission must first obtain a final cease and desist order applicable to that person for the act or practice in question, through a completed administrative proceeding. The Commission must then bring a civil action in federal court and satisfy the court that the act or practice is one which a reasonable person would have known under the circumstances was dishonest or fraudulent. Only after those predicates may a court grant refunds, rescission, damages, or other relief necessary to redress consumer injury.

### Q: Can the FTC police data security under Section 5?

The Commission has policed data security under Section 5 on theories of deception, where a privacy or security promise misled consumers, and unfairness, where inadequate security caused or was likely to cause substantial injury that consumers could not reasonably avoid. Courts tested that enforcement in litigation rather than in a data security rule. In FTC v. Wyndham Worldwide Corporation, decided by the Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit in 2015, the court sustained Commission authority over the data security practices of a hotel company after breaches. The LabMD litigation in the Eleventh Circuit produced a narrower outcome on the enforceability of a specific order, which shows that authority and remedy receive separate judicial treatment.
