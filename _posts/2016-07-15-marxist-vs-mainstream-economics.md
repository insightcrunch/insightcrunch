A student who has finished an introductory economics course and then opens a work of Marxist economics often reports a strange experience: the second book does not read like a rival answer to the first, but like a book about a different subject that happens to share some vocabulary. Prices appear in both, and so do wages, profits, markets, and crises, yet the arguments built around those words seem to pass one another without meeting. That experience is the most reliable starting point for the comparison, because it records something true. The two traditions are not, in the first instance, giving competing answers to a shared list of questions. They disagree about which questions economics exists to answer, and nearly everything else in the quarrel between them is downstream of that prior disagreement. A reader who grasps this at the outset will find the rest of the comparison falls into place; a reader who misses it will keep scoring the two sides against tests only one of them sat.

Consider a single concrete case before any theory is named. A garment business pays its machinists a weekly wage and sells the finished clothing at a price that leaves a margin after cloth, rent, machine wear, and interest have been covered. Ask what that margin is, and the two traditions hear two different questions. One hears a question about the return earned by the funds tied up in the business, a return that competition among capitals tends to equalise across trades and that can be compared with what the same funds would earn elsewhere. The other hears a question about the working day: how much of it replaces the value of the wage, and how much is performed beyond that point without payment. Neither hearing is a mistake about the facts of the business. Both accountants would agree on the ledger. They differ on what in the ledger calls for explanation, and that difference, multiplied across every topic in economics, is the subject of this article.

The canonical statement of the difference can be made in one sentence each way. Mainstream economics, in the definition its textbooks inherit from Lionel Robbins, studies the allocation of scarce means among competing uses under conditions of choice: given ends, given resources, and given techniques, it asks how a society's decisions hang together and what they cost. Marxist economics studies capitalism as a historically specific system of production, distribution, and accumulation, a system that arose at a definite time, works by definite mechanisms, and generates definite contradictions as it develops. The first definition is deliberately timeless: scarcity and choice confront a Crusoe, a household, a firm, and a planned economy alike. The second is deliberately historical: its object would not exist in a society without generalised commodity production and wage labour, and the theory presents itself as the anatomy of that one social form rather than as a logic of choice in general.

This article compares the two traditions under a discipline the brief for it calls the domain verdict: neither tradition is generally superior, and the useful comparison is domain by domain, asking of each analytical territory which framework has asked the better questions there and which carries the stronger record. The comparison proceeds by naming the different objects of study, stating each side's treatment of profit, history, equilibrium, money, and class, and then grading the record domain by domain in a findable table before examining the two most sensitive territories in the dispute: why Marxist economics stands at the margin of the profession, and why mainstream research has, in a handful of areas, arrived independently at conclusions Marxist economists reached much earlier. The whole field of Marxist economics, its schools and its internal quarrels, is mapped in the series article on the [marxist economics complete guide](/2016/04/01/marxist-economics-complete-guide/), and what follows assumes that map rather than redrawing it.

## Two objects of study: what Marxist economics is about

The definitional difference deserves slow treatment, because it is easy to state and hard to hold onto. When Robbins defined economics as the science that studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses, he gave the mainstream its charter. The definition makes no reference to capitalism, to markets, or even to money. Its primitive terms are ends, means, and choice, and its exemplary problem is the economising individual, from whom the theory builds outward to firms, markets, and whole economies by aggregation and analogy. Questions that cannot be posed in that grammar, questions about where the ends come from, about how the means came to be distributed as they are, or about what kind of society makes choice of this form universal, fall outside the definition's jurisdiction. They are not answered wrongly; they are not admitted as economics. Much of what looks from outside like mainstream evasion is simply this charter being honoured.

The Marxist charter runs the other way. Its primitive terms are not ends and means but production relations: the social forms under which people gain access to the means of production, appropriate the product, and reproduce the arrangement from one period to the next. Where the mainstream begins from scarcity, which it treats as a condition of human existence as such, the Marxist tradition begins from a historically specific answer to scarcity, the capitalist answer, in which production is carried on by formally free workers who own no means of production, for owners who advance money in order to withdraw more money. Scarcity in the abstract interests this tradition very little. What interests it is why scarcity takes the social form of unemployment beside idle plant, of want beside unsold goods, of overwork beside enforced idleness; riddles that a logic of choice can describe but that a theory of the system's own dynamics claims to explain.

### What does the mainstream mean by scarcity?

Scarcity, in the mainstream definition, is the permanent gap between human wants and the means available to satisfy them, and it is asserted of every society rather than of capitalism alone. Because the gap never closes, choice is unavoidable, and economics becomes the study of how choices are and ought to be made under that standing condition.

The contrast sharpens when each side is asked what its theory is a theory of. The mainstream answer is that it is a theory of economising behaviour, valid wherever economising occurs, with capitalist markets as its most developed field of application rather than its defining case. The Marxist answer is that it is a theory of one mode of production, with other modes, feudal, ancient, or planned, handled by extension and analogy rather than by the core machinery. This is why the two sides so often seem to change the subject when they argue. A mainstream criticism of Marxist economics typically shows that some Marxist proposition fails as a proposition about allocation; a Marxist criticism of the mainstream typically shows that some mainstream proposition, granted entire, leaves the system's reproduction and development unexplained. Each criticism is accurate, and each is aimed at a target the other side does not regard as its home ground. The domain verdict later in this article is built to make that structure explicit instead of letting it operate in disguise.

There is a second, quieter consequence of the definitional divide. Because the mainstream object is choice, its natural unit is the decision and its natural method is to model decisions and aggregate them. Because the Marxist object is a system that reproduces itself, its natural unit is the circuit, the rotation of money into commodities and back into more money, and its natural method is to trace how the parts of the circuit condition one another. Methods that look like mere technical preferences, optimisation on one side and reproduction schemas on the other, are in fact the definitions working themselves out. A student who sees this will stop asking why Marxist economists do not simply use the mainstream's models, or why mainstream economists do not simply add class to theirs, as though methods were neutral tools lying in a shared box. Some tools do cross the border, and a later section examines which ones and at what price. But the boxes themselves were built for different contents.

## The mainstream programme on its own terms

Any comparison that treats the mainstream as a foil fails before it starts, so its programme deserves a fair statement. The mainstream of economics, meaning the broad neoclassical tradition and its Keynesian and post-Keynesian internal critics insofar as they share the professional apparatus, is a research programme with a definite core: individuals and firms respond to incentives; prices carry information and coordinate decisions made independently by millions of agents; systems of such decisions can be studied as equilibria whose properties can be derived; and claims about all of this can be disciplined by measurement. That programme has real achievements to its name, and the Marxist tradition's own serious writers have never denied them. It produced the analysis of how a price system rations without a rationer, the theory of external costs and their remedies, the statistical machinery for separating the effect of one variable from the noise of the rest, and a body of applied work on particular markets, taxes, auctions, and regulations that no rival tradition matches for precision.

The programme's strength is inseparable from its discipline about questions. It insists that a claim be stated so that the evidence could in principle embarrass it, and it has built journals, hiring standards, and training around that insistence. Whatever one thinks of the questions it admits, the answers it gives within its jurisdiction are frequently excellent, and some of its findings, on the incidence of taxes, on the behaviour of auction bidders, on the response of labour supply to benefit rules, are as secure as anything in the social sciences. A comparison that cannot say this plainly has no standing to criticise the programme elsewhere. The checkpoints governing this article require the mainstream to be presented as a serious research programme with major achievements, and the requirement is not a courtesy. It is a condition of the criticism meaning anything.

### Why do mainstream economists trust the price mechanism?

Because a price, in their account, compresses into a single number the information that millions of separate decisions require: what is scarce, what is wanted, what a thing costs to replace. No central office could gather that knowledge in usable form, so the mechanism that transmits it impersonally, through buying and selling, carries the programme's confidence.

The mainstream is also not the monolith of caricature. It contains Keynesians who give effective demand and fundamental uncertainty central places that sit awkwardly with the equilibrium charter; behavioural researchers who have dismantled, from inside, the assumption of the perfectly calculating agent; institutionalists in the mainstream's own journals studying how rules and organisations shape outcomes; and a large empirical wing whose members describe their work as letting the data speak and hold the canonical theory lightly. These internal movements matter for this comparison in two ways. They show that the mainstream can absorb ideas that began outside it, including ideas with heterodox pedigrees, when those ideas arrive in its own methodological dress. And they complicate any simple story in which the mainstream's boundaries are policed by content alone. The boundary is policed at least as much by form: by whether a claim is modelled, measured, and published in the recognised sequence. That observation returns when the article takes up marginality, because it suggests that what excludes Marxist economics from the profession is a compound of its questions and its customary forms, not either one by itself.

A final point of fairness concerns what the mainstream says about itself at its best. Its leading methodologists do not claim that the allocation definition captures everything worth knowing about economic life. They claim something narrower and stronger: that within the territory the definition marks out, the programme's questions are the ones that admit of cumulative, corrigible answers, and that a discipline is entitled to choose such a territory. The Marxist objection, to be worth anything, has to meet that claim rather than a sillier one. It does so by contesting the border itself: by arguing that some of the most consequential facts about capitalist economies, the distribution of income between classes, the recurrence of crisis, the direction of technical change, lie exactly where the charter says economics ends, and that a discipline which cannot address them has mistaken a workable fence for the edge of the world.

## Profit: unpaid labour or a factor's reward

No single topic displays the difference of objects more cleanly than profit. In the mainstream account, profit is a return: to capital as a factor of production, to the bearing of risk, to waiting, to enterprise, depending on which strand of the tradition is speaking. Its theoretical problem is to explain why that return takes the level it does and how it is connected to capital's contribution to output. The marginal productivity strand treats the return on capital as governed by what an additional unit of capital adds to production, so that distribution is settled inside the same analysis that settles prices, one price among others. Profit, so conceived, is not a residue left over after the real business of the economy is done. It is a price, doing allocative work, signalling where capital is scarce and drawing resources toward their most valued uses.

In the Marxist account, profit is a transformed form of surplus value, and its origin lies in production rather than in exchange. The worker sells not labour but labour power, the capacity to work, and is paid its value, the labour time required to produce the worker's means of subsistence. The working day then divides into a portion that replaces that value and a portion beyond it, in which the worker produces value for which no equivalent is paid. Profit, interest, and rent are shares of that unpaid portion, distributed among the fractions of the owning class by competition and by the institutions of credit and landed property. The point of the construction is not moral arithmetic. It is the claim that the distribution of income between classes is settled by the relations of production themselves, before any market in factors prices anything, so that distribution is a fact about the system's structure and not a by-product of technology and preferences.

### Where does profit come from in each account?

The mainstream locates profit among the prices: it is the return that capital, risk-bearing, or enterprise commands in a system where every contribution to production is rewarded according to its scarcity and usefulness. The Marxist account locates profit in the working day, as the monetary form of labour performed beyond the portion that replaces the wage.

The contrast is sometimes softened by noting that both sides can describe the same firm and the same accounts, and so they can. What they cannot share is the explanation of why there is a surplus to divide at all, and why it accrues to the owners of capital rather than to the performers of the labour. For the mainstream, the surplus over costs is explained by the productivity of the whole arrangement and is divided by the same market process that divides everything else; asking why capital's owners receive it is, within the charter, a question about property law and inheritance, which is to say a question for another discipline. For the Marxist tradition, that division is the central economic fact, the one from which class, accumulation, and crisis all follow, and a theory that treats it as belonging to another discipline has defined its object so as to exclude the main event. Here as everywhere in this comparison, the disagreement is not about an answer. It is about which phenomenon is the explanandum.

The value theory that underlies the Marxist account, and the marginalist alternative it displaced in the nineteenth century, have their own comparison in this series, and the present article deliberately leaves that ground to it: the article on [marx vs marginalist theory of value](/2010/09/15/marx-vs-marginalist-theory-of-value/) works through the two doctrines of value in the detail they require. What matters here is only the consequence for profit. A theory in which value is conferred by labour performed in production will treat profit as a distributional magnitude with a production origin; a theory in which value is conferred by valuation at the margin will treat profit as one price in a simultaneous system. The domain verdict does not require the reader to settle the value dispute first. It requires noticing that the dispute exists upstream, and that many downstream disagreements are its shadows.

## History inside the theory and history outside it

Ask each tradition what role history plays in its work and the definitional divide returns in a new costume. For the mainstream, the theoretical core is deliberately ahistorical. The logic of choice does not date; supply and demand in a medieval grain market and in a market for financial derivatives are, for the theory, the same diagram wearing different clothes. History enters as data, as the institutional scenery against which the timeless logic operates, and as a source of natural experiments by which the logic can be tested. Economic historians inside the mainstream win their standing by using the past this way, as a laboratory of instances, rather than by proposing that the logic itself has a past. When a mainstream economist says that an institution matters, the claim is that it shifts constraints or information within the model; the model itself stands outside time.

For the Marxist tradition, history is not the scenery but the subject. The categories themselves are held to be historical: value, capital, and wage labour are not eternal forms of human dealing but the specific forms of one epoch, absent or marginal in others, and the theory's first task is to show how those forms arose, what they presuppose, and what tendencies they carry within themselves. Accumulation is a historical process with a direction; the concentration of capital, the growth of the socialised labour process, and the periodic restructuring of industries are not disturbances of the theory's object but the object in motion. A mainstream reader often finds this maddening, because propositions keep arriving with dates attached, valid for a phase rather than for all time. A Marxist reader finds the opposite procedure maddening in return: theorems of unlimited validity purchased by emptying the categories of everything that makes capitalism capitalism.

### Why does history play different roles in the two traditions?

Because the objects differ. A theory of choice under scarcity claims a subject matter that every epoch exhibits, so its history can only be the record of applications. A theory of one mode of production claims a subject matter that begins and develops in time, so history is built into its categories themselves.

The practical consequence appears in how each side handles change. Mainstream analysis handles change as comparative statics or as growth along a path: an external impulse, a new technology, a policy, a shift in tastes, displaces the system, which then settles at a new position whose properties the theory derives. The impulse itself, the shock, comes from outside the model, unexplained by design. Marxist analysis handles change as the system's own product: competition forces each capital to cheapen its commodities, cheapening revolutionises methods, revolutionised methods redistribute income and employment, and the redistribution prepares the next round. Neither procedure is a blunder. They are answers to different ambitions. One wants to know what follows from a disturbance, given the structure; the other wants to know how the structure generates its own disturbances. The domain verdict will score technical change and crisis largely on which ambition the evidence in those territories rewards.

There is also a difference in what each side counts as an explanation of an institution. The mainstream's characteristic move is functional: an institution persists because it economises on information or transaction costs, and its origin can be told as a story of agents finding it advantageous. The Marxist characteristic move is genetic and conflictual: an institution persists because it serves the reproduction of the relations in place and is defended by those whom it serves, and its origin is a settlement of past struggles whose outcome was not foreordained. Both kinds of story can be told about the same factory system or the same labour law, and a good historian will often need both. But the theories differ in which story is the deep one, and the difference is not resolvable by accumulating instances, because it concerns what an explanation is.

## Equilibrium as benchmark and as centre of gravity

Few words cause more cross-purpose in this dispute than equilibrium. In the mainstream's usage, equilibrium is primarily an analytical benchmark: a configuration in which the plans of all agents are mutually consistent, so that no one who understands the situation wishes to revise what they are doing. The concept's work is to discipline theorising. A model that can state its equilibrium can be solved, compared, and taken to data; movements away from equilibrium are then studied as dynamics, often with the expectation that the system's resting points organise its behaviour. When mainstream economists are careful, and the careful ones are explicit about this, equilibrium is not a claim that actual economies sit still. It is a claim that the theorist needs a well-defined state against which to define motion, as a surveyor needs a benchmark that nobody supposes the landscape to resemble.

The Marxist tradition inherits from classical political economy a different device: the centre of gravity. Prices of production, around which market prices oscillate, and the general rate of profit, toward which the profit rates of particular trades are driven by the migration of capital, are centres of gravitation in this sense. They are asserted to be real tendencies of the system, operating through the chaos of daily transactions, not consistency conditions for a model. Equilibrium in the mainstream's strong sense, a state in which the conditions of reproduction are smoothly satisfied all round, appears in Marxist writing mainly as a special case whose conditions the theory sets out precisely in order to show how fragile they are: the reproduction schemas of the second volume of Capital derive what would have to hold for the system to replace its inputs and sell its outputs without interruption, and the lesson drawn is that nothing in the system's ordinary operation guarantees that those proportions will be met.

### What is equilibrium supposed to do in each framework?

For the mainstream it is a benchmark of consistency: the state in which plans mesh, defined so that models can be solved and departures from it measured. For the Marxist tradition the operative idea is a centre of gravity: an average around which actual magnitudes oscillate, asserted as a tendency of the system rather than as its resting place.

The two devices answer to the two objects of study once more. A theory of choice needs configurations in which choices are coherent, because incoherent choice is not a state its grammar can process. A theory of a self-reproducing system needs tendencies that operate through incoherence, because the system's normal condition includes glut, shortage, and disproportion as recurring features rather than as pathologies. It follows that the familiar mainstream taunt, that Marxist economics lacks a rigorous theory of equilibrium, and the familiar Marxist taunt, that mainstream economics assumes away the phenomena, are both true and both beside the point. Each side has the device its object requires. The genuinely comparative question is empirical: in which domains do actual economies behave more like systems gravitating around centres they never occupy, and in which do they behave more like systems for which the benchmark of coherent plans is the illuminating fiction? That is a domain question, and it is scored domain by domain below.

A related misunderstanding concerns stability. Mainstream theory, at its most honest, proved long ago that its general equilibrium need not be stable: the existence of a consistent configuration does not entail that an economy starting elsewhere will find its way there, and the theory's own results on this point are among its most elegant and most quietly shelved. Marxist critics who announce that equilibrium is a fantasy the mainstream naively believes have therefore not read the mainstream's own mathematics. The defensible criticism is different: that the benchmark, even when handled with full sophistication, draws attention toward the properties of resting states and away from the processes of accumulation and breakdown that constitute the object the other tradition studies. Conversely, the defensible mainstream criticism of gravitation language is that a tendency invoked without a specified mechanism and speed can absorb any observation, a charge the Marxist tradition must answer with mechanisms, and in its better work does: the equalisation of profit rates through capital migration, for instance, is a mechanism with identifiable channels in credit and investment, not an incantation.

## Money: veil, device, or constitutive form

The classical dichotomy, the mainstream inheritance from its classical ancestors, separates the real economy of goods and services from the monetary economy laid over it. In the long run, on this view, money determines the price level and nothing else; the real magnitudes, output, employment, relative prices, are settled by real forces, and money is a veil that the theorist may lift to see the machinery beneath. Keynes broke with the dichotomy for the short run, and the mainstream's monetary economics since then is a long negotiation with that break, but the dichotomy survives as the default architecture: first the real model, then the monetary overlay. Money is a device for economising on barter, a unit in which contracts are written, a store whose quantity the authorities may manage well or badly, but not, in the theory's deep structure, a constituent of the economic relation itself.

Marxist economics begins at the opposite pole. Money, in Marx's construction, is the necessary form of appearance of value: a commodity world cannot express the social character of its labour except in the exchange value of one commodity set apart as universal equivalent, and the money form is therefore constitutive of the system rather than an convenience laid over it. Capital itself is defined monetarily, as money advanced to beget more money, and the circuit of capital starts and ends in the money form. From this architecture several consequences flow that the tradition regards as among its distinctive findings: that the possibility of crisis is present as soon as sale and purchase separate in time, which money alone makes possible; that hoarding, credit, and the autonomisation of finance are not accidents befalling a real economy but developments of forms the system generates; and that the demand for money is a demand for the social validation of private labour, a category with no counterpart in the dichotomy's world.

### Can money be neutral in a capitalist economy?

The mainstream inheritance answers yes in the long run: money fixes the level of prices while real magnitudes are settled elsewhere, though few practitioners defend the strong form for short periods. The Marxist answer is no throughout: money is the form in which value exists and capital moves, so a change in monetary conditions reaches the system's core immediately.

The fairest test of the two architectures is what each does with finance. The mainstream's finance theory, in its benchmark form, treated financial structure as largely irrelevant to real outcomes and finance itself as a frictionless veil of a sophisticated kind; the discipline's more empirical wings always knew better, and the financial crisis at the end of the first decade of the twenty-first century cost the benchmark dearly in standing, a point the convergence sections revisit. The Marxist tradition, by contrast, never had a period in which finance was theoretically inert: from Marx's treatment of interest-bearing capital through Hilferding's finance capital to the later literature on financialisation, the credit system is analysed as a power inside accumulation, capable of disciplining firms, redistributing claims, and preparing crises. On this domain the Marxist architecture at least asks the questions early. Whether its answers are better than the answers the mainstream's empirical finance now gives is, again, a domain question, and the verdict below scores money and finance as a territory where the Marxist framing proved fertile and the mainstream record is mixed rather than negligible.

It is worth pausing on a point of method that the money dispute exposes. The two traditions do not merely disagree about money; they disagree about what sort of thing a monetary economy is. For one, money is an institution that agents adopt because it lowers the costs of transacting, and it can be modelled as they model other institutions, by the incentives it creates. For the other, money is a social relation that constitutes the agents' world before any transacting begins: commodities confront one another as values only because money already stands over against them all. Readers who find the second claim metaphysical should notice that the first claim has a corresponding austerity: it must treat the near-universal compulsion to obtain money, the fact that every agent's survival runs through sale, as a mere technicality. Each tradition, that is, has a point where its explanations stop and its primitives begin. Comparison is fairest when it names those points rather than pretending only one side has them.

## Class and power: structure or friction

In the mainstream's grammar, the population of the economy is made of agents differentiated by their endowments, preferences, and information, and any classification of them, by income, occupation, or wealth, is a statistical convenience layered over the fundamental units. Class in the strong sense, a division of society into groups defined by their opposed positions in production, with interests that follow from those positions, has no place in the grammar. Where mainstream analysis meets phenomena that look like class, it translates them: into human capital differences, into bargaining power within a firm, into the frictions of incomplete contracts, into the politics of redistribution conducted through a median voter. Power likewise enters as a deviation, a market imperfection, something an agent has when the model's competitive assumptions fail. The translations are often ingenious and sometimes illuminating, but they are translations: the original category is dissolved into the grammar of endowments and exchanges.

For the Marxist tradition, class is the load-bearing category. The division between those who own means of production and those who must sell their labour power is not one social fact among others but the relation that defines the system, from which the theory derives the distribution of income, the dynamics of accumulation, and the characteristic conflicts of capitalist societies. Power, correspondingly, is not a friction in the machine but part of the machine's design: the employer's authority within production, the reserve of unemployed labour disciplining those in work, the dependence of governments on the confidence of investors, are structural features, present even when every market is competitive in the textbook sense. A capitalism in which no employer could dismiss, no creditor could refuse, and no government feared a strike of capital would not be a purified capitalism for this tradition. It would be a different system mislabelled.

### Is class a category or a complication?

In the mainstream grammar it is at most a complication, a convenient label for clusters of endowments and outcomes within a theory whose real units are choosing individuals. In the Marxist grammar it is the foundational category, the relation from which the theory's account of distribution, accumulation, and conflict is built.

The empirical stakes of this difference are larger than the methodological one. A theory with class in its foundations expects the distribution of income between wages and profits to be a contested magnitude, settled by the relative strength of the parties, by law, by the state of the labour market, and by the organisation of both sides; it expects technical change to be steered by its effects on that contest; and it expects the political life of capitalist societies to be structured, beneath the surface of parties and personalities, by the underlying division. A theory without class expects distribution to track contributions and scarcities, and treats persistent conflict over shares as a puzzle requiring special explanation. The postwar history of the advanced economies contains long stretches that flatter the second expectation and long stretches that flatter the first, and the labour-share research discussed later in this article is precisely the mainstream measuring, in its own vocabulary, a movement that the Marxist vocabulary had always placed at the centre. The verdict on distribution as a domain follows from that record.

None of this requires the Marxist category to be beyond criticism, and its critics inside and outside the tradition have pressed real difficulties. The boundary between classes is untidy in societies rich in managers, professionals, pension funds, and small proprietors; the inference from class position to class interest to class action is a chain with a weak link at every step; and the tradition's own best sociologists spend much of their time on the mediations its economists sometimes skip. But a category can be untidy at the edges and still be the right primitive, as the mainstream's own category of the firm is untidy in the age of platforms and franchises. The comparative question is not which grammar is tidier. It is which grammar's primitive turns out to be the one the evidence keeps needing.

## The overlaps the quarrel erases

Polemic on both sides profits from presenting the two traditions as sealed worlds, and the first casualty of that presentation is the stock of ideas they hold in common. Both descend from classical political economy, and the descent is not merely genealogical. The concept of a surplus, a social product exceeding what the reproduction of the producers and the replacement of the used-up means of production require, is the organising idea of Smith and Ricardo before it is Marx's, and it survives inside the mainstream in places the mainstream does not always acknowledge: in the national accounts' treatment of operating surplus, in growth theory's residual, in every model in which output divides between the claims that reproduce the system and the claims that enlarge it. When a mainstream growth theorist decomposes output into factor payments and a remainder, the classical surplus approach is operating in modern dress, whether or not its user would accept the kinship.

The kinship extends to method in one important respect. Both traditions, at their best, are committed to the idea that the economy is a system whose parts cannot be understood in isolation, and both have produced their characteristic instruments for honouring that commitment: general equilibrium theory on one side, reproduction and circuit analysis on the other. The instruments differ, one solving for mutual consistency and the other tracing the conditions of continued motion, but the impulse is the same refusal of the single market studied alone. It is one of the quiet ironies of the dispute that the two sides reserve their sharpest contempt for each other while sharing this refusal with nobody else in the social sciences. The economist who believes the whole economy must be modelled as an interdependent system has more in common, methodologically, with the economist on the other side of the value dispute than with the sociologist or the historian who proceeds case by case.

There is also an overlap of findings, distinct from the overlap of concepts, and it is the overlap the later convergence sections document: on the behaviour of markups, on the trend of the labour share, on the consequences of concentration, mainstream empirical work has arrived, by its own routes, at positions that Marxist economists had occupied for decades. An overlap of findings between traditions with different objects is not a paradox. Two sciences with different maps of the same coastline will agree about particular headlands. What the convergence sections must resist is the temptation, strong on the Marxist side, to read agreement about headlands as surrender of the rival map. It is not, and the article returns to the point with the care it requires.

## The ground between: the classical and Sraffian approaches

Between the two capitals stands a territory that belongs fully to neither, and its existence is itself evidence about the comparison. The classical approach, reconstructed in the twentieth century from Ricardo and Marx by Piero Sraffa and his successors, treats prices and distribution together: given the methods of production in use and given the division of the surplus between wages and profits, settled by forces outside the price system, the prices of commodities follow. This construction occupies ground between the traditions in both directions. Against the mainstream, it denies that distribution is settled by the same pricing process that settles everything else, and its critique of the marginal theory of capital, in the controversies centred on Cambridge, showed that the mainstream's own aggregate concepts could not always bear the weight placed on them. Against a simple Marxism, it shows that the determination of prices does not require the labour theory of value as a prior step, a result some Marxists absorbed as a correction and others resisted as a dissolution of the theory's core.

### Did the classical economists belong to either camp?

Neither, because the camps are later constructions. Smith, Ricardo, and Mill wrote before the marginal revolution divided the subject, and each modern tradition claims them selectively: the mainstream takes the analysis of competition and exchange, the Marxist tradition takes the surplus framework and the theory of distribution as a social settlement.

The Sraffian territory matters to this comparison for a reason beyond its intrinsic interest. It demonstrates that the space of possible economics is not exhausted by the two poles the polemic describes. A framework can hold that distribution is socially determined, with the Marxist tradition, while holding that prices are governed by technique and distribution jointly, against the labour theory's derivation of them; it can be rigorously formal, with the mainstream, while rejecting the mainstream's theory of distribution root and branch. Students who meet the Sraffian construction early are inoculated against the two-sealed-worlds picture, and seminar leaders have long used it for exactly that purpose. The domain comparison below is drawn between the two main traditions because those are the terms of the question posed, but the reader should remember that the map has a middle, and that some of the most technically accomplished work in either camp's broader family was done there.

The middle territory also supplies a control case for the marginality question. Sraffian economics is not Marxist, does not carry the political associations of Marxism, and shares the Marxist tradition's conclusions on several technical points, yet it too stands at the profession's margin, honoured in a handful of centres and absent from most curricula. Its marginality suggests that whatever excludes heterodox frameworks from the mainstream operates on method and object together, not on doctrine or politics alone: a theory that refuses to derive distribution from marginal products, however impeccable its mathematics and however quiet its politics, finds the profession's doors nearly as closed as the Marxist tradition finds them. That observation disciplines the account of marginality given later: professional closure is real, but it is closure around a technical apparatus and a definitional charter, not simply around a politics.

## The post-Keynesian bridge

If the Sraffian territory is the ground between the traditions in the theory of prices, post-Keynesian economics is the bridge between them in macroeconomics. Descending from Keynes and from Michal Kalecki, who had arrived independently at much of the Keynesian structure from Marxist starting points, post-Keynesian work shares with the Marxist tradition a set of commitments the mainstream charter excludes or marginalises: that effective demand governs output and employment not only in the short run; that money and finance are endogenous, created inside the system's operations rather than supplied from outside; that fundamental uncertainty, as distinct from calculable risk, is a permanent condition of investment; and that distribution between wages and profits is a determinant of demand and growth rather than a sideshow. A post-Keynesian and a Marxist economist reading the same crisis will diagnose it in recognisably related terms, and each will find the other's library more useful than the mainstream's benchmark models.

### What does the post-Keynesian bridge carry across?

It carries the propositions both banks accept: that demand governs output beyond the short run, that money arises inside the credit system, that investment answers to expected profit under uncertainty no calculation removes, and that the division between wages and profits steers growth. On those four planks, traffic between the Marxist tradition and Keynes's heirs has never stopped.

The bridge has limits, and marking them keeps the comparison honest. Post-Keynesian economics generally lacks the Marxist theory of value and production: its firms set prices by markups over costs and its classes appear as spending groups rather than as parties to a production relation, so that exploitation, the extraction of surplus in production itself, drops out of the analysis even where distribution is central. Its politics, correspondingly, tend toward the management of capitalism rather than its supersession, and its theory of crisis is a theory of instability to be damped rather than of contradictions to be resolved by transformation. Traffic across the bridge is therefore real but selective. Distribution, demand, money, and crisis cross freely; the theory of the capital relation itself does not. The point matters for the domain verdict, because several domains where the Marxist record scores well, distribution and endogenous crisis among them, are domains where the bridge tradition also performs strongly, and the credit in those domains belongs to a family of approaches rather than to Marxist economics alone. Verdicts in this article are given to the Marxist tradition only where its distinctive machinery, not the family's shared inheritance, does the work.

The bridge also illustrates the mainstream's absorptive capacity from the other side. Elements of the post-Keynesian programme have repeatedly been taken up inside the mainstream in domesticated forms: endogenous money in central bank practice and in the teaching of monetary economics, demand-led growth in the policy models of international institutions, uncertainty in the behavioural and information literatures. Each absorption strips the element of the framework that made it heterodox, rather as the mainstream absorbed Keynes himself in the postwar synthesis. A Marxist economist watching this process learns to predict the pattern: the finding crosses, the framework does not. That asymmetry, findings portable and frameworks not, is the single most important fact about how the traditions interact, and the convergence sections are really an extended footnote to it.

## The domain comparison

The preceding sections have separated the two traditions concept by concept. The domain comparison now sets the record side by side, territory by territory, in the findable form this series uses for its judgments. The domains are the ones the brief specifies: relative prices, distribution, technical change, crisis, growth, allocation, and money. For each, the table states what the Marxist tradition claims, what the mainstream claims, which side carries the stronger empirical record in that domain, and why the record falls that way. The prose sections that follow the table then take the domains in turn, at the length their evidence requires, and the reader who wants the argument rather than the summary should treat the table as a map to be checked against the territory, not as a substitute for visiting it.

### How should the domain comparison be read?

Row by row, as seven separate judgments rather than as one verdict in seven copies. Each row asks which tradition's questions fit the domain and which record the evidence supports there, and the rows are permitted to disagree with one another. The article's claim, the domain verdict, is exactly that the rows do disagree, systematically.

| Domain | The Marxist claim | The mainstream claim | Stronger record | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Relative prices | Prices gravitate around values or prices of production set by conditions of production and the general profit rate | Prices are set by supply and demand, reflecting scarcity and marginal valuation in each market | Mainstream | Its apparatus predicts and organises price movements market by market, where the Marxist derivation operates at a level of aggregation the evidence rarely tests |
| Distribution | The division between wages and profits is settled by class relations, bargaining strength, and the state of accumulation | Shares reflect the contributions and scarcities of factors, modified by institutions and bargaining arrangements | Marxist | The contested, power-laden movement of shares over long periods matches the conflict account better than the contribution account |
| Technical change | Competition compels labour-saving innovation, so the direction of technique is endogenous and biased by the wage-profit contest | Innovation responds to relative prices and research incentives within a broadly exogenous stream of discovery | Marxist | The observed bias of innovation toward saving labour and cheapening commodities follows the compulsion mechanism rather than a neutral stream |
| Crisis | Crises are generated inside accumulation, by disproportions, profit pressures, and credit expansion the system itself produces | Crises arrive as shocks, policy errors, or financial accidents disturbing an otherwise self-adjusting system | Marxist | Recurrence across unlike policy regimes points to an internal generator, which the shock vocabulary describes but does not explain |
| Growth | Accumulation is the engine: profits fund investment, investment transforms technique, and the path is driven by the profit rate | Growth reflects saving, population, and technical progress combined in an aggregate production relationship | Divided | Each tradition predicts some features the other misses, and the long record honours both in different decades |
| Allocation | Allocation under capital follows profitability, which systematically diverges from need and from social cost | Prices guide resources toward their highest-valued uses, subject to identifiable and corrigible failures | Mainstream | Within the questions allocation poses, the price system's information work is demonstrated daily and the Marxist critique identifies costs rather than offering a rival mechanism |
| Money | Money is constitutive: the form of value, the start and end of capital's circuit, and the seat of a finance with its own dynamics | Money is a device over a real economy, neutral in the long run, though potent in the short | Marxist on framing, mainstream on operations | The Marxist architecture expected finance to matter structurally; the mainstream's monetary technique nonetheless manages the system that exists |

The table's judgments are stated without hedging because a comparison that hedges every row informs nobody, but each judgment is a judgment about a record, open to the evidence the following sections lay out, and the reader is invited to quarrel with any row whose prose defence seems thinner than its neighbours. Two rows deserve a preliminary word. Growth is scored divided not from indecision but because the evidence genuinely divides: the Marxist tradition's account of accumulation as a driven, profit-led process fits the great expansions and the long stagnation literature, while the mainstream's growth accounting organised the measurement of the phenomenon and its policy science, for better and worse, across the postwar world. Money is scored in a split form because the domain itself splits: on the question of what money is in a capitalist economy, the Marxist framing has worn better; on the question of how a monetary authority should act on Monday morning, the mainstream's operational technique is the only developed one on the field, and Marxist monetary writing, for all its architectural insight, has produced no comparable craft.

## The domain verdict

The claim this article exists to carry can now be stated in its full form. Neither tradition is generally superior, and the appearance that one must be arises from asking the comparison at the wrong level. Asked question by question and domain by domain, a pattern emerges that is too regular to be accident: Marxist economics performs better where the phenomenon is generated by the relations of production themselves, in distribution, in the endogenous production of crisis, and in the dynamics of technical change, because its object of study was built for exactly those territories. Mainstream economics performs better where the phenomenon is the working of the price system within a going structure, in relative prices, in allocation, and in the short-run analysis of particular markets and policies, because its object was built for those. The pattern is not a compromise manufactured to offend nobody. It follows from the definitions with which the article began: a theory of a historical system's dynamics will out-perform on the system's dynamics, and a theory of choice under scarcity will out-perform on choice and scarcity, and each will look clumsy on the other's ground because it is.

The verdict has a practical corollary that students and practitioners can use. A question addressed to the wrong framework does not merely get a weak answer; it gets a confident answer to a different question, which is worse. Ask the mainstream why the wage share fell across a generation and the apparatus returns an account of technology and factor substitution that may be internally impeccable while treating as a parameter the very contest the question was about. Ask the Marxist framework what a particular tax will do to a particular market next year and the tradition returns a meditation on accumulation that may be profound while being useless for the decision at hand. Grading questions before answering them, assigning each to the framework whose object it matches, is the skill the domain verdict names, and it is a skill partisans of both sides conspicuously lack, because partisanship consists precisely in refusing the assignment.

The verdict also disciplines the two sensitive topics still to come. Marginality, when the article reaches it, will be explained in a way consistent with the pattern: a profession organised around one object will marginalise a tradition organised around another, partly by power and partly by the genuine unsuitability of that tradition's tools for the profession's daily questions. Convergence, likewise, will be stated as the pattern predicts: where the mainstream's own empirical work has wandered into the Marxist tradition's home territories, distribution, concentration, the behaviour of finance, it has tended to find what the residents reported. Neither fact requires conspiracy on one side or surrender on the other. Both are what a domain-structured comparison leads one to expect.

## Relative prices: the mainstream's home ground

The first row of the table is also the least contested among honest practitioners of either side. The day-to-day and year-to-year movement of particular prices, why this metal rose against that grain, why housing in one city outran housing in another, why a harvest failure shows up in bread before it shows up in shoes, is the mainstream apparatus's native territory, and it works there. Supply and demand, elasticity, substitution at the margin, the incidence of a tax or a tariff shifted between buyer and seller: these are instruments of proven power for questions of this shape, sharpened by two centuries of use and by an econometrics built to wield them. A Marxist economist asked to forecast the effect of a duty on imported steel will reach, in practice, for the same partial-equilibrium apparatus as everyone else, and the tradition's own journals, when they address particular markets, do the same while grumbling about the frame.

The Marxist claim in this domain was never really about particular prices. It is a claim about the level at which prices are anchored: that behind the fluctuations of market price stand prices of production, set by conditions of production and a general rate of profit, and behind those, in Marx's construction, values set by labour time. The claim's weakness as an empirical instrument is that its magnitudes are typically unobserved and its predicted deviations are small relative to the noise of actual markets, so that the tests which could discriminate between the anchor story and its rivals are delicate and their results contested. Studies correlating prices with labour contents do find strong associations, as one would expect in any cost-based account, and the mainstream's reply, that production prices in the classical construction do the same work without the labour detour, has never been answered to the profession's satisfaction. The domain verdict scores the row for the mainstream not because the Marxist claim is empty but because the row's question, what moves this price, is the mainstream's question, posed in its grammar, and answered daily in its terms.

There is a lesson in the row about how the verdict should be used. To concede relative prices to the mainstream is not to concede that prices are unimportant or that the mainstream's account of them is complete; the gravitation of market prices around centres set by production conditions is a real phenomenon the classical tradition named first. It is to concede that the instrument which handles the domain's characteristic questions belongs to one side. Traditions, like tradespeople, are entitled to be judged by whether the tools they bring fit the jobs they are set, and part of intellectual honesty in this comparison is noticing when a tradition is being graded on another tradition's job sheet.

## Distribution: where the Marxist questions bite

Distribution reverses the polarity. The question here is not what a price will do next quarter but why the social product divides as it does between the great classes of income, and why that division moves as it does over decades: why the wage share held roughly steady through the postwar generation in the advanced economies and then fell, across countries with very different institutions, over the following one; why the profit share and the concentration of income at the top rose in the same period; why these movements track the fortunes of organised labour, the openness of economies, and the stance of policy more closely than they track any measurable change in technology's bias alone. Posed at that level, the question is about power, institutions, and the state of the accumulation process, and it is the question the Marxist framework was built to ask.

The mainstream's characteristic answer derives shares from the technology of production and the scarcities of factors: labour is paid its marginal product, capital earns its return, and the shares fall out of the elasticities. The answer has the virtue of definiteness and the defect, documented within the mainstream itself, that its central aggregate, the stock of capital whose marginal product is to settle the profit rate, cannot be measured independently of the distribution it is meant to explain, the difficulty at the heart of the Cambridge controversies. In applied work the mainstream has accordingly moved, over the decades, toward accounts in which bargaining, institutions, market structure, and policy carry much of the explanatory weight for shares, which is to say toward the terrain the Marxist account always occupied, a migration the convergence sections examine in detail. The movement does not make the applied mainstream Marxist; its models remain models of agents and markets, and class as a production relation does not appear in them. But on this domain the direction of travel is unmistakable, and it is toward, not away from, the questions the marginal tradition insists on.

The Marxist record here is strong in a specific way worth naming precisely. Its strength is not that it predicted the postwar stability of shares and their later fall; on timing it has no special glory. Its strength is that its framework never treated the shares as technological data in the first place, so the long fall in the wage share arrived as the kind of event the theory exists to explain, connected in its pages to the weakening of organised labour, the globalisation of production, the discipline of unemployment, and the restructuring of firms, at a time when frameworks that derived shares from production functions were reduced to hunting for a change in an elasticity to hang the fact on. A framework is vindicated less by the predictions it nails than by the class of events it is unsurprised by, and by that standard distribution belongs to the Marxist column.

## Technical change: its direction and its bias

Why does innovation take the direction it does? The question sounds innocuous until one notices that the stream of invention is not random in its incidence: across two centuries it has run persistently toward saving labour, cheapening wage goods, speeding the turnover of capital, and tightening managerial control over the labour process, and it has run only fitfully toward saving capital or easing the worker's day where no cost saving beckoned. The mainstream account explains the direction by relative prices: firms economise on the factor that is dear, and since labour in the advanced economies is the dear and growing dearer factor, innovation is labour-saving. The account is coherent and, within its terms, correct as far as it goes. What it treats as given, the path of wages itself, is the point where the Marxist account begins.

In the Marxist construction, the direction of technical change is set by the contest at the heart of the system. Competition compels each firm to cheapen its product or perish; the readiest lever is the reorganisation of production so that less labour, or cheaper labour, enters each unit; the rising productivity that results cheapens the goods workers consume, which lowers the value of labour power and raises the share of the working day accruing as surplus, even where money wages rise. Machinery, in this telling, is also a weapon: introduced amid strikes, designed to deskill, paced to discipline, and the historical literature of the factory, much of it written from the archives by scholars of no party, documents the intention often enough that the tradition's insistence cannot be waved away. The bias of innovation is thus not a response to scarcity in the abstract but a sediment of the wage-profit struggle itself, and the framework predicts the bias will persist as long as the struggle does, in whatever the era's characteristic machinery happens to be.

The evidence the verdict rests on is the persistence of that bias across regimes of relative prices that should, on the scarcity account, have turned it. Periods of cheap and abundant labour have not produced a corresponding turn toward labour-using innovation in the commanding heights of industry; the direction of research effort in pharmaceuticals, in agriculture, in software follows the pattern of profitability and control more faithfully than it follows any map of social need or factor scarcity. None of this shows the mainstream mechanism to be fictitious: firms do respond to factor prices, and the econometrics of induced innovation is a respectable literature. It shows the mechanism to be a surface current over a deeper one. The domain verdict assigns technical change to the Marxist column because the deeper current is the one that explains the phenomenon's most striking feature, its stubborn direction across centuries.

## Crisis: shock from outside or rupture from inside

Every few years, at intervals no calendar predicts and no generation escapes, the economies of the capitalist world seize: credit freezes, orders vanish, workers are dismissed from factories whose products were wanted the month before, and the machinery of prosperity idles in the presence of the needs it was serving. The mainstream's characteristic vocabulary for these events is the vocabulary of disturbance: shocks, exogenous or policy-made; frictions that prevent adjustment; financial accidents that amplify a blow struck from elsewhere. Even its richest crisis models, and the mainstream has built serious ones, especially since the crisis of the early twenty-first century forced the subject back into its journals, tend to locate the originating impulse outside the normal operation of the system, in a burst bubble, a policy error, a contagion from abroad. The system's normal operation is sound; the crisis is what interrupts it.

The Marxist tradition's claim is the inversion. Crisis, on its account, is the system's normal operation arriving at its periodic impasse: the same accumulation that expands production expands it unevenly, department against department; the same credit that bridges the gaps stretches them; the same pressure on profits that drives rationalisation undermines the demand the rationalised output requires. The specific theories differ within the tradition, disproportionality, underconsumption, the falling tendency of the profit rate, the profit squeeze, and the internal quarrels among them are genuine and unresolved; the comparison with Keynes's very different construction of the same phenomenon is the business of the series article on [marx vs keynes on crisis](/2014/01/15/marx-vs-keynes-on-crisis/) and is left there. What unites the Marxist family is the endogeneity: a crisis is not weather. It is the crop a particular way of organising production reliably grows.

The evidence for the row is the recurrence itself. Crises have arrived under gold standards and fiat money, under balanced budgets and deficits, under financial repression and financial liberalisation, in economies led by manufacturing and in economies led by services, and the intervals between them bear the mark of the credit and investment cycles the Marxist literature describes. A theory family that treats each arrival as a fresh accident can describe each one perfectly and explain the pattern of their coming not at all. The mainstream's own most distinguished crisis scholarship, its historians of financial manias and its students of debt cycles, in practice writes a recognisably endogenous story while declining the framework that would unify it, a point to which the convergence discussion returns. The verdict gives crisis to the Marxist column on the strength of the pattern: the tradition that expects the fire understands the fire brigade's calendar better than the tradition perpetually surprised by smoke.

## Growth: accumulation as the engine

Growth is the row the table scores divided, and the division deserves its reasons. The mainstream built the instruments with which growth is measured and much of the language in which it is discussed: the aggregate production function, the decomposition of growth into the contributions of capital, labour, and a residual, the analysis of convergence among economies. Whatever their defects, and the Marxist and Sraffian critiques of the aggregate capital stock apply here with undiminished force, these instruments organised a half-century of empirical work and policy argument, and no honest verdict can strike them from the record. Where the mainstream programme asked how much of growth is accumulation and how much is technical progress, it produced answers that, however contestable in construction, disciplined every subsequent discussion, including the discussions of its critics.

The Marxist tradition's contribution to the domain is of a different kind: not measurement but mechanism. Its growth theory is its accumulation theory, in which the reinvestment of surplus under competitive compulsion drives the expansion and transformation of the capital stock, technical change arrives embodied in new investment rather than as manna, and the path of the system is stamped by the rate of profit that governs the whole. The long waves of expansion and stagnation that punctuate capitalist history, and the tradition's literature on the stagnation tendency of mature monopoly capitalism, belong to this line, and the development economics of the postwar decades drew on it heavily wherever the question was how a poor economy could force the pace of accumulation against the grain of its markets. Each tradition, in short, predicts features of the record the other handles badly: the mainstream, the accounting shape of growth in settled times; the Marxist tradition, its driven, uneven, crisis-punctuated character across the long run. Divided is the honest score, and the reader who distrusts divided rows is invited to name the decade whose growth history flatters only one side.

## Allocation and particular markets: the mainstream's craft

Allocation is the domain where the definitional charter and the professional record coincide most completely, and the verdict here is the mainstream's without serious qualification. Given a structure of production and a distribution of income, the question of which resources go where, of what a tax does to the market it falls on, of how a quota, a licence, an auction, or a regulation will shift behaviour, is the question the mainstream apparatus exists to answer, and it answers such questions with a precision and a cumulativeness no rival approaches. The Marxist tradition, asked the same questions, has characteristically answered a different one: it has shown what the price system's allocations leave out, the needs that carry no money and so no signal, the costs pushed onto parties outside the contract, the wants manufactured by the selling process itself. Those are true and important observations, and entire literatures, on externalities and on public goods, exist partly because the mainstream absorbed versions of them. But an observation about what a mechanism ignores is not a rival mechanism, and on allocation the Marxist tradition brings a critique where the mainstream brings a craft.

The row matters beyond its content because it disciplines the Marxist side of the comparison against a characteristic vice: the slide from showing that the price system allocates according to purchasing power rather than need, which is true by definition, to implying that its allocative work is therefore illusory or unimportant, which is false. Resources really are rationed by prices, signals really do coordinate millions of decisions no office could coordinate, and a tradition that cannot say so without flinching will not be believed when it says the harder things. Conversely, the row disciplines the mainstream against its own characteristic vice, which is to treat competence at allocation as competence at economics entire, and to mistake the domains where its craft shines for the whole of the subject. The domain verdict exists to block both slides at once.

## Money and finance: the architecture against the operations

The money row of the table was scored in split form, and the split can now be explained. On the architectural question, what money is in a capitalist economy and what finance does to the system's stability, the Marxist framing has worn remarkably well. A tradition for which money is the form of value, credit an internal organ of accumulation, and interest-bearing capital a power with its own trajectory was never going to be surprised that a crisis could be born in the financial system and rule the real one for a decade; its literature on finance capital, on credit cycles, and on the state's management of money runs in an unbroken line from the nineteenth century to the analyses of financialisation in the twenty-first. The mainstream benchmark, by contrast, reached the crisis at the end of the first decade of the twenty-first century with models in which the financial system was, in the strong versions, literally absent, and its subsequent rebuilding has been an admission conducted in the passive voice.

On the operational question, how the monetary system is to be run, the record reverses. The mainstream possesses the developed technique: the theory and practice of central banking, the management of expectations, the design of lender-of-last-resort operations, the econometrics of monetary transmission, honed in institutions that must act on a schedule and be judged on outcomes. Marxist monetary writing is rich in architecture and poor in craft; it will tell a central banker what money is and why her institution exists, and almost nothing about what to do on the morning of a bank run that she does not already know from the mainstream's own manuals and the institution's memory. The domain verdict, true to its rule, scores the row by its questions rather than by loyalty: framing to the Marxist tradition, operations to the mainstream, and the row left deliberately in that untidy state as a standing lesson in what domain-by-domain judgment looks like when it is not forced into a single column.

## Convergence on market power and markups

The Marxist tradition has held, since the opening of the twentieth century, that the capitalism of competitive small producers was giving way to a capitalism of concentration: that capitals grow by absorbing one another as well as by accumulating, that the commanding heights of industry come to be held by a small number of very large firms, and that those firms administer their prices rather than taking them from a market, setting markups over costs that competition no longer grinds to the bone. The monopoly capital literature built an entire account of the modern economy on that foundation, and the series article on [monopoly capital stagnation theory](/2014/02/01/monopoly-capital-stagnation-theory/) states it in full. For most of the twentieth century the mainstream regarded this literature as a polemical survival: its own theory of the firm was built for price-takers, and where market power appeared it was filed as an exception, a monopoly problem for antitrust rather than a property of the system's normal development.

The empirical situation has changed, and the change is the first documented convergence this article records. Over the past generation, mainstream empirical research on American and then on other economies has built a substantial literature on the behaviour of markups: the margins firms charge over their marginal costs, estimated from production data across the whole economy rather than industry by industry. Its central finding is that markups have risen substantially over the period since the later twentieth century, that the rise is driven by the growth of high-markup firms at the top of each industry rather than by a general drift, and that the firms concerned have converted their margins into profit shares of a size that earlier data did not show. Parallel literatures document rising concentration within industries, the growing weight of superstar firms, and the declining dynamism of entry. This article cites no figures for any of this, in keeping with the protocol its brief lays down for contested magnitudes; the point is the direction and existence of the literature, not its estimates. The direction is toward the picture the monopoly capital tradition painted, arrived at by scholars working inside the mainstream's own methods, publishing in its own journals, and for the most part innocent of any intention to corroborate a Marxist claim.

### What did the markup literature actually find?

Working from firm and industry data inside the mainstream's own production-function methods, it found that the margins firms set over marginal cost have risen substantially across the later twentieth century and into the twenty-first, concentrated among the largest firms in each industry, whose growing weight lifts the average for the whole economy.

Why does the convergence matter, and why does it need careful handling? It matters because the Marxist claim was not a stray guess. The expectation that competition tends to abolish itself, that accumulation concentrates, and that concentrated capitals administer prices and earn a surplus that competition cannot discipline, follows from the tradition's core machinery: the drive to accumulate, the advantages of scale in credit and technique, the absorption of the weak by the strong in every crisis. When data assembled for other purposes exhibits the pattern the machinery predicted, the framework has scored the kind of success science is supposed to reward, a risky expectation borne out by investigators with no stake in it. The careful handling is required because bearing out a pattern is not bearing out a framework, a distinction the not-vindication section below develops at the length it deserves. The markup literature explains its findings in the mainstream's own terms, through technology, globalisation, and the rising weight of intangible capital, and its authors are under no obligation to accept the theory that reached the finding first. Convergence is a fact about results. It is not a transfer of allegiance.

The convergence extends beyond markups to the surrounding phenomena the monopoly capital literature always bundled with them. The stagnation strand of that literature held that the great firm's surplus, difficult to absorb in productive investment at the required scale, would be spent on selling effort, on financial placement, and on the state's expenditures, and that the system would tend toward chronic underuse of its capacity. Mainstream macroeconomics has since built, under other names, an account of the advanced economies' later decades in which a persistent shortfall of demand, a glut of saving seeking assets, and the swelling of finance relative to production do the explanatory work. The names differ, the politics differ, and the policy conclusions differ most of all. But a reader who sets the two accounts side by side sees one landscape drawn by two cartographers who do not speak, and the domain verdict counts that as evidence about the landscape.

## Convergence on the labour share

The second documented convergence concerns the division of the product itself. The Marxist tradition, for which distribution is the settled outcome of a standing contest, always treated the share of national income going to wages as a variable with a history: rising when labour was organised and labour markets tight, falling when accumulation demanded cheapened labour power and the institutions protecting wages were dismantled. The mainstream's benchmark growth model, by contrast, long treated the constancy of factor shares as one of the stylised facts a successful theory should reproduce, and for the postwar generation the fact held well enough to be built into the foundations. A variable the theory registered as a constant was behaving, in the leading model, exactly as registered, and no research programme grows around a number that does not move.

The number moved. Across the advanced economies and many others, the share of income accruing to labour trended downward over the closing decades of the twentieth century and the opening ones of the twenty-first, and the measurement of that movement became, in time, a substantial mainstream literature of its own, confirming the trend across datasets and countries and arguing over its decomposition. The companion article on [labour share evidence](/2021/03/01/labour-share-evidence/) assembles the record in the detail it requires; here, under the same no-figures protocol as before, what matters is the literature's existence and what it argued about. Its candidate causes are instructive: the falling price of investment goods and the automation they embody, the integration of low-wage economies into world production, the weakening of unions and of the welfare state's bargaining floor, the rise of the superstar firms already met in the markup literature, whose small workforces divide enormous revenues. Every one of those causes, whatever its author's intent, is a mechanism the Marxist tradition's account of distribution either names or has room for, and several, the institutional weakening and the global recomposition of the workforce, are its native vocabulary in translation.

### Why did the labour share become a mainstream research topic?

Because the share stopped behaving as the benchmark model required. A magnitude the textbook growth framework treated as roughly constant trended downward across many countries for decades, and an anomaly of that size assembles its own literature: first measurement, then decomposition, then the search for causes, conducted inside the mainstream's journals on the mainstream's terms.

Notice what the episode reveals about the two traditions' different relationships to their objects. The Marxist tradition did not discover the falling labour share either; its writers, in the main, were late and unrigorous in measuring a movement their framework should have led them to hunt. The mainstream discovered the movement when its own instruments registered an anomaly, and then measured it superbly. Discovery by anomaly and measurement by apparatus is the mainstream's characteristic rhythm, and it is a genuine scientific virtue. But the episode also shows the cost of the mainstream's charter: a framework that defines distribution as the pricing of factors had no reason to watch the wage share as a central variable, and watched it late. The Marxist framework, watching it always, watched it badly. The domain verdict on distribution rests on the judgment that knowing what to watch is the prior virtue, and that the measurement, once the mainstream attends, will be better made there; the first half of that judgment gives the domain to the Marxist tradition, and the second half is the standing offer the convergence describes: results the mainstream reaches, when it reaches into the Marxist tradition's home territories, tend to confirm what the residents said was there.

The two convergences share a moral that the next section states as a rule. In both cases, the mainstream arrived by its own road, using its own instruments, typically in ignorance of the heterodox literature that had held the position for decades, and it has integrated the findings into frameworks that owe nothing to Marxist categories. The findings are now part of the common stock of economics. The priority belongs to the Marxist tradition, and priority, in science, is a fact that matters for the record and for the assessment of frameworks. It is not a deed of ownership over the conclusion, and it confers no authority over the uses the conclusion is put to.

## Convergence is not vindication

This section discharges the most delicate obligation in the article's brief. When a tradition that has been marginal for generations watches the centre of its discipline adopt, one by one, findings it has long maintained, the temptation to declare vindication is overwhelming and must be refused, on grounds of logic rather than modesty. Vindication of a framework requires more than the success of some of its expectations. It requires that the expectations succeeded because the framework's machinery is sound, and that is exactly what an independent convergence cannot show. The mainstream researchers who documented rising markups and the falling labour share did not use surplus value, the circuit of capital, or the theory of the reserve army anywhere in their work. Their findings can be, and are, housed in frameworks without those categories. A finding that several incompatible frameworks can house is evidence for the finding, not for any one of the houses.

### What separates a shared result from a shared theory?

Everything that does the work in science. A result records what the evidence shows in one territory; a theory is the machinery that generated the expectation before the evidence arrived. Two traditions can share the result while their machinery differs at every joint, and convergence of results therefore revises the score of findings, not the standing of frameworks.

What convergence does establish is more limited and still considerable. It establishes that the Marxist tradition's choice of objects was not a mistake: the territories it insisted on watching, distribution, concentration, finance, crisis, contain real phenomena that the mainstream's charter caused it to watch late, and a framework whose map marks the coastline where the coastline is has passed a test that frameworks can fail. It also establishes something about the mainstream: that its empirical wing, following its own norms, will in time register the phenomena its theoretical charter overlooks, which is a point in the mainstream's favour that Marxist polemic rarely concedes. And it establishes a fact about the division of labour between the traditions that the domain verdict already encodes: the Marxist framework has functioned, in these territories, as an early-warning system whose reports the mainstream later verified with better instruments. Early warning is a honourable scientific function. It is not the whole of science, and the tradition that performs it still owes the measurements, the derivations, and the discipline its rival supplies.

The refusal of vindication has a mirror image that binds the other side. If convergence does not vindicate the Marxist framework, neither does the mainstream's late arrival refute the mainstream framework in the territories concerned; a programme is allowed its anomalies and its repairs, and the mainstream's capacity to absorb the findings into models of imperfect competition and bargaining is normal science, not evasion. Both polemical shortcuts, surrender and rout, are blocked by the same logic. What remains is the slower, less thrilling, and more useful judgment the domain verdict embodies: score territory by territory, credit priority where priority is due, and let no single result carry a framework on its shoulders.

## Why the mainstream moved where it moved

If the convergence is not a conversion, what explains it? The causes lie inside the mainstream's own development, and naming them completes the guard against the vindication reading. The first cause is instrumentation. The measurement of markups and shares at scale waited on firm-level data and on the computing power to process it, and both arrived in the mainstream's workshops in the closing decades of the twentieth century as part of its general empirical turn, the shift of the discipline's centre of gravity from pure theory toward data work that transformed every field it touched. The second cause is generational experience: economists formed during decades of rising inequality, financial crisis, and visible corporate concentration posed the questions their formation suggested, as every generation of economists has done, including the generation formed by the Depression that made Keynesians of its teachers. The third cause is the mainstream's internal dynamics: a profession that rewards novelty within its forms will generate, by its own competition, expeditions into any territory where publishable anomalies accumulate, and the territories the Marxist tradition had marked were, by the time the data allowed, rich in anomalies.

None of these causes requires any mainstream economist to have read a page of the Marxist literature, and in the main they did not; the bibliographies of the convergence literatures run to each other and to the mainstream's own ancestors, not to the heterodox journals where the same findings had slept. That bibliographic fact is itself one of the more melancholy data points in this article, and the section on why the traditions stopped reading each other takes it up. For the present purpose the point is analytical. Convergence produced by independent causes inside the receiving tradition is the strongest possible evidence that the phenomena are real, since the witnesses are hostile and their testimony unpaid. It is the weakest possible evidence that the receiving tradition has adopted the framework of the tradition that reported the phenomena first. Both halves of that sentence must be held together, because each half alone is a polemic.

## Two causes of marginality, and the refusal to choose

No topic in this comparison attracts more dishonesty than the question of why Marxist economics stands where it stands: present in the universities but at their edge, taught in a minority of economics departments, published in its own journals, cited across the social sciences and humanities while remaining nearly invisible in the flagship journals of its own discipline. The Marxist explanation, in its popular form, is professional and political: the tradition was excluded, by a discipline consolidating around an apparatus that has no place for it and, in the middle of the twentieth century, by direct pressure on the economists who practised it. The mainstream explanation, in its popular form, is intellectual: the tradition was weighed and found wanting, its central magnitudes unmeasurable, its disputes interminable, its predictions falsified. This article's brief requires both causes to be given, and the requirement is not even-handedness for its own sake. Each cause is independently documented, each explains features of the record the other cannot, and an account offering only one is not a tougher account. It is a partisan one, and the reader should learn to recognise the omission as the tell.

### Why is Marxist economics marginal in the profession?

For both professional and intellectual reasons. The discipline consolidated around a technical apparatus that leaves little room for its questions, and radical economists faced documented pressure in the mid-twentieth century. It also carries unresolved internal disputes over value magnitudes and a record of specific predictions that failed.

Begin with the structure of the fact to be explained, because the two causes divide it between them. Marxist economics is not marginal everywhere in the same way. It sustains journals, conferences, learned societies, and a continuous literature of high technical quality; it holds posts, more often in sociology, politics, geography, and history departments than in economics ones; its concepts circulate through the humanities with an energy its home discipline denies them. What it lacks is standing inside economics as the profession defines itself: presence in the core journals, in the standard graduate training, in the textbooks through which the discipline reproduces. Marginality of that specific shape, exiled from the centre of one's own discipline while flourishing at the edges of others, is the fact. A purely intellectual explanation struggles with the flourishing: worthless work does not usually thrive next door. A purely professional explanation struggles with the exile's precision: power alone does not explain why this tradition, rather than some other dissent, drew the boundary where it fell. The two causes are both needed because the fact has two faces.

## The professional closure

The professional cause begins with consolidation. In the decades after the Second World War, economics in the United States, and by imitation elsewhere, standardised itself around a shared technical apparatus: optimisation, equilibrium modelling, and econometrics, taught from converging textbooks, examined by converging standards, and certified by a hierarchy of journals whose refereeing enforced the standard. Consolidation of this kind is not a conspiracy; it is what professions do, and it brought real gains in rigour and cumulation. But a standard is also a filter. Work that could not be stated in the apparatus's forms, whatever its merits, could not be assessed by the apparatus's referees, published in its journals, or taught in its core courses, and a tradition whose founding texts are works of historical and philosophical political economy, whose central text is a thousand-page critique written in the previous century, failed the filter at every stage. The exclusion operated through form before it ever operated through content, which is why it could be, and was, administered by people of no particular ill will.

Documented pressure supplied the cruder instrument where the filter was not enough. In the United States of the late nineteen forties and the nineteen fifties, radical economists faced investigations, loyalty proceedings, dismissal campaigns, and the quieter blacklist of unreturned applications; the cases are a matter of record in the profession's own later historiography, which does not flinch from them. Paul Sweezy, one of the most gifted economists of his generation, left Harvard and never held a regular university chair again, building instead, with the Monthly Review, an institution outside the academy from which to work. Paul Baran held his chair at Stanford in celebrated isolation, for years the only self-described Marxist with tenure at a major American economics department, a singularity that was itself a statistic about the profession. The pressure did not need to be total to be effective: a generation of graduate students learned, from the visible fates of their elders, where the boundaries of a career lay, and the lesson reproduced the boundary more cheaply than any proceeding could.

The closure then sustained itself by ordinary mechanisms that required no further animus. Hiring follows publication, publication follows refereeing, refereeing follows training, and training follows the textbooks, in a circle that a tradition outside it cannot break by producing good work alone, because the definition of good work is one of the things the circle controls. Add the profession's reward structure, in which the marginal returns to working on a question the centre recognises vastly exceed those to working on one it does not, and the wonder is not that Marxist economics is marginal but that it persists at all. Its persistence, through its own journals, its own associations, and a diaspora into neighbouring disciplines, is itself evidence in the intellectual half of the account: traditions do not sustain a century of technical literature on nothing.

Two qualifications keep the professional account honest. The first is the Sraffian control case from earlier in this article: a framework without Marxism's politics, impeccable in form, drew a similar boundary, which shows the filter operating on object and method, not on allegiance. The second is that the boundary is not unique to economics, nor the profession unusually wicked: every discipline consolidates, and consolidation always exiles something, sometimes justly. The professional cause explains the mechanism and much of the timing of the Marxist tradition's marginality. It does not, by itself, explain why the exiled tradition could not force the gates from outside by the sheer weight of results, and for that the account must turn to the causes the tradition would rather omit.

## The internal account: unresolved disputes and failed predictions

The intellectual cause begins with an embarrassment the Marxist tradition has never fully discharged: a hundred years after the third volume of Capital, its economists do not agree among themselves on the theory of value that is supposed to be the foundation of the whole, or on whether the transformation of values into prices of production is a solved problem, a dissolved one, or an open wound. The preceding articles in this cluster document the dispute's fertility, and reconstructions of real ingenuity have come from it. But a profession watching from outside sees something simpler: a research programme whose central magnitude, value, is computed differently by its different schools, whose members' deepest energies go into disputes among themselves, and whose foundational quarrel shows no sign of the convergence by which maturing sciences are recognised. Programmes are entitled to growing pains. A century of them invites the inference that the pain is constitutional, and the inference, however unfair to the dispute's actual subtleties, has been drawn by generations of curriculum committees.

The second intellectual cause is a record of specific predictions that did not hold, and intellectual honesty requires them to be named in the tradition's own terms rather than in its opponents' caricatures. Marxist economics, in the forms most widely held across the twentieth century, led its adherents to expect the progressive immiseration of the working class in the advanced economies; what occurred there, across the middle decades of the century, was a sustained rise in mass living standards without precedent in history. It led them to expect revolution where capitalism was most developed; revolution came where it was least developed, and the developed world settled into a stability the tradition had to explain with auxiliary theories of labour aristocracies, state management, and consumer integration that look, to unsympathetic eyes, like the framework absorbing its falsification one epicycle at a time. It led many to expect the planned economies to outgrow the capitalist ones, and the verdict of the century's end on that expectation is known. Each failure has its explanations, some of them good ones, and the mainstream's own ledger, examined next, is far from clean. But a tradition is responsible for what its widely held forms led reasonable adherents to expect, and by that standard the failures are its property.

A third intellectual cause is subtler and, in the long run, may matter most: the tradition's difficulty in producing from its own resources the kind of result the modern profession is built to consume. Its characteristic products are interpretations of wholes, reconstructions of structures, histories of tendencies: long-form works whose assessment requires reading them, whose errors are matters of judgment, and whose successes accumulate slowly and argumentatively rather than in tables of settled findings. Even where the tradition's claims have proven fertile, the credit tends to migrate, as the convergence sections showed, to whoever restates them in the consumable form. A programme can be right in its orientation and still lose the institutional competition to a rival whose outputs fit the machinery of certification. That is not a refutation of the programme. It is a fact about the ecology of knowledge, and any honest account of marginality must include it alongside the proceedings and the blacklists, because it predicts, correctly, that the marginality would persist long after the proceedings ended, as it has.

## The mainstream's own failed predictions

Fairness in this accounting requires the other ledger to be opened, because the mainstream's predictive record, taken whole, would not survive the standard its popularisers apply to Marx. The benchmark macroeconomic models in use at the time failed, nearly without exception, to foresee the financial crisis at the end of the first decade of the twenty-first century, and the profession's own post-mortems concede that the models in the policy institutions excluded the mechanisms that mattered, an omission structurally similar to the ones for which heterodox work is dismissed. The profession's confident mid-century expectation that the business cycle had been mastered by policy belongs to the same ledger. So does the long reign of the expectations-augmented Phillips curve's policy reading, and the stagflation that embarrassed it; so does the efficient-market benchmark's difficulty with the recurring, patterned character of asset bubbles; so, arguably, does the growth literature's repeated surprise at the performance of economies that declined its policy recipes and at the stagnation of economies that followed them.

### Which failed predictions belong to the mainstream?

The famous ones cluster where its charter is weakest. Its policy models did not foresee the financial crisis of the century's opening decade and its installed theory had pronounced the cycle tamed a generation earlier. Its trade and growth recipes were repeatedly surprised by the economies that ignored them and by the stagnation of economies that obeyed.

The point of the second ledger is not to level the scores. It is to expose the asymmetry in how the two ledgers are audited. Mainstream failures are absorbed as research frontiers: the crisis produced a decade of funded work on financial frictions inside the programme, and the programme's standing survived its largest predictive failure intact, because the authority to say what counts as a failure, and what repair it requires, rests inside the apparatus. Marxist failures are audited by the prosecution. A tradition without institutional power does not get to mark its own homework, and its failures harden into its reputation in a way the mainstream's never do. This asymmetry is real, and naming it is part of the professional cause of marginality. But it must not be inflated into the claim that the Marxist tradition's failures were manufactured by its enemies. They were not; they were published by its friends. The asymmetry governs how failures are remembered, not whether they occurred, and the two-cause account keeps both halves of that sentence.

## The prediction ledger read whole

What, then, is the comparative verdict on prediction, the test both popular polemics reach for first? Read whole, the ledger shows two traditions that fail differently because they predict differently. The mainstream predicts events and magnitudes within a going structure: the path of a price, the effect of a tax, the quarter's growth; its failures are frequent, dated, and usually repairable, and its successes in that register are real and cumulative. The Marxist tradition predicts tendencies and transformations of the structure itself: the concentration of capital, the recurrence of crisis, the globalisation of production, the commodification of new provinces of life; its successes in that register are also real, and several of them, concentration above all, have the character the convergence sections documented, of registering early with the residents what the visitors' instruments confirmed later. Its failures are correspondingly structural: where the structure did not transform as expected, where living standards rose instead of falling and the system's political stability outlasted its economic contradictions, the failure is of the framework's expectations about its own object, and no quarterly revision absorbs it.

A fair judge would conclude that the prediction test, applied at the level of whole traditions, discriminates less than either side advertises, because each side's predictions are strongest exactly where the domain verdict said its questions fit. Prediction is not a separate arena from the domains; it is the domains, scored over time. The mainstream's predictive glory is in prices and short runs, the Marxist tradition's in tendencies and long runs, and the polemical practice of quoting only the other side's failures is simply the domain verdict with the inconvenient rows torn out. The reader who has followed the comparison this far has the instrument to resist that practice, which is most of what this article exists to supply.

## Heterodox is a bigger tent

One of the commonest confusions in this territory, repeated in bookshops, syllabi, and journalism, treats heterodox economics and Marxist economics as two names for one thing. They are not, and the distinction matters practically, because the tent labelled heterodox shelters traditions that agree with one another about little beyond their exclusion. Inside it stand the post-Keynesians, met on the bridge earlier, with their demand-led macroeconomics; the Sraffians, with their classical price theory; the institutionalists, descending from Veblen, for whom habits, law, and organisation are the substance of economic life; feminist economists, who rebuild the discipline's categories around the unpaid labour of social reproduction; ecological economists, for whom the economy is a subsystem of a finite biosphere; and the Austrian school, whose members share the tent's exclusion while rejecting nearly every proposition the other tenants hold, including the labour theory, the planning conclusions many draw, and the legitimacy of most of the mathematics the Sraffians practise. A category that contains both the Austrians and the Marxists is a category about position, about standing outside a centre, not about doctrine.

Marxist economics is one tradition within that tent, distinguished by its definite core: the labour theory of value in one of its contested forms, the theory of surplus extraction in production, the analysis of accumulation and crisis, and the claim that capitalism is a historical system rather than the natural form of economic life. Several of the tent's other tenants reject one or more of those commitments outright. The post-Keynesian who derives distribution from markups and demand need not believe that profit originates in unpaid labour; the institutionalist may find the value theory a scholastic survival; the ecological economist may regard the tradition's productivism as part of the disease. Conversely, some Marxist economists have regarded the tent itself with suspicion, as a dilution: a pluralism that papers over the difference between criticising the mainstream's conclusions and replacing its foundations. The pluralist organisations of heterodox economics exist precisely to hold these incompatible tenants in one coalition, and their conferences have the cheerful incoherence of a parliament of exiles.

For the purposes of this comparison, the tent's existence cuts two ways. It weakens any account of marginality that rests on the unique wickedness of the exclusion: the mainstream marginalises a whole ecology of rivals, by the same filters, and Marxist economics is the largest and oldest of the exiled populations rather than a singled-out victim. And it complicates the domain verdict's bookkeeping, because in several domains, distribution and crisis among them, the strong heterodox performances belong to the family rather than to the Marxist line alone, a caution already entered for the post-Keynesian bridge and to be borne in mind throughout. The comparison asked of this article is between the Marxist tradition and the mainstream, and it is conducted on those terms; but a reader who mistakes the tent for the tradition will misread both the marginality and the record.

## The myth of the rejected formalism

A second confusion holds that Marxist economics is marginal because it refuses the formal and quantitative methods of modern economics, preferring literary interpretation to models and measurement. The confusion survives because it is half true of the tradition's most visible products and wholly false of the tradition as a whole. Marx himself left mathematical manuscripts, worked his reproduction schemas in numerical tables, and corresponded about the calculus; the twentieth century's Marxist economics includes the linear models of the Soviet planning literature, the input-output constructions of Leontief, who learned his trade partly in that milieu, the formal growth models of the Feldman tradition, a substantial econometrics of profit rates and labour values, and game-theoretic reconstructions of the theory of exploitation and class built in the mainstream's own idiom by economists working in its own leading departments. Whatever else excluded the tradition from the profession, it was not an incapacity or a principled refusal to formalise. The formalism was produced, in quantity, and the gates stayed shut, which is one of the cleaner pieces of evidence that the filter operates on the object and the questions, not on the presence of technique.

The half-truth deserves its due. The tradition's centre of literary gravity does sit in long-form, historically saturated argument, and its internal prestige economy rewards the comprehensive interpretation over the estimable model more reliably than the mainstream's does. Marxist journals publish, alongside their formal work, a great deal of exegesis, and a young economist choosing where to invest a career can see plainly that the tradition's highest honours go to works the professional machinery cannot process. That is a real feature of the tradition's culture, and it belongs in the intellectual account of its marginality: not a rejection of formalism, but a reward structure in which formalism is one dialect among several and not the obligatory one. The mainstream, by contrast, made one dialect compulsory, and the difference between a tradition where the technical language is optional and one where it is the price of admission explains the shape of the border better than any story about innumeracy.

There is a consequence for readers. When a Marxist proposition arrives dressed in the mainstream's own formal clothes, as the analytical Marxism of the late twentieth century dressed the theory of exploitation in the clothes of general equilibrium and game theory, the profession reads it, cites it, and argues with it on equal terms, and then files it, instructively, under the author's name rather than under the tradition's: the finding crosses, the framework does not, the pattern this article has met twice already. The episode is the strongest single demonstration that the marginality is of the framework and its questions, not of the competence of its practitioners or the rigour of their tools.

## Tools that cross the border

If the frameworks are at odds, what of the instruments? A working economist's kit, in either tradition, contains devices that turn out to be less loyal than their owners. Input-output analysis, built by a mainstream Nobel laureate from materials that include the reproduction schemas of the second volume of Capital and the physiocratic tableau behind them, serves planners, mainstream empiricists, and Marxist model-builders indifferently. Econometrics is nobody's property: the regression that tests a mainstream demand curve tests, in other hands, the correlation of prices with labour values or the trend of the profit rate. The national accounts themselves, the data infrastructure of all modern macroeconomics, were built by men working in the classical surplus tradition's shadow and divide the product into categories, wages, profits, operating surplus, that a classical economist of any party can read. Even game theory, the mainstream's signature instrument of the later twentieth century, has been turned to modelling class conflict, bargaining over the working day, and the strategic structure of accumulation by writers inside the Marxist tradition, with results the mainstream's journals have published.

The traffic is not symmetrical, and the asymmetry is informative. The Marxist tradition imports mainstream instruments continuously and without doctrinal crisis: a Marxist econometrician estimating a profit-rate series, or a Marxist theorist using an optimisation result, is doing ordinary work, and the tradition's journals are full of it, because the tradition's identity lies in its object and its questions, which survive the borrowing of a technique. The mainstream imports Marxist instruments rarely, and then usually by independent reinvention rather than by borrowing, as the convergence literature showed: the Kaleckian markup, the reserve army's reappearance as the natural rate's disciplinary cousin, the endogenous credit of the post-Keynesian bridge rediscovered inside central banking. The difference tracks the definitional charters once more. A tradition defined by its object can borrow any tool that serves the object. A tradition defined partly by its method must be careful about tools that carry another object inside them, and its caution, not any ignorance, is what keeps the border where it is.

For the student reader, the practical upshot is liberating in both directions. Learning the mainstream's instruments is not apostasy for a Marxist, and never has been in the tradition's own practice; the instruments are techniques of measurement and inference whose findings then have to be interpreted, and interpretation is where the frameworks contend. Equally, a mainstream economist who picks up a Marxist category as an instrument, a markup theory of distribution, a bargaining theory of the wage share, is not thereby converted, and the category will do its work in the new house only after renovation. Tools cross borders the way words cross languages: changed by the journey, and never carrying the whole country with them.

## Why the two traditions stopped reading each other

The bibliographic silence between the traditions, each citing its own ancestors and ignoring the other's journals, is a fact with a history, and the history is partly one of mutual provocation. The marginal revolution of the eighteen seventies refounded economics on ground from which Marx's construction was absent, and the founding generation of the new economics engaged Marx mostly to refute him, most famously in the Austrian critique that pronounced his system closed and contradictory at its birth. Marx's side answered in kind where it answered at all, treating the new economics as a vulgarisation that had traded the anatomy of the system for the psychology of the consumer, and the second and third generations of each tradition inherited the other's refutation as a settled verdict requiring no re-reading. By the middle of the twentieth century the positions had hardened into curricula: the mainstream's students met Marx, if at all, in a history-of-thought week as a superseded classical; the Marxist tradition's students met the mainstream as a technique to be used and an ideology to be unmasked, rarely as a literature to be argued with page by page.

Institutional machinery then did what machinery does. Separate journals, separate conferences, separate associations, and separate hiring networks removed the occasions on which reading across the border is unavoidable; a referee system that returns cross-border work unread for want of a competent referee completed the circuit. The Cold War added its chill, particularly in the United States, where engagement with the Marxist literature carried professional risks quite apart from its merits, and the postwar synthesis in macroeconomics gave the mainstream a settlement it could teach without reference to any rival at all. By the time the risks faded, the habit was structural. Each tradition now possesses a complete internal conversation, with its own classics, its own controversies, its own career ladder, and a conversation that is complete from inside is the hardest kind to interrupt.

The costs of the silence are distributed as the domain verdict predicts. The mainstream loses the early warnings: literature that had marked concentration, the labour share, and financial instability as central variables waited unread while the instruments to verify those markings were built by people who did not know the markings existed. The Marxist tradition loses the discipline: criticisms that would have forced its claims into testable shape, measurements that would have separated its strong magnitudes from its weak ones, arrived decades late or not at all, because the audience trained to supply them was reading something else. A comparison like this one is, among other things, an argument that the silence is expensive for both parties, priced in the coin each side values most: for the mainstream, in phenomena met unprepared; for the Marxist tradition, in claims left unmeasured past the point where measurement could have saved them.

## The different-questions pattern, a third instance

This article is the third time the series has run the same comparison and arrived at the same shape of result, and the brief requires the recurrence to be stated rather than left for the reader to notice. The comparison of Marx with the marginalist theory of value found two doctrines answering different questions: one asking what regulates the exchange proportions of commodities in a system of generalised production for profit, the other asking how a choosing agent ranks the increments of goods before him, with the celebrated quarrel between them fuelled by each side's insistence on marking the other's paper. The comparison of Marx with Keynes on crisis found the same geometry in macroeconomics: a theory of effective demand asking why output settles below capacity in the short period, and a theory of accumulation asking why the system periodically cannot realise the surplus it has produced, with the two constructions overlapping on the phenomenon and diverging on the question. The present comparison generalises both: across the whole territory of economics, the traditions differ first in their object, and their substantive agreements and disagreements alike are best read downstream of that difference.

Three independent instances matter because they convert a rhetorical move into a finding. A single comparison that concludes the parties were answering different questions can be suspected of diplomacy, of dissolving a genuine contradiction into a misunderstanding to spare both sides. The suspicion fails when the same structure appears in the theory of value, in the theory of crisis, and in the general confrontation of the frameworks, arrived at by examining different materials with no need to force the pattern. The pattern is a property of the relationship between Marxist economics and its rivals, not a courtesy of the comparison's author. It also has a testable implication, which the strongest-objection section must face squarely: if the pattern is real, the traditions should be expected to keep talking past each other in new territories as those territories open, in the economics of platforms, of climate, of care, and the early literature of each new territory should show the characteristic double exposure, two literatures forming around different questions under shared names. Watching whether that happens is one way the pattern proposed here can itself be held to account.

The pattern also explains a feature of the dispute that puzzles newcomers: its extraordinary longevity relative to the progress made in settling it. Disputes about answers are settled by evidence, slowly and imperfectly, but they are settled in the sense that positions retire. Disputes about questions are not settled by evidence at all, because the evidence is gathered under the guidance of the questions in dispute. They are settled, if at all, by exhaustion, by institutional change, or by one side's questions ceasing to interest a generation. The Marx and mainstream dispute has lasted a century and a half not because economists are stubborn but because its subject is the charter of the discipline, and charters are not amended by regression results. The domain verdict is the most that honest comparison can extract from such a dispute: not a winner, but a map of where each charter's questions have earned their keep.

## Is Marxist economics wrong?

The question is asked in search boxes and seminar corridors more often than any other in this territory, and it deserves a direct treatment because its grammatical simplicity conceals three different questions that have different answers. Taken first as a question about the tradition's core empirical expectations, its tendencies, the answer the preceding sections have built is mixed by domain: borne out in the direction of concentration and the contested movement of distribution, unborne in the immiseration its popular forms expected, unresolved in the long dispute over the profit rate's tendency. Taken second as a question about its analytical machinery, whether the value framework is coherent and what it explains that rivals do not, the answer is that the machinery is contested at its foundation by competent judges inside and outside the tradition, that its strongest results are the structural ones the domain verdict credits, and that its weakest point is exactly where its founder's presentation was left unfinished, in the passage from values to prices. Taken third as a question about its charter, whether capitalism is the right object and class the right primitive, the answer is that charters are not the kind of thing that is wrong the way a sum is wrong; they are vindicated or exhausted by the work they organise, and this one is still organising work that the rival charter does not generate.

The question's persistence also has a sociological answer. It is asked about Marxist economics as it is asked about no comparably sized research programme, because the tradition's name binds it to a political history its rivals do not carry: no departmental corridor rings with the question whether Sraffian economics is wrong, though its technical claims are as contestable. The political binding inflates the question's stakes and corrupts its answers in both directions, licensing dismissals that no examination of the work supports and defences that no examination of the record permits. The working rule this article has followed, and recommends, is to refuse the question at the level of the whole and to insist on it at the level of the claim: this magnitude, this mechanism, this expectation, in this domain, against this evidence. Traditions are not wrong. Propositions are, and the domain verdict is a machine for finding out which ones.

## The strongest objection

The objection this comparison must state at full strength is not the familiar list of failed prophecies or disputed magnitudes. It is a methodological charge, and it goes to the root. A tradition that claims to study the same economies as the mainstream, the objector says, while producing almost no results that the mainstream finds usable, has failed the ordinary test of a research programme. Sciences earn their standing by yielding findings that others, including their rivals, can build on; a programme whose outputs circulate only among its adherents is not a science but a commentary tradition, whatever the sophistication of the commentary. Worse, the defence this article has mounted throughout, that the traditions answer different questions, is on inspection an unfalsifiable one. Any comparative failure can be absorbed by it: every domain in which the Marxist record is weak is reclassified as a domain belonging to the rival's questions, every success is retained as home ground, and the domain verdict itself becomes a machine for converting defeats into boundary disputes. A defence that no evidence could embarrass is not a defence but an immunising strategy, and the history of ideas knows exactly what such strategies are worth.

The objection gains force from the comparison's own evidence. The convergence sections credited the Marxist tradition with early reports on concentration, distribution, and finance; the objector replies that an early report which the reporting tradition could not itself measure, model, or stabilise into findings its rivals must use is closer to a hunch vindicated than to a programme succeeding, and that the history of thought is littered with frameworks that guessed the coastline and drew the interior wrong. On the objection's reading, the domain verdict's pattern is an artefact: of course the Marxist tradition looks strong where the comparison scores tendencies over centuries, because its claims are framed at a level where disconfirmation takes centuries too, while the mainstream submits to the indignity of quarterly tests and fails them in public. Rigorously pressed, the objection concludes that the two-cause account of marginality collapses into the intellectual cause alone, dressed in sociology: the profession marginalised the tradition because the tradition would not, and at its centre could not, meet the standard by which the profession recognises knowledge, and the proceedings and blacklists, where they occurred, were an ugly enforcement of a defensible boundary.

That is the objection at the strength its best proponents would want, and it should be read twice. Any answer that does not feel its weight has not heard it.

## The answer, and the concession

The answer begins by accepting the objection's standard and disputing its bookkeeping. A research programme is indeed to be judged by results others can use, and the Marxist tradition's usable results exist; they are simply misfiled in the objection's ledger, because the users so often received them without the label. The analysis of effective demand's insufficiency that Keynes built was built partly from Marxian materials by Kalecki working inside the tradition; the input-output architecture of modern empirical economics descends in part from the reproduction schemas; the theory of the firm as a coalition of conflicting interests, the economics of imperfect competition's markup, the development literature's dual economies and surplus labour, carry Marxian and classical-surplus ancestry that the bibliographies of a less silent century would show. A programme whose findings must be relabelled before the mainstream can use them is not thereby shown to have no findings. It is shown to stand in the relation to the mainstream that this whole article describes, in which results cross and frameworks do not, and the objection's test, fairly administered, has to count crossings as uses.

The unfalsifiability charge is answered by naming what would falsify the defence. The different-questions account predicts that the traditions' comparative strengths will sort by domain in the pattern the verdict records, and that prediction can fail: had the mainstream's distribution theory, deriving shares from factor scarcities, tracked the long movement of the wage share as successfully as the conflict account did, or had the Marxist tradition produced the better instruments for the allocation questions it dismisses, the domain pattern would collapse and the defence with it. The verdict is therefore not an immunising strategy but a scored empirical claim about two literatures, of exactly the kind the objection demands, and the scoring has been done in the open, row by row, with the mainstream awarded three domains and a share of two more. Immunising strategies do not award the rival the prices row.

Then the concession, which the brief requires to be ungrudging. Outside the domains where its corroborated results lie, the objection has force, and the force should be stated in the objector's own terms. On the theory of value as a derivation of prices, the tradition has not produced, in a century and a half, the settlement its founder's ambition promised, and a candid Marxist economics will teach the transformation dispute as an open problem rather than as a solved one. On measurement, the tradition's weak magnitudes, the rate of profit's contested empirics above all, remain weaker than its rhetoric, and its practitioners' habit of citing tendency where rivals cite estimates is a genuine intellectual fault and not merely a professional handicap. And on the standard of usable results itself, the tradition's ratio of commentary to construction is higher than a healthy programme's should be; too much of its best intelligence goes into interpreting its classics and too little into the instruments by which classics are superseded. The concession does not save the objection whole, because the objection claimed the whole territory and the ledger does not support it there. It saves the objection's core: that in the territories where the mainstream's tests are the operative ones, the Marxist tradition has too often answered with a charter when it owed a result.

## The firm: production function or theatre of command

Step inside the enterprise and the two charters produce two different interiors. In the mainstream's benchmark, the firm is a production function wearing legal dress: a set of technical possibilities for converting inputs into outputs, fronted by a decision rule that selects the most profitable combination. What happens inside, the organisation of the working day, the hierarchy of command, the effort bargain between those who direct and those who perform, is for the benchmark a matter of engineering and contract, tractable when needed by the theory of the firm as a nexus of agreements, but not constitutive of the economics. The worker inside the production function is an input among inputs, distinguished by being rented rather than bought, and the authority the employer exercises over the input's use during the rented hours is a detail of the rental agreement.

In the Marxist interior, the firm is the place where the theory's central transaction is completed or fails. Labour power has been purchased; labour, the actual expenditure of effort, has not and cannot be, since it exists only in the performance and its quantity depends on supervision, custom, resistance, and the state of the contest between the parties. Production is therefore simultaneously a technical process and a relation of command, and the organisation of the labour process, the division of tasks, the pacing of machinery, the architecture of oversight, is shaped by the employer's need to extract the purchased capacity's use as much as by the engineer's need to combine materials efficiently. The two interiors are the same building surveyed by different professions, and each survey misses what the other maps: the production function cannot see the effort bargain, and the labour-process account cannot price the output. The domain pattern repeats at the scale of the single firm, which is why this section stands here as a miniature of the whole comparison: the frameworks are most illuminating about each other when their claims are set at the same site and allowed to be about different floors of it.

The empirical literature has, characteristically, drifted toward the middle of the building. The mainstream's own personnel economics and its theory of incentives now model the employment relation as a continuing negotiation under asymmetric information, in which effort is elicited rather than assumed, and the firm's internal hierarchy is an object of analysis rather than a veil. Nothing in that literature needs the labour-process theory, and its authors rarely cite it; but the floor it now works on is the floor the Marxist survey always said was the load-bearing one, and a reader comparing the interiors across the century can watch the mainstream's floor plan being redrawn, room by room, toward the older survey, without the surveyors being consulted. The convergence protocol applies: findings shared, framework distinct, priority noted, vindication declined.

## Competition: leveller or concentrator

The market's central institution carries opposite signs in the two theories. For the mainstream, competition is the great leveller and the engine of the system's virtues: it drives price toward cost, profit toward the normal return, and resources toward their highest-valued employment, and its absence is the definition of the problems, monopoly, rent, and stagnation, that policy exists to correct. The theory's normative glow comes from this source; a competitive economy is the benchmark against which every actual economy is measured and found improvable. For the Marxist tradition, competition is real and ferocious but its function is different: it is the coercive mechanism by which the system's laws are imposed on each individual capital, the external compulsion that forces accumulation, cheapening, and expansion whether or not the individual capitalist wills them. Competition, in a celebrated image, makes each capitalist the executioner of a logic he did not choose. And its long-run product, on this account, is not the perpetuation of itself but its own partial abolition, as the victors of each round absorb the vanquished and the field narrows toward the concentration the markup literature measures.

The two signs are reconcilable once the time horizons are separated, and separating them is the kind of work the domain verdict exists to encourage. In the short run of the particular market, competition levels: margins are contested, prices are disciplined, entrants threaten incumbents, exactly as the mainstream describes, and the Marxist account requires the same facts, since its equalisation of profit rates runs through competition's channels. In the long run of the system's development, the same process concentrates: the weapons of competition are the larger scale, the deeper pocket, the cheaper credit, and they are distributed by past victories, so that the leveller of margins is simultaneously the manufacturer of giants. Each tradition has built its theory at the horizon that flatters its charter: choice and allocation in the market's present tense, accumulation and transformation in the system's long exposure. The evidence of the convergence sections, rising concentration alongside vigorously contested margins in most markets most of the time, is what both horizons look like when they are photographed together.

## Labour markets: the reserve army and the natural rate

Unemployment is the phenomenon each tradition explains in the other's despite. The Marxist account treats the unemployed not as a malfunction but as a functional population of the system: a reserve army of labour, in the founding phrase, generated by accumulation itself as machinery displaces workers faster than expansion reabsorbs them, and serving accumulation in turn by disciplining the employed, whose wage claims are moderated by the queue at the gate. The size of the reserve governs the balance of the wage bargain, and its perpetual regeneration explains why full employment, when it approaches, is experienced by the system as a crisis of discipline and profitability rather than as an unqualified good. The mainstream account, in its benchmark forms, treats unemployment as a deviation with named causes: wages held above their clearing level by institutions or custom, frictions in matching workers to vacancies, the search behaviour of the jobless themselves, and in its Keynesian register, a deficiency of demand that policy can repair. At full employment the mainstream expects pressure on wages and prices, and has built its policy doctrine around the rate of unemployment at which that pressure becomes inflation.

Set side by side, the two accounts agree on the mechanism closest to the surface, that slack in the labour market weakens wage claims and fullness strengthens them, and they disagree about the provenance and the prognosis. For one, the slack is a policy variable and a friction; for the other, it is a product the system secretes and a condition of its normal governance, so that a durable full employment under capitalism is not a target to be hit but a contradiction to be managed. The postwar decades ran the experiment in several countries at once: long stretches near full employment accompanied, as the Marxist account expects, by wage militancy, profit squeeze, and political crisis of the settlement, and then a restoration of slack, by policy and by globalisation, accompanied by the moderation of claims and the fall in the labour share the convergence sections recorded. The mainstream's own framework digested those decades by moving its natural rate and adding hysteresis, the process by which slack, once created, perpetuates itself, which is the reserve army's mechanism in the vocabulary of the other charter. The labour market is thus one of the clearest sites in the whole comparison: two theories, one mechanism, two depths of explanation, and a record on which the deeper one was written first.

## The state: referee, committee, or contested terrain

Economic policy forces the question of what the state is, and the charters answer characteristically. The mainstream's working state is a referee standing outside the game: an agent, imperfect and occasionally corruptible in the public-choice variant, but conceptually external to the economy it regulates, taxing, spending, and correcting failures according to a welfare calculus the economist supplies. The Marxist tradition's state is inside the game it supposedly referees: formed within a society divided by the production relation, conditioned by its dependence on the accumulation it must keep going, since its revenues, its employment figures, and its electoral survival all ride on investment decisions it does not make, and staffed and lobbied through channels that weight its ear toward the owners of capital. The famous aphorisms of the tradition overstate the case into conspiracy; the serious literature states it as a structural constraint, that a government of any party which frightens capital into an investment strike will be disciplined by the consequences, and that this discipline operates without a single meeting needing to be held.

The convergence pattern appears here in a muted form. The mainstream's own political economy has spent two generations building the state back into its models as an interested party: rent-seeking, regulatory capture, the median voter's arithmetic, the revolving door, are its own categories now, and they carry much of the critical freight the Marxist account loaded onto class. What remains distinctively Marxist is the claim that the state's partiality is structural rather than incidental: not a failure of refereeing to be corrected by better rules, but a consequence of the state's dependence on a process owned by one of the parties, which better rules can moderate and cannot repeal. The historical record of social democratic governments, elected to tame capital and disciplined instead by its confidence, is the tradition's standing exhibit. The mainstream's counter-exhibit is the real variation among capitalist states, the welfare states that did redistribute on a scale the simple version of the theory should have forbidden, and the honest Marxist literature treats that variation as its own problem, to be explained by the balance of forces in each case rather than waved away. On the state as on money, the verdict is architectural sympathy for the Marxist question and operational respect for the mainstream's technique of governing the state that exists.

## The world economy: exchange between equals and between unequals

Between nations the two charters diverge with particular clarity because the mainstream's proudest theorem lives here. The theory of comparative advantage shows that trade benefits both parties even when one is more productive at everything, and it remains, after two centuries, among the most robust and least intuitive results in the discipline: exchange between unequals in efficiency is nevertheless exchange to mutual gain, provided the specialisation follows comparative rather than absolute costs. The Marxist tradition's world-economy literature, from the theory of imperialism through the dependency writers to the theory of unequal exchange, answers not by denying the theorem but by changing the question, from the gains of trade in a period to the reproduction of hierarchy across periods. Its claim is that the world market's normal operation transfers value toward the centres of high productivity and capital intensity, locks the periphery into supplying primary products whose producers' wages are held at levels the centre's are not, and so reproduces, through exchange itself and without any fraud, the underdevelopment it appears merely to reflect.

The record of the later twentieth century gives each side its exhibits and its embarrassments. The East Asian economies that industrialised through trade are the mainstream's standing answer to the hierarchy thesis, proof that the ladder can be climbed by the route the theorem blesses, though the climbing, on inspection, was done with states directing credit, protecting infants, and disciplining capital in ways neither pure charter prescribed. The primary exporters whose terms of trade ground downward across decades, and the manufacturing peripheries whose wages stayed pinned while their productivity rose, are the other tradition's exhibits, and the falling labour share within the rich economies themselves, as production was globalised, is the mechanism by which the two stories meet: the world market's hierarchy was imported into the centre's own distribution. The domain verdict does not award the world economy its own row, since the brief fixes the rows at seven, but the pattern of the seven repeats here faithfully: within the period, the theorem and the price system, the mainstream's; across the development of the whole, the hierarchy and its reproduction, the questions the Marxist tradition taught the subject to ask.

## The classroom as evidence

A reader who wants to see the definitional charter at work need not read the controversies; the textbooks will do. The standard introductory course, in the form taught to millions, opens with scarcity and choice, proceeds through supply and demand to the theory of the firm and the factor markets, and arrives, if it arrives anywhere historical at all, at a closing chapter of applications. Marx appears, when he appears, in a history-of-thought interlude, between Ricardo and the marginalists, as the classical school's rebellious heir whose system the profession examined and set aside. The placement is the charter's verdict in curricular form: Marxist economics is presented as a episode in the discipline's past rather than as a living alternative in its present, and the student who wishes to pursue it learns that the pursuit leads out of the economics building, into the departments where the tradition's diaspora teaches. The question why Marxist economics is not taught, which the search queries for this topic ask with real puzzlement, is answered by that placement more honestly than by any polemic: it is not taught, in the main, because the training that certifies economists is built from the rival charter's materials, and a course is a small profession in itself, with its own filter of prerequisites, its own examinable forms, its own sense of what a question looks like.

The curricular fact has a second face that the intellectual account of marginality needs. A living tradition requires a reproduction mechanism: students taught by practitioners, trained in the tradition's techniques, and placed where they can teach in turn. Marxist economics lost that mechanism inside economics departments in the postwar decades, and its survival since then has run on substitute mechanisms, the neighbouring disciplines, the independent journals and institutes, the summer schools and reading groups, the international networks where its strength in Latin America, India, and parts of Europe has refreshed its Anglophone remnant. A tradition reproducing by substitutes reproduces unevenly: strong in interpretation and history, thin in the cumulative technical training a department provides without thinking about it. Some of the intellectual weaknesses the objection section charged, the ratio of commentary to construction, the unmeasured magnitudes, are downstream of the classroom fact, and belong in the professional column of marginality's ledger after all. The two causes, followed far enough, braid into one another, which is what an honest two-cause account should discover.

## Want beside plenty: the standing riddle

The Marxist tradition's most durable rhetorical asset is a riddle it did not invent but made its emblem: that capitalist economies characteristically produce poverty in the midst of potential plenty, workers idle beside machines idle beside needs unmet, in slumps that no scarcity of resources explains. For the mainstream charter, grounded in scarcity, the riddle is a paradox to be dissolved by frictions and demand failures, real but exceptional states of a system whose elementary problem is that there is never enough. For the Marxist charter, the riddle is the system's signature: production is production for sale at a profit, not for need, and the mediation of money and profitability between capacity and want means the two can fall apart without any resource running out, so that the economy's elementary problem is not scarcity as such but the social form that manufactures scarcity of access amid abundance of capacity. The unemployed worker and the shuttered factory in the same town are, on this reading, not a coincidence of mismatches but the system photographed in its normal negative.

The riddle's force should not be oversold, and the mainstream has answers at its own level that deserve their weight. Most wants in settled times are indeed served through the price system with a reliability no alternative arrangement has matched at scale, and the plenty of the riddle is potential in a strong sense: capacities exist, but coaxing them into the right goods, in the right places, through voluntary cooperation, is the allocation problem, which the mainstream's craft addresses and the riddle does not. Where the riddle retains its bite is at the level of the system's own accounting of itself. A civilisation that measures its wealth by what can be sold will periodically find its warehouses full and its people wanting, and will describe the finding, in its official economics, as a series of unfortunate frictions. The tradition that was built to find the description inadequate has, at minimum, chosen its emblem well, and the emblem's persistence in popular argument, long after the technical disputes have been forgotten by everyone involved, is evidence that it names an experience the rival charter handles awkwardly.

## Rent: the classical survival

One territory shows the classical ancestry of both traditions with unusual clarity, because both still work it with recognisably classical tools. Rent, the payment for the use of land and other assets in fixed supply, was for Ricardo the paradigm of a distributional magnitude: not a reward for a contribution but a share of the surplus, determined by the differential fertility and location of sites and by the pressure of accumulation against their limits. Marx took the construction over entire, adding the distinction between the differential rent of better sites and the absolute rent that landed property as a class extracts by withholding land from production until it is paid. The mainstream, for its part, generalised the concept in the opposite direction: economic rent became any payment to a factor above what is needed to keep it in its current use, a category that now covers the earnings of sports stars and the profits of firms with market power as comfortably as the ground rents of landlords, and that powers a clean analytics of taxation, since a tax on pure rent distorts nothing.

The two treatments meet and diverge exactly as the pattern predicts. They meet on the phenomenon: an income that is a claim on the product rather than a prize for producing, whose size is set by scarcity and position rather than by effort or cost. They diverge on the moral and analytical weight the fact is asked to carry. For the mainstream, rent is a special case at the margin of the price system, theoretically tidy and practically important mainly to tax designers. For the Marxist and classical line, rent is a window into the general truth about distribution: that the great incomes of a capitalist economy are shares of a surplus, settled by property and position, and that the landlord's obvious case differs from the industrialist's only in being harder to disguise. The modern city has made the window larger than either tradition's founders could have expected: the housing costs that dominate the budgets of the young in the successful economies are ground rent in contemporary dress, and the literatures now growing around land, zoning, and urban inequality in the mainstream's own journals are, in the strictest sense, Ricardian. Priority, again, is a fact; vindication, again, is not on offer; but a student deciding which tradition taught the subject to see land clearly has the record to consult.

## Nature and the environment: externality or rift

The economy's relation to the natural world has become, in the span of a generation, a test both traditions are required to sit, and their answers display the charters in modern dress. The mainstream's characteristic construction is the externality: pollution and depletion are costs pushed outside the price system, and the remedy is to bring them inside, by tax, by tradable permit, by assignment of property rights, so that the allocation mechanism can do with carbon what it does with cloth. The construction is powerful within its terms and has produced the working instruments of actual climate policy. Its limit is the charter's limit: nature enters as a set of constraints and missing markets around an economy whose growth and structure are otherwise given, and the question whether the accumulation process itself has a metabolic direction, an appetite for throughput that pricing can moderate but not reverse, falls outside the frame. The Marxist tradition's counter-construction, developed from Marx's remarks on the soil and the town-country division into the literature of the metabolic rift, holds that capitalism's relation to nature is one of extraction built into the production relation: nutrients, energy, and materials are drawn from the periphery and the earth, concentrated in the centres, and discharged as waste, with the rift widening as accumulation proceeds and each repair displacing the damage in space or time.

Between the constructions, the empirical century has supplied evidence in the pattern this article has learned to expect. The mainstream's instruments work where they are applied: priced emissions fall, assigned rights get husbanded, and the craft of environmental policy is a real achievement of the applied mainstream. The trajectory of the whole, the rising throughput and the relocation of dirty production from the regulating centres to the unregulated periphery rather than its abolition, follows the rift literature's expectation more closely than the externality frame's hope that internalising costs would bend the system's total metabolism. A domain verdict extended to this territory, which the brief's seven rows do not include, would repeat the familiar distribution of honours: the mechanisms of repair to the mainstream, the diagnosis of why repair keeps being outrun to the tradition that treats the economy's metabolism as a property of its social form. That the newest territory reproduces the oldest pattern is the pattern's most recent piece of evidence, entered here with the same caution as the rest: a pattern that explains everything explains nothing, and this one earns its credence only by being scored, domain by domain, in the open.

## Are higher wages good for growth? The wage-led question

Buried inside the distribution domain is a question with direct policy stakes on which the traditions' machinery gives opposed answers, and it makes a good specimen of the comparison at working level. Are higher wages a cost that depresses output, or a source of the demand that calls output forth? The mainstream's partial instinct is the first: a wage is the price of labour, and a higher price, other things equal, means less employment of it. Its macroeconomic register complicates the instinct with demand effects, but the microeconomic grammar keeps reasserting itself in policy argument, where the wage appears in the employer's cost line and the worker's income line is somebody else's problem. The Kaleckian and Marxist line begins from the composition of demand: wages are spent, promptly and almost entirely, on the economy's products, while profits are partly saved, so a shift of income toward wages raises consumption demand relative to investment demand, and whether the whole economy expands or contracts under such a shift depends on the balance of the two responses, an empirical property of the economy's structure that the literature calls its demand regime.

The resulting research programme, on wage-led and profit-led growth, is one of the heterodox tradition's most successful exports, taken up by international labour and development institutions and argued over with mainstream econometric tools by scholars of several loyalties. Its interest for this comparison is double. Substantively, it shows a characteristically Marxist question, the wage as simultaneously cost and income, the dual character that follows from treating distribution as the system's central contest, generating a testable literature the mainstream's grammar alone did not produce: within the price-of-labour frame, the question in its general form cannot even be posed, because each firm's cost saving is the system's demand loss only when the firms are aggregated through the class accounts the frame does not keep. Methodologically, it shows the export pattern again: the literature travels under post-Keynesian colours, is estimated with the profession's instruments, and is cited without the Marxist framework that supplies its founding distinction. The wage share's fall, documented in the convergence sections, is the experiment the planet ran on the question; economies that cheapened labour for a generation discovered, in stagnating demand and swelling household debt, the second term of the dual character asserting itself.

## Household debt: the demand engine's substitute

The falling wage share posed a puzzle the system's subsequent history answered in a way neither tradition's founders foresaw but one tradition's framework accommodates more naturally. If wages stagnated while output grew, who bought the product? Part of the answer was exports and investment, and part, in the leading Anglophone economies, was borrowing: households sustained and raised their consumption across decades of flat real wages by taking on debt, against houses whose rising prices the credit expansion itself was inflating. A literature within and around the Marxist tradition named the arrangement early: the privatisation of the demand-management function, the replacement of the welfare state's transfers and the wage's growth by the credit system's advances, with the financial sector collecting the interest on the substitution. On that reading, the financialisation the money sections described and the distribution the labour-share sections measured are one process, the system bridging its own demand gap with its workers' future wages, and the crisis that ended the arrangement was the bridge reaching its span.

The mainstream's account of the same decades was written first in the vocabulary of financial innovation and the democratisation of credit, and after the collapse in the vocabulary of household leverage, deleveraging, and balance-sheet recession, which is to say in a vocabulary converging on the mechanisms the other account had named while declining its frame. The episode is entered here not as another priority claim but as an illustration of the domain pattern's forward use. A reader equipped with the two charters, set the problem of an economy whose wages have stagnated for a generation, would use the Marxist charter to ask where the demand is coming from and what is bridging the gap, and the mainstream charter to model the bridge's stability and price its risks. The crisis arrived in the gap between the questions: the first was asked, in print, by the marginal tradition, and the second was answered, too late, by the central one. Neither performance cancels the other; together they are the strongest argument this article can assemble for the proposition that the two traditions are complements misfiled as rivals, which is the domain verdict's final form.

## Platforms and the new provinces of the capital relation

The newest territory on which the pattern can be watched forming is the digital platform economy, and it is worth a section precisely because the literature there is young enough that the double exposure has not yet hardened into mutual silence. The mainstream's construction of the platform is an exercise in its characteristic strengths: the platform as a two-sided market, solving a coordination problem between groups whose presence attracts one another, pricing access on one side to subsidise the other, and raising, in its maturity, questions of market power that the antitrust apparatus can process. The construction is genuinely illuminating about how such businesses price and grow, and the policy debates it informs are conducted in its terms. The Marxist construction begins elsewhere, from the production relation: the platform as an owner of infrastructure it did not merely build but enclosed, collecting what its critics describe in the classical vocabulary as rent on access to a privately held condition of production and circulation; the labour performed through it reclassified, by legal form, out of the wage relation while remaining subject to direction as detailed as any factory's, by algorithm rather than by foreman; and the data exhaust of social life itself drawn into accumulation as a free input.

Both constructions are in print, developing, and largely not citing each other, which is the early literature of a new territory behaving as the third-instance section predicted. The evidence that will score the territory is still arriving: whether the platform's margins behave as rents on an enclosed commons or as returns competed away in the mainstream's story; whether its labour relation stabilises as employment, as independent contracting, or as a third legal thing the contest between the charters will name; whether its concentration follows the accumulation dynamic or the antitrust remedy's cycle. A comparison written in the domain verdict's discipline does not need to settle the territory now. It needs to show the reader the two instruments being unpacked at the territory's edge, so that the arriving evidence can be scored as it comes, and to note that the reader who has followed the argument this far can predict, with uncomfortable precision, what each side's survey will find salient and what each will file as somebody else's department.

## The shape of the wage structure

Distribution has a second storey the comparison should visit, the distribution inside the wage itself. Across the later twentieth century the dispersion of earnings in the leading economies widened strikingly: the premium earned by the educated rose, the middle of the structure hollowed, and the top of it, the salaries of executives and financiers, detached from the rest by multiples that earlier postwar decades would have found grotesque. The mainstream's flagship explanation is skill-biased technical change: the new technologies raised the productivity of, and therefore the demand for, skilled labour relative to unskilled, and the wage structure repriced accordingly. The explanation is a genuine mechanism, documented in hundreds of careful studies, and its limits are also documented, largely from inside the same literature: the timing of the dispersion's rise does not follow the technology's diffusion as neatly as the account wants, the dispersion rose fastest in the countries whose institutions changed most rather than whose technology changed fastest, and the theory has nothing native to say about the executive multiple, where the scarce skill being rewarded is suspiciously coincident with the power to set one's own pay.

The institutional and class account, shared between the Marxist tradition and the mainstream's own labour-institution wing, assigns the leading roles to the weakening of unions, the erosion of minimum wage floors, the fissuring of employment into contracting chains that strip the periphery of the core's standards, and the conversion of the executive suite into a self-dealing estate. Its evidence is the cross-country pattern: economies that adopted the same technologies with intact bargaining institutions show markedly less dispersion, a comparison no technology story can absorb without strain. The episode belongs in this article because it is the domain pattern at second order. The mainstream's mechanism is real, its instruments measured the phenomenon superbly, and the explanation that best fits the assembled measurements is the one whose variables, bargaining power, institutional floors, the social organisation of the firm, are the other charter's native furniture. Mainstream labour economics, to its credit, has been the most permeable wing of the whole discipline, and its internal argument between the technology and institution accounts is one of the places where the border this article describes is thinnest and the traffic across it most nearly two-way.

## Saving and investment: which causes which

Macroeconomics harbours a disagreement about causal direction so basic that the two sides' textbooks draw the arrows oppositely, and it maps onto the comparison with instructive fidelity. In the mainstream's classical construction, saving comes first: households abstain, the abstention accumulates as a fund of loanable resources, and investment is financed from the fund, with the interest rate adjusting to balance the two. The construction embodies the charter's priorities, choice and abstinence at the origin, the market coordinating them, production following. In the construction descending from Keynes and Kalecki, and native to the Marxist circuit analysis behind them, investment comes first: firms invest on the strength of expected profit and bank credit created for the purpose, the spending generates the income out of which the period's saving is then recorded, and the interest rate is a monetary phenomenon governed by the banking system and the central bank rather than the price that rations a pre-existing fund. On this view saving is the residue of the process, not its fuel, and an economy that tries to save its way to growth by abstinence alone will find the abstinence shrinking the income from which saving was to come.

The practical stakes of the arrow's direction are not small: they decide whether austerity in a slump is a discipline that frees resources or a contraction that destroys them, whether the state's deficit crowds out private investment or crowds it in, and whether the glut of desired saving that later mainstream macroeconomics diagnosed in the advanced economies, under the name of secular stagnation, is a puzzle or a prediction. That diagnosis, reached inside the mainstream's own institutions from its own data, is the Kaleckian construction's conclusion in the other charter's vocabulary, and it arrived, characteristically, without reference to the literature that had held the arrow reversed for eighty years. The episode is the convergence pattern in macroeconomics' engine room, and it is recorded here under the standing protocol: the finding is shared, the frameworks remain distinct, the priority is the heterodox line's, and the vindication of a whole framework by a single reversed arrow is declined, because a single arrow, however central, is not a system.

## A working protocol for reading across the border

The comparison yields, for the reader who must actually use both literatures, a protocol of five habits, offered here as the practical residue of the domain verdict. Grade the question before the answer: decide whether the problem in hand is a problem of allocation within a structure or of the structure's own motion, and let the assignment choose the literature, not loyalty. Read each tradition's strong form: the mainstream in its best empirical work rather than its textbook catechism, the Marxist tradition in its measured magnitudes and serious histories rather than its slogans, since each side's popularisers are the other side's best recruiting officers. Watch the variables the other charter watches: the mainstream reader who adds the wage share, the markup trend, and the credit cycle to a dashboard, and the Marxist reader who adds relative prices and policy elasticities, will each be surprised less often than their traditions left them. Distrust conversions: when a finding crosses the border, ask what framework received it and what the receiving framework had to change to house it, because the change is where the information is. And keep the ledgers double: every failure quoted from one side's record should send the reader to the other's, not to cancel the debt but to restore the exchange rate at which failures are honestly compared.

The protocol sounds modest, and is. Its modesty is the point. A dispute about charters is not ended by a comparison article; it is made usable by one. The reader who came asking which economics is true leaves, if this article has done its work, asking a better question: which economics is true of this, and how would anyone know.

## What would change the verdict

Because the domain verdict claims to be an empirical scoring rather than a diplomatic formula, it owes the reader a statement of the evidence that would rescore its rows. The prices row would move if a value-based or production-price account produced, at scale, forecasts of relative price movements that beat the working apparatus in open competition; nothing in the verdict's logic protects the row from that event, and its occurrence would be the single most damaging possible blow to the mainstream's side of the table. The distribution row would move if the long movements of the shares were shown, across countries and decades, to track measurable technology parameters more faithfully than they track the fortunes of bargaining institutions, reversing the reading the convergence sections gave. The crisis row would move if the economies of the coming generation, under whatever regimes they adopt, ceased to generate crises from their internal operations and suffered them only at the hands of identifiable external blows competently insured against. The technical change row would move if the direction of innovation, under conditions of cheap and abundant labour, turned persistently toward using it rather than saving it.

Conversely, the rows the mainstream holds would be strengthened in ways the Marxist tradition should be able to recognise without ceasing to be itself. If the professional closure loosened, as in places it slowly is, and the tradition's measured claims were taken up, tested, and found wanting in the open, the intellectual column of marginality's ledger would grow at the professional column's expense, and the two-cause account would require revision in the direction the tradition least enjoys. If the transformation dispute were settled to the satisfaction of disinterested mathematicians, in either direction, a century's ambiguity would close, to the great benefit of everyone's teaching. And if the mainstream's empirical expansion into the marked territories continued until its models of distribution, concentration, and crisis no longer needed the phenomena translated out of class terms to handle them, the domain map itself would need redrawing, with the border moved from between the traditions to between the generations. The verdict is offered as the best current reading of a record that is still being written, and the reader is invited to keep the score.

## Misreadings to correct

Five misreadings circulate through this territory so persistently that correcting them is part of the comparison itself. The first holds that the two traditions disagree about answers to the same questions, so that one must simply be wrong where they differ. The preceding sections have shown the geometry to be otherwise: the charters differ first, the questions follow the charters, and most celebrated disagreements are two true answers photographed as a contradiction. Correcting this misreading does not dissolve all disagreement; the transformation dispute and the theory of distribution contain real contradictions that evidence and argument must settle. It dissolves the false disagreements that waste the argument's energy, which is most of them.

The second misreading holds that marginality settles the intellectual question, in one direction or the other: that exclusion proves suppression of a dangerous truth, or that it proves the judgement of the profession on a failed programme. The two-cause account refuses both inferences, because the record contains both the proceedings and the failed predictions, the closure and the unresolved disputes, and any reading that deletes half the record to make the moral simpler is propaganda wearing the dress of an explanation. Marginality is a fact with two documented causes, and it is evidence about the profession and about the tradition at once, in proportions the sections above attempted to weigh rather than to assert.

The third misreading equates heterodox economics with Marxist economics, as though the tent were the tradition. The tent shelters Austrians and institutionalists and ecological economists whose propositions contradict the Marxist core as flatly as the mainstream's do, and the equation flatters the tradition with numbers it does not have while robbing it of the definite content that distinguishes it. The fourth misreading holds that Marxist economics rejects formal methods, a claim the tradition's own shelves of linear models, input-output constructions, econometric series, and game-theoretic reconstructions refute by existing; what the tradition rejects is the obligation to speak only in one formal dialect, which is a different and much more defensible refusal. The fifth misreading, the mirror of the fourth in the other camp's folklore, holds that convergence on empirical findings implies theoretical agreement, that because the mainstream now measures markups and labour shares it has become, without noticing, Marxist. Findings migrate; frameworks persist; and the difference between sharing a result and sharing a theory is, as the dedicated section argued, the whole of what the sociology of this dispute is about.

Behind the five stands a sixth misreading too large for the list, which is that the comparison itself is a blood sport requiring a corpse. The domain verdict is this article's standing correction to it. Two research programmes, built on different charters, dividing the territory of a complex object between them with a pattern regular enough to state in a table, each with documented achievements the other cannot replicate and documented failures the other is too polite or too partisan to weigh fairly: that is not a corpse and a victor. It is a map, and the reader who has followed it to this point owns the map.

## A quarrel about clocks

Underneath the charters lies a difference of timekeeping that explains more of the mutual incomprehension than any single doctrine. The mainstream's working clock is the short run shading into a long run defined as the position the system tends toward once adjustments complete: quarters for the policy models, a generation at most for the growth accounts, and beyond that a steady state in which the interesting variables have stopped changing. Keynes's gibe that in the long run we are all dead was aimed at an earlier orthodoxy, but the discipline absorbed it as a charter for its clocks: the long run is where excuses live, and serious work is dated nearer the present. The Marxist tradition's working clock runs in the opposite unit: the period of a mode of production and its phases, decades and centuries, within which the short run's events are weather. Its characteristic magnitudes, the concentration of capital, the transformation of the labour process, the rise and decline of hegemonies in production, move at speeds its instruments were built to register and the mainstream's were built to filter out as trend.

Neither clock is the true one; each is an instrument matched to an object, and the mischief begins when a reading from one clock is graded on the other. The mainstream economist who demands that the falling tendency of the profit rate show itself in next year's data, on pain of dismissal as metaphysics, is grading a secular claim by a cyclical instrument, and the dismissal tells us about the grading, not the claim. The Marxist economist who waves away a decade of well-measured price behaviour as surface froth is making the symmetric error, grading cyclical instruments by a secular clock they never claimed to keep. Much of the polemic literature of both traditions is generated by exactly this exchange of clocks, and the domain verdict can be restated in its terms with no loss: a domain is awarded to the tradition whose clock the domain's own processes keep. Distribution moves at the speed of institutions and class settlements, technical change at the speed of accumulation's rounds, crisis at the speed of the credit cycle that binds the two clocks together, which is why crisis is the territory where the traditions collide most violently: it is the one phenomenon that lives on both clocks at once, arriving in a quarter and belonging to a century.

## Each tradition's quarrel with itself

A tradition's maturity can be measured by the quality of its internal critics, and both of these pass the test in ways their polemics against each other rarely acknowledge. The mainstream's self-criticism, at its best, is relentless and specific: its own journals have published the demonstrations that its aggregate capital cannot be consistently measured, that its representative agent is a fiction with known pathologies, that its flagship macroeconomic models excluded the financial mechanisms that produced its greatest predictive failure, and that its publication system rewards novelty over verification to a degree that embarrasses its own methodologists. The criticism does not always change the practice it criticises, and a cynic can call it the programme's immune system, absorbing dissent as further proof of openness. But a programme that prints its own refutations in its leading journals is not the closed church of Marxist folklore, and the Marxist reader who has not followed the mainstream's internal quarrels is arguing with a discipline that exists mainly in the Marxist imagination.

The Marxist tradition's self-criticism is equally real and differently organised. Lacking a centre that can enforce or absorb, it conducts its quarrels in the open, between schools and journals: the value theorists against the historians, the measurers of profit rates against the philosophers of the value form, the political economists against the exegetes, with a ferocity that outsiders misread as disintegration and insiders experience as the tradition's normal metabolism. Its internal critics have charged it, from within, with every fault the strongest objection assembled: with immunising its claims against evidence, with substituting commentary for construction, with keeping magnitudes vague where vagueness protects them. A reader meeting the tradition first in its internal critics will form a higher opinion of it than a reader meeting it in its catechisms, which is the same favour the mainstream's internal critics do for their side. The working protocol for cross-border reading can therefore add a sixth habit to its five: judge each tradition by the critics it produces and keeps, for a tradition is the company its conscience keeps.

## Why the syntheses keep failing

Every generation produces attempts to merge the two frameworks, and their repeated failure is itself a datum the comparison should record. The postwar synthesis merged Keynes into the mainstream by translating his macroeconomics into the equilibrium grammar, at the price, its critics on both sides agree, of his uncertainty and his monetary production economy; the Marxist elements in the Keynesian inheritance, carried by Kalecki, were shed in the same translation. Analytical Marxism merged Marx's questions with the mainstream's methods by the opposite trade, keeping the instruments and the individualist foundations and letting the value theory and the dialectical architecture go; its products were rigorous, influential, and finally filed, as this article noted, under their authors' names rather than the tradition's, because what made them acceptable to the mainstream was precisely what made them unrepresentative of the framework they set out to rebuild. The Sraffian construction, the most technically complete bridge, stands unmerged after half a century, used by specialists of several loyalties and adopted as a home by none of the great houses. The pattern in the failures is the charters' incompatibility at the level where merger would have to occur. A synthesis must choose its primitive: choice or the production relation, scarcity or the surplus, and every historical synthesis has chosen, whereupon the unchosen primitive's tradition correctly observes that the synthesis is a annexation wearing the other's flag. What survives each attempt is not the merger but a fresh consignment of findings and instruments added to the common stock, which is perhaps how the relationship between these traditions actually progresses: not by union, and not by victory, but by sedimentation, each generation's failed synthesis leaving the shared coastline better mapped than the last.

## The limit case: the socialist calculation debate

Once in the century the two traditions met on exactly the same question, and the encounter is worth isolating because it shows what their disputes look like when the different-questions geometry is absent. The question was whether a socialist economy could calculate: whether, without markets for the means of production and the prices they generate, a planning authority could allocate resources rationally among competing uses. The challenge was issued from the Austrian wing of the emerging mainstream, which argued that rational allocation is impossible without the price system's information, and it was answered from inside a broadly Marxist frame by economists who proposed that a planning board could mimic the market's procedure, setting accounting prices and adjusting them by trial and error as surpluses and shortages appeared. For once the parties shared the question, the criterion, and the terms of victory, and the debate that followed was correspondingly precise, technical, and consequential.

Its outcome is the comparison's most instructive single episode. On the narrow question as posed, the answer that emerged across the following decades was that calculation in the static sense is possible in principle: the trial-and-error procedure is coherent, and its modern descendants run, in effect, inside every large firm's internal planning and every supply chain's software. On the wider questions the debate uncovered as it went, how a planned system would innovate, discipline its managers, or discover what to produce, the challenge's force proved greater, and the historical performance of the planned economies bore much of it out. Marxist economists divide to this day on the verdict's meaning: some treat the debate as a defeat on ground the tradition should never have accepted, the static allocation question being the rival charter's home fixture; others treat it as the tradition's necessary education in the information problem, which any serious post-capitalist construction must answer in its own terms. Either way, the episode refines the article's pattern rather than refuting it. Where the traditions genuinely share a question, they can genuinely lose to one another, and the Marxist tradition's loss, or at best draw, on the calculation question is the standing proof that the domain verdict is not rigged: on the mainstream's home ground, played by the mainstream's rules, the mainstream won, and the tradition that flourishes on tendencies and structures was beaten on allocation in its purest form, the very row the table awards to its rival.

## The blind spot both charters share: the household

One territory indicts both traditions together, and a comparison that omitted it would fail its own standard of even-handedness. The reproduction of labour power, the daily and generational work of feeding, cleaning, nursing, bearing, and raising the people whose capacity to work both theories take as an input, is performed overwhelmingly as unpaid labour in households, and neither charter was built to see it. The mainstream's household is a choosing unit with a single set of preferences, its internal labour invisible by construction; the classical Marxist account registers the reproduction of the worker as a cost inside the value of labour power, the subsistence the wage must cover, and thereby treats the labour that converts subsistence into a renewed worker as a natural process outside the economy's account, performed, as it happens, mostly by women. Feminist economists of several schools, including a major line inside the Marxist tradition itself, the theorists of social reproduction, have spent two generations documenting the omission and rebuilding the categories to repair it: labour power is not found but made, daily, by work that the wage does not pay for, and an economy's account of itself that leaves the making uncounted has missed a condition of everything it does count.

The blind spot's comparative lesson is chastening in the right direction. The domain verdict distributed the territories between the traditions as though the map were complete, and the household shows the map has a terra incognita both surveys sailed past, visible only when a third party, in this case a literature arising from the women's movement's encounter with both traditions, insisted on the survey being run again with different instruments. It is the strongest available reminder that the verdict is a scoring of two records, not a proof that the two records exhaust the phenomena, and that the next correction to the comparison is as likely to come from outside both charters as the last one came from inside the data. The household also returns the argument to its beginning with a small, perfect symmetry: two theories of economics, one founded on choice and one on production, both forgot to count the work that produces the choosers and the producers, because the work was done at home, by women, for no wage, in the place where neither the market's prices nor the factory's clock could be heard ticking.

## Joan Robinson: the economist who read both ways

The comparison has so far been conducted between traditions, and it is worth pausing on the career that shows what the border looks like to a single first-rate mind obliged to live on it. Joan Robinson was formed inside the Cambridge mainstream, a founder of the theory of imperfect competition and the closest of Keynes's collaborators, and she did what almost none of her colleagues did: she read Marx seriously, in the original architecture, and published a small book on his economics that treated it neither as scripture nor as a museum piece but as a living analytical system to be argued with proposition by proposition. The book's example was not followed. Her verdicts were double-edged in the way this article has tried to be: she held that Marx had asked the deepest questions in the subject, about accumulation, distribution, and the system's dynamics, and that his answers were entangled in a value theory that even sympathetic reconstruction could not fully clear; she held simultaneously that the mainstream's own theory of distribution and capital was in worse logical shape than the profession admitted, a charge she then spent a famous controversy proving against its leading figures, on their own ground, with their own tools.

Robinson's later career traced the border's exact contours. Honoured everywhere, she was never absorbed: the profession gave her its lectureships and its medals and declined to follow her into the territory, capital theory, long-period dynamics, the critique of equilibrium as history's substitute, where she insisted the subject's real problems lay. Marxist economists, for their part, claimed her findings and resisted her conclusions, since she granted their founder the questions and refused him the system, and a partisan of either house found her, in the end, unreliable company. Her career is the domain verdict enacted as a biography: the questions she took from the Marxist side proved the durable ones, the tools she took from the mainstream side proved the sharp ones, and the impossibility of holding both inheritances inside one professional identity, even for the most decorated economist of her generation, measures the border's depth more honestly than any institutional statistic. When she remarked that the purpose of studying economics was to learn how to avoid being deceived by economists, the plural was deliberate, and it included, as her readers on both sides discovered, the economists of every charter including her own.

## For students and exam candidates

Your examination risk on this material is the ungraded question: the essay that asks for one tradition's goods in the other tradition's currency and marks the confusion as error. Learn the domain verdict as a grading instrument. For any question set, first identify its domain: a question about the effect of a measure on a market belongs to prices and allocation, where the expected answer runs in supply, demand, and incidence; a question about the movement of shares, the bias of innovation, or the recurrence of crisis belongs to the Marxist tradition's territories, where the expected answer runs in class, accumulation, and the system's dynamics. The strongest comparative answers state the assignment explicitly, give each tradition its home-ground treatment, and then note what the other sees that the first cannot. The model answers collected in the series article on [marxist economics exam answers](/2016/08/15/marxist-economics-exam-answers/) show the expected shape. Keep the domain comparison itself where you can rehearse it until the rows are reflex: redraw the table from memory, and keep the redrawn card among your study notes, for instance in the [VaultBook Marxist economics study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), so the seven judgments and their reasons are available as a single revision instrument. Examiners reward the candidate who can say which framework a question belongs to before answering it; that sentence, earned, is most of this topic's marks.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The domain comparison was built to be taught from, because it lets a seminar stage the confrontation without rigging it. Set the table before the class as a set of wagers rather than a set of conclusions: for each row, require the strongest statement of the losing side before the row's verdict is discussed, and the rows will teach the charters more efficiently than any exposition, since a student who has had to defend the mainstream on distribution or the Marxist tradition on prices has learned where each framework's strength actually ends. The two sensitive territories reward the same treatment. On marginality, divide the class and assign each half one cause to document and the other half to demolish, and the two-cause account will assemble itself out of the wreckage. On convergence, set the markup and labour-share literatures as evidence and make the seminar argue the vindication question in both directions; the distinction between a shared result and a shared theory, once a student has had to defend it against a determined opponent, is never unlearned. The article's misreadings section converts directly into a diagnostic exercise: five errors, each attributable to a named confusion, each correctable in a paragraph. Teach the comparison as a map and the traditions as its territories, and neither side's partisans in the room will feel the lesson was fixed, which is the precondition of their learning anything from it.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For the working researcher, the comparison's yield is a set of open files rather than a set of answers. The domain rows where the verdict is a split decision, growth and money above all, mark the territories where the next synthesis, if the history of syntheses permits another, will have to be built, and the rows where one tradition's early reports await the other's measurement mark the cheapest available research programmes in the discipline: magnitudes the Marxist literature asserts and has not stabilised, the profit rate's long trend chief among them, are invitations to anyone with the mainstream's instruments and an interest in questions whose importance is already established. Historians of the discipline will find the marginality sections a compressed archive of the profession's self-construction, and the convergence sections a case study in how findings migrate between hostile frameworks. Journalists and analysts covering the economy's distributional and financial weather should route their evidentiary claims through the companion article on [testing marxist economics](/2016/08/01/testing-marxist-economics/), which assembles what can and cannot be asserted about the tradition's empirical record, and should treat every claim of vindication or refutation arriving from either camp's publicists with the suspicion this article has tried to make reflexive: ask which domain, which clock, which ledger, and the claim will usually grade itself.

## The same statistics, read two ways

The two traditions share one infrastructure neither built alone: the national accounts, the great statistical construction of the twentieth century that divides the social product into wages, profits, and the rest in categories both sides use. It is worth ending the comparison's survey at this shared workbench, because what each tradition does with the same tables displays the whole argument in miniature. The mainstream reads the accounts as the economy's report card in its own categories: compensation of employees, operating surplus, mixed income, each a magnitude to be explained by the behaviour of the agents who receive it, the profits of the corporate sector a return whose level the theory of the firm and the capital market must rationalise. The Marxist tradition reads the same tables as a classical document in modern dress: the division of the newly produced value between the class that worked and the class that owns, with the statistician's categories requiring translation, the salaries of executives and the incomes of the self-employed redistributed between the columns, before the ratio the theory cares about, the rate of exploitation's statistical shadow, can be computed.

The translation exercise is more instructive than either reading alone. Where the boundary is drawn, between the wages of supervision and the profits of enterprise, between the income of the working proprietor and the return on her little capital, decides the measured shares by margins as large as the movements the shares are then asked to explain, and the two traditions draw it differently because their theories tell them different things about what the magnitudes are. The statistician's neutrality is real but it is the neutrality of a well-made instrument: the categories were designed, in the generation of Keynes and the classical revival, by men who knew exactly which theoretical questions the tables would be used to answer, and the survival of the surplus categories inside the modern accounts is one more piece of the classical inheritance both traditions draw on. For the researcher, the moral is the comparison's moral at the level of data. There are no raw magnitudes in this dispute, only magnitudes cooked by a charter, and the competent reader of either tradition's empirical work begins by asking how the cooking was done.

## Can a Marxist be an economist? The question of double literacy

The forum version of this comparison's question is personal: whether a person can hold the Marxist framework and still be, in the professional sense, an economist. The question presupposes that the two identities are issued by different authorities, and in a way they are. The framework is adopted by reading and conviction; the title is conferred by the certification system, the doctorate, the post, the journal record. The tension the question senses is therefore real, but its resolution in practice has been found by hundreds of working economists, and the resolution has a name in this article's vocabulary: double literacy. The doubly literate economist is trained in the mainstream's apparatus to the standard the apparatus demands, publishes measurably within its rules where the work permits, and keeps the other charter's questions in a second ledger, brought out where the home framework's anomalies accumulate. Such economists staff the bridge territories, much of the labour-institutions literature, and a good share of the empirical work on the Marxist side itself, whose profit-rate series and price-value studies are built by people whose econometrics no referee could fault.

Double literacy has costs that should be recorded as plainly as its benefits. It is expensive to acquire, since it requires two trainings where colleagues need one, and it is taxed throughout a career by both sides' border guards: the mainstream's referees, who suspect the second ledger of contaminating the first, and the tradition's purists, who regard the first training as a contamination of the second. Its practitioners learn to write in two voices and to know, for any given result, which voice the room requires, a skill that can decay into mere bilingualism of convenience if the ledgers are never reconciled. At its best, though, double literacy is the comparison's ideal reader made flesh: someone who can grade a question before answering it because both machineries are actually in the workshop, not merely described in a survey. The traditions' failure to read each other is, in the end, a statistical fact about careers and incentives, not a law of minds, and every doubly literate practitioner is a small disproof of the stronger versions of the incommensurability thesis, the claim that the two frameworks cannot be held in one head. They can be. It is institutions, not heads, that find the combination hard to house.

## The quarrel's geography: where each tradition lives

The comparison has been drawn as though the two traditions floated free of place, and a final survey should put them on the map, because their institutional geographies differ as sharply as their charters and explain part of their different textures. The mainstream's geography is a geography of standardisation radiating from a centre. Its graduate training was standardised first and most completely in the United States, whose leading departments became the discipline's finishing schools for the world: economists from every continent pass through a small number of American programmes, learn a common technical language and a common sense of what a publishable question looks like, and carry the standard home to ministries, central banks, and universities on every continent. The flagship journals, the dominant textbooks, the international institutions' research departments, and the great prize that consecrates the discipline's elders are aligned with that centre, so that the mainstream is not merely a set of propositions but a world system of certification with a remarkably uniform product. A framework so housed does not need to win arguments in any particular country. It needs only to keep certifying.

The Marxist tradition's geography is an archipelago rather than an empire, a scatter of islands with local climates. In Latin America it fused with the structuralist and dependency traditions and held university ground through decades when its North American counterpart was being filtered out, so that the economics of centre and periphery was written there from inside the periphery's own faculties. In India it entered through the planning era's debates and sustained itself in universities and institutes where political economy remained a curricular subject rather than a historical one. In Japan it achieved something unique, a standing inside the economics faculties themselves, with schools of Marxist economics examining candidates and publishing multi-volume reconstructions while the Anglo-American profession looked the other way. In France it cross-bred with the regulation approach and with a strong indigenous tradition of state economic management; in Italy it fed the workerist currents that carried its questions out of the academy into the factories of the north; in Britain it survived in a double exile, in the universities' neighbouring disciplines and in an independent publishing culture of journals and bookshops that kept its classics in print through the coldest decades. None of these islands takes orders from a centre, because the tradition has none: no flagship journal certifies its world, no prize crowns its elders, and its unity is the unity of a founding library and a set of shared questions rather than of a standard.

The geographies feed back into the theories in ways a pure history of ideas would miss. A tradition housed in an empire of certification optimises for the certifiable: the well-posed question, the estimable model, the result that travels. A tradition housed in an archipelago optimises for local survival: interpretation, history, the long book that a regional audience will read whole, the school that a single faculty can shelter. Some of the intellectual traits each side's critics deplore in the other, the mainstream's narrowness of question, the Marxist tradition's looseness of magnitude, are adaptations to habitat before they are properties of doctrine, and they will change, if they change, when the habitats do. The slow loosening visible in the mainstream's centre, its empirical wing wandering into the marked territories, and the slow professionalisation visible in parts of the archipelago, its measurers building series the centre can use, are habitat changes before they are conversions. The map, like the domain table, is offered as a reading of a still-moving record.

The geographies extend beyond the academy to the two traditions' reading publics, and the difference in publics has shaped the difference in prose. The mainstream economist writes, in the last instance, for other economists and for the officials they advise: the treasury, the central bank, the regulatory agency, the fund mission, an audience that rewards compression, technical assurance, and conclusions that can be minuted. The Marxist economist has historically written for a doubled audience that no other social science quite has: the seminar on one side and the movement on the other, the party school, the union education programme, the serious general reader of the tradition's journals and classics, an audience that rewards historical sweep, moral clarity about stakes, and arguments that can survive translation into a dozen languages and a hundred reading groups. Neither audience is a corruption of the other; each is a discipline. But a literature formed by minuted conclusions and a literature formed by reading groups will differ in everything from sentence length to the treatment of uncertainty, and readers crossing between them should allow for the customs of the house before judging the furniture. The doubly literate economist of the previous section is, among other things, a writer who has learned to address both rooms without lying to either.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between Marxist and mainstream economics?

The deepest difference concerns what each takes the subject to be. One framework treats economics as the study of how scarce resources are divided among rival purposes by choosing agents, a problem posed to every society that has ever existed. The other treats economics as the anatomy of a single, datable social order: production organised through wage labour and privately owned means of production, moved by accumulation and torn by its own contradictions. From that first divergence flow the rest: opposed accounts of where profit originates, of whether history sits inside the theory or outside it, of money as a surface device or as the form value must take, and of class as a foundation or as a label pasted over individual endowments. The two literatures share words and even some tools; they do not share an object.

### Q: Why is Marxist economics marginal in the profession?

Two kinds of cause operate together, and accounts that offer only one should be distrusted. On the institutional side, the discipline standardised its training, journals, and hiring around a mathematical and statistical apparatus in the postwar decades, and work written in another idiom could not pass the successive filters that apparatus creates; in the United States, mid-century political pressure on radical academics added direct coercion to the structural exclusion. On the intellectual side, the tradition's own house is not in order: its specialists remain divided over the foundations of its value theory after a century of argument, its long-run magnitudes resist agreed measurement, and several expectations its adherents widely held, about living standards and about where revolution would arrive, were contradicted by events. Neither column of that ledger cancels the other.

### Q: Do the two traditions study the same thing?

Only at the level of the raw material. Both concern themselves with production, prices, incomes, employment, and breakdowns in modern economies, and a factory, a wage bargain, or a banking panic is available to each as evidence. What differs is the phenomenon each extracts from the shared material. Faced with a market, one framework sees choosers trading off scarce means and asks whether their plans cohere; the other sees a social relation between classes being reproduced and asks what its continuation requires and what it undermines. Because the extracted phenomena differ, results rarely contradict head-on; they pass at different depths, which is why the dispute between the traditions so seldom reaches a verdict and why this article grades it territory by territory instead of crowning a winner.

### Q: What does Marxist economics explain better?

Its comparative strength lies where the phenomenon is manufactured by the production relation itself. The division of income between wages and profits, treated in its pages as an outcome of bargaining strength, law, and the pace of accumulation rather than as a readout of technology, fits the long swings of the shares more comfortably than the contribution-based account. The persistent lean of innovation toward saving labour and cheapening commodities follows from its mechanism of competitive compulsion, which expects that lean in advance. And the return of crisis under every monetary and policy regime devised to prevent it is the sort of regularity a theory of internally generated breakdown exists to explain. In each case the advantage is the same shape: phenomena the rival framework files as disturbances are the ones this framework was constructed to place at the centre.

### Q: Where does mainstream economics have the stronger record?

Wherever the question concerns the price system working inside a structure taken as given. The movement of particular prices, the shifting of a tax between buyer and seller, the design of auctions, quotas, and regulations, the likely path of output and employment over the next few quarters: on all of this the mainstream toolkit is precise, cumulative, and tested daily against outcomes, and no rival apparatus approaches its reliability. Its monetary technique, the practical craft of running a central bank and steadying a payments system, is likewise the only developed one available. Even its growth accounting, whatever the theoretical objections to its aggregates, organised the measurement of economic expansion for half a century. A fair comparison gives it these territories without grudging, because they are the territories its definition of the subject was designed to cultivate.

### Q: Do the two traditions share any common ground?

More than their controversialists admit. Both descend from the classical political economy of Smith and Ricardo and inherit its organising notion of a social surplus, the product left after the producers are maintained and the used-up instruments replaced. Both insist, against most neighbouring disciplines, that an economy must be modelled as an interdependent whole rather than understood market by market in isolation. Between the two poles stand whole frameworks that share ground with each: the classical and Sraffian construction, which joins a socially settled distribution to a rigorous price theory, and the post-Keynesian macroeconomics of demand, endogenous money, and irreducible uncertainty, whose adherents read Marxist work with profit and are read back. Shared ancestry and shared instruments do not amount to shared theory, but they explain why findings keep crossing a border that doctrines cannot.

### Q: Is heterodox economics the same as Marxist economics?

No; the first term names a position and the second a doctrine. Heterodox economics is the collective label for every school standing outside the discipline's dominant framework, and its tenants agree mainly on their address. Post-Keynesians build on demand and uncertainty, Sraffians on the classical surplus approach, institutionalists on habit and law, feminist economists on the unpaid labour that reproduces the workforce, ecological economists on the economy's embedding in a finite nature, and Austrians on a radical individualism that rejects almost everything the others hold. Marxist economics, one tenant among these, is identified by a specific core: value grounded in labour, surplus extracted within production, accumulation as the system's engine, crisis as its periodic product. Lumping the tenants together inflates the tradition's numbers and dissolves exactly the commitments that make it distinctive.

### Q: Can a Marxist use mainstream tools?

Yes, and the tradition's own practice has never waited for permission. Marxist economists estimate profit-rate series and price-value correlations with the standard econometrics, build models with input-output tables whose lineage runs through the reproduction schemas of Capital's second volume, and have reconstructed theories of exploitation and bargaining in the idiom of game theory, publishing the results in the profession's leading journals. The compatibility holds because technique answers to the questions put to it: a regression does not care which charter commissioned it. What does not travel so easily is interpretation. A coefficient estimated inside one framework acquires its meaning from the framework's categories, so the borrower must translate, and translation is where disputes resume. Tools are portable in a way theories are not, which is why the traffic in instruments across this border has always been heavier than the traffic in ideas.

### Q: Has mainstream economics moved toward any Marxist conclusions?

In several empirical territories, yes, though always under its own flag and by its own instruments. Research programmes inside the discipline's core journals have established that firms' markups over cost have climbed substantially across recent decades, concentrated among the largest firms, and that the share of income flowing to labour has declined across many economies over the same span; both findings land where the Marxist and monopoly-capital literatures had been standing for generations. Studies of financial instability, household debt, and the discipline that unemployment exerts on wage claims tell a similar story of arrival by an independent route. The arriving researchers generally do not borrow the older framework's categories and often do not know its literature; their results corroborate particular expectations of that framework without endorsing the machinery that produced them, a distinction this article insists on under the heading of convergence.

### Q: Which tradition predicted more accurately?

The honest answer sorts by the kind of prediction rather than by the camp. On dated, measurable events inside a going structure, price movements, the effects of a tax or a rate change, the behaviour of a particular market over the coming year, the mainstream's record is stronger, because it submits to that examination constantly and has grown accurate under it. On slow structural tendencies, the gathering of industry into fewer and larger hands, the recurrence of financial crisis across unlike regimes, the global spread of the wage relation, the Marxist tradition registered earlier and more clearly. Each side also owns conspicuous failures in its supposedly strong register and in the other's: mass living standards in the advanced economies rose where immiseration was expected, and the models of the professional centre failed to warn of the largest financial collapse since the nineteen thirties. Whole-tradition scorekeeping conceals this sorting; domain-by-domain scorekeeping reveals it.

### Q: How does the domain verdict grade a question put to the wrong framework?

As worse than unanswered. A framework addressed outside its territory does not fall silent; it replies fluently to a neighbouring question of its own choosing, and the fluency is the danger. Ask a model of factor pricing why the wage share sank for a generation and it will return a story about technology and substitution elasticities that may be flawless arithmetic while treating the erosion of bargaining institutions, the very substance of the inquiry, as background. Ask a theory of accumulation what a levy will do to one market's price by spring and it will return reflections on profitability in general while the decision the question served goes unmade. The verdict's practical teaching is therefore a discipline of assignment: name the domain first, from the question's own shape, and only then choose the machinery, because a confident answer to the wrong question outranks an honest silence in every examination and misleads in every ministry.

### Q: What should a student separate between a domain where the frameworks compete and one where they do not?

Separate the territory from the rhetoric laid over it. In some territories the frameworks genuinely compete, because both claim the same phenomenon as their explanandum: distribution is the clearest case, where shares must be settled either by contributions and scarcities or by strength and institutions, and evidence can in principle arbitrate. In others they do not compete at all, because each is answering a question the other never posed: the Marxist account of why crises recur does not contest the mainstream's account of how a given crisis propagates through payments systems, and confusing the two produces phantom refutations in essays. The student's discipline is to ask of every celebrated clash whether the parties share an explanandum. If they do, weigh evidence; if they do not, translate each claim back into its own question before judging, and mark down any writer who profits from leaving the two entangled.

### Q: Why did the two traditions stop reading each other?

The silence was built in layers. The founding generations exchanged refutations and then inherited them as settled, so that each side's students met the other as a closed case in a history survey. Postwar specialisation then supplied the machinery of mutual neglect: distinct journals with distinct refereeing standards, distinct conferences and associations, distinct hiring networks, so that a career could be completed on either side without a single enforced encounter with the other's best work. Political circumstance deepened the groove, particularly in the United States during the Cold War decades, when engagement with Marxist literature carried risks unrelated to scholarship. By the time the risks receded, the habits were self-sustaining, because a literature with a complete internal conversation gives its members no professional reason to look outside it. The costs fell on both: warnings unread on one side, measurements unborrowed on the other.

### Q: How does the domain comparison assign the questions of price and of crisis?

By asking which framework's object the question belongs to. A question about price in the form this discipline usually poses it, what a thing will exchange for, how a levy or a shortage will move it, how resources follow its signals, presupposes the price system as the economy's coordinating mechanism and belongs to the framework built to analyse coordination, which is why the table's price row goes to the mainstream. A question about crisis in its characteristic form, why the system's ordinary operation periodically suspends itself, why credit expansion and disproportion prepare the seizure, presupposes an object defined by its own dynamics and belongs to the framework built to analyse accumulation, which is why the crisis row goes the other way. The assignments are not courtesies; each follows from matching the question's presuppositions to the charter that contains them, and a question that mixed both forms would have to be divided before it could be answered.

### Q: What separates convergence of results from agreement of method?

The direction of travel and everything carried on the journey. When mainstream empiricists document rising markups or a falling wage share, they reach those findings through production-function estimation, firm-level datasets, and decompositions framed in their own categories, and they explain what they find through technology, market structure, and institutions as their tradition conceives them. The Marxist literature that anticipated the findings reasoned from surplus value, the concentration of capital, and the wage bargain as a class relation. Agreement of method would mean the second party adopting the first's machinery, categories, and standards of proof; nothing of the sort has occurred. What has occurred is that two unlike instruments, pointed at the same coastline by different charts, returned the same soundings. Soundings can be pooled without charts being merged, and the pooling strengthens confidence in the coastline while leaving each chart's claims about the interior exactly where they were.
