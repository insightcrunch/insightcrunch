## The Rosaline Confusion in the Early Stage Directions: A Name in the Documents, a Body Nowhere

The Rosaline confusion begins with a claim that the documents refuse to support. The claim, repeated in classrooms and study guides, holds that the first quarto of Romeo and Juliet brings Rosaline onto the stage at Capulet's feast, entering her with the guests in a stage direction that the second quarto then contradicts or corrects. The claim is false. No stage direction in the first quarto of 1597 names her. No stage direction in the second quarto of 1599 names her. The First Folio text of 1623, set from a reprint of the second quarto, names her in no direction either. Across the whole early printed tradition of the play, the woman Romeo loves before Juliet exists in exactly two kinds of writing: in dialogue, where other characters speak her name, and in one spoken document, the guest list that Capulet's servant carries through the streets and cannot read. She has no entrance, no exit, no speech prefix and no place in any direction's census of bodies. The confusion that bears her name is real, but it is not where the tradition has usually looked for it. It sits one item deep in a dinner invitation, in the pointing between two names, and in the three centuries of editorial repair that a single unattached name provoked.

The verdict of this study can be stated at once, since the whole argument is a defence of it. The tangle around Rosaline in the early texts is genuine but small, and its smallness is the finding. The two quartos transmit the guest list's catalogue with identical membership in identical order; they differ at her entry by one mark of pointing, a conjunction in the first quarto where the second prints a comma. That difference has been made to carry a family tree, Livia's supposed niecehood, a theory of authorial revision, and a psychology of memorial reconstruction. It can carry none of them. The list's own grammar, tested against all ten of its items, attaches the word niece to Rosaline alone in both texts and leaves Livia grammatically unattached in both. What the divergence does expose is the copy behind each quarto: a block of foreign names transmitted for its shape rather than its syntax, in a scene built on the failure of a written document to reach its readers intact. The play's first act is about a list that miscarries. The list's own transmission miscarried in the same way, and the confusion is the record of that double failure, compounded by editors who could not leave an unattached name alone.

## A Census of the Name: Ten Occurrences, All in Dialogue

The census is the foundation, and it is short enough to give entire. In the second quarto, the fullest early text and the copy text of modern editions, the name Rosaline is spoken ten times. The first occurrence is the guest list itself, read aloud by Romeo at the servant's request: "my faire Neece Rosaline, Liuia" (Q2, 1.2), the entry on which the whole tangle turns. The second comes later in the same scene, when Benvolio, urging Romeo to examine other beauties, frames the feast as a comparison and names its prize: "Sups the faire Rosaline whom thou so loues" (Q2, 1.2) is Benvolio's phrase for the guest whose presence is the expedition's whole object. The third occurrence belongs to Mercutio, mocking the lover in the orchard scene and conjuring him by "Rosalines bright eyes, by her high forehead, and her scarlet lip" (Q2, 2.1). Six more fall in the Friar's cell in Act 2 Scene 3, where the name is traded between the old man and his penitent in a catechism of astonishment. The Friar opens by assuming the night was spent with her, "God pardon sinne, wast thou with Rosaline?" (Q2, 2.3), and Romeo's denial keeps the name in play: "With Rosaline, my ghostly father no" (Q2, 2.3). The exchange returns to her four more times, in the Friar's "Is Rosaline that thou didst loue so deare, so soone forsaken?" (Q2, 2.3), in his reckoning of tears washed away for her sake, in his verdict that the young man's woes were all for her, and in Romeo's own "Thou chidst me oft for louing Rosaline" (Q2, 2.3). The tenth and last occurrence is Mercutio's again, in the street in Act 2 Scene 4, summing up the displaced beloved as "that same pale hard hearted wench, that Rosaline" (Q2, 2.4). Ten occurrences, every one of them inside a speech, every one of them in the mouth of a man talking about a woman who is not there.

The first quarto's census tells the same story in fewer words. The short text, compressed throughout, keeps the name at the load bearing points: the list, the invitation's framing in the street scene, and the exchanges in which the new love is confessed and mocked. What neither quarto contains is any occurrence outside speech. There is no persons list at the front of either book in which she might be enrolled among the actors' parts, a feature the early quartos of this play simply do not print. There is no speech prefix, the apparatus by which a printed play assigns words to a voice. There is no direction of the form enter Rosaline, or exit Rosaline, or Rosaline with others, at the feast or at any other point of the action. The documentary ghost is a ghost in the strict bibliographical sense: a name that circulates through the text's dialogue while the text's stage apparatus, the directions and prefixes that govern bodies, behaves as if she had never been invented. That asymmetry, between a name everywhere in the talk and a body nowhere in the apparatus, is the true subject of the Rosaline confusion, and the guest list is where the asymmetry is sharpest, because the list is the one place where the fiction itself produces a document.

### Why is the guest list spoken aloud at all in Act 1 Scene 2?

The list is spoken aloud because nobody onstage can read it silently. Capulet hands his servant a paper naming the guests to be invited, the servant protests that he is illiterate, and Romeo reads the catalogue for him in the street, so that a document meant for private delivery reaches the audience only by being performed as dialogue.

The staging turns a household paper into public speech, and every name on it, Rosaline's included, enters the play as a voice reading a text rather than as a person entering a room. The scene's comedy and its plot both depend on that mediation: the invitation miscarries into the hands of the one young man in Verona whom its contents most concern.

## The Name After the Feast: A Distribution Confined to Two Acts

The census has a geography as well as a total, and the geography is part of the evidence. All ten occurrences of the name in the second quarto fall in the play's first two acts. Two belong to the list scene of Act 1, the catalogue itself and Benvolio's framing of the feast as a comparison; one belongs to Mercutio's conjuration as the second act opens; six belong to the Friar's cell, where the old love is formally discharged; and the last belongs to Mercutio in the street, after which the name is never spoken again. From the balcony scene onward, through the marriage, the killings, the banishment, the potion and the tomb, no character in the second quarto utters the word Rosaline. The first quarto's distribution follows the same boundary in its compressed form. The name's life in the text is coextensive with the phase of the action in which Romeo's desire has not yet found its second object, and it expires at the exact point where the fiction has finished with her. A documentary presence that begins with a guest list and ends with a dismissal in a friar's cell has the shape of a function, not of a person waiting in the wings for an entrance the apparatus forgot to print.

The contrast with the play's embodied women is measurable in the same apparatus. Juliet's name saturates the second quarto's machinery: she is entered by direction at her first appearance with her mother and the Nurse, her speeches carry her prefix through the balcony, the cell, the potion scene and the tomb, and the directions track her body from the window to the vault. Lady Capulet and the Nurse are likewise entered, prefixed and exited as the action requires, their presence in the book a continuous record of entrances and speeches. Rosaline's record is the photographic negative of theirs. Where their names appear in the apparatus dozens of times, hers appears in the apparatus not at all, and the silence is not the silence of a minor figure, since the Nurse's mistress in the fiction's hierarchy, the host's own niece, outranks several of the women the apparatus does track. The apparatus tracks bodies, not ranks. It tracks her nowhere because there was no body to track.

The balcony scene supplies the distribution's most eloquent silence. Romeo, hidden in the orchard in the scene that follows the feast, names the new beloved in a transport of specification: "It is the east, and Iuliet is the sunne" (Q2, 2.2). The old beloved is not named in the scene at all, although the scene's whole logic is comparative, the new light measured against the darkness it replaces. A lover auditing his own transfer of desire, in soliloquy, at the moment of transfer, might be expected to speak the discarded name once, if only to bury it. The text does not let him. The name is reserved for other mouths, Benvolio's, Mercutio's, the Friar's, and finally for Romeo's own only in the Friar's cell, where he speaks it in the past tense of a chiding remembered: "Thou chidst me oft for louing Rosaline" (Q2, 2.3). Even in his own mouth the name arrives as a quotation of somebody else's sentence about him. The documentary ghost is ghostly in her grammar as well as her staging, a name that lives in the second and third person of other people's speeches and never achieves the first person of the stage.

One passage in the dialogue performs the census's own test and returns the same answer. When Romeo comes to the Friar at dawn after the feast, the old man's first assumption is that the night has been spent in her company: "God pardon sinne, wast thou with Rosaline?" (Q2, 2.3). The question is the fiction asking, in so many words, whether the invited guest and her suitor were together at the feast, and Romeo's answer closes the inquiry at its source: "With Rosaline, my ghostly father no, I haue forgot that name, and that names woe" (Q2, 2.3). The exchange is the nearest the play comes to an attendance report for the feast, and it reports a non-meeting. Whatever a production chooses to embody among the ladies of the entrance direction, the dialogue of the morning after testifies that the hero neither found her nor, by then, wished to. The documentary ghost and the spoken record agree, and their agreement is the ground on which the list's little crux has to be judged: a tangle about the syntax of an invitation, in a play whose every other witness agrees that the invitation came to nothing.

One occurrence in the census carries a documentary irony that repays attention. Mercutio's conjuration in the orchard scene, the third occurrence, invokes the name at the very moment its referent has been displaced: Romeo has already met Juliet at the feast, already exchanged the shared sonnet and the pilgrim's greeting, and stands hidden in the orchard of the house he has just left, while his friend conjures him home by "Rosalines bright eyes, by her high forehead, and her scarlet lip" (Q2, 2.1). The name is being used as a key to a lock it no longer fits. Within the documents the irony has a bibliographical echo, since the conjuration is one of the passages the early texts transmit with the name serenely intact while everything around it, Romeo's desire, the fiction's direction, the audience's knowledge, has changed. A name in print does not know when its moment has passed. It stands in the list, in the conjuration, in the Friar's catechism, spelled and pointed the same, while the play moves on beneath it, and that fixity is the last service the census performs for this study: the documentary Rosaline is constant because documents are constant, and the confusion arises when readers ask a constant record to register a changing fiction.

## The List Scene in Two Texts: Clown, Servingman and a Paper Called a Letter

The scene that houses the list is staged differently in the two quartos from its first line, and the differences set the terms in which the list itself must be read. The second quarto opens the scene with the document's whole economy already assembled: "Enter Capulet, Countie Paris, and the Clowne" (Q2, 1.2). The servant is present from the start, billed not by his household office but by his playing function, the Clowne, although the speech prefixes that follow call him Servant and Servingman. Capulet's opening settlement of the feud's terms, "But Montague is bound as well as I, in penalty alike" (Q2, 1.2), is addressed to Paris with the clown already onstage to overhear the machinery of the invitation. When the paper changes hands, itself an act no direction in either quarto records, the servant's illiteracy is given its famous occupational proof: he must find the men "whose names are written there", and he can read no writing but his own trade's, for the shoemaker is known by his yard, the tailor by his last, the fisher by his pencil and the painter by his nets, in the second quarto's proverb, "the shoo maker meddle with his yard, and the tayler with his last, the fisher with his pensill, and the painter with his nets" (Q2, 1.2). The joke requires the list to be a physical object whose contents are locked to its carrier, a sheet of names travelling through the city in the keeping of a man for whom they are shapes without sound.

The first quarto strips the scene to its errand. Its opening direction, "Enter Countie Paris, old Capulet" (Q1, 1.2), admits the two principals alone, and Paris, not Capulet, speaks first, pressing the suit that the longer text's opening speech postpones. Capulet's settlement of the feud, the speech that in the second quarto frames the scene's diplomacy, is cut; the short text moves from the wooing to the feast's preparations with a dispatch that its defenders read as theatrical economy and its detractors as memorial compression. The servant is summoned only when the invitation is ready, by the direction "Enter Servingman" (Q1, 1.2), and his billing agrees with the prefixes that follow, Ser and Seru in the quarto's abbreviation. His trades proverb survives but compressed and reshuffled, the tailor set against his last, the shoemaker against his needle, the painter and the fisher trading their instruments in an order the full text does not use. The list itself, when it comes, is read under the same direction in both books, and the agreement is the scene's most telling documentary fact: "He reads the Letter" in the first quarto, "He reades the Letter" in the second. Both texts misname the document. A catalogue of dinner guests, without address, salutation or signature, is headed as a letter in the apparatus of each quarto, as if the word letter were the period's default label for any sheet of writing that had to be carried and delivered.

### What does the first quarto cut from Capulet's half of the list scene?

The first quarto cuts the frame of the scene and keeps its errand. Gone is Capulet's opening speech binding Montague in the same penalty as himself, the speech in which the host of the feast first appears as a party to the feud's fragile peace.

Gone is the servant's presence through the wooing conversation, the clown standing by while Paris presses his suit, and with it the full text's sense of the invitation as a document prepared in public. What remains is the wooing itself, compressed, the summons of the servant, the proverb of the trades, and the list. The cuts matter for Rosaline because they show the short text spending its economy everywhere except on her: the name survives the compression intact, in the catalogue, in its place, while speeches around her are shed by the page.

Two features of the scene deserve emphasis before the crux itself is taken up, because both discipline what the crux can mean. The first is that neither quarto's apparatus records the paper's transfer. Modern editions print bracketed directions, giving a paper, taking the list, at the points where the fiction requires the object to move, and the brackets honestly mark the additions as editorial. In the quartos, the document changes hands in a documentary silence exactly parallel to the silence that surrounds Rosaline at the feast: the apparatus of these books is not a record of the fiction's furniture, and it omits the paper's journey as it omits the guests' arrivals. The second feature is the voice of the list itself. The catalogue is written from the host's chair. Its first person is Capulet's: "mine vncle Capulet" is the uncle of the man giving the feast, and "my faire Neece Rosaline" is that man's niece. Romeo, reading the sheet aloud in the street, ventriloquizes his enemy's household from inside its grammar, kin by kin, so that the name on which the tangle turns is spoken in the voice of the house that has excluded the speaker's own. Any reading of the item's syntax is therefore a reading of Capulet's written intentions as transmitted by two print shops, a doubly mediated piece of evidence, and the adjudication of its pointing has to respect both mediations.

## The Carrier of the List: A Clown, a Servingman and the Play's First Misreading

Between the host who writes the list and the lover who reads it stands the figure who makes the scene's documentary comedy possible, and his own documentary identity is as unsettled as the document he carries. The second quarto's direction bills him by his playing function, the Clowne, the company's specialist in comic business, while its speech prefixes call him Servant and Servingman, the household function the fiction assigns him. The first quarto knows nothing of the clown: its direction summons a Servingman, and its prefixes agree. The discrepancy is small and entirely characteristic. The full text's direction preserves the playhouse's view of the man, the part as cast; its prefixes preserve the fiction's view, the servant as employed. A reader who asks which he is receives both answers from the same book, on the same page, and the doubleness is a reminder that the apparatus of these texts was assembled for use, not for consistency. The man who cannot read the list is himself not read consistently by the book that prints him.

His illiteracy is the engine of the plot and the emblem of the study. Capulet's invitation is a competent document: it names the right houses, in the right order, with the host's kin properly placed. It fails because its carrier cannot perform the one operation the document presupposes. The servant's trades proverb, his proof that every man meddles with his own instrument, is a small theory of the scene: shoemaker and yard, tailor and last, fisher and pencil, painter and nets, each trade legible to its practitioner and opaque to everyone else. A guest list in the hands of a man outside the trade of reading is exactly such an instrument, a tool whose meaning is locked to a guild he does not belong to. The proverb's transmission between the quartos is itself a miniature of the larger tangle. The second quarto deals the instruments in one order, shoemaker, tailor, fisher, painter; the first quarto compresses and reshuffles them, the tailor set against his last, the shoemaker against a needle the full text never gives him, the painter and the fisher exchanging places. Nobody has built a theory of the play's composition on the shoemaker's needle, and the restraint is instructive. It is the same class of variant as the Rosaline conjunction, a small reshuffling in a passage transmitted for its shape and its laugh rather than its letter, and the difference in the attention the two variants have received measures the difference in what readers want from them. The needle proves nothing because nobody needs it to. The conjunction has been made to prove drafts and genealogies because the beloved's name stands next to it.

The servant also performs, in advance, the operation that the whole editorial tradition will perform on his document. Handed a text he cannot construe, he does not discard it; he finds a reader. The comedy is that his solution delivers the invitation to the one man in Verona it was designed to exclude, the son of the house's enemy, who reads his own opportunity into the list and comes to the feast on its authority. Every later handler of the list repeats the pattern at a higher level of literacy. Editors handed a pointing they cannot leave alone find the reading their edition needs; directors handed a doorway with no census find the bodies their staging wants; critics handed a conjunction find the draft or the reporter their theory requires. The document keeps being delivered to readers who complete it according to their desires. That the completion is a structural feature of the scene, and not an accident of scholarship, is the deepest sense in which the Rosaline confusion belongs to the play rather than to its printers. Shakespeare's fiction is about a list that reaches the wrong reader and is acted on wrongly. The list's afterlife in print is the same story, told by bibliographers.

## "My Faire Neece Rosaline": The Comma, the Conjunction and the Invention of Livia's Kinship

The two readings deserve to be set out in their original dress, because modernized texts conceal the difference they embody. The second quarto, in the reading at signature B3, points the item with colons between households and a comma inside it: "mine Uncle Capulet his wife and daughters: my faire Neece Rosaline, Liuia, Seigneur Valentio, and his Cosen Tybalt" (Q2, 1.2). The first quarto runs the names together in a comma stream and binds the pair with a word: "mine vncle Capulet his wife and daughters, my faire Neece Rosaline and Liuia, Seigneur Valentio and his Cosen Tibalt" (Q1, 1.2). The difference can be stated grammatically. In the second quarto, the comma leaves Livia grammatically unattached: she follows the niece as the next guest follows any guest in a list, and the syntax neither gives her the kinship nor denies it. In the first quarto, the conjunction couples the names, and a reader who takes the coupling strictly may carry the noun niece across it, making Livia a second niece of Capulet's, paired with Rosaline as Martino is paired with his wife or Lucio with Helena. Generations of editors have treated the choice between these pointings as a choice between two family trees. The adjudication offered here is that the documents pose no such choice, because the list's own grammar, examined across all ten of its items, never attaches a kinship term to the second member of a pair in the way the strict reading of the first quarto requires.

Consider how the list handles its other relations. "Mercutio and his brother Valentine" (Q2, 1.2) places the kinship word with the second name, not the first: the brother is Valentine, and Mercutio's relation to him is expressed by a pronoun that points forward. "Signior Valentio and his cousin Tybalt" (Q2, 1.2) does the same: the cousin is Tybalt. "Mine uncle Capulet, his wife and daughters" (Q2, 1.2) does the same again: the uncle is Capulet, and the wife and daughters trail as his household. The pattern is consistent. Where the list asserts a relation, it asserts it at the name it describes, with the relation word adjacent. Now set the Rosaline item against the pattern. "My fair niece Rosaline" asserts the relation at Rosaline's name, in the list's usual way, adjective and noun before the person described. Livia follows with nothing: no pronoun, no repeated noun, no adjective of her own. In the second quarto a comma performs the separation that the pattern predicts. In the first quarto the conjunction performs the pairing that the pattern everywhere else reserves for names without a shared predicate, couples and brothers and cousins whose relation is carried by a separate word. For Livia to be a niece in the first quarto, the noun niece must do work that the list nowhere else asks a noun to do, travelling backward across a conjunction to govern a second name. The more economical reading of both texts is the same reading: Rosaline is the niece; Livia is the next name.

The second quarto's punctuation deserves a closer look, because its system is the strongest single piece of evidence in the crux. The long text's list is not pointed at random. Its items are separated by colons, heavy stops that mark the boundaries between households and parties: the Martino household closes with a colon, the Anselme household with a colon, and the sequence continues in the same articulation, "Seigneur Valentio, and his Cosen Tybalt" closing against the next item with a colon in its turn. Within items, commas do the lighter work of separating the members of a household or a pair. The system is hierarchical: colon between parties, comma within them. Set the Rosaline item into that system and its pointing explains itself. "My faire Neece Rosaline, Liuia, Seigneur Valentio" runs the niece and Livia within one item's span, separated by the light stop, before the colon's territory resumes with the next party. The comma inside the item no more merges Livia into the niecehood than the comma inside "Martino, and his wife and daughters" merges the wife into the daughters. The second quarto's pointing, read as the system it visibly is, places Livia as a fellow member of the item's party, a guest grouped with the host's kin for the reading's convenience, and asserts nothing about her blood. Editors who strengthened the comma into a semicolon were refining that system, not contradicting it; editors who replaced the comma with the first quarto's conjunction were abandoning the system for the other witness's looser habit. Either way, the document's own punctuation, taken whole, is the comma's best defence, and it has been available to every reader of the quarto since 1599.

### How does the list pair its other guests, and what pattern do the pairings make?

The pairings make a grammar. Households come first in the list's order of precedence, Martino with his wife and daughters, Anselme with his beauteous sisters, Placentio with his lovely nieces, each head trailed by his dependents in a single breath.

Then come the pairs of independent guests, Mercutio with his brother Valentine, Valentio with his cousin Tybalt, Lucio with the lively Helena, in which a named man is joined to a named companion and the joining word is always and. Between the households and the pairs stands the anomalous item, the host's own kin: mine uncle Capulet with his wife and daughters, and then, sliding out of the household's orbit, my fair niece Rosaline, followed by Livia. The item is anomalous because Rosaline is the only guest defined by her relation to the list's author rather than by a household of her own, and Livia, who follows her, inherits the anomaly's ambiguity. Read by the list's grammar, the pairing of Rosaline and Livia belongs with the pairs, two single ladies listed together as Lucio and Helena are listed together, and the niecehood stays where the words put it, on Rosaline alone.

This grammatical adjudication has a consequence that reaches beyond the comma. If Livia's kinship was never in the documents, then the editorial tradition that agonized over her was solving a phantom problem, and the phantom's career is itself evidence, of how an unattached name in a list invites annexation. The career can be traced in the apparatus. The Cambridge edition's collation, which records the substantive history of the line, notes three interventions at Livia's name across the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Rowe's second edition printed not Livia but "Livio", masculinizing the guest at a stroke, as if the list's grammar required a man at that point in the sequence, a companion for the pattern of pairs. Capell conjectured "gentle Livia", supplying the bare name with an adjective of its own, the same courtesy the list extends to the lively Helena, as if the fault were a missing epithet rather than a missing relation. And Dyce's second edition, adopting a conjecture credited to Courtenay, printed "and Livia", importing into the second quarto's text the first quarto's conjunction, thereby regularizing the item as a pair by the simple expedient of borrowing the other witness's word. Three editors, three different repairs, each aimed at the same small discomfort: a woman's name standing alone in the list with no epithet, no relation and no partner assigned by the syntax. The discomfort is real. The repairs are the confusion's true substance, since each one rewrites the document to make Livia legible in a way the early texts decline to do.

The semicolon tradition completes the picture. The Cambridge editors, Clark and Wright, printing the text that would dominate the later nineteenth century, pointed the item with heavy stops: "my fair niece Rosaline; Livia;" (Cambridge edition, 1.2). The semicolons make Livia a free-standing entry, a guest in her own right, and that pointing descended through the school and study editions that followed the Cambridge text, setting the visual expectation with which most readers still meet the line. Other modern editions, following Dyce's borrowing, print "my fair niece Rosaline and Livia" and let the conjunction suggest the pair. A reader comparing two reputable modern texts of the play therefore meets two different Livias, one a solitary lady of Verona, one half of a coupled entry, and the difference between them is not in any early document. It is in the editorial sediment. The quartos offer a comma and a conjunction; the editions offer a semicolon and a borrowed and; and the genealogical question that students are still set, whether Livia was Capulet's niece, is a question about the sediment, not about the quartos.

The comma's prestige in the editorial tradition can be measured by the editors who claimed it as their own contribution. Edward Dowden, introducing his Arden edition, recorded with some pride that he had introduced only two readings not previously found in editions of authority, and that the first of them was a comma placed in the list scene, in the item of the fair niece and the name that follows her. An editor of Dowden's generation, working when the Cambridge text and its semicolons set the standard, regarded a comma at this locus as an intervention worth announcing in his preface, a small proprietary mark in the most contested inch of the scene. The anecdote is worth preserving because it shows the crux at its true scale in the minds of the men who maintained it. Nobody announces a comma in a preface unless the comma has become a position, a flag planted in a controversy. By the end of the nineteenth century the pointing of Rosaline's entry was such a flag, and the energy that kept it flying was the energy of editors defining themselves against one another across a mark that the documents, as this study has argued, never made worth a quarrel.

The point can be sharpened by setting the whole tangle out as a table. | Servingmen at the feast | Absent; Capulet's welcome opens the scene | Present, clearing the hall and naming Susan Grindstone and Nell | The full text's feast is staffed and named where the short text's is bare |
| Rosaline in any direction | Absent | Absent | Her presence at the feast is a production tradition, not a reading of any early text |

The table's last row is the one the rest of this study keeps returning to. Every other row records a divergence of wording, pointing or personnel between two books. The last records a convergence, the single point on which the first quarto, the second quarto and the Folio that descends from them are in perfect agreement: the directions do not contain her. The Rosaline confusion, pursued into the documents, inverts itself. The texts are not confused about whether she attends. They are unanimous that, as a staged body, she does not.

## The Feast Entrances of the Two Quartos: Two Directions, Neither of Them Hers

The feast scene's entrances show the two quartos working with different theatrical imaginations, and the contrast frames the question of what a direction can certify. The second quarto stages the feast as a process: the maskers have entered at the close of the street scene, "Enter Romeo, Mercutio, Benuolio, with five or sixe other Maskers, torchbearers" (Q2, 1.4), marched about the stage, and withdrawn into the fiction's geography while servingmen clear the hall of the supper that precedes the dancing. The servingmen's business is the full text's signature at the feast: trenchers shifted, joint-stools removed, the court-cupboard looked to, a piece of marchpane saved, and the porter instructed to let in two more names for the census of the bodiless, "Susan Grindstone and Nell" (Q2, 1.5). Only then comes the entrance that fills the stage: "Enter all the guests and gentlewomen to the Maskers" (Q2, 1.5), a direction that summons a crowd by category, guests and gentlewomen in the mass, to the maskers already waiting. Capulet's welcome follows, addressed to gentlemen and to ladies whose toes are unplagued with corns, and the dance begins. In the second quarto's feast, nobody of consequence is individually entered at the door. Juliet is not named in the direction. Lady Capulet is not named. Tybalt is not named. The host himself is not named. The direction's generality is total, and it is the generality, as much as any editorial choice, that has provoked editors to staff the doorway with the persons the scene will need.

The first quarto stages the feast as a tableau. Its maskers enter more sparely, "Enter Maskers with Romeo and a Page" (Q1, 1.4), the company of intruders reduced to a collective noun, the hero and an attendant. The servingmen's hall-clearing business is absent entire: no trenchers, no joint-stools, no marchpane, no Susan Grindstone. The scene opens directly on the host's entrance and the feast's presiding figure: "Enter old Capulet with the ladies" (Q1, 1.5). Romeo's line of wonder at the dance follows within a page, "What lady is that that doth enrich the hand of yonder knight?" (Q1, 1.5), and the machinery of the meeting proceeds without the full text's upholstery of service and preparation. The ladies of the first quarto's direction are as anonymous as the gentlewomen of the second's, a category and not a company. If a reader asks which ladies the direction means, the text has no answer beyond the welcome's own arithmetic of mistresses who will or will not refuse to dance. Rosaline's name has been attached to this direction in discussion often enough that the attachment needs stating plainly: the word ladies is the direction's whole content on the female side of the feast, and to find Rosaline inside it is to perform the same annexation that Rowe performed on Livia, filling an unmarked category with the name the reader has come expecting.

### What does the first quarto leave out of the feast that the second quarto stages in full?

The first quarto leaves out the feast's entire material preamble. Gone is the supper's debris and the servants who clear it, gone the saved marchpane and the porter's list of latecomers, gone the sense of a hall being converted under the audience's eye from dining room to dancing floor.

What remains is the feast's ceremonial skeleton: the host's entrance, the welcome, the masquers' measure, the recognition, the quarrel suppressed, the meeting. The omission matters for Rosaline because the servingmen's business is where the second quarto parks its own pair of bodiless names, Susan Grindstone and Nell, women who are let in at the door by report and never otherwise appear. The full text's feast is haunted by named absentees at every level, the guest list's ladies, the porter's ladies, and above all the niece whose invitation drives the hero through the door. The short text strips the haunting down to its essence. In the first quarto there is one document, one name on it that matters, and one entrance direction that declines to embody her.

The comparison also disciplines a lazy assumption about the two texts' directions in general. The shorter quarto is often described, following the Cambridge editors' preface of 1863, as the text whose stage directions are unusually precise, a precision Clark and Wright took as evidence of its origin in observation of performance. At the feast, the precision is of a particular kind. The first quarto's directions are theatrically pointed, old Capulet with the ladies tells a stage manager exactly what the moment needs, a host and a dance floor of partners, but they are not documentary in the sense of enumerating the persons a reader wants enumerated. The second quarto's directions, for all their fullness of business, are equally uninterested in the census. Neither text behaves like a cast list. Both behave like working papers for men who already knew who was in the company and needed no names where memory supplied them. That is the deep reason the feast directions cannot settle Rosaline's attendance, and it cuts both ways for the theories built on them. A direction written for insiders does not deny her presence by omitting her name; it simply never addresses the question, because for insiders the question did not arise in that form. The silence of the directions is real, but it is the silence of a genre, and the adjudication of the confusion has to reckon with what such silence can and cannot mean.

What the silence cannot mean, given the play's own economy, is that the company staged a woman the fiction insists is not there to be seen. The dialogue of the feast scene contains no reference to Rosaline's presence: Romeo, who came for her, never looks for her onstage, never reports her found or missed, and transfers his desire to Juliet without a word of comparison spoken in her hearing or anyone's. Benvolio's promise that she would be shown among the beauties is never cashed in dialogue. If the company had placed a silent Rosaline among the ladies, the fiction would have had to ignore a body its hero had crossed the city to find, in the scene built around his looking. Productions that seat her there accept that strain as an interpretive price, and the price is sometimes worth paying for the visual rhyme it buys. But the strain is the production's, not the quartos'. As documents, the directions' refusal to name her agrees with the dialogue's refusal to find her, and the two refusals together describe the play's design more honestly than any conjectural entrance can.

## Susan Grindstone and Nell: The Feast's Other Unembodied Names

The second quarto's feast holds a control experiment that the Rosaline debate rarely notices. In the servingmen's business that precedes the entrance of the guests, the porter of Capulet's hall is instructed to admit two latecomers by name: "Susan Grindstone and Nell" (Q2, 1.5) are to be let in when they come. Neither woman appears. Neither speaks, is spoken to, or is mentioned again in any text of the play. They are names attached to an admission that the fiction registers and then drops, bodies certified by a doorkeeper's instruction and never produced. No editor has ever expanded a feast direction to include them. No production, however completist, seats two silent women at the Capulet ball on the strength of the porter's line and calls the seating a recovery of the text. The names are accepted, without controversy, as the kind of atmospheric surplus a crowded scene generates, evidence of a world extending past the stage's edge and of a dramatist content to let names do the work of bodies.

Set Rosaline beside them and the asymmetry of treatment becomes the datum. Her documentary position differs from Susan Grindstone's in degree, not in kind. Both are women named in connection with admission to the feast; both are absent from every direction; both fail to materialize in the action. The differences are that Rosaline's name arrives in a written list rather than a spoken instruction, that the hero's desire has marked her out in advance, and that four centuries of readers have cared about her in a way no reader has cared about the porter's ladies. The editorial energy expended on her, the conjectures, the pointings, the seated figures in production, is therefore not a response to her documentary status, which is identical to theirs. It is a response to her narrative status, to the fact that the plot has taught readers to want her findable. The confusion about Rosaline, examined against the control case, is a confusion generated by desire: the desire of Romeo within the fiction, who goes to the feast to find her, reproduced in the desires of editors and directors outside it, who keep arranging for her to be found. Susan Grindstone, wanted by nobody, has been left in documentary peace.

The servingmen's scene also shows where the full text spends its naming energy, and the distribution is a small satire on the feast's documentary economy. The men name the furniture with a connoisseur's precision as they clear it: the joint-stools to be removed, the court-cupboard to be looked to, the trenchers to be shifted, the piece of marchpane to be saved for the porter because he has done the company a good turn in the warning. Objects receive the scene's specificity; persons receive its categories. Susan Grindstone and Nell stand at the exact midpoint of that economy, named like the furniture and embodied like the categories, admitted to the text with a precision that guarantees nothing about their presence at the dance. The passage is the second quarto's own commentary on the confusion this study is named for. In the world the servingmen describe, a name is a household convenience, a way of talking about the traffic of a great house, and the traffic runs on names for people who are elsewhere, late, expected or imagined. Rosaline's name runs in that traffic from the moment the servant unfolds the list in the street. The servingmen, who never mention her, describe her condition exactly.

The control case also sharpens the reading of the first quarto's feast. The short text, which omits the servingmen's business entirely, omits Susan and Nell along with the trenchers and the marchpane, and its feast is thereby populated by exactly one category of unembodied name, the guest list's. In the second quarto the feast is ringed by absentees at three removes: the ladies of the list, invited and read aloud; the ladies at the door, admitted by report; and the gentlewomen of the entrance direction, summoned in the mass and individuated by no name at all. The scene's dramaturgy depends on that ringing. Capulet's feast is the one place in the play where Verona society is assembled entire, and the texts render the assembly as a shimmer of categories and names, most of them attached to nobody the audience will ever see. Rosaline is the shimmer's brightest point because the plot has focused a lamp on her, but the shimmer is the scene's substance, and a textual study that isolates her from it mistakes a lighting effect for a documentary anomaly. She is not the feast's one problem of presence. She is its most noticed instance of the general condition, that in these early texts a name is not a body, and an invitation is not an entrance.

## How Editors Filled the Feast: From Capell's Attendants to Gibbons's Nurse

The modern feast entrance, as a reader meets it in a scholarly edition, is a small crowd of named persons, and every name in it is an editorial decision. The second quarto's "all the guests and gentlewomen" and the first quarto's "the ladies" gave editors a doorway and no census, and the history of the scene's editing is the history of the census being supplied at different densities. A comparative study of the scene's stage directions across editions, counting the bodies each editor admits, lays the progression bare. Capell, in 1768, was the most restrained of the major hands: following the quarto's reticence, his direction admits Capulet and attendants, a host and an unnamed train. John Dover Wilson's edition inserts Capulet and Juliet into the direction, promoting the heroine to a named entrance the quartos never give her. George Walton Williams and Jill Levenson, in their editions, return to Capell's settlement, Capulet and attendants, declining to enumerate the guests. Furness, in the New Variorum of 1871, had already expanded the doorway to four named figures, Capulet, Lady Capulet, Juliet and Tybalt. Brian Gibbons, in the Arden edition of 1980, prints five: Capulet, Lady Capulet, Juliet, Tybalt and the Nurse, the Nurse being, as the comparative study notes, Gibbons's own contribution, a figure no earlier editor had entered at that door. G. Blakemore Evans prints six in his edition: Capulet, Lady Capulet, Juliet, Tybalt and his Page, and the Nurse. Between Capell's two categories and Evans's six persons, the direction has grown by accretion, each editor adding the bodies his staging of the scene required and each addition hardening, for the next generation of readers, into something that feels like the text.

The pattern is the list crux repeated at the scale of the doorway. A document leaves a space unmarked, and the tradition of reading cannot abide the blank. Livia alone in the list attracts a gender change, an epithet and a borrowed conjunction; the unnamed ladies of the feast entrance attract Juliet, the Nurse, a page. In both cases the additions are defensible as staging: the scene does need Juliet onstage before Romeo sees her, does need the Nurse in place for the inquiries that follow, and an edition that serves the theatre serves its readers by saying so. The bibliographical point is different. It is that the additions are invisible in the reading experience they create. A direction in brackets or without brackets, the reader cannot tell at a glance which words stood in 1599 and which were supplied in 1768 or 1980, and the general account of the play's directions in the early texts, their classes and their provenance, is a study in itself, pursued in the companion survey of [stage directions in the early texts](/2026/06/26/stage-directions-early-texts/). What concerns the Rosaline question is the drift the additions set up. Once a reader has absorbed a feast entrance staffed with six named persons, the absence of a seventh begins to feel like a fact about the fiction rather than a fact about the documents. Rosaline is not in the direction, the reader concludes, and the conclusion smuggles in the premise that directions are where attendance is recorded. The quartos record no one's attendance at that door. The confusion, at this level, is a confusion between the edited book and the early book, and it dissolves the moment the two are held apart.

A further feature of the modernized text deepens the invisibility. In an old spelling edition, the pointing of the guest list can in principle reproduce the copy text's pointing, comma, colons and all, so that the reader meets something continuous with the second quarto's page. In a modern spelling edition, which is the form in which the play is overwhelmingly read, taught and performed, every mark of punctuation is the editor's by definition: modernization repunctuates as it respells, and the comma or semicolon or conjunction standing between Rosaline and Livia on a modern page is a decision taken in an editor's study, at whatever date the edition was made, under whatever pointing policy the series imposed. The crux therefore changes its nature as the text changes its dress. In the quartos it is a fact of transmission, a mark whose authority is doubtful because its origin is mechanical. In a modern edition it is a fact of interpretation, a mark whose authority is absolute within that edition because its origin is deliberate, and whose meaning a reader is entitled to press precisely because an editor chose it. The two Livias of the modern classroom, the solitary lady of the semicolon editions and the coupled lady of the conjunction editions, are both real in the only texts their readers hold, and both are editorial artifacts in the strict sense. No classroom exercise that asks which Livia Shakespeare intended can be answered from those pages, because the pages do not transmit the evidence the question needs. They transmit its editorial history, wearing the evidence's clothes.

Levenson's restraint deserves its due in this company, because it is a considered editorial philosophy and not mere caution. An edition that follows Capell in printing Capulet and attendants declines to convert the scene's necessities into documentary claims. It leaves the direction as the quartos left it, a summons by category, and puts the burden of embodiment where it belongs, on the production. Against that philosophy stands the theatrical argument that Gibbons's expansion embodies: an edition is a score for performance, its directions are instructions, and an instruction that fails to place the Nurse before she is needed has failed at its job. Both philosophies are coherent. They disagree about what an edition is for. But on the narrow question that governs this study, the question of what the early texts can be quoted as saying, Levenson's side has the documents with it. The quartos' feast entrance, quoted exactly, says guests and gentlewomen, ladies, maskers. It does not say Juliet, it does not say the Nurse, and it does not say Rosaline, and the rhetoric of those who write as if Rosaline's exclusion from the direction were a pointed authorial stroke, a meaningful snub recorded in the book, mistakes the genre of the evidence. The direction excludes everyone by name. Singling out her exclusion is the annexation move one more time, performed in reverse.

### Why do directors keep seating a woman the text never admits?

Directors keep seating her because the play's visual logic invites it and the fiction's silence permits it. A silent Rosaline at the feast gives the audience the comparison Benvolio promised and the dialogue withheld: the swan set among the crows, visible at the edge of the dance while Romeo's eye crosses the room to the face that unmakes the comparison.

The device costs the production one strain, the hero's failure to look at the woman he came to see, and buys it one rhyme, the old love overshadowed in the flesh at the moment of its replacement. The textual point stands apart from the theatrical one and survives it. A seated Rosaline is a production's reading of a category, not a quarto's record of a person, and the history of the confusion is largely the history of those two things being mistaken for each other.

## Revision, Memory or the Printing House: The Three Explanations on Trial

Three explanations compete for the tangle, and the brief of this study requires each to be given its strength before the verdict falls. The first is the revision explanation, the most romantic of the three. On this account the guest list is a fossil. An earlier state of the play, whether a first draft of Shakespeare's or the version the first quarto imperfectly preserves, gave Rosaline a larger documentary footprint: she was written into the feast's papers as Capulet's niece with a companion niece beside her, her place in the household's inner circle marked by the list's syntax, and the revision that produced the fuller text loosened the connection, the comma of the second quarto dissolving the pair as the character herself was thinned into pure absence. Eric Sams argued the general form of this case for the first quarto as an early version later revised and augmented, reading the second quarto's title page claim to be "newly corrected, augmented, and amended" as a literal statement that a prior version existed to be amended. On the revision account, the first quarto's conjunction is the earlier syntax surviving, and Livia's phantom niecehood is the ghost of a draft in which Capulet's house held two marriageable nieces and the feast a more populated kinship.

The second explanation is the memorial one. On the orthodox account of the first quarto's origin, defended in its classic form by H. R. Hoppe and absorbed into the editorial mainstream by Gibbons and others, the short text descends from a reconstruction of the performed play by members of the company, and its deviations from the full text are the fingerprints of memory: compression where memory failed, paraphrase where it approximated, and, the detail that matters here, anticipation where it ran ahead of itself. A reporter who knew the play knew that Rosaline mattered to the feast's machinery; memory, reaching for the list as Romeo's desire had framed it, could tighten the names into a pair, binding the beloved to the name that followed her with a conjunction the copy did not have. On this account the first quarto's "and" is not an authorial survival but a psychological one, the trace of a mind for whom Rosaline could not stand alone in a sentence because she never stood alone in the reporter's understanding of the story.

The third explanation is the printing house. W. W. Greg's account of the second quarto's copy, the foul papers hypothesis that made the long text an authorial draft in transit to the stage, carries a corollary about its accidentals: the pointing of such copy was light, irregular and no one's particular care, and the compositors who set it, like the scribe who may have transcribed it, treated punctuation as a convenience of the line rather than a record of sense. On this account neither the comma nor the conjunction is evidence of anything beyond the habits of two print shops working from copy in which a block of foreign names was the hardest matter on the page. The list is set as prose, in italic for the names in the second quarto's reading, a graphic block to be reproduced as a block. A comma where a conjunction might stand, a conjunction where a comma might stand: these are the small change of transmission, and building a family tree or a revision history on them is building on the least stable stratum of the text.

Laurie E. Maguire's audit of the bad quarto evidence cuts across all three explanations with a methodological warning that this study adopts. In Shakespearean Suspect Texts, Maguire tested the diagnostic features by which memorial reconstruction had been identified, the anticipations, recollections, paraphrases and dislocations of the textbooks, against control texts of known origin, and found the features occurring where no memorial origin was possible. The consequence for the Rosaline crux is bracing. A single divergent conjunction, even a psychologically suggestive one, cannot certify a reporter, because conjunctions diverge in every kind of transmission; and by symmetry it cannot certify a draft, because the same divergence is the commonest accident of the printing house. Maguire's work does not decide what happened to the list. It decides what the list cannot prove, and it strips both the revisionist and the memorialist of their smallest and most portable piece of evidence.

Lukas Erne's defence of the first quarto presses from the other side. In Shakespeare as Literary Dramatist and in his edition of the first quarto for the New Cambridge Shakespeare's early quartos series, Erne argues that the short text is a coherent version in its own right, theatrically viable and textually respectable, whose readings deserve to be weighed as a version's readings rather than sifted as a corruption's debris. Applied to the crux, Erne's position changes the question. The first quarto's "and" is not a problem to be explained by the second quarto's comma; it is the first quarto's text, to be printed and read as it stands, and Dyce's borrowing of it into the long text was therefore not a repair but a conflation, a mixing of versions that Erne's editorial philosophy forbids. The conjunction belongs to a version of the play in which the list ran in a comma stream and the names paired off as they came. Whether that version stands closer to a draft, a performance or a report is a further question, and Erne's work is a standing reminder that the further question cannot be answered from the conjunction alone.

The adjudication, then, falls in stages. On the kinship of Livia, the documents decide against the question itself: the list's grammar, as the census of its pairings shows, attaches niece to Rosaline and to no one else, in both quartos, and Livia's niecehood is an editorial phantom, dissolved. On the origin of the divergent pointing, the printing house explanation carries the day, with Maguire's audit as its warrant: a comma and a conjunction in a block of italic names are transmission weather, not climate, and no theory of the play's composition can be hung on them. On the larger question the crux is made to serve, whether the first quarto preserves an earlier state of the play, the list evidence is neutral and must be retired from the argument; the case for revision, if it is to be made, rests on the scale and shape of whole scenes, not on the syntax of a dinner invitation. What survives the trial is a smaller and more durable finding. The two quartos transmit the same catalogue with the same membership and the same dramatic function, and diverge exactly where documents inside fictions are most vulnerable, in the pointing of a text that nobody in the transmission chain needed to construe. The confusion reveals not a lost draft and not a reporter's desire, but the ordinary fragility of written things passed hand to hand, which is, as the next section argues, the play's own subject at this point in its action.

## Taken Down in the Theatre: The Shorthand Theory and the Sound of the List

One older explanation of the first quarto's character deserves a hearing of its own, because it speaks directly to the list's texture. When William George Clark and William Aldis Wright edited the play for the Cambridge Shakespeare in 1863, they accounted for the short text's peculiar combination of theatrical precision and verbal waywardness by supposing a stenographic origin: the book, on their theory, derived from notes taken down in shorthand during performance, which would explain why its stage directions observe the staging so exactly while its wording drifts, compresses and rearranges. Edward Dowden's introduction to his Arden edition carried the idea into the next generation, remarking that the first quarto's stage directions were evidently noted down by a spectator in the theatre, perhaps by the shorthand writer who probably supplied much of the manuscript. On the shorthand account, the copy behind the first quarto was a heard text before it was a read one, and its directions are an eyewitness's record while its dialogue is an ear's reconstruction.

The list is the passage where the theory does its most suggestive work. A catalogue of eleven foreign names, read aloud at performance speed, is precisely the matter a shorthand writer would take down as sound rather than sense: the names arrive as a sequence of phonetic events, their grouping decided by breath and by the reader's delivery, not by the written page's articulations. The full text's colons, which articulate the catalogue into households like a steward reading down a sheet, are a page's punctuation. The short text's comma stream, with its names running together and its single binding conjunction where the voice happened to couple two names in one breath, is an ear's punctuation. "My faire Neece Rosaline and Liuia" (Q1, 1.2) is what the item sounds like when a reader reels it off; "my faire Neece Rosaline, Liuia" (Q2, 1.2) is what it looks like when a copyist sets it down as furniture. The shorthand theory thus converts the crux from a disagreement about kinship into a difference of medium, page against ear, and on that conversion the conjunction loses the last of its genealogical force: a stenographer's and is a record of delivery, and delivery does not deliberate about niecehood.

The first quarto's feast direction is the theory's prize exhibit, and it shows both the theory's charm and its danger in a single line. "Enter old Capulet with the ladies" (Q1, 1.5) has the flavour of a note taken by a watcher: the identifying adjective, old, is the sort of word a man jots to distinguish the figure crossing the stage, and the collective ladies is the sort of word he writes when the figures are a company rather than a cast. Set against the second quarto's "Enter all the guests and gentlewomen to the Maskers" (Q2, 1.5), which reads like an instruction composed in advance by someone who knew the scene's choreography, the short text's direction does feel observed rather than prescribed. Clark and Wright built on exactly that feeling. The danger is the one this study has met at every locus: the feeling is not evidence of the kind the Rosaline question needs. An observed direction records what a watcher saw fit to note, and a watcher noting old Capulet with the ladies is recording the feast's visual gist, not auditing its guest list. If the shorthand theory is true, the feast direction is the record of an eye, and eyes notoriously register principals and masses, not the seventh lady from the door. If the theory is false and the direction is a playhouse abridgment, it is a working note with the same selectivity. On neither account can the direction's ladies be interrogated for a name, and the attempt to interrogate them is the confusion in its most persistent form, the demand that a summary behave like a census.

The theory's limits matter as much as its reach. Shorthand systems of the period, the methods published by Timothy Bright and his successors, were laborious instruments, and the claim that a play of this length was taken down verbatim in the theatre has been doubted on practical grounds by later scholarship, quite apart from Maguire's general audit of the diagnostics on which the bad quarto theories rest. The shorthand hypothesis also explains the short text's best features by its worst mechanism, since the same ear that supposedly caught the directions with precision let whole speeches slip into paraphrase. The adjudication here is the same as the one the explanations section reached by another road. Whatever the first quarto's copy was, ear taken, memory built or playhouse derived, its pointing at the Rosaline item is a feature of transmission, not of composition. The shorthand theory's value to this study is that it shows how many roads lead to that conclusion: foul papers accidentals in the long text, memorial anticipation in the orthodox account, stenographic delivery in the Cambridge one. The theories quarrel about everything except the point the crux needs, which is that a conjunction in this position cannot testify.

The instruments by which the tangle became visible deserve a sentence of their own, since the confusion is in part an artifact of the tools used to inspect it. Tycho Mommsen's edition of 1859 printed the two quartos on facing pages, the first apparatus built to let a reader watch the witnesses diverge line by line, and the parallel page is what turned a comma and a conjunction into a crux: set side by side, the smallest differences acquire the visual weight of events. The Cambridge collations then recorded the line's editorial afterlife, Rowe's Livio, Capell's epithet, Dyce's borrowed and, in the compressed Latin of the apparatus, where conjectures from three centuries sit in a single paragraph and read like a controversy. Neither instrument invented the divergence, which is genuinely in the documents. But the instruments set its scale, and a study that prices the crux has to discount for them. The facing page magnifies; the apparatus dramatizes; the documents underneath remain what they were, two printings of one list, differing at one mark.

## The Fossil Test: What a Superseded Guest List Would Have to Show

The revision explanation can be given a fairer trial than it usually receives if its predictions are stated in advance. A guest list that genuinely fossilized a superseded draft of the play, a state of the fiction in which Rosaline's place in Capulet's household was larger and her presence at the feast more than notional, would be expected to show at least one of four marks. It might preserve a divergence of membership, a name present in one state of the list and absent in the other, as a draft's population differs from a revision's. It might be presupposed by the surrounding dialogue, a speech that referred to the feast's company in terms matching the older list and not the newer. It might be matched by a direction that staged the older state, an entrance in the short text embodying a figure the longer text had demoted to a name. Or it might leave a scar in the fiction's logic, a question the revised play asks and cannot answer because its premise belonged to the draft. The test is not pedantic. It is the test any fossil claim has to pass in any textual tradition, the demand that a supposed survival differ from its surroundings in the way survivals differ.

The guest list fails all four marks, and the failures are worth enumerating because each is positive evidence and not mere absence. Membership: the two lists are identical, name for name, in identical order, from Signior Martino and his wife and daughters down to Lucio and the lively Helena, so that the supposed revision, which on the theory rewrote the play's treatment of Rosaline root and branch, left her catalogue untouched in every particular except a mark of pointing. Presupposition: no speech in either text describes the feast's company in terms that fit one list against the other, and the morning after exchange in the Friar's cell reports the same non-meeting in both. Direction: the short text stages no figure the long text demotes; its feast direction is the barer of the two, a host and a category of ladies, and if either text were the fossil of a more embodied Rosaline, the short text's doorway is where her name would stand. Scar: the fiction's logic is seamless on the point, since the invitation's failure is its premise, stated in the street, enacted at the feast and confirmed at dawn, identically in both witnesses. A revision that changed everything about Rosaline's treatment and nothing about her list, her feast, her morning after or her dramatic logic is a revision indistinguishable from no revision, and a hypothesis indistinguishable from its negation has no claim on the evidence.

The play itself supplies the contrast case that shows what a genuine fossil looks like, and it stands in the second quarto's third act. The Friar's dawn speech survives in the long text in two states at once, an earlier and a later version of the same lines printed back to back, the phenomenon of the duplicated passage: there, a superseded draft has left exactly the kind of evidence the fossil test demands, duplicated matter, redundancy in the fiction's logic, a scar visible to any reader. The guest list shows nothing of the kind. It shows one list, twice printed, with its pointing varied in the manner of pointing everywhere in these books. The comparison fixes the proportion of the Rosaline confusion with a precision the individual theories cannot. Revision in this play is a real phenomenon with real documentary signatures, and the list bears none of them. What the list bears is the signature of transmission, which is a different and, for the study of the early texts, a more instructive thing: proof that the documents of this play were copied and printed by men who transmitted names faithfully and the marks between names carelessly, so that the text's hardest facts are its names and its softest are the relations those names are punctuated into. Rosaline's niecehood survives both printings because it is carried by a noun. Livia's phantom niecehood appears and vanishes with a comma and a conjunction because it was never carried by anything else.

## What the Tangle Reveals About the Copy Behind Each Quarto

If the pointing of the list is weather rather than climate, the weather still tells a reader which way the wind blew through each print shop, and the two quartos' handling of the Rosaline entry fits the larger character of their copy with suspicious neatness. The second quarto's text of the play has long been read, on Greg's hypothesis, as print set from authorial foul papers, a draft whose virtues are fullness of invention and whose vices are the vices of a working manuscript: exits unmarked, entrances implied, stage business left to the men who would perform it, and whole passages standing in states no tidying hand had reconciled. The play's most famous instance of that unreconciled state is the Friar's duplicated dawn passage in Act 3, where an earlier and a later version of the same lines stand back to back in the copy, the subject of the companion study of [the Friar's duplicated lines](/2026/05/22/friar-duplicated-lines-crux/). The guest list belongs to the same order of evidence at a lower temperature. A list of names in a draft is a thing written once and never reread: the author has no occasion to construe it again, the scribe copies its shape, the compositor sets its italic names one by one, and the light pointing that results, colons between households and a comma within the Rosaline item, is exactly the pointing of copy that nobody in the chain was reading for sense. The second quarto's comma is not a decision about Livia. It is the scar left by a document passing through hands that treated it as furniture.

The first quarto's copy, on any theory of its origin, had passed through the playhouse, and its handling of the list fits that passage. The catalogue arrives in the short text compressed into the scene's flow, its items run together in a comma stream, the pointing of a text written for the ear and the memory rather than for the study. Where the full text's colons articulate the list into households, like a steward reading down a page, the short text's run of commas and its one binding conjunction articulate it as speech, names paired as a voice pairs them in delivery. A systematic comparison of the two texts' habits at every scale, from whole scenes down to single speeches, belongs to the study of [Q1 against Q2 and what their differences reveal](/2026/04/10/q1-vs-q2-romeo-and-juliet-differences/); at the scale of the list, the pattern is the familiar one in miniature. The first quarto's text reads as if the list had been heard; the second quarto's as if it had been filed. Neither condition makes the pointing a witness to kinship. Both make it a witness to the route the words travelled, and the route is what a textual study is entitled to recover.

### What separates a document inside the play from a direction about the play?

A direction speaks in the book's voice and tells the company what to do; a document inside the play speaks in a character's voice and exists to be handled, carried and misread. The list is a document, and its text reaches the audience as dialogue because the fiction arranges for it to be read aloud by a stranger.

Directions, by contrast, reach only the book's users, the company first and readers after, and they obey the loose conventions of working papers, naming bodies by category when the company could be trusted to supply the persons. Confusing the two kinds of writing is the root error of the Rosaline debate in its popular form. The list is construed as if it were a census of the feast, a direction in disguise, and its syntax is then pressed for attendance records. Read as what it is, a prop that speaks Capulet's intentions in Capulet's grammar, the list records whom the host meant to invite, and the play's next hour is precisely the story of intention and event coming apart.

There is one more stratum to the tangle, and it lies in the spelling and pointing of the names themselves as physical objects on the page. In the second quarto's reading, the names stand in italic type, the print shop's conventional dress for proper names in a text set in roman, so that the list presents itself visually as a document inset in the dialogue, a foreign body of type. The names in such a block are set as shapes. A compositor setting italic names letter by letter has even less occasion than usual to construe the prose between them, and the pointing of inset matter is therefore the most accidental pointing in any early play text. The broader question of how the lovers' own names fare in this typographic weather, which spellings stabilize and which wander, is treated in the companion study of [spelling and the lovers' names in early print](/2026/07/31/spelling-lovers-names-early-print/). For the guest list, the typographic point completes the adjudication. The Rosaline crux sits at the intersection of the two least reliable strata of an early text: the accidental pointing of an inset document. If a crux had to be designed to be undecidable, it would be designed there.

The honest way to see the crux whole is to set the two pointings on a single screen and read them against each other, colon against comma, conjunction against comma, as editors since Mommsen's parallel text of 1859 have done on facing pages. Readers collating the two lists in a digital viewer, whether a parallel text edition or a presentation tool such as the [ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), discover the same proportion that the print tradition discovered: the witnesses agree on the catalogue entire and differ on one mark. Tycho Mommsen's facing page edition of the two quartos in 1859 was the first instrument built to make such proportions visible at a glance, and its lesson has not aged. Collation does not magnify small differences; it prices them. Priced against ten identical items, eleven identical Rosaline references in the dialogue of the two texts taken together, and feast directions that differ in everything except their silence about her, the divergent pointing of one item shrinks to its true size. It is a variant. It is not a version of the play in which Rosaline attended the feast, or had a niece beside her, or mattered one jot more to the documents than she does.

The print shops themselves can be named, and their identities round out the account of the copy. The first quarto was printed by John Danter in 1597, its title page advertising the play as acted by the Lord Hunsdon's servants, its sheets divided between Danter's shop and Edward Allde's. The second quarto was printed by Thomas Creede for the publisher Cuthbert Burby in 1599, its title page advertising the play as acted by the Lord Chamberlain's servants and boasting the text "newly corrected, augmented, and amended". Two shops, two patrons' names for the one company, two claims upon the play: the documents of the tangle are commercial objects before they are literary ones, produced for sale by men whose interest in a guest list's pointing was exhausted by getting its names onto the page in the right order. The Folio text of 1623 then inherited the second quarto's comma through the third quarto's reprint, so that the mark survived three printings and twenty four years without a single compositor pausing over it. Accidentals fossilize exactly in this way, by being nobody's concern, and the long life of the comma is the measure of how little any hand in the chain thought the item needed deciding. The crux was born when editors began to think it did.

The quarto afterlife extends the same lesson across the following decades. The play was reprinted in 1609, again in an undated quarto, and again in 1637, each reprint perpetuating the second quarto's text of the list with its comma undisturbed, so that for forty years the reading public of the play met Livia across a comma and asked no recorded question about her. The genealogical anxiety is a product of the editorial era, of texts that present the line as a problem to be solved rather than a list to be read, and its history can be dated almost to the year: it begins when collation begins, when Mommsen's facing pages and the Cambridge apparatus taught readers to see the item as a variation, and a variation, in the scholarly imagination, is a thing that must mean. The quartos' first readers had the comma without the crux. The crux is the monument of a later way of reading, and the Rosaline confusion, in its full historical shape, is the story of that way of reading fastening on the one name in the play that desire had already taught it to hunt for.

The verdict can now be drawn together. The Rosaline confusion in the early stage directions and lists resolves into three findings, each narrower than the legend and each better evidenced. First, the documentary finding: no early text places Rosaline at the feast or anywhere else; her existence in print is confined to dialogue and to one entry in an invitation list, and the feast directions of both quartos, read exactly, name categories of persons and not persons. Second, the grammatical finding: the list's syntax, in both quartos, makes Rosaline Capulet's niece and leaves Livia unattached; the niecehood of Livia is an editorial phantom raised by three centuries of repairs, Rowe's gender change, Capell's epithet, Dyce's borrowed conjunction and the Cambridge semicolons, each a record of an editor's discomfort with a name the syntax would not place. Third, the bibliographical finding: the divergence between the quartos at her entry, a conjunction against a comma in the pointing of an inset document, is transmission weather of the most ordinary kind, powerless to prove a lost draft, a reporter's anticipation or any other large hypothesis, and valuable chiefly as a specimen of how the copy behind each quarto handled written matter inside the fiction. The play's Rosaline is a presence felt and never seen. The documents, handled without appetite, say exactly that and nothing more, and the confusion that bears her name belongs less to Shakespeare's printers than to the long line of his editors, whose repairs are the true text of the tangle. A reader who keeps the three findings apart, the documentary, the grammatical and the bibliographical, holds the whole matter in proportion: a doorway that is not a guest book, a noun that governs one name and not two, and a comma that was never a cousin.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the InsightCrunch Rosaline tangle collect, and what single pattern unites its entries?

The InsightCrunch Rosaline tangle is the collected set of divergences around Rosaline's name in the early printed texts of the play: the guest list's pointing, the servant's shifting billing, the feast entrance directions, and the editorial repairs those loci attracted. The pattern it exposes is consistent across every locus. The early texts diverge in wording, pointing and personnel wherever the transmission of a document is involved, and they converge completely on the one point readers most want them to decide: no direction in either quarto admits Rosaline to the feast or anywhere else. The tangle is a map of small disagreements surrounding one large agreement.

### Q: Does the name Rosaline appear in any stage direction printed in the early quartos?

No. The name Rosaline appears in the early quartos only inside dialogue and inside the guest list that Romeo reads aloud, which is itself dialogue representing a document. No entrance direction, exit direction or other piece of stage apparatus in the first quarto of 1597, the second quarto of 1599 or the Folio text that descends from them contains her name. The feast entrance directions summon categories, ladies in the first quarto and guests and gentlewomen in the second, and any account that quotes a direction as placing Rosaline at the feast is quoting an editor's expansion or a production's choice, not an early text.

### Q: How does the first quarto join the names Rosaline and Livia in the guest list?

The first quarto joins them with a conjunction. Its reading of the list runs in a stream of commas, and at the crucial item it prints "my faire Neece Rosaline and Liuia", binding the two names with the word and. That binding is what allowed later readers to take the noun niece as governing both names, making Livia a second niece of Capulet's standing beside Rosaline. The first quarto's pointing elsewhere in the list is light throughout, since the whole catalogue is set as a run of names rather than as articulated households, so the conjunction sits in a texture of loose connections and cannot bear much interpretive weight on its own.

### Q: How does the second quarto separate the names Rosaline and Livia in the guest list?

The second quarto separates them with a comma and nothing more. Its list is pointed with colons between the households, and within the item it prints "my faire Neece Rosaline, Liuia", leaving Livia to follow the niece as one guest follows another. The comma neither asserts nor denies a kinship for Livia; it simply declines to attach her to the noun that precedes her. Because the second quarto is the copy text from which modern editions descend, its comma is the reading most readers inherit, and the editorial tradition has spent three centuries deciding what to do with the unattached name the comma leaves behind, by semicolon, by epithet or by borrowed conjunction.

### Q: Whose voice is speaking in the guest list when Romeo reads the words "mine uncle Capulet"?

Capulet's voice is speaking, ventriloquized by Romeo. The list is written from the host's chair: the uncle is Capulet's uncle, the wife and daughters are that uncle's household, and the fair niece Rosaline is Capulet's niece. Romeo, reading the sheet aloud in the street, speaks the first person of the man who has excluded his whole house from the feast, so that for the length of the catalogue the enemy reads the invitation in the grammar of the inviter. The point matters for the crux because it fixes the niecehood the list asserts. Rosaline is the niece of the list's author, the host, and the kinship is stated in his voice at her name and nowhere else in the item.

### Q: What did the second edition of Rowe's Shakespeare change the name Livia into?

It changed Livia into Livio, a man's name. The Cambridge edition's apparatus records the reading as Rowe's, from his second edition, and it stands as the boldest of the repairs the bare name attracted. Where the quartos leave a woman's name unattached in the list, Rowe's text supplies a male guest, regularizing the sequence of the catalogue at the cost of the name's gender. The intervention is a useful measure of the editorial discomfort the locus caused: an unattached name was felt to be a defect in the document, and changing its gender was among the remedies tried before editors settled for punctuation, epithets and borrowed conjunctions as gentler ways of placing her.

### Q: What did Capell conjecture before the name Livia, and what problem was the conjecture solving?

Capell conjectured the adjective "gentle", so that the list would read "my fair niece Rosaline; gentle Livia". The conjecture, recorded in the Cambridge apparatus, solves the problem of the bare name: Livia is the only single lady in the catalogue who carries no epithet and no relation, where Rosaline is fair and Helena is lively. Supplying an adjective gives her the same decorative standing as the list's other women and marks her as a separate entry rather than an appendage of the niece. It is an honest conjecture in form, since it announces itself as one, but it treats as a blemish what the quartos present as a fact, a name the syntax leaves unadorned.

### Q: How did Dyce's edition come to print "and Livia" in the guest list?

Dyce's second edition adopted a conjecture credited to Courtenay and printed "and Livia", importing into the second quarto's text the conjunction that the first quarto prints at that place. The Cambridge apparatus records the descent exactly: "and Livia" is Dyce's reading, on Courtenay's conjecture. In effect the conjecture conflates the two witnesses, taking the wording of the line from the fuller text and its connective from the shorter one. Editions that print "my fair niece Rosaline and Livia" descend from this intervention, and their coupled pair of names, with its suggestion of a shared niecehood, is an editorial construction of the nineteenth century rather than a reading of 1599.

### Q: What does the first quarto's direction for the feast entrance actually say?

It says, in full, "Enter old Capulet with the ladies". That is the whole of the first quarto's provision for filling the stage at the feast: the host, identified by his age, and a category of female guests. The direction follows a scene in which the maskers have entered as a collective, "Enter Maskers with Romeo and a Page", and it is not preceded by the servingmen's hall-clearing business that the fuller text stages. No woman is named in it, no guest is enumerated, and Juliet herself reaches the scene without the warrant of a named entrance. Quoted exactly, the direction is a study in the working-paper conventions of the early playhouse, in which categories stand where a modern reader expects a census.

### Q: What does the second quarto's direction for the feast entrance actually say?

It says "Enter all the guests and gentlewomen to the Maskers". The direction closes the servingmen's business of clearing the hall and summons the company by category, guests and gentlewomen in the mass, to join the maskers who have marched in during the preceding movement. Like the first quarto's direction it names nobody: not Capulet, not his wife, not Juliet, not Tybalt, all of whom the scene requires within minutes. The Folio text, set from the third quarto's reprint of the second quarto, preserves the direction unchanged, so the summons by category is the reading of the whole early tradition, and every named figure in a modern edition's feast entrance has been supplied by an editor.

### Q: Which guests and attendants do modern editions add to the feast entrance direction?

Modern editions add the figures their staging of the scene requires, in densities that grow across the centuries. Capell's edition admits Capulet and attendants, the most restrained form. John Dover Wilson inserts Capulet and Juliet. Furness's Variorum lists Capulet, Lady Capulet, Juliet and Tybalt. Brian Gibbons prints those four and adds the Nurse, entering her at a door no earlier editor had used for her. G. Blakemore Evans prints six persons: Capulet, Lady Capulet, Juliet, Tybalt and his Page, and the Nurse. Jill Levenson, with George Walton Williams, returns to Capell's settlement of Capulet and attendants. None of the named figures stands in the quartos' direction, which summons guests and gentlewomen by category alone.

### Q: Is the servant who carries the guest list billed as a clown in the early texts?

In one of them, yes, and in the other not at all. The second quarto's opening direction for the scene reads "Enter Capulet, Countie Paris, and the Clowne", billing the servant by his playing function, the company's clown, although the speech prefixes that follow call him Servant and Servingman. The first quarto never uses the word: its direction summons a Servingman into the scene after Capulet's invitation speech, and its prefixes agree with the functional billing. The Folio keeps the second quarto line's Clowne. Modern editions follow the prefixes and print Servant throughout, so the clown's presence at the scene's threshold survives only in collations, a small confusion of identity at the door of the scene of names.

### Q: Why do the directions in both quartos call the guest list a letter?

Both quartos head Romeo's reading with the same direction, "He reads the Letter", although the paper he reads is not a letter in any formal sense. It has no address, no salutation and no signature, only a catalogue of names. Letter served the period as a general word for a piece of writing, and the direction uses it in that general sense for a sheet whose contents the audience is about to hear. The loose label is of a piece with the scene's other documentary instabilities: the paper is handed over without a direction, carried by a man who cannot read it, called a letter though it is a list, and preserved in two printings that disagree on its pointing.

### Q: Does the First Folio alter the pointing between Rosaline and Livia in the list?

No. The Folio text of the play was set from the third quarto, itself a reprint of the second, and at the guest list it perpetuates the second quarto's reading, "my faire Neece Rosaline, Liuia", with the same bare comma between the names. The whole quarto and Folio tradition after 1599 therefore descends from the comma, and the first quarto's conjunction stands outside the line of transmission that produced the texts most readers have met. That descent explains the shape of the editorial record: because the inherited text had the comma, the conjunction could reach modern editions only as a conjecture, which is how Dyce, following Courtenay, introduced it.

### Q: How does the guest list itself use the word "nieces" for Signior Placentio's household?

The list gives Signior Placentio "his louely Neeces", a plural noun for a household of unnamed nieces, and the item is the control case for the list's grammar of kinship. Where the list means plural nieces, it prints the plural word; where it means a brother or a cousin, it sets the relation word beside the person it describes, as in "Mercutio and his brother Valentine" and "Valentio and his cousin Tybalt". Against those usages, the singular "Neece" set before Rosaline's name alone reads as a deliberate grammatical quantity: one niece, named. The argument is not that a compositor could not err, but that the burden of proof lies on the reading that asks the singular noun to cover a second, unmarked name across a conjunction.

### Q: Why can the second quarto's comma not decide whether Livia was Capulet's niece?

Because pointing in the second quarto is accidental rather than substantive, in the distinction W. W. Greg's rationale of copy text made canonical. The comma belongs to the layer of the text supplied or transmitted by scribes and compositors, the layer least likely to preserve an author's considered marks, and in a prose catalogue of italic names that layer is at its least reliable. A comma can show where a compositor breathed; it cannot certify a genealogy. The same caution governs the first quarto's conjunction, which is likewise an accidental of its transmission. Editors who need the question answered must answer it from the list's grammar and the play's fiction, and both of those, as the pairings show, leave Livia unattached.

### Q: What would count as evidence that the guest list preserves a superseded draft of the play?

Evidence of a different order from a conjunction would be required: a divergence in the catalogue's membership, an additional name or a missing one, a speech that presupposed the feast's population in a form the fuller text contradicts, or a direction in the short text that staged a figure the dialogue no longer knows. None of that exists. The two lists name the same guests in the same order, the feast scenes use the same personnel, and no line in either text treats Rosaline as expected in the room. The revision hypothesis, in the form Eric Sams argued for the first quarto generally, must therefore rest on the shape of whole scenes and passages. At the guest list, the one place where a superseded draft would most plausibly have left a fossil, the record shows two printings of one list, not two lists.

### Q: Does Rosaline have a speech prefix anywhere in the early printed texts?

No. A speech prefix is the book's assignment of words to a speaker, and Rosaline is assigned no words in the first quarto, the second quarto or the Folio. Her name occurs in the prefixes' world only as a topic inside other speakers' lines, in Romeo's mouth, Benvolio's, Mercutio's and the Friar's. The absence is the bibliographical form of her dramatic condition. Characters who exist in a play's fiction ordinarily leave some trace in its apparatus, a prefix, an entrance, a place in a direction's census; Rosaline leaves none, and the census of the name confirms what the fiction asserts, that she is a person made entirely of reference, present in the play only as the object of other people's sentences.

### Q: Which quarto supplies the wording of the guest list in modern editions?

The second quarto supplies it. Modern editions take the second quarto of 1599 as their copy text for the play, and the guest list descends from its reading, including its light pointing of the names, subject to each editor's pointing policy. The first quarto's variants enter modern texts only through the apparatus or by conjecture, which is the route Dyce's "and Livia" travelled. A reader who meets the list in a modern edition is therefore reading the fuller text's catalogue, with its comma between Rosaline and Livia either retained, strengthened into a semicolon, or replaced by the shorter text's conjunction, according to the editorial tradition the edition inherits.

### Q: What does the Rosaline tangle finally prove about the documents behind the two quartos?

It proves that the copy behind each quarto treated written matter inside the fiction as a block to be transmitted rather than a text to be construed. The second quarto's copy, on the foul papers account, carried the list as the author had set it down, unrevised and lightly pointed, and its print shop reproduced the block with a comma where its exemplar breathed. The first quarto's copy carried the same list through the playhouse, where it was rearticulated as speech, its households run together and its pairs joined by a conjunction. Both transmissions preserved the catalogue's membership perfectly and its syntax loosely. The tangle is the record of that asymmetry, and its lesson generalizes: in the early texts of this play, names survive better than the marks that relate them.
