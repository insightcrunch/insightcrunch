The Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority Act 2013, which most readers meet under its short name, the PFRDA Act 2013, contains a sentence that defines the whole of modern Indian pension policy and then appears to contradict it. Section 20 of the Act provides that under the National Pension System there shall not be any implicit or explicit assurance of benefits, except a market based guarantee mechanism that the subscriber purchases. The statute therefore forbids the one thing pensioners have always wanted, a promise, and permits only such assurance as the subscriber pays for in the market. Yet within eleven years of the Act coming into force, the Union Cabinet approved an assured payout scheme for government employees inside the same system, with a monthly payment fixed at half of average basic pay for a full career, and the Authority itself wrote the regulations that operate it. This article explains how both things are true at once, and why the reconciliation between them is the most instructive feature of the statute.

![The PFRDA Act 2013 and the National Pension System, showing the pension regulator, the bar on assured benefits and the assured payout option - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The Act does three things, and a reader who separates them will not be confused by anything that follows. It establishes a regulator, the Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority, and arms it with powers of registration, direction, investigation and penalty over every intermediary that touches pension money. It takes a pension system that already existed, the contributory system notified by the Government of India in December 2003, and declares that system to be the National Pension System, writing its basic features into statutory text. And it settles, in section 12, the boundary of the regulator's territory, applying the Act to that system and to pension schemes regulated by no other enactment, while expressly leaving the great provident fund statutes outside. Around those three moves the article is organised: the world the Act inherited, the decade in which the regulator worked without a statute, the text of the Act itself, the machinery the text creates, the enforcement architecture, the political argument the statute was drafted to end and did not, and the assured option through which the argument returned in a new and more precise form.

## The pension settlement the Act inherited

For most of the twentieth century, a government employee in India retired on a defined benefit. The pension was a proportion of pay, fixed by service rules, paid out of government revenue in the year it fell due, and revised upward with dearness relief as prices rose. The Central Civil Services (Pension) Rules governed the Union's own employees, and the States maintained parallel rules for theirs. In its mature form the entitlement was precise: a pension computed at one half of the emoluments drawn, measured over the closing period of service, with full pension earned on completion of the prescribed qualifying service, which the pay revisions of 2006 reduced to twenty years, a minimum pension guaranteed by rule, a family pension at thirty per cent on the employee's death, and dearness relief revised twice a year with the price index. The employee made no contribution toward the pension itself. The arrangement belonged to a family of unfunded public pension promises across the world, and it shared their central feature: the liability was real but it appeared in no balance sheet, because each year's pension bill was met from that year's revenue and the obligation to future pensioners was carried as an expectation rather than as a fund.

By the closing years of the century the fiscal arithmetic of that expectation had become the subject of official anxiety. Life expectancy had lengthened, so each pension was drawn for more years. The ratio of pensioners to serving employees had shifted. Pension expenditure of the Union and the States grew faster than revenue, and because the promise was indexed to pay commissions' revisions as well as to prices, each general revision of salaries carried a permanent revision of pensions in its train. The criticism made by finance ministries was not that the promise was ungenerous but that it was unmeasured: no government could state, in the year a recruit joined service, what that recruit's retirement would cost, and no provision was made against the cost in the years when the service was rendered.

The intellectual groundwork for a different design was laid by the Old Age Social and Income Security project, known by its acronym OASIS, whose report was submitted in 2000 under the chairmanship of Dr S. A. Dave. The OASIS report proposed that India build a funded, individual account system for old age income, in which each worker's savings accumulated in an identified account, were invested through regulated intermediaries, and were converted at retirement into an income stream, with the state's role shifting from payer of pensions to regulator of the system and provider of a safety net for the poor. The design ideas that later structured the National Pension System, the permanent individual account, the central recordkeeper, the separation between recordkeeping and fund management, and the use of competing professional fund managers at regulated cost, are all visible in that report. The reform that followed was therefore not an improvisation of a single budget. It was the execution, over fifteen years and through two changes of government, of a design that a committee had set out at the turn of the century.

The political decision to move was taken in 2003. The Union government announced that the defined benefit pension would not apply to new entrants to central government service, members of the armed forces excepted, and that a new defined contribution system would take its place. In a defined contribution system the promise runs the other way: the contribution is fixed, at stated proportions of pay from employee and employer, and the pension is whatever the accumulated fund, its investment returns and the price of an annuity at retirement will buy. The risk that the defined benefit system had placed on the state, the risk that investments disappoint, that annuity rates fall, that the retiree lives longer than the average, is transferred to the individual account holder. That transfer, more than any administrative feature, is what the next two decades of argument have been about.

The older landscape contained one defined benefit island inside the contributory world, and reformers pointed at it constantly. The Employees' Pension Scheme 1995, run within the provident fund organisation, converted a slice of the employer's provident fund contribution into a pension fund promising a pension computed from pensionable salary and years of service. In structure it was a defined benefit promise resting on a fund whose contributions were fixed by rule rather than by actuarial valuation of the promise, and its periodic valuations and litigation over pensionable salary made it a standing illustration, for the reform school, of what an unpriced pension promise does over time. The coexistence mattered for the politics of 2003: the workforce outside government service already knew a state run pension promise that strained to keep itself, and the government's own employees knew a promise that had never strained at all, and the reform asked the second group to accept the economics of the first.

## A pension system built by notification

The National Pension System was not created by a statute, and it was not created by the Act of 2013. It was created by an executive notification, number F. No. 5/7/2003-ECB and PR, issued by the Ministry of Finance on 22 December 2003, and it took effect for new entrants to central government service from 1 January 2004. Section 20 of the later Act says this in terms, providing that the contributory pension system notified on that date shall be deemed to be the National Pension System with effect from 1 January 2004. A reader who understands that sequence understands the statute's character: the Act of 2013 is a regularising statute, which adopted a going concern, confirmed its features, and built a regulator over it, rather than a founding statute that designed a system on a blank page.

The regulator, too, began as an executive creation. By a resolution of the Government of India, number F. No. 5/7/2003-ECB and PR, dated 10 October 2003, the Interim Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority was set up to prepare and then to run the new system. A second resolution, dated 14 November 2008, continued and recast the interim arrangement. The Act records both resolutions in the definition of the interim Authority in section 2, and its savings provision, section 56, declares that everything done under those resolutions and under the December 2003 notification shall be deemed to have been done under the corresponding provisions of the Act. The legal technique is familiar in Indian regulatory history, though rarely over so long a span: first the executive builds the institution and the market, and the statute follows when parliamentary time and political agreement permit.

The gap between the building and the statute was a full decade, and it was not for want of attempts. An ordinance to constitute the Authority was promulgated on 29 December 2004 and lapsed on 7 April 2005. The Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha in March 2005, examined by the Standing Committee on Finance, which reported in July 2005, and then never taken up for consideration; it lapsed on the dissolution of the Fourteenth Lok Sabha. The stall was political before it was procedural. The governing coalition of those years depended on the support of Left parties for whom a statutory pension regulator presiding over market invested retirement funds was an ideological defeat, and the Bill joined the small class of Indian financial legislation that everyone in government agreed was necessary and no government could carry. The lesson the decade taught, and which the Act's eventual text absorbed, was that a pension statute in India is not carried by its drafting but by the settlement it embodies between the finance ministry's fiscal anxiety and the organised workforce's demand for certainty, and that the settlement of 2003 had been imposed by notification precisely because it could not yet be carried by legislation. During the whole of that period the interim Authority selected pension funds, appointed a central recordkeeping agency, fixed charges, issued investment guidelines and supervised the retirement savings of a growing body of subscribers, all on the strength of resolutions, contracts and the December 2003 notification. The Authority was, in that decade, a regulator whose directions rested on the agreements intermediaries had signed with it rather than on statutory power, and the arrangement survived because the intermediaries were few, the system's sponsor was the government itself, and no intermediary had an interest in testing the foundation.

The parallel most often drawn is with the atomic energy regulator, which has likewise discharged regulatory functions for decades on an executive foundation while its governing statute spoke of other things, a comparison developed in the article on [the Atomic Energy Act 1962 and the executive creation of its regulator](/2020/09/15/atomic-energy-act-1962-explained/). The two cases differ in a way that matters. The pension regulator's statutory foundation eventually arrived, and when it arrived it did not merely authorise the institution: it wrote the system's features into law, including the feature, the prohibition on assured benefits, that the politics of the following decade would test. A regulator without a statute is a vulnerability; a statute that freezes a contested design choice is a different kind of decision, and the Act of 2013 is both the cure for the first and an instance of the second.

The system's own growth during the interim decade explains why the stakes rose. The December 2003 notification bound only new central government recruits, but State governments adopted the system for their own employees one after another, as section 12 of the Act would later contemplate, each by its own notification, so that the architecture became the default retirement arrangement for government service across most of India. From 1 May 2009 the system was opened to every citizen on a voluntary basis, and a corporate model followed for private employers who wished to enrol their staff. What had begun as a service condition for a single cohort of recruits had become, before Parliament legislated at all, the principal funded pension vehicle of the country, holding the retirement savings of government employees in the Union and the States, of private sector employees whose employers enrolled, and of individual subscribers who joined on their own account.

The interim decade was not a holding pattern; it was the system's construction period, and its design decisions, all taken without a statute, explain the Act's later silences. When the account was thrown open to all citizens in May 2009, voluntary uptake ran far below expectation, and the system's architects drew the conclusion that price and friction, not appetite, were the obstacles. The responses followed in sequence: an auto choice for subscribers who made no election, so that inertia worked for the account rather than against it; the Swavalamban co-contribution from 2010, under which the Union added one thousand rupees a year to the accounts of unorganised workers who contributed within a prescribed band, an early admission that the poorest savers needed a matching incentive the pure account did not supply; and a lighter account variant for the low balance segment, with simplified enrolment through aggregators working among workers whom bank branches did not reach. By the time the statute arrived, the system covered the central government, nearly every State that had been offered it, and a voluntary membership still small against the workforce it was built for. The Act inherited all of these executive improvisations and constitutionalised none of them, which is why the development side of the system has continued to be made by scheme and regulation while its regulatory side rests on the statute.

## The making of the Act: from the Bill of 2011 to commencement

The Bill that became the Act was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 24 March 2011 and referred to the Standing Committee on Finance on 29 March 2011. The Committee, chaired by Yashwant Sinha, reported on 30 August 2011, and its report is the hinge of the legislative history, because the enacted text adopted several of the Committee's principal recommendations and rejected others, and the choices are legible in the sections as passed. The Committee recommended that a subscriber who sought minimum assured returns be permitted to invest in a scheme providing such returns, to be notified by the Authority; that withdrawals from the individual account be permitted subject to conditions specified by regulations; that foreign investment in pension intermediaries be capped at 26 per cent or at the ceiling approved for the insurance sector, whichever was higher; that at least one pension fund be from the public sector; and that a Pension Advisory Committee with representation from the major stakeholders advise the Authority on the framing of regulations. Each of those recommendations is traceable in the Act: the assured return scheme option in section 20, the withdrawal clause in the same section, the foreign holding ceiling in section 24, the Government company requirement in section 23, and the Advisory Committee in section 45. The Committee's further recommendations that pension funds be compulsorily insured and that a fixed proportion of fund managers be drawn from the public sector were not accepted, the government reasoning that the first was met by the Authority's duty to ensure the safety of contributions and the second by the floor of one public sector fund.

The Bill's passage then stalled once more. A Cabinet note on official amendments was moved in December 2011 and withdrawn when the Bill was deferred in the winter session, and the Union Cabinet approved the amended Bill on 4 October 2012. The Lok Sabha passed it on 4 September 2013 and the Rajya Sabha on 6 September 2013, and the President's assent followed on 18 September 2013, making it Act 23 of 2013. The parliamentary debate of September 2013 rehearsed, in compressed form, every argument this article has since had to arrange. Members speaking against the Bill pressed the two objections that have never left the subject: that a pension invested in markets without a floor of assured return was a gamble with the subsistence of the old, and that permitting foreign participation in pension funds placed retirement savings within reach of interests answerable elsewhere. The treasury benches answered in the idiom of the OASIS decade: that a statutory assurance would recreate, inside the new system, the unfunded promise the reform existed to retire, and that the foreign ceiling written into the Act, tied to the insurance sector's, kept control of the funds Indian while letting the sector draw on the world's pension management experience. The Act was brought into force on 1 February 2014 by notification number S.O. 302 (E), a date worth stating exactly because the commencement matters to the savings and transfer provisions: on the establishment of the statutory Authority, every asset and liability of the interim Authority stood transferred to it under section 13, its contracts were deemed to be the Authority's contracts, and proceedings by or against the interim body could be continued by or against its statutory successor. The regulator of October 2003 and the regulator of February 2014 are, in law, one continuous institution wearing two different legal foundations.

## What the PFRDA Act 2013 reaches, and what it leaves outside

Section 12 fixes the Act's extent and application, and it is the section a reader should master first, because most errors about the Authority's jurisdiction are errors about this section. The Act applies to the National Pension System and to any other pension scheme not regulated by any other enactment, and a scheme of that second kind must conform to the Authority's regulations within the time the regulations specify. The Act then lists what it does not touch. The schemes and funds under the Coal Mines Provident Fund and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 1948, the Employees' Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 1952, the Seamen's Provident Fund Act 1966, the Assam Tea Plantations Provident Fund and Pension Fund Scheme Act 1955, and the Jammu and Kashmir Employees' Provident Funds Act 1961 are outside it, as are the annuity and pension contracts of life insurance that the Insurance Act 1938 defines, and any scheme the Central Government exempts by notification. The exclusion of the provident fund statutes is the structural fact of Indian retirement provision: the two great funded systems, the provident fund family administered under the 1952 Act and the pension system administered under this Act, sit in separate statutory universes with separate regulators, and the comparison between them is taken up in the article on [the Employees' Provident Funds Act 1952 and the retirement benefits it builds](/2021/03/01/epf-act-1952-explained/).

The second limb of section 12 deserves more attention than it usually receives. By applying the Act to any other pension scheme not regulated by any other enactment, and by requiring every such scheme to conform to the Authority's regulations within the time the regulations specify, Parliament gave the Authority a residual jurisdiction over the unregulated fringe of the pension market, the territory in which pension like schemes have historically been floated by entities outside every regulator's fence. The provision has never carried the volume of the National Pension System itself, but its presence explains the Authority's self understanding: it is not merely the supervisor of one government scheme but the pension regulator of the country, with the named system as its principal charge and a statutory watch over everything else that calls itself a pension and answers to no one else.

Section 12 also contains the federal machinery of the system, in two short provisions. Persons appointed before 1 January 2004 to the public services of the Union or to the All India Services are excluded from the Act, because their pensions remained under the defined benefit rules; persons appointed to State services are likewise outside it unless the State acts. And by section 12, sub-section (4), any State Government or the administrator of a Union territory may, by notification, extend the National Pension System to its employees. That single clause is the legal doorway through which the system entered State service, and its terms matter for the later argument about reversion: the Act contemplates the State choosing the system by notification, and it contains no clause contemplating the State choosing to leave and taking the accumulated money with it. A person governed by an excluded scheme may still join the National Pension System voluntarily under sub-section (6), which is the statutory basis on which provident fund subscribers and others hold a personal pension account alongside their occupational arrangement.

Two structural contrasts with the provident fund world complete the map. The Employees' Provident Fund is a pooled fund: contributions are credited at a rate declared year by year, the individual has a balance but not a portfolio, and investment and longevity risk are socialised inside the fund and its pension scheme. The National Pension System is the opposite construction: an individual portfolio at market value, with risk located in the account. A worker may hold both, and many do, the provident fund through employment covered by the 1952 Act and the pension account voluntarily or through government service, so the two systems divide the retirement savings of the country between them on a principle exactly contrary to each other, and the choice of emphasis between them is a live policy question that the Act answers only for its own side of the border.

The armed forces exclusion has its own political economy, and it illuminates the rest. Defence pensions are the largest single component of the Union's pension bill and are administered as a charge of the defence budget, outside the contributory frame altogether. The class the Act excludes is also the class that won, in the same decade, the most emphatic reaffirmation of the defined benefit principle in Indian public life: the One Rank One Pension settlement, under which defence pensions are equalised across retirement cohorts and revised periodically, was accepted by the government and notified in November 2015 after a prolonged public campaign by ex servicemen. The contrast is structural rather than invidious, but it is not lost on the civilian workforce: the uniformed services, whose retirement ages are the earliest and whose employment is the most state directed of all, kept the pension of assurance, and they kept it by being placed outside the Act that abolished assurance for everyone the Act reached.

## The Authority: composition, tenure and the terms of independence

Chapter II of the Act establishes the Authority as a body corporate with perpetual succession, its head office in the National Capital Region, and power to open offices elsewhere in India. Section 4 settles its composition: a Chairperson, three whole-time members and three part-time members, appointed by the Central Government from among persons of ability, integrity and standing with knowledge and experience in economics or finance or law, with at least one member drawn from each of those three disciplines. The composition is a deliberate design statement. A pension regulator's questions are actuarial, financial and legal in roughly equal measure, and the section writes that mixture into the body's constitution rather than leaving it to appointment practice.

### Who can be appointed to the PFRDA?

The Authority consists of a Chairperson, three whole-time members and three part-time members, appointed by the Central Government from persons of ability, integrity and standing who have knowledge and experience in economics, finance or law, with the Act requiring at least one member from each of those three disciplines.

Section 5 gives the Chairperson and whole-time members a term of five years, with eligibility for reappointment, subject to age ceilings of sixty five years for the Chairperson and sixty two years for a whole-time member; a part-time member holds office for a term not exceeding five years. A member's salary and conditions may not be varied to the member's disadvantage after appointment, a standard independence protection in Indian regulatory statutes. Removal under section 6 is confined to stated grounds: insolvency, incapacity, conviction of an offence involving moral turpitude, acquisition of a prejudicial financial or other interest, and abuse of position rendering continuance detrimental to the public interest, with a right to be heard before removal on the interest and abuse grounds. Section 7 imposes a two year cooling off period after office, during which the Chairperson and whole-time members may not, without the Central Government's approval, take employment under the Union or a State or any appointment in a regulated entity in the pension sector, and the same restriction was extended to those who held office in the interim Authority.

The remaining provisions of the chapter are administrative but not trivial. The Chairperson holds the powers of general superintendence over administrative matters under section 8. Meetings, quorum and decision by majority with a casting vote are dealt with in section 9, which also obliges a member who is a director of a company and has a pecuniary interest in a matter before the Authority to disclose it and stand aside from the deliberation. Section 10 protects the Authority's proceedings from invalidation by reason of a vacancy, a defect in constitution or appointment, or a procedural irregularity not affecting the merits. Section 11 empowers the Authority to appoint its own officers and employees on conditions determined by regulations. The pattern is that of the modern Indian financial regulator, modelled consciously on the securities and insurance regulators that preceded it: a small expert body, security of tenure within a fixed term, financial and administrative autonomy in staffing, and accountability running through audit, annual report and the laying of its regulations before Parliament, each of which the later chapters supply.

The independence this chapter constructs is real but qualified, and the qualification is a clause the Act shares with its regulatory siblings. The securities regulator and the insurance regulator work under materially similar provisions binding them to government directions on questions of policy, and in each case the working independence of the institution has depended less on the text than on the convention that the direction power is held in reserve. The pension regulator's case has one distinguishing feature: the government whose directions bind it is also the largest employer inside its system and the sponsor of the schemes it administers, so the distance between regulator and regulated, which the tenure provisions build so carefully, is structurally shorter here than in any comparable Indian statute, a fact the assessment at the end of this article weighs.

## Duties, powers and functions: the mandate in section 14

Section 14 states the Authority's duty in one sentence and then unfolds it. The duty is to regulate, promote and ensure the orderly growth of the National Pension System and of the pension schemes to which the Act applies, and to protect the interests of subscribers. The pairing of verbs is the Indian regulatory formula, development and protection in a single office, and the tension between them runs through the Authority's working life: the development mandate presses toward wider coverage, lower cost and new products, while the protection mandate presses toward caution in investment patterns, suspicion of guarantees, and the insistence, visible throughout the system's design, that no subscriber be promised what markets may not deliver.

The functions listed in section 14 follow the duty. The Authority regulates the system and the schemes, approves the schemes and their terms, and lays down norms for the management of the corpus of the pension funds, including investment guidelines. It registers and regulates intermediaries and grants, renews, modifies, withdraws, suspends and cancels certificates of registration. It protects subscribers by ensuring the safety of their contributions and by ensuring that intermediation and operational costs are economical and reasonable, a statutory warrant for the low cost design that is the system's signature and for the Authority's practice of selecting intermediaries through competitive bidding. It must establish a mechanism for the redressal of grievances of subscribers, to be determined by regulations. It promotes professional and other organisations connected with the pension system. It adjudicates disputes between intermediaries, and between intermediaries and subscribers. It collects data, commissions studies and research, educates subscribers and the public on pension and retirement savings, trains intermediaries, standardises the dissemination of information about the performance of pension funds and their benchmarks, regulates the regulated assets of the recordkeeping agency, levies fees and charges, prescribes the form of accounts, and calls for information, inspects, inquires and investigates.

For the investigation power, section 14 borrows the armoury of a civil court: while exercising its powers of inspection and inquiry the Authority has the powers of a civil court under the Code of Civil Procedure 1908 in respect of discovery and production of documents, summoning and enforcing attendance and examining persons on oath, inspecting books and registers, and issuing commissions for the examination of witnesses. Pending an investigation, section 14 also arms the Authority with interim measures of real severity: it may restrain persons from participating in a scheme, restrain an office bearer of an intermediary from acting, impound and retain proceeds under a scheme, direct that assets under investigation not be disposed of, and, with the approval of a Judicial Magistrate of the first class, attach bank accounts of an intermediary or of a person associated with the scheme, for a period not exceeding one month and confined to the proceeds actually involved in the violation.

The powers continue through the chapter. Section 15 authorises directions to intermediaries and associated persons where the Authority is satisfied that they are necessary in the interests of subscribers or the orderly development of the system, to prevent affairs being conducted detrimentally, or to secure proper management. Section 16 authorises investigation by an investigating authority with power to require production of books and information and to examine on oath; obstruction of an investigation is itself punishable, with imprisonment extending to one year or a fine extending to twenty five crore rupees, or both, and a further daily fine. Section 17 confers search and seizure powers on authorised officers, with the procedure of the Code of Criminal Procedure applied to the search. Section 18 empowers the Authority, after inquiry, to order a person who has violated or is likely to violate the Act or the regulations to cease and desist. Section 19 addresses the failing intermediary: where the Authority has reason to believe that the central recordkeeping agency or a pension fund is acting in a manner likely to prejudice subscribers, it reports to the Central Government, which may appoint an administrator to manage the intermediary under the Authority's direction and control. The architecture is preventive as much as punitive, designed for a sector in which the injured asset is a retirement corpus that cannot be re-earned if it is lost.

The grievance duty has a machinery of its own in practice. Subscriber complaints travel upward through the point of presence and the recordkeeping agency into the Authority's centralised grievance management system, which registers, tracks and escalates a complaint against the intermediary concerned, and the pension sector has no separate ombudsman of the kind the insurance sector maintains, so the Authority is itself the last administrative stop before the appellate and judicial routes described later in this article. The enforcement design closes the loop: it is because section 28 makes the failure to redress grievances itself a penal default, after the Authority has called upon the intermediary to act, that the grievance system has consequences beyond correspondence.

The development limb of the mandate shows most clearly in what the Authority administers beyond the core government system. The Authority ran the Swavalamban arrangement for unorganised workers from 2010, in which the government co-contributed to the accounts of workers outside every other social security net, and administers its successor, the Atal Pension Yojana, examined later in this article. It built a corporate model under which employers may co-contribute for their employees, a lighter recordkeeping variant for the low balance segment, and in September 2024, following the announcement in the Union Budget for 2024-25, it began operating the NPS Vatsalya arrangement, under which parents and guardians open and contribute to accounts for minors, the account converting into an ordinary individual account when the child attains majority. None of these variants is described in the Act. All of them are section 14's development duty, and the Authority's regulation power, at work.

The dual mandate carries a tension that commentators on the Act have identified from the beginning, and a pillar should record it. The Authority is at once the promoter of the National Pension System, charged with its orderly growth, and the policeman of the intermediaries who run it, charged with penalising them; it is the administrator, on the government's behalf, of schemes such as the Atal Pension Yojana and the assured option, and simultaneously the regulator of the system inside which those schemes operate. The insurance regulator works under a comparable pairing of development and regulation, and the Indian settlement has generally preferred the pairing to a separation, on the ground that a young sector needs a builder and that a regulator which must also build understands its own rules. The risk is equally plain: that the promoter's targets soften the policeman's hand, or that the administrator's schemes receive, inside the system, a gentleness the regulator would not extend to another sponsor's. The Act contains no structural answer to the tension beyond the integrity of the institution itself, and the record of enforcement described later in this article is the material from which a reader must judge it.

## Section 20: the system written into law, and the prohibition at its centre

Section 20 is the heart of the Act for every subscriber, because it is the provision that converts an executive scheme into a statutory system and states the features that no regulation may take away. Sub-section (1) deems the contributory pension system notified on 22 December 2003 to be the National Pension System with effect from 1 January 2004, and provides that the system may be amended from time to time by regulations, a delegation of unusual breadth whose limits the remaining sub-sections set. Sub-section (2) then prescribes the basic features that the system shall have on the commencement of the Act, notwithstanding anything in the original notification.

The features are these. Every subscriber shall have an individual pension account. Withdrawals not exceeding twenty five per cent of the contribution made by the subscriber may be permitted from the account, subject to conditions as to purpose, frequency and limits specified by regulations. The functions of recordkeeping, accounting and switching of options shall be effected by the central recordkeeping agency. There shall be a choice of multiple pension funds and multiple schemes, with two provisos: the subscriber shall have the option of investing the whole of the funds in Government securities, and a subscriber seeking minimum assured returns shall have the option of investing in such schemes providing minimum assured returns as the Authority may notify. There shall be portability of the individual pension account on a change of employment. Collection and transmission of contributions and instructions shall run through points of presence to the central recordkeeping agency. A subscriber shall not exit from the system except as specified by the regulations, and at exit the subscriber shall purchase an annuity from a life insurance company in accordance with the regulations. Sub-section (3) adds the second account, the additional account a subscriber may hold at choice, with free withdrawal of part or all of the money at any time; that additional account is the statutory seed of the two tier structure through which the system separates locked retirement savings from liquid savings.

Between the portability clause and the exit clause sits the provision on which this article turns, clause (g) of sub-section (2): there shall not be any implicit or explicit assurance of benefits except market based guarantee mechanism to be purchased by the subscriber. The sentence repays slow reading. What it prohibits is assurance, whether spoken (explicit) or conveyed by design and presentation (implicit), and what it prohibits assurance of is benefits, the outcome the subscriber will receive. The single exception is a guarantee mechanism that is market based and purchased by the subscriber: assurance is lawful in the system only when it is a priced product, bought like insurance by the person who wants it, so that its cost is visible and borne by its beneficiary. What the clause excludes is the two forms of assurance Indian pension politics had always supplied: the employer's open ended promise, of the defined benefit kind, and the regulator's quiet implication that a state sponsored system would not be allowed to disappoint.

The clause must also be read with its neighbour. The proviso to the choice clause entitles a subscriber seeking minimum assured returns to invest in schemes providing such returns as the Authority notifies. Parliament therefore did not write a system in which no assured product could exist. It wrote a system in which assurance could exist only as a notified scheme or a purchased guarantee, never as the character of the system itself. That distinction, between an assured product inside the system and an assured system, is the statutory ground on which the later assured payout option would be built, and it is why the drafting of section 20 repays the attention this article gives it: the argument of the following decade was conducted inside the space these two clauses leave open.

One related misconception should be cleared at the point where it arises. The Act contains no provision by which the Central Government guarantees the returns of the National Pension System, and no section to which a subscriber could point as a sovereign promise behind the account. The provision sometimes confused with a guarantee in summaries of the Act, section 44, does something entirely different: it empowers the Central Government, on stated grounds and for a period not exceeding six months, to supersede the Authority itself, a provision examined with the accountability provisions later in this article. The guarantee position of the Act is exhausted by section 20: the system's benefits are market determined, assurance is available only as a purchased mechanism or a notified assured return scheme, and the state's financial exposure to the subscriber's outcome is, by deliberate statutory design, nil. When an assurance later entered the system for government employees, it entered as an employer's promise funded by the employer, which is a different legal animal from a guarantee of the system, and the difference is the subject of the section on the assured option.

One further feature of the drafting deserves a name, because so much of the later history flows from it: Parliament locked the features and delegated the dials. Section 20, sub-section (1), contemplates that the National Pension System may itself be amended from time to time in accordance with the regulations, and section 52 hands the Authority a regulation power whose heads include the amendment of the system, the manner and conditions of partial withdrawal, the conditions of exit and annuity purchase, the functioning of every intermediary class and the establishment and working of the Trust. The statute thus contains, inside itself, a standing authority to rebuild the system by subordinate legislation, subject only to the laying requirement of section 53 and to the framework of features section 20 prescribes. The assured option regulations of 2025, which constructed an entire second pension design inside the system's accounts, were an exercise of exactly this delegated breadth, and they were possible without a fresh Act because the Act of 2013 had been drafted, unusually, as a frame that expects its own contents to be revised by the regulator it created. Whether that breadth is the Act's most farsighted feature or its largest democratic deficit is a question the assessment section takes up; its existence is not in doubt.

## The machinery: recordkeepers, points of presence, pension funds and the chain of custody

Chapter VI builds the machine that section 20 describes, and its design principle is the separation of functions, so that no single intermediary both holds the subscriber's record, receives the subscriber's money, invests it and keeps custody of the assets. The central recordkeeping agency, appointed under section 21 by certificate of registration, receives instructions from subscribers through the points of presence, transmits them to the pension funds, effects switching instructions, and maintains the record of every individual pension account. The Act contemplates more than one such agency being appointed in the public interest, and it attaches a distinctive discipline to the office: all assets and properties owned, leased or developed by the agency are regulated assets, which the Authority may appropriate and take over on the expiry or revocation of the certificate, compensating fair value where the departure is not for violation. The provision exists because the record of a nation's pension accounts cannot be held hostage to a vendor's contract. In the system's working life the recordkeeping function has been performed by Protean eGov Technologies, the company that began as the first recordkeeper under the interim Authority, and by KFin Technologies, and each subscriber account carries the permanent retirement account number, the PRAN, by which the record follows the subscriber across employers, States and intermediaries.

Points of presence, registered under section 22, are the system's front office: they receive contributions and instructions, transmit them to the trustee bank or the recordkeeping agency, and pay out benefits in accordance with the regulations. Pension funds, under section 23, receive contributions, accumulate them and make payments to the subscriber as the regulations specify; the number of pension funds is determined by regulations and may be varied in the public interest, and at least one of them must be a Government company, the floor the Standing Committee recommended and the Act adopted. The funds that began the system were the three public sector managers sponsored by the State Bank of India, the Life Insurance Corporation and UTI, and the register has since widened to private managers chosen through the Authority's selection processes, so that the subscriber's choice of fund is a choice among competing professional managers investing one pool of money under common investment guidelines.

### Can pension funds invest NPS money outside India?

No. Section 25 prohibits a pension fund from investing the funds of subscribers outside India, directly or indirectly. The retirement savings accumulated under the National Pension System are therefore invested entirely in Indian securities: government securities, corporate bonds, equities and the other asset classes the investment guidelines permit.

Two further provisions police the machine's ownership and standards. Section 24 caps the aggregate foreign holding in a pension fund at twenty six per cent of its paid up capital or the percentage approved for an Indian insurance company under the Insurance Act 1938, whichever is higher, a pegging device by which the pension ceiling follows the insurance ceiling without fresh amendment; when the insurance ceiling was raised to seventy four per cent in 2021, the pension ceiling moved with it by force of the comparison the section itself draws. Section 26 requires the recordkeeping agency, the points of presence and the pension funds to satisfy eligibility norms specified by regulations, extending to minimum capital, track record, costs and fees, geographical reach, customer base, information technology capability and human resources. Section 27 then supplies the gate: no intermediary may commence any activity under the Act except under a certificate of registration, with a six month transitional window for intermediaries appointed by the interim Authority, and with power in the Authority to suspend or cancel a certificate after hearing the holder.

Around the statutory intermediaries sit three further institutions that the Act names and the regulations constitute. The National Pension System Trust, defined in section 2 as the board of trustees who hold the assets of subscribers for their benefit, is the fiduciary layer of the system: the funds are held in trust for subscribers, not owned by the pension funds that manage them, and the Authority's regulation making power in section 52 extends expressly to the establishment, duties and functioning of the Trust. The trustee bank, likewise defined in section 2, is the banking channel through which contributions move from points of presence to pension funds and through which payouts return, so that investment money and recordkeeping never rest in the same hands. The custodian holds the securities in which the funds are invested. At the far end of the chain stand the annuity service providers, the life insurance companies from which the exiting subscriber must purchase an annuity; they are empanelled within the pension system, but as insurers they are licensed and supervised under the insurance statutes, whose regulator's own constitution is the subject of the article on [the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority and the development mandate it shares with the pension regulator](/2021/02/01/irdai-act-1999-explained/). The annuity is the point at which the pension system's money passes into the insurance system's hands for the rest of the subscriber's life, and the Act marks the junction by requiring the purchase to be made from a life insurance company in accordance with the regulations.

What the subscriber may choose within the machine is fixed by a mixture of statute and subordinate legislation, and the boundary is worth observing. Section 20 guarantees the existence of choice: multiple funds, multiple schemes, the option of the whole fund in Government securities, and the option of a notified assured return scheme. The content of the choices is set by regulations and investment guidelines. For subscribers outside government employment, the active choice permits allocation among asset classes up to stated ceilings, with equity permitted up to seventy five per cent of the corpus, corporate debt and Government securities each permitted up to the whole corpus, and alternative assets confined to a small fraction, while the auto choice places the account in a life cycle fund whose equity content, aggressive, moderate or conservative at seventy five, fifty or twenty five per cent at younger ages, declines as the subscriber ages. For government subscribers the default pattern, settled under the Ministry of Finance investment pattern first notified in 2008, holds the equity allocation to a ceiling of fifteen per cent, and government subscribers were later given the further choices of pension fund and of the moderate and conservative life cycle patterns. Contributions follow the same mixed pattern of authority: the statute is silent on rates, and the rates live in the system's service framework, under which the central government employee contributes ten per cent of basic pay and dearness allowance and the government, which contributed ten per cent at the system's start, has contributed fourteen per cent from 1 April 2019. The lesson generalises across the Act: Parliament fixed the architecture and the prohibitions, and left the dials to the regulator and the employer.

The failure protocol completes the machinery's logic. If a pension fund were to fail, the subscribers' money is not the fund's property: the schemes' assets are held through the Trust and the custodian, and the Authority's duties under section 14 include arranging the free transfer of the schemes of an insolvent, liquidated or deregistered pension fund to another fund, while its powers over the recordkeeping agency extend to taking over the regulated assets with compensation where the record itself is at risk. The point of presence network is likewise replaceable, since the subscriber's relationship of record is with the account and the recordkeeper, not with the office that opened the account. The architecture was designed, in other words, so that no single institution's failure can strand a retirement: every function has a substitute, every asset a custodian independent of its manager, and every record a statutory owner other than the firm that maintains it. That the protocol has remained a protocol, untested by a failure of any major intermediary, is the strongest evidence for the design it protects.

Supervision between crises is documentary. Intermediaries file periodic returns and compliance certificates with the Authority, the recordkeeping agency's reconciliation of contributions against remittances produces the exception reporting on which nodal offices are pursued for unremitted or unmatched deductions, and the pension funds' portfolios and net asset values are computed and disclosed on a daily cycle, so that the state of every account in the country is, in principle, knowable to the regulator each evening. The grievance data of the centralised system feed the same loop, patterns of complaint against an intermediary being among the triggers for the inspection and investigation powers described in the duties section. The design assumption throughout is that in a system of millions of small accounts, supervision must be by data first and by visit afterwards, and the Act's information powers in sections 14 and 16 were drafted for exactly that sequence.

Portability is the feature on which the design's claim to suit a mobile workforce stands or falls, and it deserves to be read as the Act's answer to the provident fund era's fragmentation, in which a worker's savings were stranded in a succession of employer accounts. Under section 20 the account is the subscriber's own: it survives a change of employer, of sector and of State, and the subscriber may shift the accumulated balance between pension funds, between investment choices and, where the framework allows, between recordkeeping agencies, without exiting the system or disturbing the account's identity. A second recordkeeping agency was licensed in the system's second decade, so that new subscribers for the first time chose their recordkeeper as they chose their fund. The subscriber may also bring money in: transfers from recognised provident and superannuation arrangements into the pension account are within the Act's contemplation, which positioned the account as a potential consolidator of a working life's scattered retirement savings. What the design does not permit is equally deliberate: the account cannot be pledged away from its purpose in the accumulation years, withdrawals are confined to the regulated grounds, and the corpus is not a general asset available to the subscriber's creditors in the manner of a bank balance, because the statute's object is a retirement income and not a savings account with a pension label.

## How a contribution travels: the system in motion

The architecture is easiest to grasp by following one month's contribution through it. In the government model, the drawing and disbursing officer deducts the employee's contribution from salary and the government's matching contribution is computed with it; the pay and accounts office, acting as the nodal office, uploads the contribution details to the recordkeeping agency and remits the pooled funds to the trustee bank. The trustee bank holds the money in the system's account and, on the recordkeeper's instructions reconciling the remittance against the uploaded details, transfers it to the pension funds in the proportions the subscriber's choices dictate. Each pension fund invests its receipts in the schemes it manages for the system, and the units created are allotted at the net asset value computed for that day, so the contribution becomes units in the subscriber's account, visible in the statement of transaction the recordkeeper issues. Money that cannot be matched to subscriber details does not enter the funds; it waits in the banking leg of the chain until the record is repaired, which is why the quality of the payroll data at the first step governs the experience at the last. In the voluntary models the point of presence performs the nodal office's role, collecting the subscriber's contribution and documents and passing both into the same chain. The subscriber experiences the whole apparatus as a single account and a single statement; the statute's contribution is to have made every hand the money passes through a regulated one, with duties owed to the subscriber rather than to the institution that appointed it.

## The coverage models through which the system reaches its subscribers

One statute and one account architecture carry several distinct populations, and the differences between them explain many of the system's later politics. The government model covers central government employees and, through the State extension discussed in the section on the Act's reach, State government employees, on mandatory terms: contribution rates fixed by the employer government, the default investment pattern applying unless the employee chooses otherwise, and exit conditions shaped by the employment. The corporate model is voluntary at the employer's election: a corporate entity may adopt the system for its employees, co-contributing with them or facilitating their individual contributions, on the same account architecture. The all citizens model, open since 1 May 2009, is the individual voluntary system, joined at a point of presence or through the electronic channel, with the full range of investment choices and the Tier II account available beside the pension account. Alongside these stand the scheme variants administered through the same machinery for populations the core models did not reach: the lighter variant built for low balance accounts, the Atal Pension Yojana for unorganised workers, and the minor's account arrangement. The significance of the layering is that reform pressure does not arrive uniformly. The assured payout was demanded by, and delivered to, the government model alone; the tax incentives are aimed principally at the individual model; the guarantee debate means one thing to a subscriber whose employer co-contributes and another to a subscriber funding the whole account alone. The Act's single frame holds all of them, which is its administrative triumph and the source of its political complexity.

## The pension funds in practice: appointment, fees and performance

The funds that manage the system's money were chosen by competition, and the competition shaped the economics of the whole design. When the interim Authority first appointed managers for the government sector, it selected the pension arms of three public financial institutions, the State Bank of India, the Life Insurance Corporation and UTI, after a bidding process in which the investment management fee was the decisive variable; the fees discovered were a small fraction of one per cent of assets, an order of magnitude below the charges of the mutual fund industry, and the low cost structure became one of the system's proudest claims. The opening to all citizens in 2009 widened the field to private managers, and the field has widened further since, through further rounds of appointment, to a register of managers drawn from the banking, insurance and asset management groups of the country. Appointment is for a term and renewable, the fee is capped by the terms on which the appointment is made, and the Authority's duty under section 14 to review the performance of the funds is worked out through benchmarking each scheme against published indices, so that a manager's continuation depends on measured performance rather than incumbency. The scale the machinery has reached is the background fact of the sector: the system's assets under management crossed ten lakh crore rupees in 2023, a decade after commencement, a figure that makes the pension funds among the largest domestic investors in the country's debt and equity markets and gives the investment guidelines discussed next a macroeconomic weight their drafters in 2008 did not have to consider.

## The investment guidelines: the system's working constitution

Parliament fixed the investment perimeter, confining the system's money to securities and instruments within India, and left the interior to guidelines issued under the Act's regulation and direction powers; those guidelines, revised repeatedly, are the system's working constitution, and they repay attention because they decide more of a subscriber's outcome than any other document in the structure. Government securities form the system's ballast, corporate debt its income engine, and equity its growth element, with a small allowance for alternative assets. The default pattern for government subscribers, descending from the interim Finance Ministry pattern of 2008, caps equity at fifteen per cent and distributes the balance across government securities and corporate debt, a deliberately conservative stance whose returns the Finance Ministry has periodically been pressed to defend against the higher ceilings available to individual subscribers. The life cycle funds automate the derisking that the default pattern performs by rule: equity exposure is highest for the young subscriber and tapers as retirement approaches, converting the portfolio gradually toward debt, on the reasoning that a loss at thirty can be recovered by thirty years of contributions and a loss at fifty nine cannot. The active choice inverts the philosophy, handing the asset allocation to the subscriber within ceilings, on the reasoning that the account is the subscriber's property and its growth the subscriber's affair. The two philosophies coexist inside one system because they answer to the two theories of the subscriber that run through the whole Act: the citizen as beneficiary of a careful State, and the citizen as owner of an account. The benchmark corpus of the assured option, examined in the section on that scheme, later gave the conservative philosophy a legal consequence its framers did not foresee: the default pattern became the measure against which the State's assurance itself is computed.

## Exit, withdrawal and the annuity: what the Act decides and what it leaves to regulations

No part of the Act is misread more often than the law of exit, because the figures subscribers care about, how much may be taken as a lump sum, how much must buy an annuity, at what ages and on what conditions, are not in the Act at all. Section 20 fixes the frame in three strokes. A subscriber shall not exit except as the regulations specify. Withdrawals in service are capped by the statute itself at twenty five per cent of the subscriber's own contribution, with purpose, frequency and limits left to regulations. And at exit the subscriber shall purchase an annuity from a life insurance company in accordance with the regulations. Every operative number in the exit system is therefore a creature of the Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority (Exits and Withdrawals under the National Pension System) Regulations 2015, as amended from time to time, and any statement of those numbers is a statement about regulations, which the Authority may amend, and not about the Act, which it may not. This article observes that distinction throughout, and a reader relying on a figure should treat it as the regulations' figure at the time described rather than as a promise of the statute.

Within that frame, the regulations build three doors. The ordinary door is normal exit at sixty years or superannuation, at which up to sixty per cent of the accumulated corpus may be withdrawn as a lump sum and at least forty per cent must be applied to purchase an annuity, with the whole corpus payable as a lump sum where it does not exceed a stated threshold, a threshold the regulations set at two lakh rupees at their making and raised to five lakh rupees by amendment in 2021. The second door is premature exit before sixty, permitted only after a minimum period in the system, at which the proportions reverse: at least eighty per cent must purchase an annuity and up to twenty per cent may be taken as a lump sum, with full withdrawal permitted below a smaller threshold, raised in the same 2021 amendment to two and a half lakh rupees. The third door is exit on death, under which the corpus passes to the nominee or legal heirs, with annuity obligations and full payment thresholds of their own that the regulations calibrate. Alongside the doors, the regulations govern the in service partial withdrawal that section 20 authorises, confining it to stated purposes, among them children's higher education and marriage, purchase or construction of a house, treatment of specified illnesses and the establishment of a new business, and capped in number and spacing; they permit the subscriber who does not wish to exit at sixty to continue in the system and to defer the lump sum and the annuity, the age to which continuation and deferral may run having been raised by amendment from seventy to seventy five and, by a further amendment of the regulations in 2025, the exit framework was recast again with higher full withdrawal thresholds and a longer horizon. The direction of the amendments is consistent: the system's first instinct was compulsion toward annuitisation, and each revision has traded a measure of that compulsion for flexibility at the margins, while the statutory frame, the forty per cent annuitisation principle for a normal exit of any size, has been left in the regulations untouched.

The annuity obligation deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is the point where the defined contribution design completes itself. A lump sum can be spent, outlived or lost; the system's answer to longevity risk is to compel the conversion of a substantial part of the corpus into an income for life, purchased from a life insurer at the annuity rates of the day. The subscriber chooses among empanelled insurers and among annuity variants, with or without return of purchase price, payable for life or jointly with a spouse, but the purchase itself is not optional at a normal exit above the threshold. The consequence is that the pension a subscriber actually receives depends on two market prices set decades apart in effect: the accumulation the funds achieved during service, and the annuity rate the insurers offer at retirement. The general position described in this article is legal information about that system, not advice on any individual account; an exit decision turns on the regulations in force at the date of exit and on the subscriber's own circumstances, and a subscriber facing one should take advice on the individual case before electing.

The history of the exit regulations is itself the clearest demonstration of the delegation the Act performs. The original regulations of 2015 set the full withdrawal threshold at two lakh rupees and the deferral ceiling at seventy years. Amendment in 2021 raised the full withdrawal threshold to five lakh rupees, restated the premature exit thresholds, and widened the practical operation of partial withdrawal. The amendment of 2025 carried the deferral ceiling to seventy five and recast the exit provisions again. Each change altered, for every subscriber in the system, the terms on which a lifetime of contributions becomes an income, and each was made by the Authority's regulations laid before Parliament rather than by an Act of Parliament, a distribution of law making labour that the section on section 20 has already identified as the statute's deliberate design. The direction of travel across the decade is consistent: toward larger lump sums, later exits and wider mid course access, which is to say toward treating the account more nearly as the subscriber's property and less nearly as the State's instrument, while the annuity floor, the one element that keeps the arrangement a pension rather than a provident fund, has been held in place throughout.

The annuity half of the exit deserves the same precision as the withdrawal half, because it is where the system's market philosophy meets an insurance market that prices on its own terms. The annuity service provider is a life insurer empanelled by the Authority; the subscriber chooses among the empanelled providers and among a standardised set of annuity variants, which include an annuity for life, annuities guaranteed for a stated term and payable for life thereafter, an annuity for life with return of the purchase price to the nominee, an annuity increasing at a simple rate, and joint life variants under which the annuity continues to the spouse. The rate is the insurer's rate on the day of purchase, fixed for the life of the annuity; it is not indexed to prices, and the monthly amount bought at sixty will be the same monthly amount, in nominal terms, at ninety. This absence of indexation is the least examined feature of the ordinary system and the one that most shaped the politics of the assured option: a dearness relieved payout answers, by design, the objection that the annuity half of the ordinary system leaves the retiree to absorb three decades of inflation alone. The lump sum half of the exit is not exposed to that objection, being invested or spent as the subscriber chooses, but it is exposed to the mirror objection, that a retiree may exhaust in a few years the capital that was meant to support thirty, which is why the balance between the two halves, and not either half alone, is the exit system's real policy variable.

## The tax treatment lives in another statute

No account of the system is complete without the confession that a large part of its attractiveness to individual subscribers is legislated elsewhere. The PFRDA Act contains no tax provisions. The inducements sit in the Income-tax Act 1961: deductions for contributions under section 80CCD, including an additional deduction for individual subscribers beyond the general ceiling, a deduction for the employer's contribution in the case of employees, exemption of the lump sum withdrawn at exit up to the prescribed proportion of the corpus under section 10, clause (12A), exemption of partial withdrawals up to one fourth of the subscriber's own contributions under section 10, clause (12B), and taxation of the annuity as income in the year of receipt. The result is the nearest Indian equivalent of the exempt exempt taxed pattern, and it was assembled by successive budgets rather than by the pension statute, which is why the fiscal debate about the system is conducted in the Finance Bill each year and not in the Act. The assured option was folded into the same pattern by executive clarification: a circular of the Ministry of Finance in July 2025 recorded that the tax benefits available under the ordinary system extend to the Unified Pension Scheme to the extent admissible, alongside the extension of retirement and death gratuity to its subscribers. The pension a subscriber actually receives is thus the product of three instruments acting together, the Act for the architecture, the regulations for the exits, and the Income-tax Act for the arithmetic, and a reform of any one of them changes the value of the other two.

## Enforcement: penalties, adjudication and the appeal ladder

Chapter VIII gives the Authority its teeth, and the design separates the finding of default from the punishment for it. Section 28 prescribes monetary penalties for the standard regulatory failures: carrying on a regulated activity without registration, failing to comply with the conditions of a certificate, failing to furnish information or documents, and failing to maintain books or records attract penalties of one lakh rupees for each day of default or one crore rupees, whichever is less, while failures in furnishing and in certain graver defaults are measured instead by five times the profits made or losses avoided where that is higher. An intermediary that, after being called upon by the Authority, fails to redress subscribers' grievances within the time stipulated is liable to a penalty of up to one crore rupees or five times the profits made or losses avoided. Misuse of client money, the failure to segregate the money of clients or the use of one client's money for another, sits in the same highest band. Every sum realised by way of penalty is credited under section 29 to the Subscriber Education and Protection Fund, constituted by section 41 and fed also by grants, donations and the income of its own investments, and administered by the Authority for the protection of subscribers' interests.

Adjudication under section 30 is entrusted to an adjudicating officer appointed from the Authority's officers, who inquires after giving the person concerned a reasonable opportunity of being heard, and who recommends the penalty to the member in charge of investigation and surveillance; the penalty is then imposed by a member other than that member, having regard to the amount of disproportionate gain or unfair advantage, the loss caused to subscribers, and the repetitive nature of the default. The internal separation is a deliberate fairness device: the wing that investigates does not fix the punishment. Section 31 adds the protective powers: any person aggrieved may seek interim measures from the Authority, including the retention, preservation or sale of an asset, the securing of pension fund moneys, an interim injunction or the appointment of an administrator, and where the Authority concludes after inquiry that the board or management of an intermediary is acting in contravention of the Act or the regulations, it may supersede that board or management and appoint an administrator to manage the intermediary under the regulations.

### Can the Authority take over the management of an intermediary?

Yes. Under section 31, where the Authority finds after inquiry that the governing board or management of an intermediary is acting in contravention of the Act or the regulations, it may supersede that board or management and appoint an administrator to run the intermediary, and section 19 provides a parallel route for the recordkeeping agency and pension funds.

Beyond penalties, section 32 makes contravention of the Act or of the rules and regulations an offence punishable with imprisonment extending to ten years, or a fine extending to twenty five crore rupees, or both, with a minimum term of one month where the default is failure to pay a penalty or to comply with a direction or order. Prosecution is gated by section 35: no court may take cognizance except on the Authority's complaint, and no court inferior to a Court of Session may try an offence. Section 33 empowers the Central Government, on the Authority's recommendation, to grant immunity from prosecution and penalty to a person who has made a full and true disclosure of a violation, an instrument borrowed from the securities statute book for extracting information from inside a default. Appeals run on the securities model. Section 36 gives an appeal to the Securities Appellate Tribunal against an order of the Authority or of an adjudicating officer, to be filed within forty five days, with power in the Tribunal to condone delay for sufficient cause and an endeavour to dispose of the appeal within six months. Section 37 bars the jurisdiction of civil courts over matters the adjudicating officer or the Tribunal is empowered to determine. Section 38 gives a further appeal to the Supreme Court, within sixty days, on a question of law arising out of the Tribunal's order.

### Where does an appeal from the PFRDA lie?

An appeal from an order of the Authority or of an adjudicating officer lies to the Securities Appellate Tribunal under section 36, within forty five days of the order. A further appeal lies to the Supreme Court under section 38, within sixty days, but only on a question of law arising out of the Tribunal's decision.

The Authority's own accountability is constructed in Chapter IX and Chapter X. The Central Government may make grants to the Authority after parliamentary appropriation under section 39. Section 40 constitutes the Pension Regulatory and Development Fund, into which government grants, fees and charges are credited and out of which the Authority's salaries and expenses are met. Accounts are audited by the Comptroller and Auditor General under section 42, and the audited accounts with the audit report are laid before each House of Parliament, as is the Authority's annual report, which section 46 requires to be furnished to the Central Government within nine months of the close of the financial year. Section 43 binds the Authority to the Central Government's directions on questions of policy, other than technical and administrative matters, with the government's decision on what is a question of policy made final, a clause in visible tension with the independence the tenure provisions build, and one this article returns to in its assessment. Section 44, the provision noted earlier in connection with the supposed government guarantee, in fact empowers the Central Government to supersede the Authority itself: where the government is of opinion that the Authority is unable to discharge its functions through circumstances beyond its control, or has persistently defaulted in complying with directions or in discharging its duties so that its financial position or administration has deteriorated, or that circumstances render supersession necessary in the public interest, it may, after giving the Authority an opportunity to make representations, supersede it for a period not exceeding six months, during which the members vacate office and the government discharges the Authority's functions, and the notification with a full report must be laid before Parliament.

### What does the Pension Advisory Committee do?

Section 45 empowers the Authority to establish a Pension Advisory Committee of not more than twenty five members, drawn from employees' associations, subscribers, commerce and industry, intermediaries and organisations engaged in pension research, with the Authority's Chairperson and members as ex officio members. Its object is to advise the Authority, principally on the making of regulations.

The Subscriber Education and Protection Fund, which receives the penalties under section 29, is the point at which the enforcement system pays for the development system: its objects under section 41 include the education and protection of subscribers, and the Authority's subscriber awareness work, its educational material and its outreach through the intermediary network are the visible forms of a duty that runs back to section 14. The pairing is deliberate in the design. A system that asks households to bear investment risk owes them, in return, the means to understand the risk they bear, and Parliament arranged for the fines of intermediaries who fail subscribers to finance the education of the subscribers they failed.

The rule making architecture completes the statute. Rules are made by the Central Government under section 51, covering among other things the service conditions of members and the form of accounts. Regulations are made by the Authority under section 52, and the list of regulation heads in that section is the truest index of what Parliament left open: the amendment of the National Pension System itself, the conditions of partial withdrawal, exit and annuity purchase, the duties of the recordkeeping agency, the functioning of points of presence and pension funds, the number of pension funds, the establishment and functioning of the National Pension System Trust, and the procedure for adjudication and supersession. Every rule and regulation must be laid before Parliament under section 53. Section 54 gave the Central Government five years from commencement to remove difficulties by order. Section 55 declares the Act to be in addition to, and not in derogation of, other laws, and section 56, the savings clause, validates the entire interim decade by deeming everything done under the two resolutions of 2003 and 2008 and the notification of December 2003 to have been done under the Act.

## The argument the Act did not end: defined benefit against defined contribution

The Act settled the regulator and the system; it did not settle the argument, and the stance of this article, as the series requires, is to give every side the strongest statement its own proponents give it, and to adjudicate none of them.

The case for the defined contribution design, as its proponents in the finance ministry tradition and the OASIS lineage state it, is a case about honesty in public accounting. A defined benefit promise is a debt the state incurs without recording it, falling due in years when the employees who earned it have left service and the taxpayers who must pay were not party to the promise. As life expectancy rises and the pensioner population grows relative to the workforce, the bill compounds in ways no budget voted. A funded system converts the promise into property: the contribution is made in the year of service, invested in an identified account owned by the subscriber, portable across employers and States, visible in a statement, and incapable of being quietly diluted by a future government's embarrassment. Its proponents add that the old arrangement was regressive in coverage, delivering its generous guarantee to the fraction of the workforce in government service while the unorganised majority had no pension at all, and that a universal account architecture at least builds the rail on which wider coverage can run.

The case for the defined benefit arrangement, as the employee associations state it through bodies such as the Joint Forum for Restoration of Old Pension Scheme and the federations of railway, defence and central government employees that have campaigned under that banner, is a case about what a pension is for. A retirement income, they argue, is not an investment product but deferred wages and social security, and its defining virtue was that it did not depend on the state of the market in the year a person happened to retire. The defined benefit pension was a stated proportion of last pay, revised with dearness relief, so the retiree's standard of living was anchored to prices and to the pay structure; the defined contribution account exposes the individual least able to bear risk, a retiring clerk or constable with no financial training and no second chance to earn, to equity cycles, annuity rates and inflation. The associations point to the transfer of risk as the reform's essence and its injustice: the state relieved its own balance sheet by moving longevity and market risk onto households, and the long campaign for restoration, in their telling, is the workforce declining to accept that transfer as final.

The family's position on a death in service is where the ordinary system's own custodians first qualified its purity, and the qualification belongs in the record of the debate. Under the account's own rules, death before retirement delivers the accumulated corpus to the nominee or legal heirs, a capital sum whose adequacy depends entirely on how long the employee had contributed. For government subscribers, however, orders of the Department of Pension and Pensioners' Welfare extended to the families of employees who die or are disabled in service the benefits of the older family pension framework as additional relief, computed on the pattern of the pension rules and adjusted against the account, so that the widow of a young constable or clerk is not left to a two year accumulation. The associations cite the necessity of the patch as their argument in miniature: the pure account, they say, had to be relieved by the defined benefit framework at precisely the point where the family's need was greatest. The ministry's answer is that a transitional hardship provision for death in service is a feature any humane system would carry, and that it concedes nothing about the retirement design itself. Both readings are placed here as their proponents state them.

The case of the States that have reverted, Rajasthan, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Punjab and Himachal Pradesh, which have each informed the Authority of their decision to restart the old pension scheme for their employees, is a case about federal competence. The pension of a State's own employees, the argument runs, is an incident of State public services, a subject on which the State's authority over its services is at its strongest; the National Pension System entered State service only because each State chose to extend it by notification under what became section 12, sub-section (4), of the Act, and what a State adopted as a matter of its own policy it may replace as a matter of its own policy. The Union has itself accepted the premise in Parliament, where the Minister of State for Finance, Pankaj Chaudhary, answered that such shifts fall exclusively under State policy discretion under that very sub-section. Questions of overlapping legislative competence in such cases are governed by the repugnancy doctrine examined in the article on [Article 254 and the resolution of conflicts between Union and State law](/2018/09/01/article-254-repugnancy-explained/), but the States' claim here is simpler than a repugnancy contest: they did not legislate against the Act, they withdrew a service arrangement the Act had always left to their choice.

The Constitution distributes the competence behind these claims, and the distribution explains why the argument is genuinely open. Pension payable by the Government of India is a Union subject, the pensions of State employees travel with the States' power over their own public services, and social security and social insurance sit in the Concurrent List, the field in which the Act, as Union legislation on a concurrent subject, principally rests. The States, on this reading, concede nothing by having joined the system and surrender nothing by leaving it, because the service conditions of their own employees were never the Union's to fix; the Union, on the competing reading, answers that a concurrent field occupied by a parliamentary statute for a national system is not vacated by a State's change of preference, and that the Act's careful scheme of voluntary extension means a State's obligations, once incurred toward identified subscribers, are governed by the statute it invoked.

The countervailing case, stated by the Union and by the Reserve Bank of India in its studies of State finances, is about arithmetic across generations. A State that reverts stops paying a defined contribution it can budget and assumes a defined benefit liability it cannot, whose cost falls due in the 2040s and after, when the employees in service retire in large numbers; the Reserve Bank's report on State finances warned that the reversion States were building a large future fiscal burden, and parliamentary answers on behalf of the Union have repeated that returning to the old scheme rebuilds an unfunded liability whose size the reversion decisions do not measure. On the money already accumulated, the Union's position, stated by the Finance Minister, Nirmala Sitharaman, and repeated in answers in both Houses, is that restoration for central employees is not proposed, the old scheme having been abandoned for its unsustainable fiscal liability, and that for the reversion States the accumulated corpus cannot be handed back: there is, the government has said in terms, no provision under the PFRDA Act 2013, read with the Exits and Withdrawals Regulations 2015 and the other relevant regulations, by which the accumulated corpus of subscribers, government contribution, employee contribution and accruals together, can be refunded and deposited back to a State government. The sums at stake are not small: parliamentary answers have placed the pension fund accumulated under the system for Rajasthan alone at over fifty thousand crore rupees as on 31 July 2025, with Punjab, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand and Himachal Pradesh each in the tens of thousands of crores. The States answer that the government contributions within those sums are State money contributed for employees the State will pension from its own revenues, so that the State pays twice; the Union answers that the accounts are the subscribers' individual property, built by contract with each employee, and that a government cannot be given back what in law belongs to its employees one by one. The dispute remains unresolved between the governments, and the rights of the individual subscribers caught inside it, whose accounts sit in a system their employer has disowned, are the part of the question least answered by either side.

The Act sits inside a wider framework that the argument also touches. The Code on Social Security 2020 consolidated the social security statutes into a single code whose pension chapters proceed on their own commencement timetable, and its relationship with the pension system this Act governs is mapped in the article on [the Code on Social Security 2020 and the framework it builds for social security legislation](/2013/08/15/social-security-code-2020-explained/). The two frameworks do not merge: the Code addresses the statutory schemes of the organised and unorganised workforce, while the Act's system is a contributory architecture of individual accounts, and the boundary between them is the boundary between welfare provision and pension property that the whole debate keeps crossing.

The middle position in the argument, which the events of 2024 and 2025 eventually institutionalised, holds that the binary itself is the error. A funded individual account system and a floor of assurance are not opposites, on this view, provided the assurance is honestly priced and honestly funded: let the account remain the engine, let the outcome be measured against a prudent benchmark, and let the employer or the State stand behind the floor with money set aside for the purpose, reviewed actuarially, rather than with a promise charged to future budgets. The assured option is this position enacted. Its critics from the defined benefit side answer that an assurance which shrinks when the account underperforms the benchmark is not the assurance the old scheme gave, and that a proportionately reduced payout reproduces, at the floor, exactly the market dependence the floor was meant to abolish. Its critics from the fiscal side answer that the pool corpus contribution, stated as an estimate of eight and a half per cent of pay, is a contingent liability whose true cost only actuarial valuation across a full cycle can reveal, and that the history of pension promises is a history of estimates that rose. Both criticisms are recorded here in the terms their proponents use; the scheme's own framework commits the funding of the pool to periodic actuarial evaluation, which is the mechanism by which the dispute between them will eventually be settled on evidence.

The whole argument can be reduced to one object: where the risk sits. The table below is the article's findable artifact, and the rest of the pension debate is commentary upon it.

## The assured option: how an assurance entered a system that forbids it

The Unified Pension Scheme is the centrepiece of the Act's later history, and its design is a direct answer, two decades on, to the risk objection stated in the previous section. Its institutional path ran through a committee: in 2023 the Union government constituted a committee under the Finance Secretary, T. V. Somanathan, to review the pension system for government employees and suggest improvements, and the assured option emerged from that review. The Union Cabinet approved the scheme in August 2024, the decision being announced on 24 August 2024. The Ministry of Finance, Department of Financial Services, notified the scheme by notification number F. No. FX-1/3/2024-PR, dated 24 January 2025, as an option under the National Pension System for central government employees covered by it. The Authority operationalised it through the Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority (Operationalisation of Unified Pension Scheme under National Pension System) Regulations 2025, notified on 19 March 2025, and the Central Civil Services (Implementation of the Unified Pension Scheme under the National Pension System) Rules 2025 followed for the service aspects. The scheme came into operation on 1 April 2025.

Its terms, stated precisely, are these. An employee who superannuates after twenty five years of qualifying service receives an assured payout at the rate of fifty per cent of the average basic pay drawn over the twelve months immediately before superannuation. For qualifying service between ten and twenty five years the payout is proportionate, and after a minimum qualifying service of ten years an assured minimum payout of ten thousand rupees per month is payable on superannuation. On the death of the payout holder, the legally wedded spouse receives a family payout of sixty per cent of the payout the subscriber was drawing immediately before death. Dearness relief is payable on the assured payout and the family payout, as notified by the government, which is the mechanism by which the payout is indexed after retirement. At superannuation the subscriber also receives a lump sum of one tenth of the monthly emoluments, basic pay and dearness allowance, for every completed six months of qualifying service, in addition to gratuity, which the scheme carries under the gratuity rules applicable to the system. Assured payout is not available where the employee is removed or dismissed or resigns, and the voluntary retirement route carries its own qualifying conditions, the payout in that case running from the date the employee would have superannuated.

The funding is where the design declares itself. The subscriber contributes ten per cent of basic pay and dearness allowance, and the government contributes a matching ten per cent, both to the individual corpus in the subscriber's own account, as under the ordinary system. The government then contributes a further amount, estimated at eight and a half per cent of basic pay and dearness allowance of the employees who have opted, to a separate pool corpus. The pool corpus also receives the balances transferred from individual accounts after superannuation and any further contributions the Central Government specifies. The assurance is therefore not a promise written on the subscriber's own investments. It is a promise underwritten by an employer's fund, built from an employer's additional contribution, and the subscriber's own corpus remains invested and market linked throughout.

The calibration device is the benchmark corpus, and it is the part of the scheme a careful reader should understand, because it decides whether the assurance is paid in full. The benchmark corpus is a notional corpus computed on the default investment pattern: the subscriber's monthly contributions are valued as if they had been invested in the default pattern throughout, with contributions before 1 April 2025 valued as and when received, missing contributions supplied by reference to the weighted average value of the default pattern, and both partial withdrawals and voluntary additional contributions left out of the computation. The subscriber's actual accumulation is the individual corpus, built by the investment choices actually made. At retirement the two are compared. If the individual corpus exceeds the benchmark, the excess belongs to the subscriber. If it falls short, the subscriber may add funds to close the gap, or accept a proportionately reduced assured payout. A final withdrawal of up to sixty per cent of the individual corpus or the benchmark corpus, whichever is lower, may also be taken, and it too reduces the assured payout proportionately, subject to replenishment. The central recordkeeping agency reports both figures in the account statement, so the subscriber can watch the distance between the two measures across a career. The design's logic is exact: the State assures an outcome measured against prudent default investing, and declines to underwrite the consequences of a subscriber's more aggressive choices beyond that measure.

The election to enter the scheme was framed as an option exercised within a window, and the window's history is part of the record. Existing central government employees covered by the system, employees who had already retired on or before 31 March 2025 after the qualifying service, and the legally wedded spouses of such deceased retirees, were given three months from 1 April 2025, that is until 30 June 2025, to opt; the government extended the date to 30 September 2025 and then again to 30 November 2025, in response to representations from stakeholders. Employees joining the central government on or after 1 April 2025 exercise their choice at entry, within thirty days of joining. The scheme's own documents describe the option, once exercised, as final and irrevocable, and the one way character of the election was among the features most pressed upon employees deciding within the window. The position was later qualified in one respect: the government allowed employees who had opted into the scheme a one time, one way switch facility back to the ordinary system, exercisable not later than twelve months before superannuation, or three months before voluntary retirement, or at resignation or retirement under the relevant service rule, and not available in cases of removal, dismissal, penalty retirement or pending disciplinary proceedings. Opt in was thus made practically final, and then given a single, narrowing door back, a sequence that tells its own story about the pressure the election placed on the employees asked to make it.

The populations to whom the option was offered explain its uneven reception, and they are worth separating. The first population, employees in service on 1 April 2025 who were covered by the ordinary system, faced the decision in its hardest form: a career already partly accumulated under one set of expectations, to be converted, irrevocably at the point of election, into another, with the benchmark comparison to be computed across contributions made long before the scheme existed. The second population, recruits joining on or after 1 April 2025, chose at the threshold of service, within thirty days of joining, between the ordinary system and the assured option, with no accumulated past to convert and a full career over which the benchmark could be tracked. The third population, employees who had already retired under the ordinary system on or before 31 March 2025, together with the legally wedded spouses of those who had died, were offered entry into an assurance after the event, their past withdrawals and annuities to be adjusted against the scheme's entitlements. A scheme addressed simultaneously to the serving, the entering and the retired was performing three different acts of persuasion at once, and the double extension of its option window, recorded in the preceding passage, is best read as the administrative trace of that difficulty.

The service dimension of the scheme was completed by the Central Civil Services (Implementation of the Unified Pension Scheme under the National Pension System) Rules 2025, which regulate the option, its exercise and its consequences within the service framework, and by the extension of retirement gratuity and death gratuity to subscribers under the scheme, so that the assured option travels with the same terminal benefits as the system around it. For past retirees who opt in, the regulations work the conversion through the existing corpus: the retirement benefits already released are adjusted against the scheme's entitlements, a top up is computed for the period since retirement where the assured terms exceed what was received, and the payout then runs under the scheme, a machinery of arrears and adjustments whose complexity is itself a measure of how far a mid course assurance has to reach back into accounts that were built without one.

The reconciliation with section 20, which the brief for this article identified as its central legal question, can be stated without strain. Clause (g) of section 20, sub-section (2), forbids assurance of benefits within the National Pension System except a market based guarantee mechanism purchased by the subscriber. The assured payout is not a guarantee issued by the Authority, nor a promise by any pension fund as to the return on its schemes, nor an assurance given by the system to subscribers at large. It is a benefit promised by one employer, the Government of India, to its own employees, funded by that employer's additional contribution into a pool the employer maintains, and available to no one else: not to State government employees unless their government adopts an equivalent arrangement, and not to the private and individual subscribers who form the largest part of the system's membership. The prohibition and the scheme therefore operate on different planes. The statute governs what the system may promise; the scheme is what an employer chose to promise over and above the system, using the system's accounts, records and regulator as its machinery. Whether that distinction would survive a challenge that the scheme is, in substance, an assurance of benefits within the system is a question no court has been asked to answer on the materials this article can rely on, and the article states the reconciliation as the design presents it rather than as a decided proposition of law. The political point is plainer than the legal one. The assurance the statute forbade the system to give returned through the only door the statute had left open, the employer's own purse, and it returned for the one class of subscribers whose employer is also the author of the statute.

## The Atal Pension Yojana: an assurance that lives outside the Act

The system's second assurance confirms the reading of the first. The Atal Pension Yojana, announced in the Union Budget for 2015-16 and launched on 9 May 2015, operational from 1 June 2015, offers workers in the unorganised sector a fixed monthly pension of one thousand, two thousand, three thousand, four thousand or five thousand rupees from the age of sixty, in exchange for contributions that vary with the age of entry and the slab chosen. Entry is open to citizens between eighteen and forty years who hold a savings bank account, the minimum contribution period is twenty years, and from 1 October 2022 a person who is or has been an income tax payer may not newly join. For subscribers who joined before 31 December 2015 and met its conditions, the government added a co-contribution of half the subscriber's contribution or one thousand rupees a year, whichever was lower, for five years. On the subscriber's death the spouse may continue and receive the same pension, and after both have died the accumulated corpus is returned to the nominee. The scheme replaced the earlier Swavalamban arrangement and is administered by the Authority through the architecture of the National Pension System.

The legal point is the one this article exists to make. The Atal Pension Yojana is not created by the PFRDA Act, is not named in it, and does not rest on any provision of it. It is a scheme of the Government of India, run through the pension system's machinery by executive decision, and its defining feature, the guaranteed minimum pension, is underwritten by the government as sponsor: where the accumulated corpus and its returns do not sustain the promised slab, the shortfall is the government's to fund. Twice over, then, in the assured option for government employees and in the guaranteed slabs for unorganised workers, an assurance has been supplied to subscribers of a system whose statute forbids assurance, and in both cases the assurance came from a government's own resources and not from the system, its regulator or its funds. The statute's prohibition has proved to be exactly as strong as its text: it binds the system's promises completely, and it does not bind anyone else's.

The scheme's mechanics borrow the system's wholesale. Contributions are collected through the savings bank account by auto debit, banks and other service providers act as the points of contact, the accumulated contributions are invested through the pension funds following the pattern prescribed for government subscribers, and delayed contributions attract a small overdue charge prescribed by the scheme. The guarantee, uniquely in the architecture, is fiscal rather than contractual: the pension slab is fixed in the scheme's own terms, the government co-contributed for the early eligible cohort, and the Government of India stands behind the minimum pension where the fund's accumulations fall short of it. In legal character the Atal Pension Yojana is therefore closer to the old pension promise than to the system through which it is delivered, a defined benefit in miniature wearing the machinery of a defined contribution system, and it is tolerated by the statute for the simplest of reasons: the statute never claimed to govern it.

## What the Act leaves undone

A pillar should end its analysis by naming the statute's genuine silences, because the later history collects around them. The Act fixes no minimum pension for the system's largest class, its private and individual subscribers, and supplies no mechanism by which a small account becomes an adequate income; adequacy is left to contribution discipline and market outcomes. It builds no inflation protection into the income phase, the annuity being nominal by the market's design, and it does not merge or even coordinate the parallel retirement systems of the country, the provident fund, the gratuity framework and the pension account remaining three separate answers to one question, a fragmentation the social security code examined earlier in this article addresses at the level of welfare law rather than pension architecture. It makes no provision for the exit of a participating government, the contingency the reversion States created, nor for the portability of a State's employees between pension regimes mid career. And it leaves the system's most consequential numbers, the exit fractions, the annuity proportion and the thresholds, to regulations, so that the settlement's lived terms can change, and have changed, without Parliament voting on them. Each silence is defensible in the language of flexibility. Together they describe a statute that solved the regulator completely and the pension problem only in part, and they mark the ground on which any future amendment will have to be fought.

## The litigation at the boundary of the settlement

The courts have not been asked to strike the Act down, and the settlement's validity as legislation is not where the litigation has gathered. It gathered instead at the boundary date, 1 January 2004, and on a recurring human fact: a recruitment advertisement issued under the old regime, a selection process that straddled the change, and an appointment letter that arrived after the notification, placing the appointee in the new system although every step that earned the appointment had been taken in the expectation of the old. Petitions of this class, including from personnel of the Central Armed Police Forces and other services, succeeded repeatedly in the High Courts on the reasoning that the conditions of service governing a selection are those in force when the selection process began, and that a candidate cannot be visited with a new pension regime by the accident of administrative delay in issuing appointment letters. The Union regularised the position prospectively by a one time option: an office memorandum of the Department of Pension and Pensioners' Welfare dated 3 March 2023 permitted employees who were recruited against advertisements or notifications issued on or before the notification of the new system, but who joined on or after 1 January 2004, to opt in a single exercise into the pension scheme under the 1972 rules, with the consequence that their system accounts would be closed and the accumulations dealt with as the memorandum prescribed. The episode is a small one beside the reversion dispute, but it shows the same feature of the settlement from the other side: its edges, where the notification of 2003 cut across expectations formed under the world before it, have required continuous repair, by courts first and by the executive after them. Within the system's own disputes, as distinct from disputes about the settlement, the appellate route to the Securities Appellate Tribunal and the Supreme Court on questions of law, described in the enforcement section, has carried the ordinary traffic of registration, penalty and intermediary discipline, and no decision on the materials relied on here has disturbed the Act's architecture.

## Assessment: a strong regulator's statute with a contested settlement inside it

The honest assessment separates the statute as a regulator's charter from the statute as a pension settlement, because the two have fared differently. As a charter, the Act is competent and largely complete. It gives the Authority the standard full equipment of an Indian financial regulator: registration, rule making by regulation laid before Parliament, information and investigation powers with civil court authority behind them, search and seizure, interim protective measures, adjudicated penalties with an internal separation between investigation and punishment, supersession of intermediary management, criminal liability for contravention, and a two level appeal ladder ending in the Supreme Court on questions of law. Its design choices for the system's safety are structural rather than exhortatory: functions are separated so that record, money, management and custody sit in different hands; the recordkeeper's assets are made appropriable so the national record cannot be stranded; at least one fund must be a Government company; foreign control is capped by a formula that tracks the insurance sector; and the corpus is confined to Indian investment. The provisions that have worn least well are the familiar ones: section 43 subordinates the Authority to government directions on questions of policy, with the government the final judge of what is policy, which sits awkwardly beside the tenure protections of Chapter II; and the exit system's dependence on regulations, while flexible, means that the terms on which a subscriber leaves a forty year arrangement can be and have been rewritten repeatedly by subordinate legislation, as the recasting of thresholds and ages described in the exit section of this article shows.

As a settlement, the Act froze the design of 2003 into law at the moment of its maximum confidence, and the decade since commencement has been a long negotiation with that freezing. The prohibition on assured benefits held as law and failed as politics: the demand for assurance, from government employees who had the strongest organisation and the clearest comparator in the old scheme, produced first a committee, then an assured option engineered to stand outside the prohibition's letter, while five States walked out of the settlement altogether and the money they had contributed became the subject of an intergovernmental dispute the Act makes no provision to resolve. Neither the reversion nor the assured option is available to the system's largest constituency, the individual and private sector subscribers, for whom section 20 continues to mean exactly what it says. The verdict this article defends is accordingly a divided one. The Act should be read as a durable piece of regulatory architecture carrying a provisional social bargain. The architecture has needed no rescue: the regulator it regularised has run a national system of individual accounts without a failure of custody, record or payment that the statute failed to anticipate. The bargain has been reopened on every side at once, by States, by employees and finally by the Union itself, and each reopening has been conducted in the spaces the Act left: the State's doorway in section 12, the employer's purse outside section 20, and the regulation power in section 52. A reader who wishes to test the exit and assured figures against the documents themselves can keep the Act, the exit regulations and the scheme regulations open together in [an office file viewer for working through the pension statute and its regulations side by side](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), which is the surest way to see which numbers belong to Parliament and which to the regulator.

One test of the architecture still lies ahead, and the assessment should name it. The system has spent two decades in its accumulation phase, in which money flows inward, accounts multiply and every failure is recoverable by time. Its payout phase begins in earnest when the founding cohorts retire in large numbers, the central recruits of 2004 reaching superannuation in a wave across the 2040s, joined by the State cohorts behind them. In that phase the questions reverse: whether the annuity market can absorb the mandated purchases at fair rates, whether the exit regulations hold under mass use, whether the assured option's pool survives its first full generation of payouts, and whether the corpus return dispute with the reversion States has been settled or merely outlived. A pension statute is not finally judged for fifty years, and the Act of 2013 has passed only the examinations of its accumulation youth. The verdict above, favourable to the architecture and reserved about the settlement, is therefore a verdict at the interval, stated so that the later record can be measured against it.

The specialist articles in this cluster carry the story outward from here: the provident fund comparison for the reader choosing between the two great retirement systems, the insurance regulator's profile for the annuity end of the machine, and the social security framework for the welfare statutes with which the pension system shares its border. What this pillar has tried to fix is the centre: a statute that regularised a decade old system, armed its regulator completely, prohibited the promise every pensioner wants, and thereby guaranteed that the politics of the promise would be conducted, for as long as the system lasts, in the narrow and interesting space between what the system may assure and what a government may.

## A note on reading the Act with its regulations

The reader who follows this pillar into the sources should be warned about the shape of the corpus, because the PFRDA Act cannot be understood from its own text alone, and the distribution of its contents across instruments is the single most common source of error in writing about it. The Act itself, fifty seven sections in ten chapters, supplies the Authority, the system's features, the prohibitions, the enforcement ladder and the accountability frame. The operative detail of accumulation sits in regulations and in the investment guidelines issued under them: charges, timelines, fund appointment, portfolio composition and reporting. The operative detail of departure sits in the Exits and Withdrawals Regulations, where every figure about lump sums, annuity proportions and thresholds lives, and where amendment is frequent enough that any statement of those figures must carry its instrument and year with it, as this article has done wherever a withdrawal figure appears. The assured option sits in a government notification and in a dedicated set of operationalisation regulations, with its service consequences in civil service rules. The Atal Pension Yojana sits in scheme documents of the government, outside the statute altogether. A statement about Indian pension law is therefore only as good as its attribution: the discipline this article has followed, of naming the instrument for every proposition, is offered to the aspirant reader as the method the subject demands.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the PFRDA Act regulate?

The Act establishes the Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority as a statutory body and gives it charge of the National Pension System, of any other pension scheme not regulated by any other enactment, and of the intermediaries through which the system runs: points of presence, central recordkeeping agencies, pension funds, the Trustee Bank, the custodian and annuity service providers. It prescribes the Authority's duties of regulation and development in section 14, writes the system's features into section 20, and supplies the enforcement machinery of registration, investigation, penalties, adjudication and appeals. It is at once a regulator's charter and the legal frame of a national retirement savings system, which is why its fifty seven sections divide between institutional provisions about the Authority and substantive provisions about the pension architecture the Authority governs.

### Q: How long did the pension regulator operate without a statute?

For a full decade. The interim Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority was created by a resolution of the Government of India dated 10 October 2003, and the pension system it supervised began operating for new central government recruits on 1 January 2004. A second resolution dated 14 November 2008 continued the interim body. An ordinance to give it statutory form lapsed in 2005, and a Bill introduced that year lapsed with the Fourteenth Lok Sabha; a further Bill of 2011 became the Act only in September 2013, and the Act commenced on 1 February 2014. Section 56 of the Act validates the interim decade retrospectively, deeming everything done under the two resolutions and the December 2003 notification to have been done under the Act itself.

### Q: What is the National Pension System and who is covered?

The National Pension System is a defined contribution pension arrangement built on individual accounts, described in section 20 of the Act, in which contributions are invested through regulated pension funds and the retirement outcome depends on the accumulated corpus rather than on a promised proportion of pay. It is compulsory for central government employees who joined service on or after 1 January 2004, members of the armed forces excepted, and it extends to State government employees where the State notifies its adoption under section 12. Since 1 May 2009 it has also been open to every citizen on a voluntary basis, to corporate employers for their staff, and to persons governed by excluded schemes who join individually. A subscriber may hold a Tier I pension account and a voluntary Tier II account under the same permanent retirement account number.

### Q: Does the law permit an assured pension?

Within the National Pension System, the Act's answer is no, in terms. Section 20, sub-section (2), clause (g), provides that there shall not be any implicit or explicit assurance of benefits except a market based guarantee mechanism to be purchased by the subscriber. No provision of the Act contains a sovereign guarantee of returns, and section 44, sometimes cited in this connection, is the power of the Central Government to supersede the Authority, not a guarantee provision. Assurance has reached two classes of subscribers from outside the prohibition: the Unified Pension Scheme gives central government employees an assured payout funded by the employer's additional contribution to a pool corpus, and the Atal Pension Yojana guarantees minimum pension slabs underwritten by the Government of India. In both cases the assurance is a government's promise, not the system's.

### Q: How does the Unified Pension Scheme work?

The Unified Pension Scheme is an option under the National Pension System for central government employees, notified by the Ministry of Finance on 24 January 2025 and in operation from 1 April 2025, operationalised by regulations of the Authority. The subscriber and the government each contribute ten per cent of basic pay and dearness allowance to the individual corpus, and the government adds a further contribution, estimated at eight and a half per cent, to a separate pool corpus. At superannuation after twenty five years of qualifying service the assured payout is fifty per cent of the average basic pay of the last twelve months, proportionate between ten and twenty five years, with a minimum payout of ten thousand rupees a month after ten years, a family payout of sixty per cent to the spouse, dearness relief, and a lump sum for every completed six months of service. The payout is measured against a benchmark corpus computed on the default investment pattern; a shortfall in the individual corpus reduces the payout proportionately unless replenished.

### Q: Can a state return to the old pension scheme?

As a matter of pension policy for its own employees, a State's freedom is wide: the system entered State service only because each State extended it by notification under section 12, sub-section (4), and the Union has accepted in Parliament that the choice rests within State policy discretion. Rajasthan, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Punjab and Himachal Pradesh have informed the Authority of their reversion to the old pension scheme. The obstacle is the money already accumulated. The Union's stated position is that no provision of the Act, read with the Exits and Withdrawals Regulations 2015, permits the accumulated corpus of subscribers, comprising government and employee contributions and their accruals, to be refunded to a State government, because the accounts are the individual property of subscribers. The dispute between the reversion States and the Union on that corpus remains unresolved.

### Q: Who manages pension money and who bears the investment risk?

Pension money is managed by pension funds appointed and registered under the Act, which invest the contributions credited to each subscriber's individual account through a chain that separates functions: the central recordkeeping agency keeps the record, the Trustee Bank moves the money, the custodian holds the securities, and the National Pension System Trust oversees the funds in the subscribers' interest. No intermediary guarantees the outcome. Section 20 forbids any implicit or explicit assurance of benefits within the system, so the investment risk rests on the subscriber: the corpus at exit is the value the markets have produced, and converting part of it into a lifelong income transfers the longevity risk thereafter to the annuity service provider at the rates of the day. The exceptions are the assured arrangements built for government employees and for Atal Pension Yojana subscribers, where a government stands behind the floor.

### Q: How are pension withdrawals and exits regulated?

The conditions of exit are prescribed not by the Act but by the Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority (Exits and Withdrawals under the NPS) Regulations 2015, made under the Act and amended repeatedly, most extensively in 2025. Under those regulations, exit at superannuation permits withdrawal of up to sixty per cent of the corpus as a lump sum, with at least forty per cent applied to purchase an annuity, and the whole corpus may be withdrawn where it does not exceed the prescribed threshold, five lakh rupees after the 2021 amendment. Premature exit requires at least eighty per cent to be annuitised, partial withdrawals are permitted for specified purposes within prescribed limits, and on death the corpus passes to the nominee or legal heirs, with an annuity option for the spouse. Every figure in this answer is a creature of the regulations, not of the statute.

### Q: Which pension schemes and employees does the PFRDA Act not apply to?

Section 12 of the Act carves out a defined territory. The Act does not apply to schemes and funds under the Employees' Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 1952, the Coal Mines Provident Fund and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 1948 and the other enactments listed in the section, including the seamen's and Assam tea plantations provident fund statutes, contracts and arrangements for annuity and pension under the Insurance Act 1938, and any scheme the Central Government exempts by notification. It does not reach members of the armed forces, whose pensions remain with the defence establishment, nor employees whose service began before 1 January 2004 and whose pensions continue under the defined benefit rules, nor State government employees unless the State extends the system by notification. A person inside an excluded scheme may still join the National Pension System voluntarily in an individual capacity.

### Q: What is a PRAN, and why does the National Pension System rest on a permanent retirement account number?

The PRAN, or Permanent Retirement Account Number, is the unique number allotted to each subscriber by the central recordkeeping agency, and it is the identity to which the subscriber's Tier I and Tier II accounts, contribution history and investment choices are permanently attached. The Act contemplates the individual pension account as the unit of the whole system, and the number is what makes that account portable in practice: it survives changes of employer, of employment sector, of State, of pension fund and of recordkeeping agency, so a working life spent across many jobs accumulates in one place under one identifier. The recordkeeping agency issues the account statement against the number and reports the benchmark and individual corpus figures against it under the assured option, which makes the PRAN the thread on which the system's record, and the subscriber's proof of ownership, both depend.

### Q: What is the difference between the Tier I and Tier II accounts under the National Pension System?

The Tier I account is the pension account proper. Contributions to it are locked into the system's discipline: withdrawals before exit are confined to the partial withdrawal grounds and limits prescribed by the Exits and Withdrawals Regulations, and the balance is applied at exit between a lump sum and a compulsory annuity purchase in the proportions those regulations prescribe. The Tier II account is a voluntary savings account available to a subscriber who holds a Tier I account; it carries no lock in of that character, and the subscriber may withdraw from it freely, because it is not the vehicle of the retirement promise. Both accounts sit under the same permanent retirement account number and the same investment choices. The tax treatment of the two differs under the Income-tax Act, the pension account attracting the contribution deductions and exit exemptions that the savings account largely does not.

### Q: How much do a government employee and the government each contribute to the National Pension System?

For a central government employee the contribution rates are fixed by the employer's orders under the system's framework rather than by the Act itself. The employee contributes ten per cent of basic pay together with dearness allowance. The government contributed a matching ten per cent from the system's commencement in 2004, and raised its contribution to fourteen per cent of basic pay and dearness allowance with effect from 1 April 2019, a differential intended in part to answer the comparison with the employer's provident fund contribution in the private sector. State governments fix the corresponding rates for their own employees who are covered, generally on the same pattern. Under the Unified Pension Scheme the structure changes: employee and government each contribute ten per cent to the individual corpus, and the government adds an estimated eight and a half per cent to the separate pool corpus that funds the assurance.

### Q: What investment choices does a National Pension System subscriber have, and what happens when no choice is made?

A subscriber may choose the pension fund and the investment pattern. Under the active choice the subscriber allocates among the equity, corporate debt and government securities asset classes within the prescribed ceilings, equity being capped at seventy five per cent for individual subscribers, with a small allowance for alternative assets. Under the auto choice the allocation follows a life cycle fund, aggressive, moderate or conservative, which holds a higher equity share for younger subscribers and shifts toward debt as retirement approaches. Government subscribers are subject to a far more conservative default pattern, under which equity is capped at fifteen per cent, unless they elect a permitted pattern of their own. Where a subscriber makes no choice, the default pattern applies and contributions are invested accordingly, so inertia never leaves the money idle; the default is also the pattern against which the assured option's benchmark corpus is computed.

### Q: What happens to the National Pension System corpus if a subscriber dies before retirement?

The exit on death is governed by the Exits and Withdrawals Regulations, not by the Act. The accumulated corpus in the Tier I account is paid to the nominee registered in the account or, where there is no nominee, to the legal heirs, and the regulations give the nominee, in the case of a spouse, the option of continuing within the system or applying the corpus toward an annuity in accordance with the prescribed conditions. For government subscribers, separate orders of the Department of Pension and Pensioners' Welfare extend the benefits of the family pension framework as additional relief on death or disablement in service, adjusted against the account, so the family of an employee who dies early in service is not confined to a small accumulation. The Tier II balance passes with the rest of the account to the nominee or heirs under the same record.

### Q: Where can a subscriber take a grievance against a pension fund or a point of presence?

The first forum is the intermediary itself, and the complaint then travels through the system's own machinery: the subscriber may raise the grievance with the point of presence or the central recordkeeping agency and have it registered and tracked in the Authority's centralised grievance management system, which escalates unresolved complaints to the Authority. The pension sector has no separate ombudsman on the insurance model, so the Authority is the final administrative forum, and its intervention has teeth: under section 28 of the Act an intermediary that, after being called upon by the Authority, fails to redress subscribers' grievances within the stipulated time is liable to a monetary penalty extending to one crore rupees or five times the profits made or losses avoided. Directions and orders of the Authority in a dispute are appealable to the Securities Appellate Tribunal under section 36, with a further appeal to the Supreme Court on a question of law.

### Q: What happens under the Unified Pension Scheme if the individual corpus is below the benchmark corpus at superannuation?

The assured payout is calibrated to the benchmark corpus, the notional accumulation produced by valuing the subscriber's contributions as if invested throughout in the default investment pattern. Where the individual corpus, built by the choices actually made, falls short of the benchmark at superannuation, the subscriber has two courses under the operationalisation regulations. The shortfall may be replenished by the subscriber, restoring the full assured payout. If it is not replenished, the assured payout is reduced proportionately to the shortfall. A final withdrawal of up to sixty per cent of the individual corpus or the benchmark corpus, whichever is lower, likewise reduces the payout proportionately unless the reduction is made good. Where the individual corpus exceeds the benchmark, the excess belongs to the subscriber outright. The design confines the government's assurance to the outcome of prudent default investing and leaves the consequences of more aggressive choices with the subscriber.

### Q: What powers does the Central Government retain over the Authority under the PFRDA Act?

The government retains the powers of a sponsoring state over its regulator. Under section 43 the Authority is bound by the Central Government's directions on questions of policy, technical and administrative matters excepted, and the government's decision on whether a question is one of policy is final. Under section 39 the government funds the Authority by grants after parliamentary appropriation, and the Authority's accounts are audited by the Comptroller and Auditor General and laid before Parliament under section 42, as is its annual report. Under section 44 the government may supersede the Authority itself, for reasons recorded and for a period not exceeding six months, discharging its functions during the supersession and laying the notification and a full report before Parliament. The government also makes the rules under section 51, while the Authority's regulations are laid before Parliament under section 53.

### Q: What penalties and punishments does the PFRDA Act prescribe?

The Act works at three levels. Section 28 prescribes monetary penalties adjudicated under section 30: default in registration or in the conditions of a certificate, and failures to furnish information or maintain records, attract penalties measured by one lakh rupees for each day of default or one crore rupees, whichever is less, while graver defaults, including the failure to redress grievances after being called upon and the misuse or non segregation of subscribers' money, are measured up to one crore rupees or five times the profits made or losses avoided. Sums realised are credited to the Subscriber Education and Protection Fund under section 29. Contravention of the Act or of the rules and regulations is additionally an offence under section 32, punishable with imprisonment extending to ten years, or a fine extending to twenty five crore rupees, or both, triable only on the Authority's complaint before a Court of Session. Obstruction of an investigation carries its own punishment under section 16.

### Q: Does the National Pension System cover members of the armed forces?

No. The exclusion is original to the design and is carried by section 12 of the Act. The notification of December 2003 that founded the system applied it to new entrants to central government service with an express exception for the armed forces, and defence personnel, whose recruitment, service conditions and retirement ages are governed by their own framework, have remained outside the contributory system throughout. Their pensions continue as defined benefits charged on the defence budget, administered by the defence establishment, and the principle was reaffirmed in the One Rank One Pension settlement notified in November 2015, under which defence pensions are equalised across cohorts retiring from the same rank with the same length of service and revised periodically. Civilian employees of the defence services, who are not members of the armed forces, stand on the other side of the line and are covered by the system on the ordinary terms.

### Q: Is the Atal Pension Yojana a creature of the PFRDA Act?

No. The Atal Pension Yojana is a scheme of the Government of India, announced in the Union Budget for 2015-16, launched on 9 May 2015 and operational from 1 June 2015, and it is neither created nor mentioned by the PFRDA Act. It is administered by the Authority through the architecture of the National Pension System, using its pension funds, recordkeeping and points of contact, which is the source of the common error. Its defining promise, a fixed monthly pension of one thousand to five thousand rupees from the age of sixty according to the slab chosen, is guaranteed by the Government of India as sponsor, the government bearing any shortfall where the accumulated corpus cannot sustain the slab. The scheme thus illustrates the article's central distinction: it operates inside the system's machinery while resting, in law, entirely outside the statute that built the machinery.
