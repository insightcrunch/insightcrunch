## The Bargain at the Center of the Procedure

Trade promotion authority rests on a bargain that the statute imposes on both branches at once. The President receives a defined period in which trade agreements may be negotiated under objectives written by Congress, and Congress receives in return notification duties, consultation rights, and access rights that bind the executive branch before, during, and after negotiation. If the President honors the objectives and satisfies the conditions attached to the grant, an agreement entered into during the covered period qualifies for treatment that no ordinary bill receives. The implementing bill moves through each House under a fixed timetable, committees consider the bill without amending it, floor debate is limited by statute, amendments are not in order at any stage, and each chamber votes on passage by a simple majority of those voting. If the President departs from the objectives or fails to meet the conditions, the protection weakens or disappears, because the statute builds machinery through which either House, or both Houses acting together, may deny an extension, withdraw expedited treatment from a particular agreement, or proceed under ordinary rules instead. The arrangement requires discipline from the President in the substance and conduct of negotiation, and restraint from Congress at the final stage, when the negotiated text arrives as a single package that members may approve or reject but may not rewrite. Neither side receives its benefit without accepting its burden.

That reciprocal discipline is the point of the design. The President gains speed and certainty at the end of the process only by accepting direction and oversight throughout the process, in forms that the statute specifies in detail. Congress gains direction at the beginning and oversight during negotiation only by surrendering a tool that legislators ordinarily regard as central to legislative power, the power to amend a bill in committee and on the floor. The surrender is real. Committees of jurisdiction may not amend an eligible implementing bill or recommend amendments to it. Motions that would ordinarily allow a chamber to delay, reshape, or return a bill are barred or decided without debate. Debate itself is capped, and the cap has the further consequence that a measure considered under the procedure cannot be filibustered in the Senate, because statutory limits on debate displace the open ended debate that a cloture motion exists to end. The restraint is also bounded. The final decision remains a vote on a statute, taken by both Houses and presented to the President in the ordinary constitutional form, and the restraint operates as a rule that each House has adopted for itself and retains power to change.

The formal identity of the arrangement requires precision, because two distinct legal layers are often collapsed into a single name in public discussion. The expedited procedures themselves are the procedures of sections 151 to 154 of the Trade Act of 1974 (P.L. 93-618, signed January 3, 1975), codified at 19 U.S.C. 2191 to 2194. Those sections supply the machinery for implementing bills, for certain disapproval resolutions, and for the special rules that govern delivery of documents and computation of periods. The procedures alone, however, do not authorize any negotiation, and they do not by themselves make any agreement eligible for expedited consideration. A separate authorizing statute performs that work in each era, setting the period during which agreements may be entered into, stating the negotiating objectives that govern the talks, and attaching the notification and consultation conditions that the President must satisfy. Successive authorizing statutes have switched those procedures on for defined periods, and the sequence runs to the Bipartisan Congressional Trade Priorities and Accountability Act of 2015 (P.L. 114-26, signed June 29, 2015), codified principally at 19 U.S.C. 4201 and following. Under the extension completed in 2018, the grant covers agreements entered into before July 1, 2021, after the President requested extension on March 20, 2018 and no extension disapproval resolution was adopted. Keeping the two layers separate explains why the procedures can remain on the statute books during intervals when no live grant exists, and why a fight over renewal is a fight over objectives, periods, and conditions rather than a fight over limited debate and a bar on amendments in the abstract. The parent statute receives a full treatment in [the complete guide to the Trade Act of 1974](/2020/06/01/trade-act-1974-complete-guide/).

### What must the President do for an agreement to qualify for expedited treatment?

Trade promotion authority allows the President to negotiate trade agreements under objectives set by Congress, with a promise that the implementing bill will receive an expedited vote without amendments, provided notification and consultation conditions are met and the bill contains only provisions strictly necessary or appropriate to implement the agreement.

The distinction between the permanent procedure and the temporary grant also clarifies the place of the older name in the record. Before the Trade Act of 2002 renamed the approach, the same basic arrangement was known as fast track, and the older term survives in discussion of the earlier grants and in generic reference to the procedure. The renaming changed the label without changing the underlying problem or the basic answer that the 1974 statute had supplied. A negotiator acting for the United States had to be able to tell a partner that the text settled at the table would be the text presented for a decision, and a Congress asked to accept that assurance had to be able to show that the objectives guiding the negotiator, the information flowing to the legislature during the talks, and the opportunity to reject the result had all been secured in advance by law. The 2002 act restored the authority after a lapse that had run from 1994 to 2002. The 2015 act restored the authority again after the lapse that followed the July 1, 2007 expiration of the extended 2002 grant, and the 2015 act tightened several features, including the eligibility language for implementing bills, the publication duties that precede signature, and the menu of resolutions through which Congress may police compliance.

The credibility problem that the procedure answers deserves statement at full strength, because a weak statement of the problem makes the congressional restraint appear larger than any plausible benefit could justify. Trade negotiation is an exchange of concessions, and each concession has value only in relation to the whole package in which the concession sits. A partner that lowers a barrier, accepts a discipline, or opens a sector does so in reliance on the corresponding moves promised across the rest of the text, on labor and environment provisions, on intellectual property terms, on rules of origin, on dispute settlement, and on the schedule by which commitments take effect. If a legislature may reopen the signed text line by line, amend one provision without regard to the trades that produced that provision, and send the altered package back as the basis for agreement, the reliance collapses. Every concession a partner makes at the table is then priced against the risk that the text changes after signature, and a rational partner responds to that risk in predictable ways. The partner withholds the most valuable concessions until the legislative outcome is known, which means the concessions never reach the table in a form that allows a balanced package to form. The partner demands extra compensation for concessions that may be undone, which raises the price of agreement beyond what the underlying interests support. The partner insists on reopening corresponding provisions whenever the legislature alters a provision of interest, which converts a single amendment into a general renegotiation and multiplies delay. Or the partner declines serious negotiation altogether and waits for a process in which a signature carries a more reliable meaning. None of those responses requires bad faith. Each follows from the structure of a negotiation in which one side can commit at the table and the other side reserves an unrestricted right to revise the commitment afterward.

Partners raised that concern directly in the period that produced the 1974 answer, asking how the resulting text would be protected once the text reached Congress. The question was practical rather than theoretical. Tariff and trade commitments touch domestic law, and an agreement that requires changes to United States law cannot take effect as a domestic matter through signature alone. Implementation requires a statute. A partner therefore faced a two stage process in the United States, negotiation with the President followed by legislation in Congress, and the partner needed to know whether the second stage would function as a decision on the negotiated package or as a second negotiation conducted by hundreds of legislators under rules that permitted unlimited amendment. The 1974 Congress answered with a self denying procedure rather than a transfer of power. Congress did not surrender the decision, and the statute transferred neither the power to regulate commerce with foreign nations nor the power to lay and collect duties, both of which Article I, section 8 assigns to Congress. Congress instead regulated the manner of its own decision, limiting debate, barring amendments, and guaranteeing a vote, on conditions that Congress itself set in the authorizing statute. A partner could rely on the package because the legislature had bound its own later procedure in advance, through a law that the partner could read, and the legislature retained the ultimate sanction of rejection if the package as a whole failed to satisfy the objectives that the legislature had written.

The self denying character of the answer also frames the questions that a renewal debate can actually decide. Whether the period should run for a further term, whether the objectives should place greater or lesser weight on labor standards, environmental protection, digital trade, agriculture, services, or investment, whether notification periods should lengthen, whether access to negotiating texts should broaden, and whether the withdrawal machinery should operate more easily or less easily are questions about the terms of a rule that Congress adopted for its own proceedings. The procedure frames those questions without predetermining the answers, and legislators answer through the objectives and conditions of a new grant or decline to answer by allowing the authority to lapse. The lapse record belongs to the same logic. The authority expired on April 16, 1994, renewal efforts failed in 1997 and in a House vote of 180 to 243 on September 25, 1998, and the authority expired again on July 1, 2007, with the next grant arriving in 2015.

Against that background the central claim of the article can be stated plainly and tested against each part of the statute. "Congress trades timing for direction" captures the exchange in five words. Congress gives up amendment at the end of the process, when the text is fixed and the costs of reopening the text are highest, in exchange for writing the objectives at the beginning of the process, when direction can shape negotiation without unraveling a finished package. Timing refers to the moment at which congressional influence operates. Under ordinary legislation, influence peaks late, through committee amendment, floor amendment, conference negotiation, and the threat of extended debate, all after the executive branch has framed a proposal. Under the grant, influence moves earlier and changes form. Influence operates through statutory objectives that the negotiator must pursue, through notification that must precede the opening of talks and the signature of an agreement by stated intervals, through consultation that continues while the text develops, through access to negotiating texts for every member of Congress and for cleared staff under the access provisions, through designated congressional advisers accredited to negotiating teams, and through the advisory groups that each chamber maintains for negotiation. Direction refers to the content of that earlier influence. The objectives in the 2015 act appear in three tiers, overall objectives, principal negotiating objectives, and capacity building and other priorities, and the statute treats satisfaction of the objectives as a condition of eligibility rather than as advice that the executive branch may weigh and set aside.

Every renewal fight is therefore a fight about the objectives list rather than the procedure, and the record of the grants bears out that reading. Debate over the 2015 act turned on the content and priority of objectives, on the strength of consultation and transparency duties, and on the design of the resolutions available when the executive branch falls short, far more than on the twenty hour debate limit or the bar on amendments considered as isolated rules. The same pattern appears in the operation of a live grant. The December 2019 episode involving the United States Mexico Canada Agreement illustrates where influence moved under the timing trade. The agreement had been signed on November 30, 2018, and the House majority declined to move an implementing bill through most of 2019 while a working group led by Ways and Means Chairman Richard Neal negotiated changes with United States Trade Representative Robert Lighthizer. On December 10, 2019 the three governments signed a protocol of amendment that changed dispute settlement, labor and environment enforcement, intellectual property terms, and steel and aluminum rules of origin, among other subjects. The administration submitted the implementing legislation on December 13, 2019, the House passed the bill on December 19, 2019 by 385 to 41, the Senate passed the bill on January 16, 2020 by 89 to 10, and the implementing act was signed on January 29, 2020 as P.L. 116-113, with the agreement entering into force on July 1, 2020. Withholding the bill proved to be decisive leverage, and influence shifted to the pre introduction stage rather than disappearing. The episode confirms the timing trade in operation, because the leverage that produced the protocol of amendment existed precisely where the statute and the surrounding practice place leverage, before formal introduction, through consultation, through the mock markup practice of the committees of jurisdiction, and through the credible prospect that a bill lacking majority support would not move at all.

The timing trade also disciplines the executive branch in ways that a late stage amendment power cannot replicate. An amendment adopted after signature can strike a provision, but an amendment cannot easily reconstruct the network of reciprocal concessions that the struck provision supported, and an amendment offers no guidance to a negotiator who must make hundreds of interim choices over months or years of talks. Objectives written in advance operate at the moment of choice. Notification duties force the executive branch to state specific objectives for a new partner in a detailed and comprehensive summary before talks begin, to give notice at least ninety calendar days before initiating negotiations, to give notice of intent at least ninety calendar days before entering into an agreement, and to publish the text of the agreement at least sixty days before signing under section 106(a)(1)(B) of the 2015 act. Consultation duties and access rights allow members and committees to test interim positions against the stated objectives while adjustment remains possible without formal breach of a signed text. Direction is a working system of instructions, disclosures, deadlines, and consequences, and the price of that system is the restraint that Congress accepts when the final bill reaches the floor.

Restraint at the final stage is narrower than popular description often suggests. The bar reaches the implementing bill, and the bill itself may contain only provisions approving the agreement and the statement of administrative action, plus provisions strictly necessary or appropriate to implement the agreement, with the word strictly added by the 2015 act to tighten the eligibility standard. Within the procedure, committees retain the power to report or to withhold a favorable report, and that posture carries consequences under the consultation and compliance resolution provisions added in 2015. The House retains the power, demonstrated on April 10, 2008, to change its own rules and make the timetable inapplicable to a particular implementing bill, as the House did for the Colombia bill by adopting H.Res. 1092 by 224 to 195. Sections 151 to 153 are enacted as an exercise of the rulemaking power of each House, form part of the rules of each House, supersede other rules only where inconsistent, and remain subject to change by either House at any time in the same manner as any other rule, as 19 U.S.C. 2191(a) provides. The constitutional hinge of the whole arrangement sits in that rulemaking foundation, under Article I, section 5, and the bargain remains revocable by the institution that made the bargain.

The remainder of the article walks through the arrangement in the order that the logic of the bargain suggests. The mechanism receives attention first, in the detailed operation of section 151, including introduction by request by the majority leaders or designees, referral to the committees of jurisdiction, automatic discharge after forty five days of session without a report, the nondebatable and highly privileged motion to proceed, the twenty hour limit on debate in each chamber, the bar on amendments and on motions to recommit, postpone, or reconsider, and the rule that the second chamber votes finally on the bill received from the first chamber so that no conference is required. The objectives receive attention next, followed by the notification and consultation conditions that keep direction connected to negotiation as talks proceed, including the publication duties, the advisory group structure, the role of designated advisers, and the mock markup practice through which the Ways and Means Committee and the Finance Committee advise on a draft bill before formal submission. The withdrawal machinery follows, covering the extension disapproval resolution that a single House may adopt to deny an extension, the procedural disapproval resolution that requires adoption by both Houses on the same agreement within sixty days of each other, and the consultation and compliance resolution through which one chamber may withdraw expedited treatment in that chamber alone. The lapse record then traces the grants and expirations from the 1974 act to April 1994, the failed renewal efforts of the late 1990s, the 2002 restoration and the 2007 expiration, and the 2015 grant with the extension completed in 2018. The constitutional frame closes the account, returning to Article I, section 8 and Article I, section 5, to the distinction between delegating negotiating latitude and transferring legislative power, and to the reason that an agreement changes no United States law by itself.

## The Mechanism Step by Step

The expedited procedures for trade agreements have a precise textual home, and precision about that home matters because the procedures operate only where the statute places them. Sections 151 to 154 of the Trade Act of 1974, enacted as part of Title I of that act, carry the mechanism. The Congressional Research Service identifies those sections, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2191 to 2194, as Part 5, Congressional Procedures With Respect to Presidential Actions. The procedures are a defined procedural track attached to defined presidential actions, and a bill qualifies for the track only by satisfying the conditions that those sections and the later trade promotion authority statutes together impose.

Section 151, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2191, is the center of the mechanism. That section governs implementing bills, the bills through which an agreement receives congressional approval and through which changes to United States law required by an agreement receive enactment. Section 151 supplies the introduction rule, the referral rule, the limit on committee action, the discharge timetable, the motion to proceed, the debate limit, and the bar on amendment that together distinguish an implementing bill from an ordinary bill. 

Section 152, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2192, governs resolutions disapproving certain actions. The section addresses a different congressional decision from approval of an agreement, but section 152 holds a second importance inside the statute. The floor procedure in subsections (d) and (e) of section 152 became the procedure that later statutes borrow when those statutes create expedited consideration for extension disapproval resolutions and procedural disapproval resolutions. The later act instead incorporates the section 152 procedure, and the borrowed procedure carries the same basic features that make section 151 effective, meaning limited debate, protection against amendment, and a route to a floor vote that a committee bottleneck or an extended floor contest cannot easily defeat.

Section 153, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2193, governs resolutions on extension of the section 402 waiver authority associated with the Jackson-Vanik provisions. The original procedural part reached beyond implementing bills to a set of recurring trade decisions in which Congress had delegated authority subject to periodic congressional review. Section 153 supplied an expedited route for resolutions concerning extension of that waiver authority. Together, sections 151, 152, and 153 show a Congress designing fast procedures for several distinct points at which presidential trade action returned to Congress for approval, disapproval, or review, rather than designing a single procedure for a single agreement.

Section 154, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2194, supplies special rules that support the other sections. The Congressional Research Service describes section 154 as covering matters that include same day delivery of documents to both Houses and computation of certain periods. Section 154 therefore completes the textual home. Section 151 moves the implementing bill, section 152 supplies the disapproval procedure that later acts borrow, section 153 handles the waiver authority resolutions, and section 154 keeps delivery and counting from becoming contested preliminaries to the substantive vote.

The statute then states the constitutional basis on which the whole procedural structure rests. Sections 151 to 153 are enacted as an exercise of the rulemaking power of each House. Under 19 U.S.C. 2191(a), as summarized by the Congressional Research Service, the procedures are part of the rules of each House, the procedures supersede other rules only where inconsistent, and either House may change the procedures at any time in the same manner and to the same extent as any other rule of that House. The clause does two things at once. The clause gives the procedures statutory form, so the text appears in the United States Code and binds through an enacted law. The clause also classifies the procedures as House and Senate rules, so each chamber retains the constitutional authority that Article I, section 5 confers on each House to determine the rules of proceedings.

The supersession sentence narrows the effect of the procedures on the rest of each chamber body of rules. Where a fast track rule conflicts with an ordinary rule, the fast track rule controls for the covered bill or resolution. Where no conflict exists, the ordinary rule remains in place. That narrowing matters for interpretation. The procedures do not displace committee practice, floor practice, or chamber precedent wholesale for a covered measure. The procedures displace only so much ordinary practice as cannot operate alongside a fixed introduction rule, a discharge deadline, a nondebatable motion, a capped debate period, and a prohibition on amendment. 

The changeability sentence carries the larger consequence. Because either House may change the procedures at any time as that House changes any other rule, no grant of trade promotion authority can lock a future House or Senate into the timetable against that chamber's own decision. The commitment that the procedures offer a President and a negotiating partner is therefore a commitment of a particular kind. The commitment remains revocable by the chamber that made the procedural promise. A House that concludes that consultation failed or that a particular submission should not receive expedited treatment retains a rules-based route to alter or suspend the timetable for that case, and the authority for that route sits in the same statutory sentence that creates the expedited treatment.

That rulemaking power clause also explains why the procedures do not change the constitutional requirements for enactment. The statute speaks in mandatory terms, with deadlines and bars and privileged motions, yet a chamber can depart from the statutory procedure by an exercise of rulemaking authority. The statute binds committees and floor consideration in detail, yet the binding force operates as internal chamber discipline rather than as an external transfer of legislative power. An implementing bill requires passage by both Houses and presentment to the President, because the procedures govern how each House handles the bill internally and do not purport to enact law by a shortcut. 

The walkthrough begins with presidential submission of an agreement together with the implementing bill and the accompanying materials that the governing trade promotion authority act requires. On the day the President submits an agreement, the implementing bill is introduced in the House by the majority leader or a designee and in the Senate by the majority leader or a designee, in each case by request. Every element of that sentence performs work. Introduction occurs on the day of submission, so the timetable starts from a public and identifiable event. Introduction comes from the majority leader or a designee in each chamber, so responsibility for placing the bill before the chamber rests with the leadership charged with managing the floor. Introduction occurs by request, a designation that signals that the introducer presents the administration proposal for consideration without the introduction itself carrying the ordinary implication of personal authorship of the submitted text.

Simultaneous introduction in both Houses gives the mechanism a parallel structure from the first day. Neither chamber waits for the other chamber bill as a precondition to beginning committee consideration of a companion measure. Each chamber receives a bill, each chamber makes a referral, and each committee of jurisdiction works against the same statutory clock. The parallel structure also sets up the closing feature of the procedure, under which the second chamber to act debates a companion bill but casts the final vote on the bill received from the first chamber. The day of submission introduction rule therefore does more than start a clock. The rule creates the matched pair of vehicles that allows two chambers to consider the same agreement at the same time and then to converge on a single text for final passage without a conference committee.

After introduction, the bill receives referral to the committees of jurisdiction. In the House, referral runs to the Committee on Ways and Means. In the Senate, referral runs to the Committee on Finance. Referral may also reach other committees where a provision of an implementing bill falls within another committee jurisdiction, but Ways and Means and Finance stand at the center of the trade procedure because those committees hold jurisdiction over revenue and trade matters in the respective chambers. Referral preserves a committee stage inside a procedure designed for speed. 

The committee stage operates under a restriction that defines the bargain at the heart of trade promotion authority. Committees may not amend the implementing bill and may not recommend amendments. The restriction applies at the first stage and foreshadows the identical restriction on the floor. The restriction protects the negotiated agreement from unraveling through piecemeal revision. A trade agreement reflects reciprocal concessions across many sectors, and an amendment that improves the text from the perspective of one constituency can destroy the balance that made the reciprocal concessions acceptable to the other party to the agreement. The bar on committee amendment keeps that balance intact by forcing a single decision on the submitted package.

A deadline prevents the committee stage from becoming a veto. A committee that has not reported within 45 days of session is automatically discharged from further consideration of the bill. Automatic discharge requires no motion, no vote, and no permission from the committee whose inaction triggered discharge. Passage of the forty-fifth session day without a report moves the bill to the floor stage by operation of the statute itself. The design answers a familiar legislative risk. In ordinary practice, a committee that opposes a bill can often defeat the bill by declining to act, and supporters must find an extraordinary device to extract the bill from committee. Under section 151, inaction for the specified period produces the opposite result. The bill advances, the committee loses custody, and the chamber decides through floor action whether the agreement proceeds.

After report or discharge, any member may offer the motion to proceed to consideration of the implementing bill. The statute makes that motion highly privileged and nondebatable. Privilege means the motion takes precedence over ordinary business and can bring the bill before the chamber without waiting for a separate scheduling decision. Nondebatable character means the motion itself does not open a period of argument that opponents could use to consume time before consideration begins. Availability to any member means the right to invoke the floor stage does not belong exclusively to the majority leader, to the committee chair, or to the member who introduced the bill by request. 

The meaning of the privileged motion differs by chamber because the ordinary baselines differ. In the House, ordinary practice usually requires a special rule from the Committee on Rules to structure consideration of a major bill, setting the terms of debate and determining which amendments, if any, receive consideration. The statutory motion removes that requirement for an implementing bill. No special rule is needed because the statute itself supplies the rule for consideration, fixing debate time, barring amendments, and governing the motions that members may offer. In the Senate, ordinary practice gives the majority leader a privileged position in recognition and in the management of motions to proceed, and an ordinary motion to proceed can itself attract extended debate and a supermajority problem at the threshold. The statutory motion displaces both features for an implementing bill. Recognition practice does not defer to the majority leader for the offering of the motion, any member may offer the motion, and the motion is nondebatable, so no supermajority vote on the motion to proceed stands between the committee stage and floor consideration.

Debate on the bill in each chamber is limited to 20 hours. The limit applies in the House and in the Senate alike, and the time is divided equally between supporters and opponents in the House formulation and controlled in the Senate by the two floor leaders. Equal division gives the minority side a guaranteed share of the scarcest floor resource. Opponents receive time by right under the statute rather than by courtesy of the majority, and supporters receive a defined period in which to present the case for approval without exposure to an open ended debate. The 20 hour figure also converts the debate from an endurance contest into a bounded argument. 

During and after that debate, the statute closes the avenues through which ordinary bills change shape or lose momentum on the floor. No amendments are in order at any stage. A motion to recommit the bill is not in order. Motions to postpone consideration and motions to reconsider a vote are barred or decided without debate. Each prohibition closes a distinct delaying or revising technique. An amendment could rewrite the implementing text and break the negotiated balance. Recommitment could return the bill to committee with instructions that function as an amendment or as a means of burial. Section 151 removes or truncates each device so that the debate ends in the decision that the procedure promises, approval or disapproval of the submitted bill in the submitted form.

The House faces an additional deadline for the final decision. The House vote on final passage must occur on or before the fifteenth day after the bill is reported or discharged. The deadline operates in combination with the 45 session days allowed for committee consideration. Passage requires a simple majority of those voting. No supermajority threshold applies to approval of an implementing bill, and the absence of a heightened threshold completes the logic of the timetable. A procedure that limits debate, bars amendment, and guarantees a vote would lose much of the intended effect if the final question then required more than the majority that the Constitution ordinarily requires for passage of a bill.

The Senate Finance Committee operates under a special discharge rule when a House bill reaches the Senate. For a House bill, the Finance Committee is discharged if the committee does not report within 15 session days after receiving the House bill or by the end of the 45 session days allowed for consideration of the Senate companion bill, whichever date is later. That later-date formulation respects both clocks. The Senate committee receives a full period measured from arrival of the House bill, and the Senate committee also retains the full period allowed for the Senate companion where that period ends after the period measured from receipt. The rule prevents the arrival of a House passed bill from shortening Senate committee consideration in a way that the companion bill timetable would not have allowed, while preserving automatic discharge as the consequence of inaction beyond the later date.

The closing sequence in the second chamber explains how parallel consideration produces a single enacted text. The second chamber debates that chamber's own introduced bill but votes finally on the bill received from the first chamber. Final passage on the received bill ensures that the text approved by the second chamber matches the text approved by the first chamber word for word. Identical passage satisfies the constitutional requirement that both Houses pass the same bill before presentment to the President. Because the second chamber final vote adopts the first chamber text rather than a separately passed companion text, no differences between two passed versions exist for a conference committee to reconcile, and no conference report requires approval in either chamber.

The Senate debate cap produces the most consequential single effect of the mechanism. A filibuster depends on the ability of opponents to extend debate beyond the point at which a majority stands ready to decide. Section 151 removes that ability for an implementing bill by fixing Senate debate at 20 hours by statute. When the allotted hours expire, debate ends and the Senate proceeds toward the final vote under the statute terms. Opponents retain the full share of debate time that the equal control structure gives the two floor leaders, and opponents retain the right to vote against passage and to defeat the bill by majority vote. Opponents do not retain a procedural means of preventing a vote by prolonging debate past the statutory limit. The guarantee of a decision, rather than a guarantee of approval, is the precise effect. A majority that opposes an agreement can reject the agreement. A minority that opposes an agreement cannot stop the majority from reaching the question. The ordinary Senate practice that the cap displaces is examined in [an explanation of the filibuster and cloture](/2022/02/15/filibuster-and-cloture-explained/).

Eligibility for the entire mechanism depends on the contents of the implementing bill. An eligible implementing bill may contain only the provision approving the agreement and the statement of administrative action, together with provisions strictly necessary or appropriate to implement the agreement. The approval provision performs the central congressional act, giving the agreement the approval that the governing trade promotion authority act contemplates. The statement of administrative action describes the administrative steps that the executive branch proposes for carrying out the agreement and forms part of the submitted package that Congress evaluates. Implementing provisions then carry out the changes to United States law that the agreement requires, such as adjustments to tariff schedules or amendments to domestic statutes that the agreement makes necessary. Material outside those categories does not belong in the vehicle that receives expedited consideration.

The word strictly in the phrase strictly necessary or appropriate carries a date and a purpose. The 2015 act added the word strictly to the eligibility standard. The addition tightened the test that a provision must satisfy for inclusion in an implementing bill considered under the expedited procedures. A provision that bears only a loose or convenient relationship to implementation falls outside a standard that demands strict necessity or strict appropriateness. The tightening serves the same protective purpose as the bar on amendment, viewed from the opposite direction. The bar on amendment prevents Congress from adding unrelated matter during consideration. The strict eligibility standard prevents the submitted bill itself from arriving loaded with unrelated matter that could then claim protection from amendment and a guaranteed vote. Together, the two limits confine the extraordinary procedure to the subject that justifies the procedure, the approval and implementation of a negotiated trade agreement.

### Why can a trade agreement bill not be filibustered in the Senate?

Senate debate on an implementing bill is capped at 20 hours by statute. Once that time expires, the chamber proceeds to a vote. The fixed limit leaves no opening for extended debate to delay passage indefinitely, so opponents cannot block the bill by holding the floor beyond the allotted hours.

The elements of section 151 reinforce one another when read as a single sequence rather than as isolated rules. Introduction by request on the day of submission creates matched vehicles and starts a common clock. Referral preserves committee examination while the bar on amendment and the discharge deadline deny committees both revision and indefinite custody. The privileged nondebatable motion transfers control of the floor stage from scheduling discretion to a statutory right available to any member. Capped and equally divided debate guarantees argument without permitting argument to become obstruction. The bars on amendment, recommitment, postponement, and reconsideration protect the submitted text through the vote. The second chamber device of debating one vehicle and passing the received vehicle delivers identical text to the President without conference delay.

## What Congress Receives Before a Signature

The front loaded half of the trade promotion authority bargain sits in writing that reaches Congress long before an implementing bill exists. Under the Bipartisan Congressional Trade Priorities and Accountability Act of 2015, Public Law 114-26, statutory negotiating objectives operate as a condition of eligibility for the expedited procedures rather than as advice offered to the executive branch. Advice can be received, weighed, and set aside without legal consequence under the statute that offered the advice. A condition works in the opposite direction. A condition defines the class of agreements and bills that qualify for a procedural privilege, and a failure to satisfy the condition places qualification itself in dispute.

That record begins with direction rather than with results. The objectives tell negotiators, in advance and in statutory language, the purposes that a qualifying agreement has to serve. Because the direction appears in the same statute that promises limited debate, a bar on amendments, and automatic committee discharge for a qualifying implementing bill, the direction and the promise rise or fall together. A President seeking the procedural benefits of the grant accepts the objectives as the price set by Congress for those benefits. The objectives therefore do work that a sense of Congress resolution cannot do. The objectives sort agreements into categories, and the categories determine which legislative road an implementing bill travels, an ordinary road with full debate and amendment or a statutory road with debate capped and amendments barred.

The 2015 act arranges the objectives in three tiers, and the tiering itself carries meaning. The first tier states overall objectives. Overall objectives frame the broad purposes of trade negotiation under the grant, the ends against which the whole of an agreement can be measured after negotiation ends. Language at that level speaks to the agreement as a whole rather than to a single chapter or commitment. The breadth is deliberate. A test framed only at the level of individual commitments would allow an agreement to satisfy each narrow item while departing from the larger purposes that justified the delegation of negotiating latitude. The overall tier prevents that form of compliance by fragments.

The second tier states principal negotiating objectives. Principal objectives sit below the overall tier in generality and above individual bargaining choices in authority. The principal tier translates broad purposes into the central subjects on which negotiators have to seek results, and the tier gives Congress a finer instrument for judging a finished agreement than the overall statement alone could supply. A negotiator retains discretion over tactics, sequencing, concessions, and the tradeoffs that any negotiation requires, because a statute cannot conduct a negotiation across a table. Discretion over tactics, however, differs from discretion over ends. The principal objectives mark the ends that Congress regarded as principal, and a finished agreement that leaves a principal objective unaddressed, or that advances a result at odds with a principal objective, faces a compliance question grounded in statutory text rather than in after the fact political preference.

The third tier covers capacity building and other priorities. Placement in a third tier does not convert the material into decoration. The 2015 act groups capacity building with other priorities because a trade agreement produces effects through institutions, administrative systems, and the ability of a partner government to carry obligations into practice, and Congress wrote those concerns into the same eligibility framework that contains the overall and principal tiers. A commitment on paper has limited value if the partner lacks the administrative capacity to apply the commitment in customs houses, regulatory offices, courts, and enforcement systems.

Read together, the three tiers form a ladder of generality. The overall objectives supply the standard for the agreement as a whole. The principal objectives supply the standard for the central subjects of negotiation. Capacity building and other priorities supply the standard for supporting conditions that determine whether negotiated commitments function after entry into force. The ladder matters for enforcement because disagreement over compliance often turns on the level at which a critic pitches the complaint. A complaint pitched only at the level of a single concession may fail against an agreement that serves the overall purposes. A complaint pitched at the overall level gains weight if the same weakness appears across principal objectives and supporting priorities.

The objectives list grew across successive grants, and the growth explains a large part of the politics surrounding each renewal. The Trade Act of 1974 created fast track in sections 151 to 154 of Title I, signed on January 3, 1975 as Public Law 93-618, at a moment when the procedural invention itself occupied the center of the legislative bargain. The core exchange in that era joined presidential negotiating latitude to congressional control over the final implementing vote through expedited consideration. Once the procedural exchange existed, each renewal presented an opportunity to revisit the substantive instructions attached to the exchange, and members who cared intensely about a subject that an earlier grant had treated lightly, briefly, or not at all had reason to demand space for that subject in the next statute. The list lengthened because renewal was the moment of leverage, and leverage unused during a renewal might not return for years.

The Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988 renewed the authority for agreements entered into before June 1, 1991, subject to extension, and a 1991 extension carried the authority into April 1994 so that the Uruguay Round could finish before the authority expired on April 16, 1994. The 1988 renewal illustrates the pattern in an early form. By that period, trade legislation carried a wider set of congressional concerns than the founding grant had carried, and a renewal bill offered a vehicle for writing those concerns into the instructions that accompanied the procedural promise.

The Trade Act of 2002, Public Law 107-210, signed on August 6, 2002, renamed fast track as trade promotion authority through Title XXI, the Bipartisan Trade Promotion Authority Act of 2002. The 2002 grant covered agreements entered into before June 1, 2005, extendable by two years, and the extension ran before the authority expired on July 1, 2007. The renaming signaled presentation, but the substantive fight again centered on the instructions. Drafters loaded added subjects onto the objectives framework because the framework was the place where support could be earned, opposition could be answered, and negotiators could be bound in advance.

The 2015 act repeated the pattern at greater length. Enacted after another long lapse that began in 2007, the 2015 statute arrived carrying the accumulated demands of members who had watched agreements negotiated, signed, and implemented under earlier instruction sets and who had identified gaps that a fresh grant could close. The statute passed as Public Law 114-26 on June 29, 2015, after the House approved the measure by 218 to 208 on June 18, 2015 and the Senate approved the measure by 60 to 38 on June 24, 2015, following the failure of the combined bill H.R. 1314 in the House on June 12, 2015. The length of the list is therefore evidence rather than excess, recording the price of assembling majorities in both Houses for a delegation that many members grant only with detailed instructions attached.

For that reason, renewal fights are fights about the list. Debate over duration, extension mechanics, and floor procedure matters, and later portions of the article take up those subjects in full. The objectives list, however, is where the substantive future of negotiations gets decided in advance. A subject written into the objectives enters every instruction, every consultation, and every later compliance judgment under the grant. A subject left outside the objectives depends on executive discretion and on after the fact persuasion. Members understand the asymmetry, and the understanding drives the intensity of drafting fights that can appear technical to an outside reader. Procedure from sections 151 to 154 supplied a stable chassis across decades, while the instruction set mounted on that chassis expanded each time Congress reopened the bargain and each faction with a trade concern sought a statutory foothold before the next round of negotiations began.

Direction alone would remain incomplete without a calendar that forces information toward Congress at moments when information can affect outcomes. The 2015 act therefore pairs the objectives with notification duties stated as operative text, with fixed intervals measured in calendar days, rather than as general encouragement to keep Congress informed. The first major duty attaches before negotiations begin. The President must notify Congress at least 90 calendar days before initiating negotiations, and before talks begin the executive branch must make available a detailed and comprehensive summary of the specific objectives for the negotiation with a new partner. The summary matters as much as the notice. A detailed and comprehensive summary of specific objectives converts notice into a document against which later texts can be compared, and the comparison begins from a baseline that the executive branch itself supplied at the outset, before positions hardened and before a finished text narrowed the range of possible congressional responses.

The interval before signature carries the heaviest concentration of duties. At least 90 calendar days before entering into an agreement, the President must provide notice of intention to enter into the agreement, and that notice receives prompt publication in the Federal Register. Federal Register publication widens the audience beyond the committees and beyond members with access to negotiating texts. In the same pre signature window, and also at least 90 days before signing, the executive branch submits the details of the agreement to the International Trade Commission.

Two further duties refine the pre signature record on the domestic law side of the bargain. At least 60 days before signing, the President must notify Congress of changes in United States law that compliance with the agreement would require. The duty forces an early accounting of the domestic legal consequences of the negotiated text, at a stage when the implementing bill remains unwritten and when committees can begin testing whether the forthcoming bill stays within the limit that allows only provisions approving the agreement and the statement of administrative action, plus provisions strictly necessary or appropriate to implement the agreement. Separate from the general notice of legal changes, a report on required changes to the trade remedy laws goes to the Committee on Ways and Means and the Committee on Finance a full 180 days before signing. The longer interval for trade remedy laws reflects the sensitivity of that body of law within the jurisdiction of the two revenue committees, and the statute singles the subject out for the earliest warning in the notification calendar.

Sequenced as a single calendar, the duties run from the trade remedy laws report at 180 days before signing, through notice of intention and submission to the International Trade Commission at 90 days before signing, to notice of required changes in United States law at 60 days before signing. No single disclosure carries the full burden of congressional oversight. Duties layer across the life of a negotiation, each duty arrives while a different kind of intervention remains possible, and the record thickens as the agreement approaches signature.

### How long before signing must the agreement text be published?

The 2015 act requires the President to publish the text of a trade agreement on a publicly available website maintained by the United States Trade Representative at least sixty days before the President enters into the agreement, under section 106(a)(1)(B) of Public Law 114-26.

That publication duty stands as the transparency innovation of the 2015 act, and the placement of the duty inside section 106(a)(1)(B) of Public Law 114-26 gives the innovation a precise statutory address. Publication reaches a different audience through a different mechanism. A publicly available website maintained by the Office of the United States Trade Representative places the full text of an agreement before any reader with internet access, at least 60 days before the President enters into the agreement. The interval matters as much as the act of publication. Sixty days before signature gives committees, advisory bodies, journalists, scholars, and affected parties a window in which the actual language can be read, criticized, and compared against the objectives and against the specific objectives summary supplied before negotiations opened.

The 30 day duty that follows in the statutory sequence moves the focus from the public to the formal legislative record. At least 30 days before the implementing materials are submitted, the President must provide Congress with the final legal text of the agreement and a draft statement of administrative action. The draft statement of administrative action performs a parallel function for executive branch interpretation, describing how the administration proposes to carry the agreement into effect through administrative measures. Submission of a draft, rather than only a finished statement, gives Ways and Means and Finance an opportunity to react before the formal package arrives, and that opportunity connects to the established practice of mock markups, and where the two committees differ a mock conference, through which the committees advise on the draft implementing bill before formal submission in a process that carries influence without legal binding force.

After signature, the paper trail continues on three further tracks, each with a stated interval. Within 60 days after signing, the President must provide a description of the changes to existing law that the agreement requires, converting the earlier pre signature notice of required changes into a post signature accounting tied to the finished text. Within 30 days after signing, the advisory committee reports arrive, bringing the judgments of the advisory committee system, headed by the Advisory Committee for Trade Policy and Negotiations, into the record while congressional consideration remains ahead. The International Trade Commission assessment completes the sequence, due no later than 105 days after the President enters into the agreement. Under the 2015 act, that assessment must be made public.

Taken as a group, the publication and post signature duties redistribute the burden of proof in later disputes. A dispute over whether Congress and the public had received a fair opportunity to examine an agreement could otherwise depend on competing accounts of briefings, access arrangements, and informal practice. A statutory publication date, a statutory submission date for final legal text, and statutory due dates for a law change description, advisory reports, and a public commission assessment create facts that either side can establish from the calendar. Either the final legal text and the draft statement of administrative action reached Congress at least 30 days before submission of the implementing materials or the interval fell short. Precision of that kind serves members who support an agreement as well as members who oppose the agreement, because precision narrows disputes that rest on recollection and broadens disputes that rest on documents.

Each condition in the objectives, notification, and publication framework operates as a lever rather than as a courtesy, because each condition connects to consequences that the same statute supplies. Under the 2015 act, the transmitting mechanism runs from a missed condition to the withdrawal machinery that later portions of the article examine in detail. A failure or refusal by the President to notify or consult as the act requires forms one of the two stated grounds for a procedural disapproval resolution under section 106(b), a simple resolution that takes effect only if both Houses adopt such a resolution on the same agreement within 60 days of each other. The second stated ground reaches the objectives directly, covering an agreement that fails to make progress in achieving the purposes, policies, priorities, and objectives of the act. An objectives failure and a notification failure therefore stand on the same statutory footing as triggers, even though one trigger sounds in substance and the other sounds in process.

The lever also operates at the level of a single chamber. The Consultation and Compliance Resolution, new in the 2015 act under section 106(b)(3) and (4), allows one chamber to withdraw expedited treatment in that chamber alone for failure to notify or consult, once the committee posture described in the statute has been reached. In the House, the mechanism follows a report of the implementing bill by Ways and Means with other than a favorable recommendation. In the Senate, the mechanism follows a meeting by Finance on whether to report the bill, a decision not to report favorably, and a resulting duty to report a Consultation and Compliance Resolution instead. No expedited procedure supports that resolution, and in the Senate the resolution can face a filibuster requiring 60 votes for cloture. Difficulty of use does not erase the lever.

Even the conditions that lack a dedicated resolution carry leverage through the eligibility frame established at the outset. Because the objectives operate as a condition of eligibility and because the notification and publication duties appear in the same statutory scheme, a documented failure gives opponents of expedited treatment a statutory argument rather than a plea for fairness. The argument can be pressed in committee, in debate over a procedural disapproval resolution, in debate over a Consultation and Compliance Resolution, and in the broader contest over whether an implementing bill deserves the procedural privilege that the expedited procedures supply. The 2015 act also retains a trade remedy laws resolution whose legal effect the statute does not specify, a reminder that Congress has repeatedly reached for resolution based instruments in trade procedure even where the legal consequence remained uncertain.

The aggregate effect completes the picture of what Congress receives. Congress receives direction in three tiers, written before negotiation and framed as a condition. Congress receives notice on a calendar that begins before talks open, concentrates at 90 days before signature, extends to 180 days before signature for trade remedy laws, and specifies the audience for each item, from the two revenue committees to the readers of the Federal Register. Congress receives the text itself on a public website at least 60 days before signature, the final legal text and a draft statement of administrative action at least 30 days before submission of the implementing materials, and a post signature trail of a law change description within 60 days, advisory committee reports within 30 days, and a public International Trade Commission assessment within 105 days. Each item in that inventory answers to the same purpose. The grant trades speed at the end of the process for information, direction, and documented compliance throughout the process, and the withdrawal machinery covered later in the article exists to give that trade an enforcement edge when the information, the direction, or the compliance falls short.

The bargain can be read as a single table, with each row joining a congressional surrender or receipt to the stage at which the obligation attaches and to the consequence that follows when a condition fails.

## The Bargain Table

| Element | What Congress gives or receives | Stage at which the obligation attaches | Consequence if the condition fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amendment | Congress gives up amendment of the implementing bill in committee and on the floor | Consideration stage | The submitted text receives an up or down vote as presented |
| Floor time | Congress guarantees consideration on a statutory clock | Committee report or discharge after 45 session days | Debate closes after 20 hours in each chamber |
| Negotiating objectives | Congress writes overall, principal, and other objectives into the authorizing statute | Before negotiations open | Failure to advance the objectives is a ground for a procedural disapproval resolution |

## What Congress Receives Through Consultation and Informal Review

Consultation under trade promotion authority is a continuing statutory duty rather than a single event. The Bipartisan Congressional Trade Priorities and Accountability Act of 2015 does not treat consultation as a briefing delivered after a negotiation has hardened into a finished agreement, nor as a courtesy extended when the executive branch considers the timing convenient. The statute builds consultation into the sequence of a negotiation from notification through signature and into the preparation of the implementing bill. That design follows from the structure of the grant itself. Congress sets negotiating objectives as a condition of eligibility for the expedited procedures, and consultation is the means by which Congress learns whether the negotiation in progress remains inside those objectives. A single conversation at the end of the process could not perform that function, because by the end the text would already reflect choices about market access, labor protections, environmental commitments, intellectual property, and changes to United States law that the committees of jurisdiction would have had no opportunity to question while questioning could affect the result.

Two bodies give consultation an organized form in each chamber, the House Advisory Group on Negotiations and the Senate Advisory Group on Negotiations. A negotiation can run through many rounds over a long period, and its subject matter crosses the jurisdiction of more than one committee. Without a designated group in each chamber, consultation could fragment into separate conversations with individual members, with no body charged to follow the negotiation as a whole and no regular channel through which concerns in one chamber could be gathered and put to the negotiators in a coherent form. The advisory groups answer that institutional problem. They provide a defined membership through which each chamber participates in the consultation process, receives information about the direction of the talks, and conveys the perspective of the chamber to the executive branch. Their existence also gives the trade representative a counterpart whose role is recognized in the statute, so consultation has an address in each House rather than depending entirely on personal relationships between negotiators and individual legislators.

Designated congressional advisers accredited to negotiating teams carry the connection closer to the talks themselves. Accreditation places congressional advisers in a recognized relationship to a negotiating team, rather than leaving congressional knowledge of a round to reports prepared after the round has closed. The distinction is one of timing and proximity. A report after a round can record what the negotiators chose to emphasize, while an accredited adviser follows the work of a team as proposals are tabled, revised, and combined. That proximity serves the committees as well as the individual adviser, because knowledge gained through the negotiating process informs the questions that the House Committee on Ways and Means and the Senate Committee on Finance can put to the administration during the consultation that continues between rounds. The adviser mechanism also reinforces the point that consultation under the grant is not limited to the leadership of the two revenue committees. The statute distributes participation through advisory groups, designated advisers, member access to texts, and cleared staff, creating several channels that overlap by design rather than a single gate through which all information must pass.

Consultation guidelines developed with the committees give the process a further layer of structure. Guidelines of that kind translate a general statutory duty to consult into working expectations about how consultation will occur, what subjects it will cover, and how the committees will be kept informed as a negotiation moves from broad objectives toward specific text. The statute contemplates guidelines formed through engagement with the committees rather than instructions issued by the executive branch alone, and that origin matters. A process designed only by the party being consulted about would risk measuring consultation by the convenience of the negotiators. Guidelines developed with Ways and Means and Finance allow the committees to state what meaningful consultation requires from the legislative side, including consultation on texts as they evolve. Evolving text is the central difficulty in trade oversight, because a provision acceptable in one draft may change meaning when definitions, exceptions, or annexes change around it. Consultation tied to the development of the text, rather than to a fixed set of briefing dates, follows the negotiation in the form in which the negotiation will eventually reach Congress.

The duty to consult before, during, and after rounds of negotiation gives that structure its rhythm. Consultation before a round allows Congress to state priorities and concerns while the negotiators are preparing positions, when advice can shape the instructions under which a team operates. Consultation during a negotiation addresses developments that arise as proposals meet resistance, compromises take shape, and tradeoffs among chapters begin to appear. Consultation after a round allows the committees to assess what changed, compare the result against the objectives, and identify issues to press before the next stage hardens. The sequence also protects the value of the later statutory steps. Notification before signing, publication of the text, advisory reports, and the assessment by the International Trade Commission all arrive after much of the bargaining has closed. If consultation operated only at those later points, Congress would receive a well-documented agreement whose main choices had been made outside its view. The before, during, and after pattern instead treats each round as part of a record that the committees follow, question, and influence across the life of the talks.

### Can members of Congress read the negotiating text during talks?

Yes. The statute gives every member of Congress the right to review negotiating texts as a negotiation proceeds, and cleared staff may review those texts under access provisions added by the 2015 act, alongside designated congressional advisers accredited to the negotiating teams themselves.

That access matters because consultation without access to the text would reduce the duty to a summary provided by the negotiators themselves. A summary can describe a proposal in general terms, emphasize the gains the negotiators expect, and pass lightly over concessions whose significance appears only in the precise language of a draft chapter or annex. Review of the negotiating text allows a member to compare the language in progress against the statutory objectives, to see where bracketed alternatives remain open, and to identify provisions that might require changes in existing United States law. The Congressional Research Service treatment of the authority presents access and consultation as connected parts of the same architecture, and the connection is practical as well as formal. Advice offered before a text settles has a different value from comment offered on a signed agreement, and the statute places the right of review during the talks, while the language remains subject to change through further rounds.

Cleared staff access under the 2015 act extended that architecture beyond the members themselves. Members carry the constitutional responsibility and the vote, but the detailed work of reading lengthy and technical negotiating texts falls in large part on staff with subject knowledge in agriculture, services, intellectual property, labor, environment, customs, and other fields covered by a modern trade agreement. A right of review confined to members in person would have left much of the text examined only at a distance from the expertise available within the committee system. By providing for cleared staff to review negotiating texts, the 2015 act aligned the formal right of access with the way Congress actually performs oversight. The clearance condition also reflects the character of the material. Negotiating texts are sensitive while talks are underway, because disclosure of positions and fallback language can affect the bargaining itself. The statute answered that sensitivity with controlled access through cleared personnel rather than with exclusion of Congress from the text.

The Chief Transparency Officer at the Office of the United States Trade Representative was another addition of the 2015 act, charged with consultation on transparency policy. The office sits on the executive side of the relationship, within the agency conducting the negotiations, and its charge concerns how transparency operates in practice. That placement gives the transparency function an identified holder inside the trade representative's office rather than leaving the subject dispersed across negotiating teams and public affairs functions. Consultation on transparency policy connects the internal handling of texts and information to the wider obligations the act imposes, including publication of the agreement text on a publicly available website of the trade representative at least 60 days before signing. The officer does not replace the rights of members or the role of the committees, and the statute does not present the office as a substitute for consultation with Congress. The office adds a designated point of responsibility for advising on and consulting about the policies that govern openness, while the substantive rights of review and consultation remain where the act placed them, with Congress and its designated advisers.

The consultation architecture also plugs into a wider advisory structure that reaches beyond Congress. At the top of the advisory committee system stands the Advisory Committee for Trade Policy and Negotiations. That system brings organized advice from outside the government into the trade policy process, and the statute connects the system to the congressional process through the reports the system produces. The placement of the Advisory Committee for Trade Policy and Negotiations at the head of the system gives the structure a senior body charged with advice on trade policy and negotiations at the broadest level, beneath which the wider committee apparatus addresses more specific subjects and sectors. For Congress, the value of the system lies in part in the written record it generates. Consultation with members and advisers occurs through meetings, access, and exchanges that may leave only a limited public trace. Advisory committee reports, by contrast, become documents that the committees of jurisdiction can read, compare against the agreement, and use in judging whether the negotiation achieved the objectives the statute set.

After signature, that record arrives on a defined timetable. The advisory committees produce their reports within 30 days of signing, and the International Trade Commission assessment follows within 105 days after signing. The two products serve different functions. Advisory reports present the views of the advisory system on the agreement that the negotiators have concluded, giving Congress an organized outside assessment close to the moment of signature. The commission assessment supplies an independent institutional evaluation from the agency charged by the statute with that task, and the 2015 act requires the assessment to be made public. Public release matters to the legislative process because debate over an implementing bill does not occur only inside committee rooms. A public assessment allows members, affected industries, workers, and other interested parties to read the same evaluation and to frame their representations to Congress around a common document. Together, the 30 day advisory reports and the 105 day commission assessment ensure that the period between signature and submission of the implementing materials is not empty. Congress receives outside advice, an independent assessment, the final legal text, and a draft statement of administrative action through steps the act sequences in advance.

The most consequential informal control, however, operates outside the statutory text altogether. The mock markup is an informal, nonstatutory practice with real force, developed through the working relationship between the administration and the two committees that hold jurisdiction over trade legislation. Under the practice, the administration prepares a draft implementing bill before formal submission, and the House Committee on Ways and Means and the Senate Committee on Finance hold hearings on that draft and mark it up in advisory form. The hearings allow the committees to hear testimony on the draft, test its provisions against the agreement and the statement of administrative action, and expose disagreements while the document remains a draft. The advisory markup then allows each committee to state, in the concrete form of amendments and votes on language, what it would change. Because a markup works on text rather than on general statements of concern, the administration receives advice at the level of precision that legislation requires. A concern expressed in a letter can be acknowledged and set aside. A concern embodied in an adopted advisory amendment shows the administration the exact language a committee majority supports.

Where the two committees differ, the practice extends to a mock conference that reconciles their advice. Ways and Means and Finance approach an agreement from the perspective of different chambers, different memberships, and sometimes different judgments about the same provision, yet the implementing bill that eventually reaches the floor must be a single text capable of passing both Houses. A mock conference allows the committees to resolve differences in their advisory positions before submission, so the administration receives a reconciled statement of congressional advice rather than two conflicting markups. The exercise also previews the politics of passage. An implementing bill under the expedited procedures cannot be amended on the floor, debate is limited, and the vote is a direct judgment on the package as submitted. Difficulty that surfaces in a mock conference therefore carries information the administration ignores at considerable risk, because the same difficulty may reappear as opposition to the unamendable bill itself. The mock process gives both branches a rehearsal in which problems can be identified and addressed while the only cost of disagreement is further negotiation over a draft.

The legal limits of the mock markup define its place in the system. The President is not bound to accept the result, and the committees cannot change the submitted bill afterward. Once the President formally submits the implementing bill, section 151 of the Trade Act of 1974 governs, committees may not amend the bill or recommend amendments, and the expedited timetable moves the measure toward a vote in the form in which it was submitted. That bar on later amendment is precisely why the advice is given before submission. The mock markup is the last point in the process at which committee preferences can be written into the text through ordinary legislative craftsmanship. After submission, the choice before each chamber narrows to approval or rejection of the package. The informality of the practice does not make the advice weak in practical terms. Submitted bills have often tracked the mock markup, because an administration that disregards a clear committee result must ask the same committees and chambers to pass the bill under procedures that deny members any chance to repair it. Agreement at the mock stage converts potential floor opposition into a text that the committees have already helped to shape.

The distinction that governs the whole architecture concerns enforcement. Consultation rights under the authority are not freestanding promises that a disappointed member can carry into a separate forum for correction. The statute enforces consultation through the political and procedural consequences it attaches to a breach. A failure or refusal to notify or consult as the act requires can support a procedural disapproval resolution, and a failure to notify or consult can also support the chamber specific withdrawal machinery that the 2015 act created through the Consultation and Compliance Resolution. Those mechanisms operate inside Congress, through committee gatekeeping, reports, privileged floor procedures in defined cases, and votes of one or both Houses. Their effect is procedural and powerful, because withdrawal of expedited treatment removes the guaranteed timetable, the bar on amendments, and the protection against extended Senate debate that make the grant valuable to the executive branch in the first place. Consultation is therefore backed by a sanction, but the sanction is the loss of the procedural bargain rather than an order directed at the negotiators.

No court polices the consultation duties. The statute locates the judgment about the adequacy of consultation in the institutions that made the bargain, the House and the Senate acting through the procedures the act and their own rules provide. That allocation follows from the character of sections 151 to 154 as rules of each House, which either House may change in the same manner as any other rule, and from the nature of the questions involved. Judging whether consultation was meaningful requires an assessment of meetings held, texts shared, advice offered, and responsiveness shown across a negotiation, all matters intertwined with the internal proceedings of Congress and the conduct of diplomacy by the President. The act answers a breach by changing how Congress will treat the resulting bill, not by inviting judicial supervision of conversations between the branches. The result is a system in which consultation rights are real, documented, and consequential, while their enforcement remains political in the institutional sense. Congress protects its place in trade policymaking by controlling the procedure that the President needs, and the executive branch consults because the cost of failing to consult is measured in the possible loss of that procedure.

## Withdrawing the Privilege and the Relocation of Influence

A grant of expedited treatment raises a problem of control. Congress lends the President negotiating latitude and, in return, accepts a floor procedure that bars amendment, caps debate, and moves an implementing bill toward a vote on a fixed timetable. If that bargain contained no exit, the lending institution would surrender judgment after negotiators had produced a signed text and before either chamber had cast a final vote. The Bipartisan Congressional Trade Priorities and Accountability Act of 2015 answered that problem by surrounding the privilege with exits of several kinds. Some exits sit inside the statute and operate through defined resolutions, and beyond them sits an older power grounded in the authority of each House over its own rules, which no grant transferred away. The record through August 15, 2020, showed those exits working in very different ways. The formal resolutions had never carried a chamber. The rules power had been used once, decisively, against a pending implementing bill. The most consequential exercise of congressional leverage in the period came through neither route, but through a House majority that declined to move a bill at all until the signed text changed. Influence did not vanish when Congress accepted limits on amendment and debate. Influence moved, in part to committees guarding the gates of the statutory resolutions, and in part to the stage before formal introduction, when a determined majority could hold a bill in place and bargain over the text that the bill would approve.

The first exit concerns time rather than any single agreement. As enacted, the 2015 act covered agreements entered into before July 1, 2018, and the grant carried a possible extension of three more years. Under the extension completed in 2018, the grant covers agreements entered into before July 1, 2021. Section 103(c) of the 2015 act supplied the mechanism by which Congress could refuse that additional period, and its threshold is unusually low. The instrument is an extension disapproval resolution, and it is a resolution of one House. Adoption by either chamber alone before the extension date denies the extension. No bicameral agreement is required, and no presidential signature enters the design, because the question is not whether to enact a new law but whether a continuation that Congress had made conditional would take effect at all. A single chamber, acting on its own judgment about trade policy or about the conduct of negotiations, could close the grant at its original end and leave the President to seek authority again through fresh legislation. In most settings, change requires majorities in both chambers and survival of a veto, and section 103(c) required only one chamber to stop an extension.

The low threshold for adoption is balanced by a demanding threshold for reaching the floor. Any member may introduce an extension disapproval resolution, but introduction alone accomplishes nothing under the statute. Floor consideration requires a report from the committees that guard trade legislation. In the House, the Committee on Ways and Means and the Committee on Rules stand in that position. In the Senate, the Committee on Finance stands there. If a resolution cleared that gate and was reported, the procedure changed character. The reported resolution was considered under the expedited procedure of section 152 of the Trade Act of 1974, the provision codified at 19 U.S.C. 2192 that governs resolutions disapproving certain actions and supplies the floor procedure that later statutes borrow. That procedure brings a privileged motion, limited debate, and no amendment. Access is narrow and committee controlled. Once access is granted, decision is fast and unamendable, so a chamber that receives a reported resolution confronts a clean choice on the extension itself.

The Congressional Research Service recorded how that machinery had performed under earlier grants, and the record cuts against any assumption that a low adoption threshold produces frequent use. Extension disapproval resolutions have been reported out of committee under earlier grants, so the gate has opened in the past and the expedited floor procedure has been within reach. None has ever been adopted by either House. Committees were willing, on occasion, to send the question to the floor. No chamber was willing to answer it by ending an extension. The extension disapproval resolution therefore functioned as a reserve power and as a signaling device, but not as an instrument that any chamber had chosen to fire. The history of the 2015 grant followed the same pattern at its one test point. The President requested extension on March 20, 2018. No extension disapproval resolution was adopted, so the extension took effect. Inaction had a definite legal consequence, because Congress wrote the default in favor of continuation unless one House affirmatively disapproved. As a result, the authority runs to July 1, 2021 as a scheduled end fixed by the statute and the completed extension, covering agreements entered into before that date.

The second exit is narrower in scope and heavier in its voting requirement. Section 106(b) of the 2015 act provides the procedural disapproval resolution, aimed not at the grant in general but at the treatment of one agreement. The instrument is a simple resolution of either House, yet a single adoption accomplishes nothing by itself. A procedural disapproval resolution takes effect only if both Houses adopt one on the same agreement within 60 days of each other. One chamber cannot strip expedited treatment on its own through this route, however strong its objections. The objecting chamber must persuade the other chamber to reach the same conclusion about the same agreement, and must do so promptly enough that the two adoptions fall within the statutory window. Delay defeats the device just as surely as disagreement does. A Senate adoption followed by months of House hesitation produces no effect, and a House adoption that the Senate answers after the window closes produces no effect either.

The grounds for the resolution are limited to two, and both grounds concern the relationship between the executive branch and Congress or between the agreement and the statute that authorized it. The first ground is that the President failed or refused to notify or consult as the act requires. Notification and consultation are not courtesies under the 2015 act. They are statutory conditions woven through the grant, covering notice before negotiations begin, notice before an agreement is entered into, publication of the agreement text before signing, and a consultation architecture that includes advisory groups in each chamber and designated congressional advisers accredited to negotiating teams. The second ground reaches substance through a statutory standard. A resolution may rest on a determination that the agreement fails to make progress in achieving the act's purposes, policies, priorities, and objectives. Those purposes and objectives include the negotiating objectives that the 2015 act states in three tiers, covering overall objectives, principal negotiating objectives, and capacity building and other priorities. The statutory phrase measures progress, which leaves room for argument about how much movement toward the stated objectives suffices and about how to weigh gains in one area against concessions in another.

Access to the expedited route for a procedural disapproval resolution is gated as firmly as access for an extension disapproval resolution, and the gate belongs to the revenue committees, joined in the House by the Committee on Rules. A resolution that the responsible committees declined to report had no claim on the borrowed expedited procedure of section 152, with its privileged motion, limited debate, and bar on amendment. The 2015 act adds a rationing rule that has no equivalent in the extension context. Only the first reported procedural disapproval resolution per agreement per chamber receives the section 152 expedited procedure. Each chamber gets one attempt per agreement through the fast route. The one attempt rule also protects the implementing bill from serial procedural attacks. Opponents receive a genuine opportunity to strip the privilege, with a floor vote under expedited conditions, but the opportunity is singular.

The consequence of an effective procedural disapproval resolution requires exact statement, because the device is easily misunderstood as a veto over the agreement itself. An effective resolution removes expedited treatment. The resolution does not kill the implementing bill. If both Houses adopt resolutions within the statutory window and on a statutory ground, the agreement loses the benefit of the section 151 procedure, with its bar on amendments, its cap on debate, and its guaranteed movement toward a vote. The bill may be considered under the ordinary rules of each House. Under ordinary rules, committees and floor majorities recover the procedural options that the expedited procedure suspends, including amendment where the chamber's rules allow it and extended debate in the Senate with the cloture burden that extended debate ordinarily carries. The statute withdraws a procedure. The statute does not annul the agreement, and the statute does not bar either House from approving the agreement through the regular legislative process if majorities in both chambers wish to do so.

The third exit is the newest and the most chamber bound. The Consultation and Compliance Resolution was new in the 2015 act, provided for in section 106(b)(3) and (4), and it answers a gap in the procedural disapproval design. A chamber might conclude, on its own record, that the President had failed to notify or consult as the act requires, yet be unable to secure a matching adoption in the other chamber within 60 days. Under the procedural disapproval route alone, that chamber's conclusion would have no consequence for proceedings on its own floor. The Consultation and Compliance Resolution lets one chamber withdraw expedited treatment in that chamber alone for failure to notify or consult. The limited reach of the remedy also preserves the other chamber's autonomy. A House resolution does not govern Senate floor procedure, and a Senate resolution does not govern the House.

The trigger for such a resolution is deliberately indirect. A Consultation and Compliance Resolution does not arise from a free-standing complaint filed at any moment. In the House, the trigger is a report posture in which the Committee on Ways and Means reports the implementing bill with other than a favorable recommendation. In the Senate, the trigger is a meeting posture in which the Committee on Finance meets on whether to report the implementing bill and does not report favorably, after which the committee must report a Consultation and Compliance Resolution instead. In each chamber, the revenue committee must first place itself on record against a favorable disposition of the bill itself. Only then does the compliance question move forward as a resolution. That structure ties the withdrawal of expedited treatment to the committee's judgment on the process as a whole, rather than allowing the resolution to become a preliminary skirmish detached from the bill.

Once triggered, the Consultation and Compliance Resolution travels without the assistance that the other resolutions receive. The 2015 act provides no expedited procedure for it. Such a resolution must make its way under the ordinary rules of the chamber where it arises, and in the Senate that path carries a serious burden. Senate consideration can be filibustered, and 60 votes are needed for cloture. The contrast with the implementing bill itself is sharp. An implementing bill under section 151 cannot be filibustered, because Senate debate is capped at 20 hours by statute and no cloture motion is needed. A Senate resolution seeking to remove that protection must survive the very practice of extended debate that the protection exists to prevent. A bare Senate majority acting over sustained objection could not carry such a resolution, which means the device protects consultation rights in proportion to the breadth of the complaint about them. The 2015 act also retains a trade remedy laws resolution, a companion instrument connected to the statutory reports on changes to trade remedy laws that the President must deliver to the revenue committees before signing. For that resolution, the statute does not specify its legal effect.

Beyond the resolutions that the statute defines sits the power that makes all of the expedited procedures legally possible. Sections 151 to 153 of the Trade Act of 1974 were enacted as an exercise of the rulemaking power of each House. They are part of the rules of each House, they supersede other rules only where inconsistent, and either House may change them at any time in the same manner as any other rule. The citation at 19 U.S.C. 2191(a) states that reservation expressly. A chamber therefore does not need a statutory ground, a committee report of a particular kind, a matching adoption in the other chamber, or a window of any length in order to reclaim control of its own floor. A chamber can simply change its rules. The override rests on Article I, section 5 of the Constitution, under which each House determines the rules of its proceedings, and the fast track statutes themselves acknowledge that foundation rather than displacing it.

The House used that power on April 10, 2008, in circumstances that tested the consultation premise of the entire arrangement. The Colombia implementing bill, H.R. 5724, had been introduced by request on April 8, 2008, by Majority Leader Hoyer and Minority Leader Boehner. The House leadership judged consultation inadequate. On April 10, 2008, the House adopted H.Res. 1092 by a vote of 224 to 195. The resolution was a change to the rules of the House, and its effect was to make the fast track timetable inapplicable to the Colombia implementing bill. With the timetable removed, the bill lost the guaranteed schedule that expedited procedure supplies, and no further action on H.R. 5724 occurred in the 110th Congress. The action of April 10, 2008, was a rules change, not a procedural disapproval resolution. No pair of simple resolutions was adopted by both Houses within 60 days of each other, and no statutory ground was invoked or found. The House acted alone, for its own floor, under its rulemaking power, and the episode shows that the timetable binds a chamber only for as long as that chamber chooses to be bound.

### How did the House make the timetable inapplicable to the Colombia bill?

Yes. Congress built several exits into the grant itself. One House alone could block an extension, both Houses together could end expedited treatment for a particular agreement, one chamber could withdraw the privilege on its own floor, and either House could change its own rules.

The strongest counter reading of the whole arrangement begins from the observation that none of the formal exits described the most important congressional intervention of the period. Between the signing of one agreement and the passage of its implementing act, the decisive events of 2019 involved no extension disapproval resolution, no procedural disapproval resolution, no Consultation and Compliance Resolution, and no rules change. The intervention was a refusal to move. The agreement between the United States, Mexico, and Canada was signed on November 30, 2018. The signed text instead waited through most of 2019. The House majority declined to move an implementing bill during that period, while a working group led by Ways and Means Chairman Richard Neal negotiated changes with United States Trade Representative Robert Lighthizer. The delay was not passive. The working group used the interval to bargain over the content of an agreement that had already been signed, and the administration negotiated because the alternative was an implementing bill that the House majority would not advance. Withholding the bill was itself the leverage, exercised at the one stage where the expedited statute had the least to say, the stage before formal submission set the section 151 machinery in motion.

The bargain produced a formal change to the signed agreement, not merely assurances about its administration. On December 10, 2019, the three governments signed a protocol of amendment. The protocol changed dispute settlement. The protocol changed labor and environment enforcement, including a facility-specific rapid response labor mechanism, an enforcement tool directed at particular workplaces rather than at a trading partner's labor system in general terms alone. The protocol changed intellectual property by removing the ten-year biologics data protection commitment that the signed text had carried. The protocol also changed the steel and aluminum rules of origin, the provisions that determine how much regional content a product must contain and how particular metals figure in that calculation. The sequence refutes any suggestion that the post-signing stage under trade promotion authority is a formality in which Congress merely ratifies a finished product. In the United States Mexico Canada case, the product was reopened, amended, and signed again in amended form before an implementing bill existed.

Only after the protocol was signed did the formal machinery begin to move, and the dates show how quickly that machinery operated once the pre-introduction bargain had been struck. The administration submitted the implementing legislation on December 13, 2019. H.R. 5430 was introduced that day, and the Senate companion, S. 3052, followed on December 16, 2019. The House passed H.R. 5430 on December 19, 2019, by a vote of 385 to 41. The Senate passed the bill on January 16, 2020, by a vote of 89 to 10. The United States Mexico Canada Agreement Implementation Act was signed on January 29, 2020, as P.L. 116-113, and the agreement entered into force on July 1, 2020. The expedited procedure performed the function that its defenders assign to it, delivering prompt passage without amendment once the bill arrived. The congressional influence that shaped the outcome had been exercised entirely before arrival, through the working group negotiations and through the credible prospect that no bill would move until those negotiations succeeded. A dedicated account of that implementing act appears in [the guide to the USMCA implementation act](/2020/07/01/usmca-implementation-act-2020-guide/).

That sequence is the proof that influence shifted rather than disappeared, and the shift has a definite location. Under the expedited procedures, the floor is the weakest point of congressional leverage, because the floor may not amend, may debate only within a fixed allowance of hours, and must vote on the bill as submitted. The committee reporting stage carries more leverage, because the revenue committees guard access to the statutory resolutions and conduct the mock markup practice through which Ways and Means and Finance advise on a draft implementing bill before formal submission. The pre-introduction stage carries the most leverage of all, because nothing in section 151 compels a House majority to advance a bill on the President's schedule, and an administration that wants the benefits of expedited passage must first deliver a package that the majority will allow to enter the machinery. The absence of a withdrawal resolution in 2019 and 2020 was not evidence that Congress lacked influence over the agreement. The absence was evidence that influence had been exercised early enough, and credibly enough, that the text itself changed before the withdrawal question ever had to be put.

## Lapse, Renewal, Misread Provisions, and the Constitutional Frame

The record of trade promotion authority is a record of grants that ran for a defined period, ended on a date fixed in advance, and then waited on a fresh decision by Congress. The Trade Act of 1974, Public Law 93-618, signed on January 3, 1975, created the expedited procedures in sections 151 to 154 of Title I, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2191 to 2194. Under that first grant, the authority to enter into agreements ran to 1980. The design paired presidential negotiating latitude with a congressional promise about later procedure. An implementing bill would be introduced by request, referred to the committees of jurisdiction, protected against amendment, discharged on a statutory timetable if a committee failed to report, and debated under a strict cap before passage by simple majority. The grant therefore had a life set by Congress from the beginning rather than an open-ended life set by practice.

The Trade Agreements Act of 1979 extended the authority for eight years. The Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988 renewed the grant for agreements entered into before June 1, 1991, with provision for extension, and a 1991 extension carried the authority into April 1994 so that the Uruguay Round could finish. The authority expired on April 16, 1994, on the dating given by the Congressional Research Service. The Uruguay Round Agreements Act was signed on December 8, 1994 under the expiring authority, a sequence that shows how a closing grant could cover work completed at the end of its run while leaving no authority in place for the next negotiation. The scheduled end did the work Congress had assigned to the end date. Once April 16, 1994 passed without a renewal statute, the expedited procedures had no new agreements to govern.

Renewal efforts during the lapse years failed in two distinct ways. In 1997, bills were reported by the House Committee on Ways and Means and the Senate Committee on Finance, but the House bill was pulled before a floor vote for lack of support. A committee report therefore did not deliver enactment, and the gap between committee approval and floor support became the controlling fact of that attempt. On September 25, 1998, the House defeated a renewal bill by 180 to 243. That vote closed the second attempt with a recorded rejection rather than a withdrawal. Between the expiry in 1994 and the next renewal in 2002, Congress considered the device again and declined to restore the device on the terms presented in those bills.

The lapse had a practical meaning that the Jordan agreement made concrete. An agreement could be negotiated and approved during a lapse, but approval proceeded without the guarantee of the expedited procedures. The United States-Jordan agreement was signed in 2000 and implemented by Public Law 107-43 in 2001, and the Congressional Research Service records that agreement as approved without fast track during the 1994 to 2002 lapse. The example matters because the lapse removed a procedure rather than a subject. Trade agreements did not become legally impossible after April 16, 1994, and Congress did not lose the power to pass an implementing statute. What disappeared was the advance commitment that an implementing bill would move through each House without amendment, under limited debate, and on a timetable fixed by sections 151 to 154.

Renewal came in the Trade Act of 2002, Public Law 107-210, signed on August 6, 2002. The House passed the measure by 215 to 212 on July 27, 2002, and the Senate passed the measure by 64 to 34 on August 1, 2002. Title XXI of that act, the Bipartisan Trade Promotion Authority Act of 2002, renamed fast track as trade promotion authority. The new name did not change the underlying bargain. Congress set negotiating objectives, consultation duties, and submission conditions, and Congress promised expedited consideration for an implementing bill that met the statutory terms. The narrow House margin recorded the contested character of that bargain at the moment of renewal, and the Senate margin recorded broader support in the second chamber for the same text.

The 2002 grant covered agreements entered into before June 1, 2005, and the grant carried a two-year extension mechanism tied to a presidential request and the absence of an extension disapproval resolution in either House. The extension ran, and the authority expired on July 1, 2007. Expiry did not erase eligibility for agreements signed before the end date. The Peru implementing act was enacted on December 14, 2007 as Public Law 110-138, after the July 1, 2007 expiry. The Colombia, Panama, and South Korea agreements, signed in 2006 and 2007, were implemented on October 12, 2011 through Public Laws 112-41, 112-42, and 112-43. That tail followed from the structure of the grant. Eligibility attached to agreements entered into before the scheduled end, while the implementing legislation for an eligible agreement could reach enactment after the end date had passed.

The next renewal came in the Bipartisan Congressional Trade Priorities and Accountability Act of 2015, Public Law 114-26, signed on June 29, 2015, and codified principally at 19 U.S.C. 4201 and following. The path to enactment took two steps in the House during June 2015. The Senate had passed the combined bill, H.R. 1314, on May 22, 2015 by 62 to 37. In the House on June 12, 2015, Title I on trade promotion authority passed by 219 to 211, but Title II on trade adjustment assistance failed by 126 to 302, and the combined bill failed on that division. Enactment then proceeded through H.R. 2146, which carried the authority as an amendment. The House passed H.R. 2146 on June 18, 2015 by 218 to 208, and the Senate passed the measure on June 24, 2015 by 60 to 38. The two-step path preserved the distinction between support for the authority itself and support for the larger package in which the authority had first moved.

As enacted in 2015, the grant covered agreements entered into before July 1, 2018. The statute allowed a three-year extension if the President requested the extension and Congress did not block the extension. The President requested extension on March 20, 2018. No extension disapproval resolution was adopted, so the extension took effect. Under the extension completed in 2018, the grant covers agreements entered into before July 1, 2021, and the authority runs to July 1, 2021. Those dates supply the state of the authority at the date of the article, August 15, 2020. The 2015 grant, as extended in 2018, was in force at that date under an enacted schedule. The end date of July 1, 2021 functioned as a date fixed by statute for a later congressional decision, not as a description of an event completed before August 15, 2020.

The pattern across the record is a grant, a scheduled end, and a congressional decision taken at the end. Congress granted the authority in 1974 and extended the authority in 1979. Congress renewed the authority in 1988, extended the renewed grant in 1991, and then let the grant expire on April 16, 1994. Congress renewed the authority in 2002, the extension under that grant ran, and Congress then let the grant expire on July 1, 2007. Congress renewed the authority in 2015, and the extension completed in 2018 carried the grant toward the scheduled end of July 1, 2021. The 1994 lapse and the 2007 lapse are the completed examples of Congress letting a grant run out. Each lapse began because a scheduled end arrived without a renewal statute, and each renewal required a new statute passed through the ordinary legislative process.

The Congressional Research Service records the scale on which the successive grants operated. The procedures served for 14 bilateral or regional free trade agreements, together with the Tokyo Round and Uruguay Round multilateral packages. That count belongs in the chronology because each agreement in the count depended on a grant in force for the negotiation in question or on eligibility preserved through the tail of an expiring grant. A lapse therefore had consequences beyond symbolism for any negotiation that began without a grant in place. A negotiator working during a lapse could reach an agreement, as the Jordan case showed, but could not promise a partner the amendment bar, the debate caps, or the discharge timetable that sections 151 to 154 supplied during a grant period. The count also explains why renewal debates drew sustained attention in both chambers. Each renewal decided the procedural terms for a class of future agreements rather than the merits of one agreement alone.

Five readings of the device recur in public discussion, and the statutory text answers each reading directly. The first reading treats trade promotion authority as a transfer of the commerce power from Congress to the President. The statute does not perform that transfer, as the constitutional discussion below explains in full. The second reading treats the authority as permanent. Every grant in the record has carried a scheduled end, from the first run to 1980 through the 2015 grant and the extension completed in 2018. A device that Congress has repeatedly allowed to expire, and repeatedly restored only by statute, does not operate as a permanent feature of trade law. The scheduled end is a substantive part of the grant because the end forces the question of renewal back onto the legislative calendar.

The third reading treats consultation as a courtesy that the executive extends to Congress during negotiations. The 2015 act treats consultation as a statutory condition with procedural consequences. The act sets notification duties measured in calendar days, including notice at least 90 calendar days before negotiations begin and notice at least 90 calendar days before an agreement is entered into. The act requires publication of agreement text on a publicly available website of the United States Trade Representative at least 60 days before signing, submission of agreement details to the International Trade Commission at least 90 days before signing, and notice of changes in United States law required for compliance at least 60 days before signing. The act also builds a consultation architecture around the House Advisory Group on Negotiations, the Senate Advisory Group on Negotiations, designated congressional advisers accredited to negotiating teams, and access to negotiating texts for every member of Congress. A condition written into the eligibility structure in that detail does not read as a courtesy.

The consequences attached to consultation confirm that reading. Under section 106(b) of the 2015 act, a procedural disapproval resolution may rest on a finding that the President failed or refused to notify or consult as the act requires. Such a resolution takes effect only if both Houses adopt a resolution on the same agreement within 60 days of each other, and floor access is gated by the revenue committees, with the Committee on Rules sharing that gate in the House. A consultation and compliance resolution supplies a narrower remedy in one chamber after an adverse committee posture on the implementing bill. The details differ by instrument, but the shared premise is constant across the instruments. Compliance with notification and consultation duties affects whether expedited treatment remains available for an agreement.

The fourth reading treats a disapproval resolution as an instrument that kills an agreement. The statutory effect is narrower. An effective procedural disapproval resolution removes expedited treatment for the implementing bill, and the bill may then be considered under ordinary rules. Ordinary rules restore amendment, extended debate, and the usual obstacles of the legislative process, so loss of expedited treatment carries real weight. Loss of expedited treatment does not, by itself, enact a prohibition on the agreement, repeal an implementing statute, or bar either House from passing an implementing bill through regular order. An extension disapproval resolution has a different object altogether, because adoption by either House alone before the extension date denies an extension of the grant rather than passing judgment on a single agreement. Confusing the two instruments converts a procedural consequence into a substantive veto that the statute does not create.

The fifth reading treats submission of an implementing bill as a step the President may take at leisure after signing an agreement. The submission machinery and the clocks that follow submission are statutory. On the day the President submits an agreement, the implementing bill is introduced in the House by the majority leader or a designee and in the Senate by the majority leader or a designee, in each case by request. Referral follows to the committees of jurisdiction. A committee may not amend the bill or recommend amendments, and a committee that has not reported within 45 days of session is automatically discharged. After report or discharge, any member may offer a nondebatable and highly privileged motion to proceed. Debate in each chamber is limited to 20 hours, and the House vote on final passage must occur on or before the fifteenth day after report or discharge. The eligible bill itself may contain only provisions approving the agreement and the statement of administrative action, plus provisions strictly necessary or appropriate to implement the agreement. A timetable written at that level of detail leaves no open-ended discretion about the pace of consideration once submission occurs.

### Does trade promotion authority transfer the power to regulate foreign commerce to the President?

No. Article I assigns the duties power and the foreign commerce power to Congress. Trade promotion authority sets rules for how each House considers an implementing bill and gives the President defined latitude to negotiate. Only a statute passed by both Houses and presented to the President changes United States law.

The constitutional frame begins with Article I, section 8, which commits to Congress the power to lay and collect duties and the power to regulate commerce with foreign nations. Trade promotion authority does not relocate either power. The President negotiates under objectives that Congress enacted, and an agreement changes United States law only through an implementing statute. That statute passes both Houses and is presented to the President like any other law. The final legal act therefore remains legislation, and the vote on final passage remains a vote by each House on a bill whose text Congress may approve or reject. Nothing in sections 151 to 154 allows an agreement, standing alone, to amend a tariff schedule, repeal a statute, or impose a duty.

Article I, section 5 supplies the second half of the frame, because that provision lets each House determine the rules of proceedings in that House. Sections 151 to 154 operate in that capacity. Section 151 states the point directly in the codified text at 19 U.S.C. 2191(a). Sections 151 to 153 are enacted as an exercise of the rulemaking power of each House, form part of the rules of each House, supersede other rules only where inconsistent, and may be changed by either House at any time in the same manner as any other rule. The expedited procedures bind because each House adopted the procedures as rules for the consideration of a defined class of bills. The procedures constrain debate, amendment, and timing inside each chamber rather than conferring legislative power on the executive branch.

The House action of April 10, 2008 illustrates the rulemaking character of the arrangement. On that date the House adopted H.Res. 1092 by 224 to 195, a change to House rules that made the fast track timetable inapplicable to the Colombia implementing bill, H.R. 5724, after House leadership judged consultation inadequate. No further action on H.R. 5724 occurred in the 110th Congress. The episode was not a procedural disapproval resolution, and the distinction matters for an accurate account of the device. A chamber acting under Article I, section 5 changed the rule that governed consideration in that chamber. The statutory timetable therefore operated subject to the same rulemaking authority that had created the timetable as a rule of the House.

Properly framed, the arrangement delegates negotiating latitude and constrains later congressional procedure. Negotiation requires a single voice at the table, authority to explore packages across sectors, and room to trade concessions within objectives that Congress set in advance. The 2015 act expresses those objectives in three tiers, covering overall objectives, principal negotiating objectives, and capacity building and other priorities, and the act makes the objectives a condition of eligibility rather than advice. Congress thereby directed the negotiation before talks began and reserved the final vote for the end of the process. The delegation runs to the conduct of negotiation. The constraint runs against Congress itself, through rules that limit amendment and debate when the negotiated result returns for a decision.

Two arguments about that arrangement have accompanied the device across the renewal debates, and each argument identifies a real feature of the design. Members of Congress who pressed the accountability argument observed that amendment is the stage at which legislative judgment traditionally operates on a text. A member who may vote for or against an implementing bill but may not amend the bill has surrendered the instrument through which members ordinarily correct defects, protect a district interest, or force a choice between competing provisions. On that account, the surrender concentrates practical power in the executive branch, which drafts the agreement and the implementing bill, and in the Committee on Ways and Means and the Committee on Finance, which control consultation access, the mock markup process, and the gates for disapproval resolutions. The argument treats the loss as one of institutional position rather than formal power, because the formal vote remains with each House while the capacity to shape the text moves elsewhere.

Presidents and trade negotiators who pressed the credibility argument began from the position of the foreign partner across the table. A partner asked to make concessions on agriculture, services, intellectual property, or market access must price those concessions against the text that the partner signed. If the signed text could return from Congress amended in ways that reopened settled trades, the partner would discount the value of American commitments or withhold concessions until the congressional process ended. On that account, the bar on amendment and the cap on debate supply assurance that the bargain presented for a vote is the bargain that negotiation produced. The argument treats the procedural surrender as the price of obtaining concessions that an amendable process would make harder to secure. The statutory record holds both arguments in view at once, because each renewal statute restored the amendment bar while adding tighter objectives, broader text access, and new procedural remedies for failures of consultation.

## Reading the Text as a Map of the Bargain

The procedure lives in sections 151 to 154 of the Trade Act of 1974, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2191 to 2194. Section 151 governs implementing bills, section 152 governs resolutions disapproving certain actions and supplies the floor procedure that later statutes borrow, section 153 governs resolutions on extension of the section 402 waiver authority, and section 154 supplies special rules on delivery of documents and computation of periods. The direction lives in the authorizing statute, in the grant enacted in 2015, which states the negotiating objectives, the notification duties, the consultation architecture, and the disapproval routes that police compliance.

The map gives the claim a textual address. Congress trades timing for direction: amendment surrendered at the end is paid for with objectives, notice, consultation, and publication duties that operate from the beginning. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does trade promotion authority actually do?

Trade promotion authority allows the President to negotiate trade agreements under negotiating objectives written by Congress, on the condition that notification and consultation duties are met. An agreement entered into during the covered period receives expedited consideration in Congress. The implementing bill is introduced by request by the majority leader or a designee in each House on the day of submission, referred to the committees of jurisdiction, and protected against amendment in committee and on the floor. Debate is capped at 20 hours in each chamber, committees face automatic discharge after 45 session days, and passage requires a simple majority of those voting.

### Q: Why does trade promotion authority exist?

Trade promotion authority exists to solve a credibility problem in trade negotiation. A foreign partner makes concessions across a whole package, pricing each concession against the rest of the text, on labor, environment, intellectual property, rules of origin, and dispute settlement. If Congress could reopen the signed text line by line, each concession would carry a risk of revision after signature, and a partner would withhold concessions, demand extra compensation, insist on reopening corresponding provisions, or decline serious negotiation. The procedure protects the package by guaranteeing a vote on the text as presented, while Congress writes objectives in advance and receives notice and consultation during the talks.

### Q: Can Congress amend a bill under trade promotion authority?

No. Amendment is the central surrender in the bargain. Committees of jurisdiction may not amend an eligible implementing bill or recommend amendments to the bill, and no amendments are in order at any stage of floor consideration. Motions to recommit, postpone, or reconsider are barred or decided without debate. The protection keeps the negotiated balance of reciprocal concessions intact, because an amendment that improves one provision for one constituency can destroy the tradeoffs that made the package acceptable to the other party. Congress retains the decisive power to approve or reject the bill as submitted, by a simple majority of those voting in each House.

### Q: What does Congress get in exchange for trade promotion authority?

Congress receives direction, information, and remedies in exchange for surrendering amendment. The authorizing statute states negotiating objectives in three tiers, overall objectives, principal negotiating objectives, and capacity building and other priorities, and the objectives operate as a condition of eligibility rather than advice. Congress receives notice at least 90 calendar days before negotiations begin and at least 90 calendar days before an agreement is signed, publication of the agreement text at least 60 days before signing, consultation through advisory groups and designated congressional advisers, and access to negotiating texts for every member and for cleared staff. Failures support the disapproval and compliance resolutions described in the statute.

### Q: Can Congress withdraw trade promotion authority for one deal?

Yes, through several routes of differing width. A procedural disapproval resolution under section 106(b) of the 2015 act removes expedited treatment for one agreement if both Houses adopt a resolution on the same agreement within 60 days of each other, on the ground that the President failed or refused to notify or consult, or that the agreement fails to make progress in achieving the statutory purposes and objectives. A Consultation and Compliance Resolution lets one chamber withdraw expedited treatment on its own floor for failure to notify or consult, after the committee posture set by the statute. Either House may also change its own rules.

### Q: When did trade promotion authority lapse?

The completed lapses in the record before August 15, 2020 were two. The authority expired on April 16, 1994, after the 1988 renewal and the 1991 extension carried the grant into April 1994 so that the Uruguay Round could finish, and renewal efforts failed in 1997 and in a House vote of 180 to 243 on September 25, 1998. The authority expired again on July 1, 2007, at the end of the extended 2002 grant. The 2015 grant began a separate period. Under the extension completed in 2018, the 2015 authority runs to July 1, 2021 as an enacted schedule, a date stated as the schedule fixed by statute rather than as a lapse that had occurred.

### Q: How long is debate under trade promotion authority?

Debate on an implementing bill is limited to 20 hours in each chamber. In the House, the time is divided equally between supporters and opponents. In the Senate, the time is controlled by the two floor leaders. The cap applies to the bill itself, and the motion to proceed is nondebatable and highly privileged, so argument over whether to take up the bill does not consume the allotted period. After report or discharge, the House vote on final passage must occur on or before the fifteenth day. Because Senate debate ends when the statutory hours expire, opponents cannot extend consideration beyond the limit, and passage follows a simple majority vote.

### Q: Is trade promotion authority constitutional?

The arrangement rests on two provisions of Article I. Section 8 gives Congress the power to lay and collect duties and to regulate commerce with foreign nations, and trade promotion authority does not transfer either power to the President. Section 5 lets each House determine the rules of its proceedings, and sections 151 to 153 operate as rules of each House, enacted as an exercise of that rulemaking power, superseding other rules only where inconsistent and changeable by either House at any time. An agreement changes United States law only through an implementing statute passed by both Houses and presented to the President, so the final legal act remains legislation.

### Q: What is the difference between fast track and trade promotion authority?

Fast track and trade promotion authority name the same basic arrangement at different points in the record. Fast track was the name used from the Trade Act of 1974, which created the expedited procedures in sections 151 to 154, through the renewals of 1979, 1988, and 1991. The Trade Act of 2002 renamed the approach trade promotion authority through Title XXI, the Bipartisan Trade Promotion Authority Act of 2002. The renaming changed the label without changing the underlying bargain of objectives, notice, and consultation in exchange for limited debate, a bar on amendment, and a guaranteed vote. The 2015 act continued the newer name while tightening eligibility, publication, and compliance features.

### Q: What happens if a committee does not report a trade implementing bill?

A committee that has not reported an implementing bill within 45 days of session is automatically discharged from further consideration. Automatic discharge requires no motion, no vote, and no permission from the committee whose inaction triggered the discharge. The bill moves to the floor stage by operation of the statute, and any member may then offer the nondebatable, highly privileged motion to proceed. For a House bill reaching the Senate, the Finance Committee is discharged if the committee fails to report within 15 session days after receiving the House bill or by the end of the 45 session days for the Senate companion bill, whichever is later.

### Q: What is a mock markup in trade legislation?

A mock markup is the established informal practice by which the Committee on Ways and Means and the Committee on Finance advise the administration on a draft implementing bill before formal submission. The committees hold hearings on the draft and mark the draft up in advisory form, stating in concrete amendments and votes the language that a committee majority supports. Where the two committees differ, a mock conference reconciles the advice. The practice is not legally binding, and the President is not bound to accept the result. Submitted bills have often tracked the mock markup result, because the formal bill cannot be amended after submission under section 151.

### Q: What is an extension disapproval resolution?

An extension disapproval resolution is the instrument in section 103(c) of the 2015 act through which one House alone can deny an extension of the grant. Any member may introduce such a resolution, but floor consideration requires a report from Ways and Means and Rules in the House or from Finance in the Senate. A reported resolution is considered under the section 152 procedure, with a privileged motion, limited debate, and no amendment. Adoption by either chamber alone before the extension date denies the added years. The Congressional Research Service records that such resolutions have been reported under earlier grants, but none has ever been adopted by either House.

### Q: What is a procedural disapproval resolution?

A procedural disapproval resolution is a simple resolution under section 106(b) of the 2015 act that removes expedited treatment for one agreement. The resolution takes effect only if both Houses adopt one on the same agreement within 60 days of each other. Two grounds support the resolution: the President failed or refused to notify or consult as the act requires, or the agreement fails to make progress in achieving the purposes, policies, priorities, and objectives of the act. Floor access is gated by the revenue committees, plus Rules in the House, and only the first reported resolution per agreement per chamber receives the expedited section 152 procedure.

### Q: What is a consultation and compliance resolution?

A Consultation and Compliance Resolution, new in the 2015 act under section 106(b)(3) and (4), lets one chamber withdraw expedited treatment in that chamber alone for failure to notify or consult. The route opens only after a defined committee posture. In the House, Ways and Means must report the implementing bill with other than a favorable recommendation. In the Senate, Finance must meet on whether to report, decide against a favorable report, and then report a Consultation and Compliance Resolution instead. No expedited procedure is provided for the resolution, and in the Senate the resolution can be filibustered, with 60 votes needed for cloture.

### Q: Which committees oversee trade promotion authority in Congress?

The committees of jurisdiction are the House Committee on Ways and Means and the Senate Committee on Finance. Implementing bills are referred to those committees, the committees conduct the mock markup practice, and the committees gate access to the statutory resolutions. In the House, the Committee on Rules shares the gate for extension disapproval resolutions and procedural disapproval resolutions. Consultation also runs through the House Advisory Group on Negotiations and the Senate Advisory Group on Negotiations, and through designated congressional advisers accredited to negotiating teams. The two revenue committees receive the report on required changes to trade remedy laws 180 days before signing.

### Q: What must the President submit with a trade implementing bill?

An eligible submission is confined to a defined package. The implementing bill itself may contain only provisions approving the agreement and the statement of administrative action, plus provisions strictly necessary or appropriate to implement the agreement, with the word strictly added by the 2015 act. At least 30 days before the implementing materials are submitted, the President must provide Congress with the final legal text of the agreement and a draft statement of administrative action. The wider record includes a description of changes to existing law within 60 days after signing, advisory committee reports within 30 days after signing, and the International Trade Commission assessment due no later than 105 days after signing.

### Q: Does trade promotion authority apply to every trade agreement?

No. Expedited treatment attaches only to agreements that satisfy the conditions of a grant in force. The agreement must be entered into during the period covered by an authorizing statute, the President must satisfy the notification and consultation conditions attached to the grant, and the implementing bill must stay within the eligibility limits for its contents. Agreements reached during a lapse lack the guarantee, as the United States Jordan agreement showed: signed in 2000 and implemented by Public Law 107-43 in 2001, that agreement was approved without fast track during the 1994 to 2002 lapse. An agreement can be approved under ordinary rules, but ordinary rules restore amendment and extended debate.

### Q: How many trade agreements have been considered under fast track procedures?

The Congressional Research Service records the use of the procedures for 14 bilateral or regional free trade agreements plus the Tokyo and Uruguay Round multilateral packages. That attributed count measures the reach of successive grants rather than the reach of a single statute. Each agreement in the count depended on a grant in force for the negotiation in question, or on eligibility preserved through the tail of an expiring grant for agreements signed before a scheduled end. The count therefore belongs beside the lapse record: grants that expired in April 1994 and in July 2007 left intervals in which a negotiator could reach an agreement without being able to promise the amendment bar, the debate caps, or the discharge timetable.

### Q: What happens to signed agreements when the authority lapses?

Eligibility attaches to agreements entered into before the scheduled end of a grant, and implementing legislation for an eligible agreement can reach enactment after the end date has passed. The Peru implementing act was enacted on December 14, 2007 as Public Law 110-138, after the July 1, 2007 expiration of the extended 2002 grant. The Colombia, Panama, and South Korea agreements, signed in 2006 and 2007, were implemented on October 12, 2011 through Public Laws 112-41, 112-42, and 112-43. A lapse removes authority for new agreements outside the covered period. A lapse does not erase the eligibility that a timely signed agreement had already acquired.

### Q: When is the text of a trade agreement made public?

Under the 2015 act, the President must publish the text of a trade agreement on a publicly available website maintained by the United States Trade Representative at least 60 days before the President enters into the agreement, under section 106(a)(1)(B). Publication places the full text before any reader with internet access, and the 60 day interval gives committees, advisory bodies, journalists, scholars, and affected parties a window to read the language and compare the text against the objectives and against the specific objectives summary supplied before negotiations opened. Separately, the final legal text and a draft statement of administrative action go to Congress at least 30 days before the implementing materials are submitted.
