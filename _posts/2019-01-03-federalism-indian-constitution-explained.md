Federalism in the Indian Constitution was not a compromise the founders stumbled into while copying other constitutions. It was a deliberate design, built state by state, in which different units of the Union held different constitutional relationships with the Centre from the first day the text took effect. Jammu and Kashmir entered under a negotiated article that limited Parliament's reach. The tribal areas of Assam entered under a schedule that handed law-making power over land and custom to locally elected councils. The residuary power of legislation went to the Union, boundary revision went to Parliament, and yet the states received exclusive fields that the Centre could not enter in ordinary times. Any account that files this arrangement under a label and moves on misses the architecture. The asymmetry is the architecture.

## The Quasi-Federal Label and What It Flattens

K. C. Wheare, whose Federal Government reached its fourth edition in 1963, examined the Indian text and placed it outside the federal family proper. Wheare defined a federal constitution as one that divides power so that general and regional governments are each, within their own sphere, coordinate and independent. Measured against that definition, India failed the test on several counts: Parliament could redraw state boundaries, the Centre held residuary legislation, governors were appointed from Delhi, and emergency provisions let the Union govern a state directly. Wheare therefore called India quasi-federal, and he gave the classification its sharpest form by describing the Indian system as a unitary state with subsidiary federal features rather than a federal state with subsidiary unitary features.

The classification deserves its strongest statement before it is answered. Wheare was not careless, and the features he listed are in the text for any reader to verify. Articles 352 to 360 do permit the Union to displace state government in an emergency. Article 3 does allow Parliament to form new states and alter areas, boundaries and names by ordinary legislation. Article 248 does place residuary subjects with the Union rather than the states, reversing the choice the United States made in the Tenth Amendment. A reader who stops at Wheare's list knows real things about the Constitution. The weakness of the classification lies in what the list cannot register. A checklist of centralising features measures distance from one model, the American and Australian model of coordinate federalism, and records every departure as a deduction. Difference becomes deficiency by construction.

Textbook treatments inherited the flattening. The standard description, federal in form but unitary in spirit, repeats Wheare's conclusion without his argument, and it quietly teaches students to treat the state-specific provisions as exceptions cluttering an otherwise regular design. On that reading, Article 370 was an oddity awaiting correction, the Sixth Schedule a concession awaiting assimilation, and the lettered clauses of Article 371 a series of afterthoughts. The chronology alone refutes the teaching. Article 370 was debated and adopted in October 1949, before the Constitution commenced. The Sixth Schedule was debated in September 1949 as part of the original settlement for the tribal areas of Assam. These provisions were not barnacles on a finished hull. They were laid down with the keel.

M. P. Singh and Rekha Saxena, in Federalising India in the Age of Globalisation (2013), supplied the alternative reading this article defends. On their account, India is a multinational federation whose Constitution recognises that its units are not interchangeable, and variation between units is a federal technique rather than a federal failure. The claim defended across the sections that follow is stated plainly: Indian federalism is fully federal, and the asymmetry across states is the design, not the exception. Granville Austin, writing in The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation (1966), had already pointed part of the way there with his description of the Indian system as cooperative federalism, a working arrangement in which Centre and states bargained inside constitutional forms. The sections ahead test the claim against the founding record in sequence: the August 1947 redesign, the text of the Union, the three lists, the Jammu and Kashmir negotiation, the tribal schedule, the widening circle of special provisions, the boundary power, the money, and the institutions built to manage friction. Each step returns to the Constituent Assembly Debates, because the deliberateness of the design is a question about decisions, and decisions leave a record.

### Why did the founders reject a federation of the American kind?

The founders rejected the American model because the units of the Indian Union were not sovereign states contracting into a federation. The provinces of British India had been administrative creations of a unitary colonial state, and the princely states were never parties to a compact among equals, so a treaty theory of the Union misdescribed the founding facts.

The Assembly said so on the record. Introducing the Draft Constitution on 4 November 1948, B. R. Ambedkar told the Assembly that the Drafting Committee had used the word Union deliberately, because the Indian federation was not the result of an agreement by the states to join a federation, and because no state held a right to secede from it. The princely states acceded individually under Instruments of Accession negotiated in 1947. The founders therefore built a federation that a single people constituted at once, rather than a treaty that separate sovereignties signed.

## August 1947 and the Redesign of the Federal Scheme

The federal scheme the Assembly finally adopted was not the scheme it had been convened to write. The Cabinet Mission Plan of 16 May 1946 proposed a three-tier structure with a Union confined to defence, foreign affairs and communications, holding only the finances its subjects required, while residuary power rested with the provinces and provinces were free to form groups. The Constituent Assembly that met on 9 December 1946 began work inside that frame. Jawaharlal Nehru's Objectives Resolution, moved on 13 December 1946 and adopted on 22 January 1947, still spoke of autonomous units possessing residuary powers. The Union Constitution Committee and the Provincial Constitution Committee, both appointed in the spring of 1947, drafted against a background in which a weak centre was the agreed starting point, since a stronger one was unacceptable to the Muslim League.

Partition destroyed that starting point. The Mountbatten Plan of 3 June 1947 settled the division of British India, the Muslim League members from the departing territories left the Assembly, and the body that reconvened was reconstituted at 299 members. In the weeks that followed, the Union Constitution Committee under Nehru reconsidered its interim assumptions, and the debates recorded in Volume V of the Constituent Assembly Debates, conducted in August 1947, register the turn. The committee's revised thinking favoured a strong centre with residuary power, a single citizenship, and a Union list far wider than the Cabinet Mission's three subjects. Members who had treated provincial autonomy as the safeguard against majority domination began to argue that a divided subcontinent, absorbing millions of refugees and integrating more than five hundred princely states, could not be governed from a centre confined to three departments.

The redesign should be described precisely, because its precision carries the argument. The Assembly did not abandon federalism in August 1947. It abandoned one distribution inside federalism, the distribution that left the centre dependent on provincial goodwill, and substituted another distribution that the members then spent two further years elaborating. Provincial lists survived. State exclusivity survived. What changed was the default: where the Cabinet Mission scheme presumed the province and admitted the Union by exception, the revised scheme presumed a strong Union and protected the states by enumerating their fields. The debates of Volume V also show members insisting that the change responded to Partition's facts rather than to a taste for central power. K. M. Munshi and others argued that the experience of a collapsing centre in 1946 and 1947, with interim government paralysis between Congress and League ministers, had demonstrated the cost of a Union that could not act.

Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel's position inside this turn matters for everything that follows, and it is treated in the account of [the founding triangulation, with Patel's centralisation impulse and Ambedkar's principled federalism](/2018/12/17/patel-nehru-ambedkar-founding-triangulation/). Patel, who chaired the Provincial Constitution Committee and the Advisory Committee on minorities and tribal areas, approached the centre as an instrument of integration. As Deputy Prime Minister and Minister for States from August 1947, he and V. P. Menon negotiated the accessions that brought the princely states into the Union, and the man conducting that negotiation had daily evidence that uniformity was neither available nor required. Kashmir acceded on limited subjects. Hyderabad required a standstill agreement and then, in September 1948, a police action. The tribal areas of Assam required a schedule of their own. The strong centre of the August 1947 redesign was thus built by men whose other great project, integration, proceeded unit by unit on differing terms. Central strength and state variation were not rival commitments inside the founding. They were the same policy viewed from two ends.

### How did Partition change the federal scheme the Assembly had planned?

Partition changed the scheme by removing the political condition that had made a weak centre necessary. The Cabinet Mission's loose Union, with residuary power in the provinces, was designed to hold the Muslim League inside one state; once the Plan of 3 June 1947 divided the subcontinent and League members withdrew, that constraint vanished.

The centre the Mission had limited to defence, foreign affairs and communications was then rebuilt by the reconstituted Assembly: residuary power moved to the Union, the Union List widened, and a single citizenship replaced the grouped-province design, while enumerated state fields remained protected. The change is documented in the August 1947 debates in Volume V of the Debates.

## Article 1 and the Grammar of the Union

Article 1 performs the founding in a single sentence: India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States. Every word was weighed. When the Draft Constitution reached the Assembly in November 1948, Ambedkar explained the choice of Union over Federation in the speech of 4 November 1948, and his reasoning was functional rather than ceremonial. A federation, in the classical account the Drafting Committee had studied, arises from a compact among independent states. India's states had never been independent in the relevant sense, and the Union was therefore not a compact and could not be dissolved by any party to it. Ambedkar paired the point with its complement in the same speech: the Union is indestructible, but the states are not, since Parliament may refashion their boundaries under the procedure the Constitution itself supplies. The sentence that textbooks quote as a slogan was, in the debate, an argument about where constituent power sits.

The First Schedule's list of states, and the classification that originally divided states into Part A, Part B and Part C categories, already displayed variation inside the founding text. Part A states were the former governors' provinces. Part B states were unions of princely states, headed by a Rajpramukh rather than a governor, a distinction that carried the accession settlements into the constitutional order. Part C states were centrally administered territories. The Seventh Amendment of 1956 abolished the classification and reorganised the map, but the original threefold division is evidence about 1949: the founders were comfortable writing different grades of statehood into the document where the facts of accession differed. Symmetry would have required pretending that a former province and a union of princely states stood in identical positions. The founders declined to pretend.

Single citizenship reinforces the same grammar. The United States pairs federal citizenship with state citizenship; India, under Articles 5 to 11, knows one citizenship only, and Ambedkar defended the singleness as a deliberate instrument for building a common political identity across the Union. Domicile requirements for state employment survived in limited form under Article 16(3), which allows Parliament, and Parliament alone, to prescribe residence conditions for classes of public employment in a state. Even the exception is centrally gated. A reader assembling these clauses sees a pattern that recurs through the whole federal design: the Union fixes the frame within which variation is permitted, and the variation is real, but it runs downward from a single constituent act rather than upward from many.

It follows that [Tension Two, the federal-unitary settlement, that this article works out in detail](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) cannot be settled by counting central powers, as Wheare's method invites. The settlement the Assembly reached combined an indestructible Union, a modifiable map, one citizenship and a protected state sphere, and it distributed asymmetry through that combination on purpose. The Constituent Assembly's treatment of amendment confirms the deliberateness. Under Article 368, most amendments require special majorities in Parliament alone, but amendments touching the federal features listed in the proviso, including the Seventh Schedule lists, the representation of states in Parliament and Article 368 itself, additionally require ratification by at least half of the state legislatures. The founders who had made the states destructible in boundary also made the federal bargain partially entrenched against unilateral central revision. A purely unitary text does not entrench anything against the centre. This text did, at chosen points, which is what design means.

## The Seventh Schedule and the Division of Legislative Power

Articles 245 to 263 organise the legislative relations between Union and states, and the Seventh Schedule gives the organisation its content in three lists. The Union List originally carried 97 entries, including defence, foreign affairs, railways, banking, currency and inter-state trade, on which Parliament legislates exclusively. The State List originally carried 66 entries, including public order, police, public health, agriculture and land, on which state legislatures legislate exclusively in normal conditions. The Concurrent List originally carried 47 entries, including criminal law, marriage, contracts and economic planning in its early form, on which both levels may legislate subject to the repugnancy rule of Article 254. The counts matter because they quantify a judgment: the founders enumerated state exclusivity at length instead of leaving states to whatever the centre omitted. A residual federation, in which the units hold the leftovers, expresses suspicion of the centre. An enumerated federation of this kind expresses a settled allocation, debated entry by entry.

The debates on the lists repay attention for what they show about intention. Members pressed for the transfer of particular subjects in both directions. Proposals to move agriculture or education were argued on administrative knowledge: who actually governs a subject, a ministry in Delhi or a secretariat in a provincial capital that answers to cultivators and school boards? The Drafting Committee's allocations largely held, but the argumentative standard the Assembly applied is revealing. Speakers justified entries by the scale of the problem a subject presented, national uniformity where a market or a river system crossed boundaries, local knowledge where conditions varied by district. That standard is a federal standard. It presumes two genuine levels of government and asks which should act, rather than presuming one level and tolerating the other.

Later amendments shifted entries without displacing the architecture. The Forty-Second Amendment of 1976, enacted during the Emergency, moved five subjects from the State List to the Concurrent List: education, forests, weights and measures, protection of wild animals and birds, and administration of justice. The change is often narrated as the moment the federal design was betrayed, and the circumstances of 1976 give the narration force. Yet the mechanics of the change cut the other way as evidence about design. Altering the lists required the ratification procedure of the Article 368 proviso, with state legislatures participating, precisely because the founders had entrenched the lists as a federal feature. The 1976 transfers also left the three-list structure untouched and left police, land and public order in state hands, which is where the daily coercive and agrarian business of Indian government still sits. Centralisation within a federation is a real phenomenon, and the Emergency years supply instances enough. It does not convert the structure into a unitary one unless the state sphere becomes revocable at the centre's pleasure in normal times, and the text withholds that revocation.

Residuary power, assigned to Parliament by Article 248 and entry 97 of the Union List, is the clause Wheare's reading leans on hardest, and it deserves a direct answer. Ambedkar addressed the point in the Assembly by comparison: Canada, whose British North America Act of 1867 placed residuary power with the federal Parliament, remained a federation in every serious account, including Wheare's own treatment, which classed Canada as federal while noting its centralised features. The founders knew the Canadian precedent and chose it over the American, consciously, in the August 1947 climate. A design decision taken with the comparative record open on the table, defended in debate by the chairman of the Drafting Committee, whose work is examined in the account of [Ambedkar and the Drafting Committee](/2018/12/06/ambedkar-drafting-committee-constitution/), cannot be reclassified as an oversight. India took the Canadian allocation and then went beyond Canada in one respect that matters more: it wrote state-specific constitutional variation into the text, which Canada in 1867 had done for Quebec in civil law and language but which India generalised into a technique. The lists divide power by subject. The provisions examined next divide it by place, and place-based division is where the asymmetry becomes unmistakable.

## Article 370 and the October 1949 Negotiation

No provision tests the two readings of Indian federalism as directly as Article 370, and no provision is more often discussed without its founding document, the speech that introduced it. Maharaja Hari Singh signed the Instrument of Accession for Jammu and Kashmir on 26 October 1947, acceding on the three subjects of defence, external affairs and communications, and the Governor-General's acceptance letter of 27 October 1947 recorded the undertaking that the state's future would be settled by reference to its people once invasion had been repelled and order restored. Sheikh Abdullah became head of the emergency administration in October 1947 and Prime Minister of the state in March 1948. The constitutional relationship between Delhi and Srinagar was therefore a negotiation between governments long before it was a clause, and in 1949 the state's representatives in the Constituent Assembly, led by Sheikh Abdullah, pressed for an arrangement that preserved the accession's limited terms.

N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar, who had served as Prime Minister of Kashmir in the late 1930s and who handled the Kashmir file in Nehru's cabinet, moved the draft article, numbered 306A in the Draft Constitution, on 17 October 1949. The speech, recorded in Volume X of the Constituent Assembly Debates, is the under-cited primary source at the centre of this article's argument, and its reasoning is worth following step by step. Ayyangar told the Assembly that Jammu and Kashmir stood in a special position, that the state was not yet ripe for complete integration, that war conditions and the reference of the dispute to the United Nations made its circumstances abnormal, and that the government of the state, whose concurrence the draft required, was a popular government whose views carried weight. The draft article therefore limited Parliament's legislative power over the state to the accession subjects, permitted extension to other subjects only with the concurrence of the state government, and provided that the article itself could cease to operate on the recommendation of the state's own Constituent Assembly, conveyed through a presidential notification.

Read the speech against Wheare and the classification collapses at its strongest point. What Ayyangar presented, and what the Assembly adopted without a division, was not a unitary override dressed in federal language. It was the constitutionalisation of a negotiated accession, in which the unit's government held a concurrence power over the extension of central authority that no unitary constitution grants its provinces. The founders faced a choice the complication in the brief names directly: override the negotiation in the name of symmetry, or write the negotiation into the Constitution. They chose to write it in. Ayyangar's candour about the provision's interim character strengthens rather than weakens the design reading. The article's own machinery, including the role assigned to a Jammu and Kashmir Constituent Assembly that had yet to be elected, shows drafters calibrating a constitutional provision to a particular unit's situation and expected trajectory. Calibration to circumstance is design work, even when the circumstance is a negotiation.

Subsequent instruments elaborated the settlement on its own terms. The Delhi Agreement of July 1952 between Nehru and Sheikh Abdullah settled questions of citizenship, the head of state's election by the state legislature, and the state's flag alongside the Union flag. The Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order of 1954 extended much of the Indian Constitution to the state with modifications, again through the concurrence route the article prescribed, and the Jammu and Kashmir Constituent Assembly adopted a state constitution that came into force on 26 January 1957, declaring the state an integral part of India before that body dissolved. Each step is contestable as politics, and the politics is contested in the scholarship. For the narrower question this article adjudicates, what matters is the form the steps took: for seven decades, change in the relationship ran through instruments whose authority derived from the asymmetric provision itself. A unitary centre does not spend seventy years working through concurrence machinery it supposedly never granted.

### Why did Ayyangar describe Article 370 as an interim arrangement in October 1949?

Ayyangar called the arrangement interim because the conditions behind it were unfinished in October 1949: part of the state was under occupation after the invasion of October 1947, the dispute stood before the United Nations, a plebiscite commitment was on record, and the state's own Constituent Assembly had yet to be elected.

In his presentation to the Assembly on 17 October 1949, the interim label described the state's circumstances, not the sincerity of the grant. The draft article assigned the final recommendation on the provision's future to that state Constituent Assembly once elected. The concurrence machinery he defended was the operative constitution for the relationship while those circumstances lasted.

## The Sixth Schedule and the Tribal Areas Settlement

The Sixth Schedule carries the asymmetry argument into territory Wheare's checklist never visits. The Constituent Assembly's Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights, Minorities and Tribal and Excluded Areas, chaired by Patel, appointed a sub-committee under Gopinath Bordoloi, the Premier of Assam, to examine the hill districts of the province. The Bordoloi sub-committee toured the Naga Hills, the Lushai Hills, the Khasi and Jaintia Hills, the Garo Hills and the North Cachar Hills in 1947 and reported a consistent finding: the hill peoples feared domination by the plains, suspected that provincial ministries in Shillong and Guwahati would open their land to outside settlement and their markets to outside traders, and wanted institutions of their own through which land, forests, inheritance and custom would be governed. The Assembly debated the sub-committee's scheme in September 1949, with Bordoloi defending it against members who warned that autonomous councils would harden separation.

The scheme the Assembly adopted, in Articles 244(2) and the Sixth Schedule, created Autonomous District Councils and Regional Councils with legislative power over a listed domain: allotment and use of land other than reserved forests, management of forests outside reserves, shifting cultivation, village administration, inheritance of property, marriage and divorce, and social customs. Council laws required the Governor's assent. The councils received powers to levy specified taxes, to constitute village courts for suits between tribal parties, and to regulate money-lending and trade by non-tribals within the district. Jaipal Singh Munda, the Adivasi member from Bihar, supported the arrangements in debate while pressing their extension, and speakers from the hill districts themselves, including the Khasi leader Rev. J. J. M. Nichols-Roy, treated the schedule as the condition on which the hills would enter the Indian Union with confidence.

Consider what the schedule does to the quasi-federal classification. Inside a single state of the Union, Assam as originally constituted, the Constitution erected a third order of law-making authority, elected locally, with a protected domain into which even the state legislature's enactments did not automatically extend: Acts of Parliament and of the state legislature applied to the autonomous districts only subject to the schedule's own application machinery and exceptions. The vertical division of power in India therefore never ran along one axis with two levels. From 1950 it ran along several, and the number of levels varied by territory. Wheare's definition, built for two-level systems, has no vocabulary for a district council armed with legislative competence over inheritance and land. Singh and Saxena's reading has the vocabulary ready, because it expects the federation to recognise distinct peoples distinctly. The Sixth Schedule is asymmetric federalism at its most literal: same Union, same state, different constitutional order for a named population, adopted after a fact-finding tour and a floor debate, in the founding session itself.

Reorganisation extended the schedule's reach. The schedule originally applied to districts within Assam; after the North-Eastern Areas (Reorganisation) Act of 1971 and the statehood settlements that followed, it came to cover tribal areas in four states, Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram, with councils including the Khasi Hills, Garo Hills, Jaintia Hills, Chakma, Lai and Mara councils, and the Tripura Tribal Areas council. The Bodoland Territorial Council, created by the Memorandum of Settlement of February 2003 and brought under the schedule by amendment that year, added a council for the Bodo areas of Assam with an expanded subject list. Each extension repeats the founding gesture: a negotiated settlement with a distinct people, written into the schedule rather than imposed as uniformity. Critics of the councils, including state governments that have resisted their fiscal claims and scholars who document uneven administrative capacity, dispute their performance. The performance question is real and separate. As evidence about the constitutional design, the schedule and its extensions show a federation whose standard response to internal difference is differentiated constitutional status, negotiated case by case.

### Why were the Sixth Schedule councils given law-making power over land and custom?

The councils received legislative power because the Bordoloi sub-committee's 1947 tour of the Assam hill districts found that land and custom were the matters on which the hill peoples feared plains domination most: alienation of tribal land, outside control of forests and shifting cultivation, and displacement of customary inheritance, marriage and village authority.

Handing those subjects to elected district councils, whose laws required the Governor's assent, was the Assembly's September 1949 answer to that documented fear, reached after the sub-committee reported the hill districts' suspicion that provincial ministries would open their land to outside settlement and their markets to outside traders. The grant converted a protective promise into an institution the hill districts governed themselves.

## Article 371 and the Widening Circle of Special Provisions

Article 370 and the Sixth Schedule were the founding instances of a technique the republic kept using, and Article 371 with its lettered descendants is where the technique became a standing feature of the text. The original Article 371, in the Constitution as enacted and then recast by the Seventh Amendment of 1956 and the Bombay Reorganisation Act of 1960, addressed Maharashtra and Gujarat, empowering the President to provide for separate development boards for Vidarbha, Marathwada and the rest of Maharashtra, and for Saurashtra, Kutch and the rest of Gujarat, with equitable allocation of development funds and facilities for technical education and employment. The problem addressed was intra-state imbalance after the bilingual Bombay State was divided on 1 May 1960: regions inside a single linguistic state stood in unequal positions, and the Constitution, rather than the ordinary budget process alone, carried the equalisation commitment.

The lettered provisions then accumulated, each tied to a named settlement. Article 371A, inserted by the Thirteenth Amendment of 1962 as Nagaland became a state on 1 December 1963, bars Parliament's Acts on Naga religious and social practices, Naga customary law and procedure, civil and criminal justice under customary law, and ownership and transfer of land and its resources, unless the Nagaland Legislative Assembly resolves otherwise; the same article gave the Governor a special responsibility for law and order while internal disturbance continued. The provision constitutionalised the Sixteen Point Agreement of July 1960 between the Government of India and the Naga People's Convention. Article 371B provided an assembly committee for the tribal areas of Assam. Article 371C, added in 1971, provided a Hill Areas Committee of the Manipur assembly and a gubernatorial reporting duty. Article 371D, added in 1973 during the Andhra agitation over the Mulki rules, empowered presidential orders securing equitable employment and education across regions of Andhra Pradesh, the seed of the zonal system. Article 371F accompanied Sikkim's admission as a state in 1975 under the Thirty-Sixth Amendment, protecting laws in force and reserving assembly seats, including the Sangha seat. Article 371G for Mizoram in 1986 replicated the Nagaland pattern for Mizo customary law following the Mizo accord of 30 June 1986. Article 371H gave Arunachal Pradesh's Governor a special responsibility for law and order in 1986, Article 371I addressed Goa's assembly size in 1987, and Article 371J, added in 2012, extended the development-board pattern to the Hyderabad-Karnataka region after decades of regional demand.

A reader can see why the exception framing fails as description. A technique used at the founding for Kashmir and the Assam hills, extended by amendment for Nagaland, Manipur, Andhra Pradesh, Sikkim, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh, Goa and Karnataka across five decades, under governments of different parties, is not a set of exceptions to the federal design. It is one of the design's principal instruments, the means by which the Union admits units and regions on differentiated terms and keeps faith with negotiated settlements. The amendments were passed through the Article 368 procedure, several with the state ratification the proviso requires, which means the federal centre and the states jointly operated the machinery. The table below, labelled the State-by-State Asymmetry Table, gathers the principal instances with their founding or settlement citations so that the pattern can be inspected at a glance and cited by name.

## Article 3 and the Power to Remake the Map

Article 3 authorises Parliament, by law, to form a new state by separation of territory or by uniting states or parts of states, to increase or diminish any state's area, and to alter boundaries or names. The procedure requires the President's recommendation for the bill and, where a state's area, boundaries or name are affected, reference of the bill to that state's legislature for its views within a period the President specifies. The views are solicited, not obeyed. In Babulal Parate v State of Bombay (1960), the Supreme Court upheld the Bombay Reorganisation Act against the argument that the reference procedure had been defeated by subsequent alterations, confirming that Parliament may proceed on the views expressed without being bound by them and may amend the bill thereafter. Ambedkar's indestructible-Union reasoning of November 1948 is the doctrine behind the clause: the Union's territory is one field for constituent action, and internal boundaries are administrative conveniences within it, revisable by the national legislature.

The clause has been used on a scale no other federation attempts. The States Reorganisation Act of 1956 redrew the map on linguistic lines following the report of the States Reorganisation Commission under Fazl Ali, submitted in September 1955. Bombay was divided into Maharashtra and Gujarat in 1960. Punjab was divided in 1966, creating Haryana and transferring hill areas to Himachal Pradesh. The north-eastern states were carved from Assam between 1963 and 1987. Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand and Jharkhand were created in November 2000 with the consent of their parent assemblies. The Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, passed in February 2014, created Telangana with effect from the appointed day of 2 June 2014, although the Andhra Pradesh Legislative Assembly had returned the bill with a resolution rejecting it in January 2014: the reference procedure ran, the views were adverse, and Parliament legislated regardless, exactly as Article 3 permits. The 1956 settlement is treated fully in this series' later account of linguistic reorganisation as constitutional politics, which takes the story of the Fazl Ali Commission and its aftermath as its subject.

Wheare's tradition counts Article 3 among the unitary features, and a state whose boundaries another body may redraw without its consent does lack a guarantee that federations classically supply. The honest adjudication concedes the fact and disputes the inference. Boundary guarantees protect units against dismemberment by a hostile centre; the Indian record shows Article 3 used overwhelmingly to accommodate demands arising inside the affected populations, for linguistic states in the 1950s, for tribal statehood in the north-east, for Telangana after a movement that had run, in its second phase, since the late 1960s and with force after 2001. A boundary of the power deserves marking, because the Supreme Court drew it within the republic's first decade. In the Berubari reference, In re Berubari Union and Exchange of Enclaves, decided in March 1960, the Court advised that Article 3 governs the internal reorganisation of states and does not extend to ceding Indian territory to a foreign state; cession required a constitutional amendment under Article 368, and Parliament accordingly enacted the Ninth Amendment of 1960 to implement the 1958 agreement with Pakistan. The distinction is exact and instructive. Inside the Union, boundaries are administrative and revisable by ordinary law, because the states are units of one constituent whole. At the Union's edge, the constituent power itself must act. The same power that could override a state has repeatedly served to register a region's claim against its own state government, as in 2014, when the affected population of Telangana sought separation from a state whose assembly refused it. A federation in which regions can appeal past their state capitals to the national legislature possesses a safety valve that symmetric federations lack, and the valve has operated as federalism's servant in the cases the record supplies. The design judgment of 1948, that internal boundaries should not harden into sovereignties, is what makes the valve possible.

## Money: Finance, Devolution and the Fiscal Terms of Asymmetry

Political federalism without fiscal machinery is a map without roads, and the founders knew it; Articles 268 to 293 build the fiscal constitution, and its terms are asymmetric by construction. Article 268 assigns duties levied by the Union but collected and retained by the states. Article 269 assigns taxes levied and collected by the Union but assigned wholly to the states. Article 270 shares the proceeds of Union taxes between the levels. Article 271 reserves surcharges to the Union alone. Article 275 provides grants-in-aid from the Consolidated Fund of India to states in need of assistance, with particular provision for the welfare of Scheduled Tribes and the administration of Scheduled Areas, a clause that differentiates among states by need on the face of the fiscal text. Article 282 permits grants for any public purpose by either level, the elastic provision through which much discretionary transfer later flowed. A fiscal constitution that wished to treat states uniformly could have stopped at a sharing formula. This one layers shared taxes, assigned taxes, need-based grants and a standing commission on top of one another.

The commission is the Finance Commission of Article 280, constituted by the President every five years or earlier, charged with recommending the distribution of tax proceeds and the principles of grants-in-aid. The first Finance Commission, chaired by K. C. Neogy, was appointed in November 1951 and reported in 1952, beginning an institutional rhythm that has repeated fifteen times by the period this article covers. Devolution recommendations, which moved from the First Commission's share arrangements through the Fourteenth Finance Commission's award of 42 percent of the divisible pool to the states for 2015 to 2020, are recommendations to the President, tabled in Parliament with an explanatory memorandum under Article 281. The arrangement deliberately places the vertical division of money beyond the annual reach of either level's politics, in a body whose members are appointed for the task. Students assembling the devolution record for themselves, often from the commission reports and state budget documents circulated as spreadsheets, can open and inspect those files with the [office file viewer for Excel, Word and PowerPoint documents](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), which reads the formats in which the awards and their tables travel.

Onto this constitutional machinery, practice layered a distinctly asymmetric fiscal status. In 1969, applying criteria associated with the formula work of D. R. Gadgil and adopted by the National Development Council, the Union began designating special category states, initially Assam, Nagaland and Jammu and Kashmir: states marked by hilly terrain, low population density, strategic location, economic and infrastructural backwardness and non-viable state finances. Special category status carried concrete fiscal consequences, including a 90 to 10 grant-to-loan ratio for central assistance where ordinary states received 70 to 30, priority in the allocation of plan funds, and concessions on excise and income tax intended to draw industry to difficult terrain. The category grew to eleven states, concentrated in the north-east and the Himalayan belt. Whatever verdict a reader reaches on the category's economics, its constitutional significance for this article is plain: for five decades the Indian fiscal system formally sorted states into classes with different transfer terms, by criteria of geography and capacity, and the sorting was defended publicly as federal fairness rather than apologised for as federal deviation. The Fourteenth Finance Commission's higher general devolution was accompanied by the Union's position, stated in 2015, that special category status would not be extended to new claimants, and the argument between Andhra Pradesh, which claimed the status on the strength of assurances given during the 2014 bifurcation, and the Union government ran precisely over whether a differentiated fiscal promise made at a state's creation bound the centre thereafter. The argument's very terms presuppose the asymmetric design.

Borrowing completes the fiscal picture, and it tilts the other way. Article 292 empowers the Union to borrow on the security of the Consolidated Fund of India within limits Parliament fixes. Article 293 empowers a state to borrow within India on the security of its own consolidated fund within limits its legislature fixes, but a state may not raise a loan without the Union's consent while any part of a previous Union loan to it, or a loan the Union has guaranteed, remains outstanding, and the Union may attach conditions to its consent. In practice every state carries Union loans from the plan era, so the consent requirement touches the whole map. Fiscal federalism in India is therefore not a general tilt but a clause-by-clause allocation: devolution insulated in a commission, grants differentiated by need, borrowing gated by the centre. A reader who totals the clauses into a single score has missed what the drafters did, which was to decide each fiscal question separately on its administrative merits.

### How does the Finance Commission divide money between the Centre and the states?

The Finance Commission, appointed by the President under Article 280 every five years, recommends the states' collective share of Union tax proceeds and the formula dividing that share among states, plus the principles for grants-in-aid under Article 275. Its awards are recommendations laid before Parliament, and in practice they govern the award period.

The commission's award is laid before Parliament under Article 281 with a memorandum of the action taken on it. The Fourteenth Commission, reporting in December 2014, raised the states' share of the divisible pool to 42 percent for the years from 2015 to 2020. The criteria it applies, population, area, income distance, forest cover and demographic performance among them, weight states unequally by need and capacity, so the division among states is asymmetric by formula as well as by total.

## The Inter-State Council, the Sarkaria Commission and Managed Friction

A federation of differentiated units needs machinery for the friction differentiation guarantees, and the Constitution foresaw it. Article 263 empowers the President, where public interest appears served, to establish an Inter-State Council charged with inquiring into and advising on disputes between states, investigating subjects of common interest to the Union and the states or among states, and recommending better coordination of policy and action. The power slept for four decades. It was the Sarkaria Commission, appointed by the Union government in June 1983 under Justice R. S. Sarkaria, formerly of the Supreme Court, and reporting in January 1988, that pressed the case for waking it: the commission, appointed after years of complaint by opposition-ruled state governments about gubernatorial conduct, Article 356 and fiscal dependence, recommended a permanent Inter-State Council as a standing consultative forum, alongside restraints on the misuse of President's Rule and on the appointment of governors from active politics.

The council was established by presidential order on 28 May 1990, during the prime ministership of V. P. Singh, whose National Front government had campaigned on federal grievances. Its record as a deliberative body has been intermittent, with long gaps between meetings and a standing committee of ministers doing preparatory work from 1996 onward, and the Punchhi Commission on Centre-State Relations, appointed in April 2007 under Justice Madan Mohan Punchhi and reporting in March 2010, returned to the same file with recommendations to regularise the council's meetings and to localise emergency provisions so that Article 356 need not displace an entire state government to remedy a local breakdown. The institutional history carries a lesson the design reading predicts. The machinery of Article 263 is consultative, not adjudicative: it presumes that friction between the Union and differentiated units is permanent, political and to be managed by a forum in which the units sit with the centre, rather than eliminated by a rule that pretends the units are identical. Sarkaria's restraint on Article 356 points the same way. In S. R. Bommai v Union of India, decided on 11 March 1994, a nine-judge bench of the Supreme Court held that a proclamation under Article 356 is subject to judicial review, that a ministry's majority is to be tested on the floor of the assembly, and that a state government pursuing policies contrary to secularism, which the Court held to be part of the basic structure, could constitutionally be dismissed, with the corollary that proclamations failing review could restore the dismissed government. The frequency of Article 356 impositions, which had run above one hundred by the mid-1990s across the republic's history, fell sharply in the decades after Bommai. The federation's corrective came through its own institutions applying its own text, which is the behaviour of a working federal order rather than of a unitary state borrowing federal dress.

## Wheare Against the Asymmetric Reading: The Adjudication

The disagreement can be decided on the evidence assembled. Wheare's case, at its strongest, rests on four pillars: residuary power with the Union, boundary revision without state consent, appointed governors armed with reservation powers under Articles 200 and 201, and emergency displacement under Article 356. Each pillar is a fact of the text. The asymmetric reading, as Singh and Saxena develop it and as Austin's cooperative federalism anticipates, answers that the same text entrenches the state lists behind the ratification proviso of Article 368, enumerates exclusive state fields across 66 original entries, constitutionalises negotiated terms for particular units in Articles 370 and 371A and their kin, erects a third order of government in the Sixth Schedule, and distributes money through a commission whose awards differentiate by need. Both sides quote the Constitution accurately. The adjudication therefore turns on a prior question: what does a classification of constitutions owe the systems it classifies?

Wheare's definition was drawn from the federations he knew best, the United States, Canada, Australia and Switzerland, and it treats one historical family as the genus. On that method, every Indian feature departing from the family is scored as unitary residue, including features the founders adopted for reasons internal to India's circumstances and defended in debate as federal choices: the Canadian allocation of residuum, the negotiated accession terms, the tribal councils recommended by a fact-finding sub-committee. The method cannot distinguish a central power seized from the units from a central power the constituent body deliberately assigned while simultaneously entrenching the units' remaining sphere, and the distinction is the one that matters for understanding how the system behaves. It behaves, as the record of linguistic reorganisation, of Bommai, of the Finance Commissions and of the 2014 bifurcation shows, as a federation whose units bargain, litigate and mobilise inside constitutional forms, and whose centre's supremacy operates at specified points rather than at pleasure.

One further piece of evidence weighs in the adjudication, and it comes from Austin himself. Austin's 1966 study described the Constitution as a seamless web, its national unity, democracy and social revolution strands woven so that pulling one disturbed the others, and he treated the federal provisions as part of the weave rather than as a concession outside it. His cooperative federalism was not a compliment paid to administrative habit; it was a reading of the founding intention, formed from the Debates and from interviews with surviving framers, that the Assembly expected Centre and states to govern jointly because the subjects of government refused separation. Where Wheare classified from a definition, Austin classified from the record of the body that wrote the text. On a question of what the founders designed, the method that begins from the founders' own proceedings carries the greater authority, and the proceedings, from August 1947 through October 1949, show allocation and differentiation chosen clause by clause.

The verdict of this article follows the namable claim with which it began. Indian federalism is fully federal; the asymmetry across states is the design, not the exception. Austin's cooperative federalism names the working style of the system, the bargaining between levels that runs through Finance Commission consultations, Inter-State Council agendas and accord politics. Singh and Saxena name its structure correctly, a federation that differentiates among units as a technique of union. Wheare's quasi-federal label survives as a warning against one error, the error of assuming India replicates the American allocation, and it should be taught in exactly that capacity. As a classification of the whole, it fails on the founding evidence: a Constituent Assembly that debated its federal choices for three years, that constitutionalised the Kashmir negotiation in October 1949 and the tribal settlement in September 1949, and that entrenched the lists against unilateral revision did not produce a half-federation by accident. It produced a different kind of federation on purpose.

## Renegotiations Inside the Design: 2014, 2016 and 2019

A design is known by what it makes possible, and the three great federal renegotiations of the decade in which this series is published each ran on rails the founders laid. The Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act of February 2014 executed a bifurcation through Article 3 over the parent assembly's recorded objection, and the terms of the settlement, a shared capital at Hyderabad for a period not exceeding ten years, the apportionment of assets and water disputes machinery, were themselves asymmetric, written for one pair of successor states. The Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, assented to on 8 September 2016 and operative for the goods and services tax from 1 July 2017, inserted Article 246A, giving Parliament and state legislatures simultaneous power to tax supplies, and Article 279A, constituting the Goods and Services Tax Council, chaired by the Union Finance Minister, in which the Union's vote carries one-third weight, the states together two-thirds, and decisions require a three-fourths majority of weighted votes. The GST Council is the asymmetric design expressed as a voting formula: neither level can tax the national market alone, the states outvote the centre collectively, and the centre holds a blocking share. Ratification by more than half the state legislatures preceded the amendment's assent, the Article 368 proviso operating as designed.

The Jammu and Kashmir renegotiation of August 2019 used the asymmetric provision's own machinery for its transformation. Constitution Order 272 of 5 August 2019, issued by President Ram Nath Kovind under Article 370(1) with the concurrence of the state government, then administered by the Governor under a proclamation of President's Rule in force since December 2018, applied the entire Constitution to the state and amended the interpretation clause so that the state's Constituent Assembly, in the article's clause 3 proviso, was read as its Legislative Assembly, and the state government as the Governor. The Rajya Sabha adopted the statutory resolution recommending action under clause 3 on 5 August 2019 and the Lok Sabha on 6 August 2019, whereupon Constitution Order 273 of 6 August 2019 declared Article 370 inoperative save as modified. The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, passed in the same week, divided the state into the Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir, with a legislature, and the Union Territory of Ladakh, without one, with effect from 31 October 2019. Supporters of the government's action, including Home Minister Amit Shah in the parliamentary debates of 5 and 6 August 2019, presented the step as the completion of integration on uniform terms; opponents, including the state's former chief ministers and constitutional scholars who challenged the orders in the Supreme Court, presented it as the use of a concurrence provision against the people whose consent the provision existed to protect. Both characterisations are attributed here because the adjudication of their merits belongs to the Court and to a later article in this series devoted to the abrogation. What falls within this article's claim is narrower and demonstrable: the most contested federal act of the decade was executed entirely through instruments the founding text supplied, and the arguments on both sides were arguments about the meaning of the asymmetric settlement of October 1949. The design did not prevent its own renegotiation. It structured it, as the founding's Tension Two settlement has structured every renegotiation since.

## The Fifth Schedule and the Second Tribal Settlement

The Sixth Schedule governed the hill districts of the north-east. For the tribal populations of central and peninsular India, the founders built a different instrument, the Fifth Schedule under Article 244(1), and the difference between the two schedules is itself a lesson in the asymmetric method. The Fifth Schedule applies to Scheduled Areas declared by the President in states including Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha and Rajasthan. It creates no elected legislature. Instead it arms the Governor with protective powers: the Governor may direct that an Act of Parliament or of the state legislature shall not apply to a Scheduled Area, or shall apply with exceptions and modifications, and may make regulations for the peace and good government of the area, in particular to prohibit or restrict the transfer of land by or among members of the Scheduled Tribes, to regulate allotment of land to them, and to regulate money-lending to them. A Tribes Advisory Council, composed of representatives of the Scheduled Tribes in the state assembly, advises the Governor on matters referred to it, and the Governor reports annually to the President on the administration of the Scheduled Areas.

Two tribal settlements, two different instruments, chosen for two different situations. The Bordoloi sub-committee had found hill peoples with strong traditions of self-governing village and district institutions and a fear of the plains, and the answer was elected councils with legislative power. The Dhebar Commission of 1960 to 1961, appointed under Article 339 to report on the administration of the Scheduled Areas and the welfare of the Scheduled Tribes, worked within a Fifth Schedule frame in which the tribal populations lived intermingled with other communities inside ordinary districts, and the protective problem ran through land records, credit and the district administration rather than through separate councils. U. N. Dhebar's commission documented land alienation continuing despite protective laws, and its recommendations pressed enforcement and administrative vigilance rather than new legislatures. A constitutional order committed to uniformity would have picked one tribal policy. The Indian order picked two, and then defended each by reference to the population it served. The pattern generalises the claim of this article beyond its two best-known instances: differentiation was the founders' standard response to documented difference, in the hills by legislation of their own, in the plains districts by gubernatorial protection and advisory councils.

The later history kept the instrument in use. The Bhuria Committee of 1995, chaired by Dilip Singh Bhuria, examined the extension of self-government provisions to the Scheduled Areas, and the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act of 1996, PESA, extended the panchayat framework of Part IX to the Fifth Schedule areas with modifications that vest the gram sabha with authority over village markets, money-lending, minor forest produce and the approval of development plans, and require consultation before land acquisition. PESA is an Act of Parliament, not an amendment, but its method is the schedule's method carried forward: the general institution, the panchayat, is applied to the Scheduled Areas in a modified form that reserves the decisive powers to the tribal village assembly. Administrators and activists dispute how far PESA's promises have been honoured in the mining districts of Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand and Odisha. The dispute presupposes the architecture. Nobody on either side proposes that the Scheduled Areas be governed by the unmodified general law, because the Constitution settled in 1949 that they would not be.

## One Judiciary, One Citizenship, Shared Services: The Common Infrastructure

Wheare's list of unitary features usually includes three institutions that sit beneath the legislative division entirely, and each deserves examination because each was chosen with its federal cost in view. The first is the integrated judiciary. India has a single hierarchy of courts: the Supreme Court under Articles 124 to 147, the High Courts for the states under Articles 214 to 231, and the subordinate judiciary beneath them, with appeals running to one apex. The United States maintains separate federal and state court systems. The founders, working in a country where the Government of India Act of 1935 had already integrated the higher judiciary, retained the single pyramid so that the Constitution would receive one authoritative interpretation across all units, a choice that later made the basic structure doctrine of Kesavananda Bharati (1973) enforceable as a limit on every legislature including Parliament itself. A single judiciary constrains the states, and it constrains the Union with the same hand, which is not the profile of a unitary convenience.

The second institution is the All-India Services. Article 312, examined in the questions that close this article, lets Parliament create services common to both levels on a Rajya Sabha resolution passed by a two-thirds majority. The Indian Administrative Service and the Indian Police Service, successors to the services Sardar Patel defended before the Constituent Assembly in October 1949 as the steel frame of the new state, officer the districts and secretariats of every state while remaining services whose cadre control the Union shares. State governments have periodically resented the arrangement, and the Sarkaria Commission heard the resentment at length between 1983 and 1988 before recommending that the services be retained and strengthened. The founders' reasoning is on the record in Patel's intervention: a country integrating princely states and building administrations in provinces with thin senior cadres needed a service whose members could serve anywhere and whose loyalties ran to the Constitution rather than to a provincial ministry. The service is a unitary feature that exists to make the federal units governable, and the Rajya Sabha threshold means the states, voting in their council, hold the key to any new service of the kind.

The third institution is the common machinery of elections and audit. The Election Commission of Article 324 conducts elections to Parliament and to every state legislature through one authority, a design Sukumar Sen operated for the general election of 1951 to 1952 across both levels simultaneously. The Comptroller and Auditor General of Article 148 audits the accounts of the Union and of every state. Article 315 creates a Union Public Service Commission and state commissions, with provision for joint commissions where states request them. In each case the founders chose one instrument for both levels, and in each case the alternative of separate state machinery was available and familiar from the American example. The pattern across judiciary, services, elections and audit is consistent with the rest of the design: where a function defines the common citizenship of the Union, the founders built it once and built it for all; where a function touches land, custom, language and the texture of local life, they divided it, and where a population stood in a distinct position, they divided it unevenly. Uniformity and variation in the Indian text are not in tension. They are allocated, function by function, which is what a settlement is.

## Emergency Powers and Article 356 in the Federal Balance

The emergency provisions are the strongest card in the unitary hand, and a defence of the federal reading that dodges them defends nothing. Article 352 permits a proclamation of emergency on grounds of war, external aggression or armed rebellion, whereupon Parliament may legislate on the State List and the Union executive's directions bind the states. Article 356 permits the President, on the Governor's report or otherwise, to assume the functions of a state government where the government of the state cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution. Article 360 provides for a financial emergency and has never been proclaimed. During the emergency proclaimed in June 1975 on the ground of internal disturbance, the federal system operated in a suspended condition for nineteen months, and the Forty-Second Amendment of 1976 used the suspension to move subjects between the lists. Any honest account begins there: the text hands the Union a legal route to displacing the states, and a Union government once took it.

Three considerations discipline the inference Wheare's tradition draws from that card. The first is textual: emergency displacement under Articles 352 and 356 is conditional, temporally limited by parliamentary approval requirements, and, since the Forty-Fourth Amendment of 1978, fenced by safeguards the Janata government wrote from the experience of 1975, including the replacement of internal disturbance by armed rebellion as a ground and the requirement of written cabinet advice for a proclamation. A power drafted as an exception, debated as an exception in the Constituent Assembly, where Ambedkar told members on the discussion of the draft emergency articles that he hoped the provisions would remain a dead letter, is evidence about crisis government rather than about the ordinary constitution. Federations are classified by their ordinary working constitutions, and in ordinary times the Union cannot legislate on police, land or public health.

The second consideration is judicial. The Bommai judgment of 11 March 1994 converted Article 356 from a political weapon of first resort into a reviewed and increasingly rare instrument, and the Court in Rameshwar Prasad v Union of India (2006) struck down the dissolution of the Bihar assembly as unconstitutional even while declining, on the facts of an intervening election, to restore it. The third consideration is comparative candour: the German Basic Law contains federal execution against a Land, and Canada's constitution long carried disallowance and reservation powers that Ottawa used against provincial statutes into the twentieth century, without either federation forfeiting the name. The emergency card therefore shows that the Indian federation was designed by men who had watched a state fail to hold together in 1947 and who priced that memory into the text. It does not show that the federation is a facade. A facade does not generate a nine-judge bench decision restraining the centre's favourite instrument, delivered by the centre's own court, and obeyed.

## Federalism in the Indian Constitution in Practice: Planning and Bargaining

Between the constitutional text and the lived federation stands a layer of institutions the text did not create, and their behaviour confirms Austin's description of the system as cooperative bargaining. The Planning Commission, established by a cabinet resolution of 15 March 1950 with the Prime Minister as chair, allocated plan investment across the states for six decades through a process of negotiation between the Union and each state government over the size and composition of state plans. The National Development Council, created in August 1952 and composed of the Prime Minister, Union ministers and every chief minister, approved the plans the commission prepared, giving the states a collective forum at the apex of economic decision-making that the Constitution nowhere mentions. The Gadgil formula, adopted by the council in 1969 for distributing central plan assistance among states by weighted criteria of population, per capita income and other factors, regularised the bargaining into an allocation rule that states could predict and contest on stated grounds. None of this machinery appears in the Seventh Schedule's world, yet the federal system's centre of gravity sat inside it for two generations, and it worked by negotiation between levels rather than by command.

The Zonal Councils supply the second instance. The States Reorganisation Act of 1956 established five Zonal Councils, for the northern, central, eastern, western and southern zones, each chaired by the Union Home Minister and bringing the chief ministers of the member states together as an advisory body for matters of common regional interest, from boundary disputes to inter-state transport. A separate North Eastern Council followed under its own Act of 1971. The councils have no binding authority and meet irregularly, and their significance is diagnostic rather than decisive: confronted in 1956 with a map redrawn by language, the republic built standing forums in which neighbouring states bargain horizontally, with the Union in the chair but without a vote that settles anything. A unitary state settles inter-provincial questions by instruction from the capital. India built rooms for argument, and the habit of building such rooms, repeated in the Inter-State Council of 1990 and the GST Council of 2016, is Austin's cooperative federalism as institutional practice.

The bargaining record also supplies the asymmetric reading with its hardest test, because bargaining between unequals can mask command. State governments of the 1970s and 1980s, particularly those ruled by parties opposed to the Union government, charged that plan transfers, discretionary grants under Article 282 and the licensing system of the planned economy converted formal federalism into dependence, and the commissions of inquiry the charge produced, from the Rajamannar Committee appointed by the Tamil Nadu government in 1969 to the Sarkaria Commission of 1983, took the complaint seriously enough to document it. The Rajamannar Committee's report of 1971 recommended deleting Articles 256, 257 and 365, the direction-issuing provisions, and curtailing Article 356. Sarkaria, twelve years later, heard the same grievances and recommended discipline rather than deletion. The adjudication this article offers is that the grievances identify abuses of a bargaining system, not the absence of one: states that could document, publish and politically prosecute their complaints through commissions of their own appointment were operating as constitutional actors inside a federation, and the eventual corrections, Gadgil weights, Finance Commission devolution rising to 42 percent under the Fourteenth Commission, and the GST Council's weighted votes, all moved by reallocating bargaining power rather than by abolishing the bargain.

## Union Territories and the National Capital: Degrees of Self-Government

At the edge of the state system, the Union Territories display the design's gradations in their purest form, since here the Constitution distributes self-government in measured degrees rather than in the two conditions of statehood and subjection. Article 239 places the territories under the President acting through an administrator. Article 239A, inserted in 1963, empowered Parliament to create legislatures and councils of ministers for specified territories, the route by which Puducherry received its assembly. The Government of National Capital Territory of Delhi Act of 1991, following the Sixty-Ninth Amendment's insertion of Article 239AA, gave Delhi an assembly and a council of ministers responsible to it, while reserving public order, police and land to the Union, a three-way split of the State List inside one city that no symmetric template generates. Chandigarh serves as joint capital of Punjab and Haryana while administered by the Union. The Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Lakshadweep and Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu have been administered without legislatures. Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh joined the list in October 2019 at two different degrees, the first with a legislature and the second without.

Textbooks often file the territories as a residual administrative category, and for the smallest island territories that is fair. The category's analytical weight lies at its upper end, where the graduated statuses encode political settlements as precisely as Article 371 does. Delhi's arrangement records a judgment, contested in litigation between the elected government of the National Capital Territory and the Lieutenant Governor and addressed by the Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court in Government of NCT of Delhi v Union of India on 4 July 2018, that the capital of the Union requires both a democratic mandate for its residents and a Union reserve over the functions on which the national government's own seat depends. Puducherry's assembly records the terms on which a former French establishment, transferred under the treaty of 1956 and merged by the Fourteenth Amendment of 1962, entered the Union. The territories therefore complete the argument of this article. Between the full statehood of a Maharashtra and the direct administration of an island territory, the Indian Union operates a continuous scale of self-government, calibrated territory by territory, and the calibration is constitutional, deliberate and old. It is the founding method still at work: fit the terms to the place.

## Rivers, Language and the Disputes the Design Expected

Some subjects refuse to respect any list, and the founders' handling of inter-state rivers shows the design anticipating its own hardest cases. Entry 56 of the Union List empowers Parliament to declare that the regulation and development of inter-state rivers and river valleys by the Union is expedient in the public interest, and Article 262 empowers Parliament to provide for the adjudication of disputes over inter-state waters and to bar the jurisdiction of every court in such disputes. Parliament used the power in the Inter-State Water Disputes Act of 1956, creating tribunals for disputes referred by the Union government. The Krishna, Godavari, Narmada, Ravi-Beas and Cauvery disputes each ran through that machinery, the Cauvery tribunal delivering its final award in February 2007 after constituting in 1990, and the Supreme Court modifying the award's allocations in its judgment of February 2018 under its appellate authority notwithstanding the Article 262 bar. The subject matter forced the institutional invention: a river is one physical system administered by several states, and neither exclusive state control nor simple Union control matched the fact. The tribunal, an ad hoc federal arbiter staffed by judges, is the Constitution's answer, another device in the family of differentiated instruments, built for the cases where the two-level scheme runs out.

The same anticipation marks the treatment of inter-state trade. Articles 301 to 307 guarantee freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse throughout the territory of India, subject to restrictions Parliament or a state legislature may impose within stated limits, with Article 304 requiring the President's prior sanction for state bills that discriminate or tax imports from other states. The founders, writing for a subcontinent that colonial policy had divided into customs and tariff fragments, constitutionalised a common market before the phrase became fashionable, and they placed its guardianship partly with the Union and partly, through the sanction requirement, in a negotiated procedure between levels. Read with the fiscal provisions and the river machinery, the trade articles show the federal design operating as economic statecraft: one market, one citizenship, one judiciary, laid over a deliberately variegated political map. The variegation is where the citizens' daily governments sit, in the states with their exclusive lists and their special provisions, and the unity is where the common frame holds. Holding both facts in view at once is the entire discipline the quasi-federal label discourages and the asymmetric reading teaches.

## The Third Tier: Panchayats, Municipalities and the Exemptions of 1992

The Seventy-Third and Seventy-Fourth Amendments of 1992, effective in April and June 1993 respectively, constitutionalised a third order of elected government, the panchayats of Part IX and the municipalities of Part IXA, and their fine print may be the single most instructive exhibit in this article. A uniform devolution would have applied the new parts across the whole territory. The amendments did the opposite. Article 243M exempts Nagaland, Meghalaya and Mizoram from Part IX altogether unless their assemblies resolve to adopt it, exempts the Scheduled Areas and tribal areas covered by the Fifth and Sixth Schedules from its ordinary operation, and permits the hill areas of Darjeeling their own council arrangement. Article 243ZC carries parallel exemptions for Part IXA. The Eleventh Schedule's twenty-nine subjects and the Twelfth Schedule's eighteen are indicative lists from which states choose what to devolve, so the depth of the third tier varies state by state in law as well as in practice. Kerala's People's Plan Campaign of 1996 devolved a third or more of the state's plan outlay to local bodies through gram sabha planning; other states devolved functions on paper while retaining funds and functionaries. The constitutional floor is common. Everything above the floor is calibrated.

The amendments also reproduced the fiscal asymmetry at the lower level. Articles 243I and 243Y require a State Finance Commission every five years in each state to recommend the distribution of state revenues to panchayats and municipalities, mirroring Article 280's Union-level device, and Article 280 itself was amended to require the Union Finance Commission to recommend measures to augment state consolidated funds for local bodies on the basis of the state commissions' reports. The chain of commissions is the design thinking in miniature: money between levels is to be allocated by periodic expert bodies applying stated criteria, not by the stronger level's annual discretion. When the Fifteenth Finance Commission, constituted in November 2017 under N. K. Singh, took up local-body grants within its terms of reference, it worked inside the same architecture. The third tier therefore strengthens rather than dilutes the article's claim. Even in 1992, four decades after the founding, the republic's instinct when extending democracy downward was to differentiate the extension by territory, exempting precisely the populations whose distinct settlements the founding had already recognised in the schedules and in Article 371A and its Mizoram counterpart.

## Reading the Design From the States' Side: Memoranda and Conclaves

The states have read their own Constitution as federal actors, and their readings constitute evidence no classification can ignore. The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam government of Tamil Nadu appointed the Rajamannar Committee in 1969, whose 1971 report remains the most systematic state-side brief for a looser federation. The Left Front government of West Bengal adopted a memorandum on Centre-State relations in December 1977, circulated to the Union government and the states, demanding that the Union be confined to genuinely national subjects and that Article 356 be effectively disarmed. The Shiromani Akali Dal's Anandpur Sahib Resolution, adopted in 1973 and confirmed at the Ludhiana session of 1978, demanded a genuine federal structure with autonomy for the states in all subjects other than foreign relations, defence, currency and communications, a formulation that consciously echoed the Cabinet Mission's three-subject Union of 1946. In 1983, opposition chief ministers met in conclaves at Vijayawada, Bengaluru and Srinagar to press federal demands jointly, and the political pressure of that season formed the immediate background to the Union government's appointment of the Sarkaria Commission in June 1983.

These documents matter to the adjudication because they show the units behaving as the asymmetric reading predicts. None of the memoranda treated the Constitution as a unitary document to be escaped; each treated it as a federal bargain to be rebalanced, and each argued from the text, from the lists, from the Finance Commission's role and from the founding debates. The Anandpur Sahib Resolution's echo of the Cabinet Mission scheme is the clearest instance: a state-level movement reached back to the founding's own discarded alternative and claimed it as constitutional memory. Wheare's framework has difficulty explaining why supposedly provincial governments inside a unitary state produce federal theory of this quality and persistence. The asymmetric reading explains it directly: the states are constitutional orders with resources of text, precedent and institutional memory, and their periodic renegotiation campaigns are the federation working as designed, through argument rather than through secession. The Union's responses, commissions of inquiry, revised formulas, the Bommai discipline on Article 356, belong to the same pattern. Renegotiation is not the failure mode of the Indian federation. It is the operating system the founders installed, visible in 1949 in the concurrence clause and in 1983 in the conclaves alike.

One further paragraph belongs to the lists themselves, because the debate over their entries never closed. When the Goods and Services Tax subsumed state taxes on sale of goods, the states surrendered their most buoyant exclusive revenue source in exchange for the council's weighted votes and a compensation guarantee of fourteen percent annual growth for five years written into the Compensation to States Act of 2017. Commentators who read the bargain as centralisation and those who read it as pooled sovereignty divide along the line this article has drawn throughout. The compensation clause is instructive: the states priced their surrender, in a statute, against a stated growth rate, and the Union paid the price through the transition. Bargains with prices are struck between parties that hold assets. The states held the asset, the entry in the State List, because the founders had put it there in 1949, and the history of the asset's defence and sale is as good a summary as any of what federalism in the Indian Constitution has meant in practice.

## From Planning Commission to NITI Aayog: The Bargaining Forum Rebuilt

On 1 January 2015 the Union government replaced the Planning Commission with NITI Aayog, the National Institution for Transforming India, again by cabinet resolution rather than by statute, and the substitution restaged the federal argument in a new vocabulary. The resolution constituting the new body named cooperative federalism as its operating principle and created a Governing Council composed of the Prime Minister, the chief ministers of all states and the lieutenant governors of the Union Territories, meeting to deliberate on national development priorities with the states seated as members rather than as supplicants for plan approval. The change mattered because the old commission's authority had rested on the Union's control of plan transfers: states came to Delhi to negotiate the size of their annual plans, and the negotiation, however institutionalised through the Gadgil formula, ran downhill from the holder of the funds. The Fourteenth Finance Commission's award, raising untied devolution to 42 percent of the divisible pool for the period from 2015, shrank the discretionary stream the planning body had administered, and the abolition of the plan and non-plan distinction in the Union budget from 2017 to 2018 completed the shift. What remained for the new institution was the forum function: teams and task forces of state and Union officials, indices ranking states on health, education and water management, and the Governing Council's periodic sessions, the first held on 8 February 2015.

Critics of the substitution, including chief ministers who recorded their objections in the Governing Council's early meetings and economists who had served the old commission, charged that a think tank without transfer authority leaves the states with voice and without leverage, and that the Union's growing reliance on cesses and surcharges, which under Article 271 are not shared with the states, rebuilds by fiscal technique the dependence that devolution had reduced. Defenders of the arrangement, including the Union finance ministry in its budget presentations of the period, answered that untied devolution at 42 percent is a larger and freer resource than the tied plan transfers it replaced, and that ranking and deliberation are the instruments a mature federation uses between Finance Commission awards. The dispute is recorded here without a verdict because its terms, once again, are the federation's own: both sides argue from devolution shares, weighted council votes and the text of Article 271, and both sides treat the states as principals whose consent and capacity the Union must engage. Institutions outside the Constitution have carried the federal system's bargaining load since the cabinet resolution of March 1950 created the first of them. The load-bearing habit, not any single institution, is the continuity, and it descends from the founders' decision to build a Union strong enough to bargain from the centre and states strong enough to bargain back.

## The Governor in the Design: Agent, Arbiter or Fault Line

No office concentrates the federation's tensions like the Governor, and the founding debates on the office display the founders choosing, with open eyes, a centrally appointed head for each state over the elected alternative. The Provincial Constitution Committee and the Assembly debated election against appointment in May and June 1949, and the appointment model carried the argument on grounds B. R. Ambedkar and others stated: an elected governor, armed with a personal mandate, would rival the chief minister in a parliamentary system where real executive power sits with the council of ministers, while an appointed governor could serve as a constitutional sentinel and a link between the state and the Union without a competing electoral base. Articles 153 to 162 build the office on that reasoning. The Governor is appointed by the President under Article 155, holds office during the President's pleasure under Article 156 with a stated term of five years, acts on the aid and advice of the council of ministers under Article 163 save in matters where the Constitution requires discretion, assents to or reserves bills under Article 200, and reports under Article 356 in the contingency that has defined the office's public reputation.

The record of the office since 1950 divides into an institutional history and a controversy history, and the Sarkaria Commission worked through both between 1983 and 1988. Sarkaria's recommendations on the office remain the standard corrective: the Governor should be a person of eminence, from outside the state, detached from active politics, appointed after consultation with the chief minister, and not removed before the term except for stated cause. The Punchhi Commission of 2010 added a fixed tenure and an impeachment-like removal procedure by the state legislature. The Supreme Court supplied legal discipline in stages: in B. P. Singhal v Union of India (2010), a Constitution Bench held that removal at pleasure is not removal at whim and that a change of government in Delhi is not by itself a ground for displacing a governor, and in Nabam Rebia v Deputy Speaker (2016), arising from the Arunachal Pradesh crisis of 2015 to 2016, the Court read the Governor's discretion narrowly and restored a dismissed state government to office. Read against Wheare, the governorship is indeed a unitary feature lodged inside every state secretariat. Read in the sequence the system itself produced, it is a feature the federation repeatedly disciplines through commissions, conventions urged by its own reviewers and judgments of its own court. The design question the office poses is therefore not whether the founders inserted a central agent into the states. They did, deliberately, in June 1949, for reasons they defended. The question is whether the federation's corrective institutions have kept the agent within the sentinel role the founders described, and the Sarkaria and Punchhi reports exist because the honest answer, decade by decade, has been contested rather than settled.

## A Union Built to Differ

The conventional question, whether India is really federal, dissolves once the founding record is read whole. The Assembly of 1946 to 1949 inherited a weak-centre scheme built to hold a coalition that Partition dissolved, rebuilt the distribution in August 1947 around a strong Union, and then spent its remaining sessions differentiating that Union's terms: concurrence for Kashmir, councils for the Assam hills, development boards and special responsibilities for named regions, a fiscal constitution that sorts by need, a boundary power that lets regions appeal past their capitals, and an amendment procedure that locks the federal features behind state ratification. Every later renegotiation, the linguistic map of 1956, the accord articles of 1962 to 2012, the devolution awards, the tax council of 2016 and the Kashmir orders of 2019, extended or contested settlements of the same kind. The quasi-federal label describes the shadow this structure casts when measured against a federation it never tried to be. The structure itself, read in its debates and its text, is a federation designed to differ among its units, and the differing is the holding together.

The evidence chain, assembled in order, is what a competing account would have to answer. A reconstituted Assembly in August 1947 chose a strong Union and enumerated state exclusivity in the same revision. The Assembly's spokesman in November 1948 grounded the word Union in the absence of a compact and the presence of a single constituent people. The Assembly in September 1949 voted legislative councils for the tribal hills and in October 1949 voted a concurrence regime for Kashmir, differentiating twice in six weeks, on evidence, after debate. The text then entrenched the lists against unilateral central amendment, built a fiscal commission that differentiates by need, and authorised a boundary power the republic has used chiefly to register regional claims. Every element of that chain is a dated act of the founding recorded in the Debates or the text. Classifications that cannot hold the chain should yield to the chain.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Is India a federal country?

Yes. India is a federal country because its Constitution divides legislative, executive and financial power between the Union and the states in a written text neither level can alter alone, gives the states exclusive fields such as police, public order and land, and entrenches the division behind the ratification procedure of Article 368. The Union is strong, holding residuary power, boundary revision and emergency authority, and particular states hold differentiated terms under Articles 370 and 371 and the Sixth Schedule. Those features make the federation asymmetric and centrally weighted. They do not remove the states' constitutional standing, which courts enforce and which no ordinary Union legislation can abolish.

### Q: What is asymmetric federalism in India?

Asymmetric federalism in India is the arrangement under which different states hold different constitutional relationships with the Union by design rather than by administrative favour. Jammu and Kashmir entered under Article 370, which required the state government's concurrence before most Union powers could be extended. The tribal areas of Assam, and later of Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram, received Autonomous District Councils under the Sixth Schedule with legislative power over land and custom. Nagaland and Mizoram hold Article 371A and 371G protections for customary law and land. Maharashtra, Gujarat and Karnataka carry development-board provisions. The variation is written into the constitutional text and its amendments, state by state, settlement by settlement.

### Q: Why is India called quasi-federal?

India is called quasi-federal because K. C. Wheare, in Federal Government, measured the Constitution against a definition of federalism drawn from the United States and Australia, under which the two levels of government must be coordinate and independent within their spheres. Wheare found India departing from that pattern in the Union's residuary power, Parliament's authority to redraw state boundaries, appointed governors, and emergency provisions that let the Centre govern a state directly, and he concluded that India was a unitary state with subsidiary federal features. The label records those centralising provisions accurately. Critics answer that it misclassifies state-specific design, such as Article 370 and the Sixth Schedule, as deviation rather than as a federal technique.

### Q: What is Article 370 of the Indian Constitution?

Article 370 was the provision, adopted by the Constituent Assembly on 17 October 1949, that governed the constitutional relationship between the Union and Jammu and Kashmir on the terms of the state's accession. N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar, who moved the draft article, explained that the state's circumstances were abnormal and that its government must concur in the extension of Union authority. The article limited Parliament's legislative power over the state to the accession subjects of defence, external affairs and communications, together with other subjects the President specified with the state government's concurrence, and it allowed the article to cease operating on the recommendation of the state's Constituent Assembly conveyed by presidential order.

### Q: What is the Sixth Schedule of the Indian Constitution?

The Sixth Schedule is the part of the Constitution, adopted in September 1949 under Article 244(2), that establishes Autonomous District Councils and Regional Councils for the tribal areas first identified in Assam and later extending to areas of Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram. Following the recommendations of the sub-committee chaired by Gopinath Bordoloi, the councils received power to legislate on the allotment and use of land other than reserved forests, forest management outside reserves, shifting cultivation, village administration, inheritance, marriage and divorce, and social customs, with the Governor's assent. The councils also levy specified taxes and constitute village courts. The schedule created a locally elected order of law-making inside the states for named tribal populations.

### Q: What does the Seventh Schedule contain?

The Seventh Schedule contains the three lists that divide legislative subjects between the Union and the states under Articles 245 to 263. The Union List, originally 97 entries, covers defence, foreign affairs, railways, banking, currency and other subjects of national scale, on which Parliament legislates exclusively. The State List, originally 66 entries, covers public order, police, public health, agriculture and land, on which state legislatures legislate exclusively in normal conditions. The Concurrent List, originally 47 entries, covers subjects such as criminal law and marriage on which both levels may legislate, with Article 254 resolving conflicts. The lists are entrenched: amending them requires the ratification procedure in the proviso to Article 368.

### Q: Why did Patel and Ambedkar want a strong centre?

Patel and Ambedkar wanted a strong centre for reasons the founding record states. Vallabhbhai Patel, conducting the integration of more than five hundred princely states as Minister for States from 1947, treated a capable Union government as the instrument that held the territory together and negotiated accessions unit by unit. B. R. Ambedkar, introducing the Draft Constitution on 4 November 1948, argued that the Union must be indestructible, that the federation was not a compact from which states could secede, and that a single citizenship and a strong centre guarded the republic against the fissiparous pressures the Assembly had watched during Partition. Both men nonetheless accepted enumerated state exclusivity and state-specific settlements as parts of the same design.

### Q: Does a state legislature have to consent before Parliament redraws its boundaries under Article 3?

No. Article 3 requires that a bill affecting a state's area, boundaries or name be introduced only on the President's recommendation and be referred to that state's legislature for its views within a period the President fixes, but the legislature's consent is not required and its views do not bind Parliament. The Supreme Court confirmed the position in Babulal Parate v State of Bombay in 1960. The Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act of February 2014 illustrates the rule at full strength: the Andhra Pradesh Legislative Assembly resolved against the bifurcation in January 2014, the reference procedure was completed, and Parliament enacted the creation of Telangana with effect from 2 June 2014 regardless.

### Q: What is the Inter-State Council under Article 263, and when was it established?

The Inter-State Council is the consultative body Article 263 empowers the President to create for inquiring into disputes between states, investigating subjects of common interest to the Union and the states, and recommending better coordination of policy. Although the power existed from 1950, the council was first established by presidential order on 28 May 1990, after the Sarkaria Commission's report of January 1988 recommended a permanent council as a standing forum for Centre-State consultation. The council brings the Prime Minister, chief ministers and Union ministers into one body, and a standing committee of ministers has handled its preparatory work since 1996. Its authority is advisory: it manages federal friction politically rather than deciding disputes as a court would.

### Q: How does Article 254 settle a conflict between Union and state laws on the Concurrent List?

Article 254 provides that where a state law on a Concurrent List subject conflicts with a law Parliament is competent to enact, the Union law prevails and the state law is void to the extent of the repugnancy. The article carries a deliberate asymmetry with a state-side opening: under clause 2, a state law reserved for the President's consideration and receiving the President's assent prevails within that state notwithstanding the conflict, although Parliament may legislate again on the matter, including by adding to, amending or repealing the state law. The rule therefore defaults to the Union while allowing a state, through the assent route, to sustain a divergent law for its own territory until Parliament chooses to override it expressly.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide in S. R. Bommai v Union of India about Article 356?

In S. R. Bommai v Union of India, decided on 11 March 1994, a nine-judge bench of the Supreme Court held that a presidential proclamation under Article 356 dismissing a state government is subject to judicial review on grounds including mala fides and irrelevant considerations, that the majority of a state ministry must be tested on the floor of the legislative assembly rather than assessed by the Governor, and that secularism forms part of the Constitution's basic structure, so a state government acting against secular principles could be removed while a proclamation struck down could lead to the dismissed government's restoration. The judgment sharply reduced the routine use of President's Rule in the decades that followed.

### Q: What did the Sarkaria Commission recommend on Centre-State relations?

The Sarkaria Commission, appointed in June 1983 under Justice R. S. Sarkaria and reporting in January 1988, examined the working of Centre-State relations after years of complaint by state governments and recommended restraint rather than redesign. Its principal recommendations included establishing a permanent Inter-State Council under Article 263, using Article 356 rarely and only as a last resort after warning the state, appointing governors who were eminent, detached from active politics and from outside the state, consulting the chief minister on a governor's appointment, and allowing states a larger and more predictable share of revenues. The commission largely defended the constitutional allocation of powers while disciplining the manner in which the Centre exercised its advantages inside that allocation.

### Q: How does the Goods and Services Tax Council under Article 279A take decisions?

The Goods and Services Tax Council, created by the Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act of 2016, is chaired by the Union Finance Minister and includes the Union Minister of State for Finance and the finance ministers of every state. Article 279A weights the voting: the Union's vote carries one-third of the total weight, the states together carry two-thirds, and every decision requires a majority of not less than three-fourths of the weighted votes of the members present and voting. The structure means the states collectively can outvote the Centre, the Centre retains a blocking share on any proposal, and tax changes for the national market require a negotiated consensus between the two levels rather than a unilateral act by either.

### Q: What special provisions does Article 371A give Nagaland?

Article 371A, inserted by the Thirteenth Amendment of 1962 as Nagaland moved to statehood on 1 December 1963, provides that no Act of Parliament concerning Naga religious or social practices, Naga customary law and procedure, the administration of civil and criminal justice involving decisions according to Naga customary law, or the ownership and transfer of land and its resources applies to Nagaland unless the Nagaland Legislative Assembly so resolves. The article constitutionalised the Sixteen Point Agreement of July 1960 between the Government of India and the Naga People's Convention. It also gave the Governor a special responsibility for law and order while internal disturbance continued in the Naga Hills, exercised after consultation with the council of ministers.

### Q: Why does the Constitution place residuary legislative power with the Union under Article 248?

Article 248 gives Parliament exclusive power to legislate on any matter not enumerated in the Concurrent List or the State List, and entry 97 of the Union List repeats the grant. The Constituent Assembly chose the Canadian allocation deliberately: the British North America Act of 1867 had placed residuary power with the federal Parliament, and the founders, redesigning the federal scheme after Partition had dissolved the Cabinet Mission's weak-centre plan, preferred a centre able to legislate on subjects the lists had not foreseen, from new technologies to new forms of taxation. B. R. Ambedkar defended the choice in debate against the American model of the Tenth Amendment, under which residuary power rests with the states or the people.

### Q: What were special category states, and what fiscal advantages did the status carry?

Special category status was a fiscal classification applied from 1969, on criteria associated with D. R. Gadgil and adopted by the National Development Council, to states marked by hilly terrain, low density, strategic borders, backwardness and non-viable finances, beginning with Assam, Nagaland and Jammu and Kashmir and growing to eleven states. The status carried a grant-to-loan ratio of 90 to 10 for central assistance, against 70 to 30 for general category states, priority in plan allocations, and tax concessions intended to attract industry. The Fourteenth Finance Commission's higher general devolution from 2015 changed the context, and the Union stated that the status would not be extended to new claimants, a position Andhra Pradesh contested after the 2014 bifurcation.

### Q: What role do Governors play in Centre-State relations under Articles 200 and 201?

Under Article 200, a bill passed by a state legislature is presented to the Governor, who may assent to it, withhold assent, return it for reconsideration if it is not a money bill, or reserve it for the consideration of the President. Under Article 201, the President may assent to or withhold assent from a reserved bill, or direct the Governor to return it to the legislature with a message; if the legislature passes it again, the President is not bound to assent. Because Governors are appointed by the President under Article 155 and hold office during the President's pleasure under Article 156, the reservation power gives the Union a checkpoint over state legislation, and its use has been among the most persistent sources of Centre-State friction in the republic's record.

### Q: How did the events of August 2019 change Article 370's operation?

On 5 August 2019, Constitution Order 272, issued by the President under Article 370(1) with the concurrence of the state government, then represented by the Governor during President's Rule, applied the whole Constitution to Jammu and Kashmir and amended the interpretation of the article so that references to the state's Constituent Assembly were read as references to its Legislative Assembly. The Rajya Sabha adopted the statutory resolution under clause 3 on 5 August and the Lok Sabha on 6 August, after which Constitution Order 273 of 6 August 2019 declared Article 370 inoperative except as modified. The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act then created two Union Territories, Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh, with effect from 31 October 2019.

### Q: What does Article 312 allow Parliament to create, and why is it a federal question?

Article 312 allows Parliament, if the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution by a two-thirds majority of members present and voting declaring it necessary or expedient in the national interest, to create one or more All-India Services common to the Union and the states, and to regulate their recruitment and conditions of service. The services created under this power, the Indian Administrative Service and the Indian Police Service, staff the senior administration of the states while being recruited, trained and ultimately controlled through Union institutions. The provision is a federal question because the design deliberately runs the states' administrative steel frame through a shared service, and the requirement of a special resolution in the Rajya Sabha, the council of states, is the safeguard the founders placed on the power.

### Q: How did the Forty-Second Amendment of 1976 change the balance between the lists?

The Forty-Second Amendment of 1976, enacted during the Emergency, transferred five subjects from the State List to the Concurrent List: education, forests, weights and measures, protection of wild animals and birds, and the administration of justice, including the constitution and organisation of courts below the Supreme Court and the High Courts. The transfers enlarged the field on which Parliament could legislate over subjects states had previously held exclusively, and they were carried through the ratification procedure the proviso to Article 368 requires for Seventh Schedule changes. The structure of three lists survived the amendment intact, and the subjects at the core of state government, police, public order, land and public health, remained in the State List.
